## Summary

This [[ShangyeJiushiZheyang|商业就是这样]] episode examines [[Starbucks]] China's plan to invest more than RMB 300 million in service hours, staffing, equipment, older-store refurbishment, seating, and breakfast capability. It argues that the plan is a localized version of “Back to Starbucks”: Chinese store economics may favor labor and service investment over copying North America's heavier asset model, while the chain must balance expansion, interaction, and profitability. The episode recasts the [[CoffeehousePublicSphere|third place]] for China as an [[InterestBasedThirdPlace|interest-based community venue]] and treats repeatable local service, rather than store count or discounting alone, as the test of differentiation.

## Key Claims

- Starbucks China plans to add peak-hour staff in nearly half its stores and, together with added bar service in Reserve stores, provide 270,000 more service hours per month; the broader fiscal-2027 plan is reported at more than RMB 300 million.
- The investment also covers mobile point-of-sale devices, bar, seating, lighting, and equipment refurbishment, plus hot breakfast products and baking capability in selected stores.
- The episode interprets the plan as the operating layer of Starbucks China's “one store, one face” localization, following the reported 60% [[BoyuCapital]] and 40% Starbucks ownership structure and an ambition to grow from more than 8,000 to 20,000 China stores.
- North America's “Back to Starbucks” program is presented as a comparison rather than a template: the source reports lighter refurbishment after much higher initial estimates, a USD 500 million service investment, scheduling and order-sequencing changes, and subsequent same-store sales and margin improvement.
- The source estimates that Starbucks China has much lower revenue, profit, and fixed assets per store than North America; it therefore argues that adding labor hours may be more efficient in China than importing the same hardware-heavy program.
- [[CoffeeChainScaleServiceProfitTriangle|store scale, interactive service, and profitability]] are framed as difficult to maximize together. [[LuckinCoffee]] prioritizes speed, convenience, and price through pickup-oriented stores, while Starbucks is presented as accepting slower expansion and near-term margin pressure to repair experience.
- Direct price competition would weaken Starbucks's distinctiveness because low prices are easy to measure but can reduce loyalty; service and brand returns are slower and harder to attribute.
- In China, the third place must compete with free public space, malls, private rooms, and mobile-mediated social life. Starbucks's partnerships around pets, crafts, cycling, running, reading, and music suggest a shift from proximity-based neighborhood community toward interest-based gathering.
- Interest communities require store-level tools, events, and judgment and are therefore harder to reproduce through headquarters standards alone.
