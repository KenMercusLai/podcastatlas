## Summary
This [[HiddenBrain]] episode follows [[AbhijitBanerjee]] from childhood doubts about simple poverty stories to experimental [[DevelopmentEconomics]]. Teacher, vaccination, cash-transfer, and malaria-bed-net cases show how [[RandomizedControlledTrials]] can reject plausible interventions, identify workable ones, and support [[InterventionMechanismLearning]] rather than merely rank policies. The episode also limits its own method: experiments inherit framing choices, and prestige does not remove the need for [[ExpertUncertaintyCommunication]] or [[RationalHumility]].

## Key Claims
- Banerjee's encounters with talented children living in a Kolkata slum made both helpless-victim and deserved-poverty stories seem inadequate.
- Adding a second teacher in rural Rajasthan and halving class size in a later Mumbai study did not improve measured learning when instruction continued to follow a syllabus that many pupils could not access.
- Later level-matched instruction produced much larger gains, supporting [[RemedialEducationTargeting]] rather than a simple resources-only account.
- Reliable vaccination access increased full vaccination from about 6 percent to 17 percent in the episode's Udaipur account, while a small lentil incentive produced a stronger response by changing the immediate reason to act.
- The source reports that cash transfers did not reduce recipients' work and that charging for malaria bed nets did not predict their use, challenging laziness and pay-to-value assumptions.
- Experiments can improve theory by revealing mechanisms behind success or failure, making [[InterventionMechanismLearning]] part of [[EvidenceBasedDevelopmentPolicy]].
- Random assignment does not guarantee a complete answer because researchers still choose the question, intervention, outcomes, and alternatives that enter the design.
