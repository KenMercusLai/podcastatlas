# 591. The Assassination of Abraham Lincoln: Manhunt for the Killer (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode reconstructs [[JohnWilkesBooth|John Wilkes Booth]]'s shooting of [[AbrahamLincoln|Abraham Lincoln]] at Ford's Theatre, Lincoln's death and national funeral, the attempted killings of [[AndrewJohnson|Andrew Johnson]] and William Seward, and the manhunt that ended when [[BostonCorbett|Boston Corbett]] shot Booth at the Garrett farm. It presents the [[LincolnAssassinationConspiracy|Lincoln assassination conspiracy]] as a real multi-target plot supported by Confederate-sympathizer networks while keeping claims of authorization by Jefferson Davis or the Confederate high command unproved.

The episode also interprets consequences rather than treating the crime only as a chase narrative. Booth's Brutus fantasy, theatrical self-dramatization, and white supremacy helped turn Lincoln into the martyr Booth failed to anticipate. A closing [[HistoricalCounterfactualReasoning|counterfactual]] rejects the certainty that Lincoln's survival would have secured racial equality: presidential skill mattered, but congressional conflict, entrenched white Southern resistance, paramilitary violence, and Northern unwillingness to sustain long military rule remained powerful constraints.

## Key Claims

- Booth used his knowledge of *Our American Cousin* to time the shot for a laugh line, wounded Major Henry Rathbone, escaped despite a fractured leg, and was quickly identified.
- Conflicting accounts of Booth's jump, spur, and shouted words show why chaotic eyewitness testimony should be ranked rather than forced into one seamless scene.
- The plot aimed beyond Lincoln: George Atzerodt was assigned to kill Johnson, while Lewis Powell attacked Seward and wounded several people.
- Confederate-sympathizer networks helped Booth and David Herold escape, but the episode finds no decisive evidence that Davis or formal Confederate leadership ordered the plot.
- Doctors moved the mortally wounded Lincoln to the Petersen House, where he died at 7:22 a.m.; public mourning, Black military participation, and the funeral train made his death a national political ritual.
- Booth's journal cast him as Brutus or William Tell, yet his killing strengthened Lincoln's martyr memory and harmed the cause he claimed to defend.
- Lincoln's survival might have changed leadership and coalition management, but it would not by itself have removed congressional conflict, white-supremacist resistance, the likely rise of groups such as the Ku Klux Klan, or the limits of Northern commitment.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode strengthens the existing view that Lincoln's political skill could have mattered while rejecting certainty that his survival would have secured a successful multiracial Reconstruction.
- Eyewitness accounts disagree over Booth's spur, fall, and shouted words; the source preserves the disagreement rather than selecting a definitive version.
- Confederate-sympathizer assistance is distinguished from proof that Davis or the Confederate high command authorized the conspiracy.
- The peephole's origin, reported dialogue and last words, precise motives, the knowledge of individual helpers, Mary Lincoln's disputed removal of White House property, and the full effect of Lincoln's death remain episode-attributed, contested, or counterfactual.
