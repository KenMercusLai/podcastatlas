# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》317｜“牛郎织女”发源地，我在长安找到了

## Summary

This short [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode presents [[KunmingPoolWesternHan|昆明池]] as a multi-purpose [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] project southwest of [[ChangAnHanCapital|长安]]: a reservoir and channel hub, an irrigation and transport work, a naval-training site, a fishery and ritual supplier, and an imperial leisure landscape. It says the pool was named after and modeled on the southwestern Dianchi setting associated with Kunming, then later inspired the Qianlong-era naming and military use of Beijing's Kunming Lake.

The episode's cultural focus is the pair of stone figures placed east and west of the pool and identified as 牵牛 and 织女. Their separation makes the pool a terrestrial image of the Milky Way and supplies early material evidence for imperial monumentalization of [[CowherdAndWeaverGirl|牛郎织女]], but it does not by itself establish one birthplace for the complete modern romance. The source further attributes the pool's eventual disappearance to sedimentation, upstream deforestation, population pressure, and insufficient maintenance after the Tang, while saying the stone figures survived and later received temple worship.

## Key Claims

- Han Wudi's Kunming Pool combined military training, water storage, irrigation, transport, urban drainage, fish production, ritual supply, and imperial recreation.
- The episode says the pool imitated Dianchi after the Han court learned of the southwestern Kunming polity, linking frontier strategy to capital construction.
- Artificial channels around the pool are presented as joining natural waterways south of Chang'an and helping both water supply and drainage.
- Stone figures of 牵牛 and 织女 stood on opposite sides of the pool so that the water represented the Milky Way.
- Those figures support an early material and spatial layer of the Cowherd-Weaver tradition, but the episode's “birthplace” language exceeds what the supplied summary alone can establish.
- The pool's later decline is attributed to sediment, ecological change, population pressure, and maintenance failure; the figures allegedly survived and became objects of temple worship.
- The episode interprets the celestial layout as an expression of Han Wudi's sacral imperial imagination, although that political reading remains the host's inference.

## Key Quotes

> “二石人，牵牛、织女分立池之东西” - the episode's cited formulation for the pool's celestial layout.

> “天河” - the symbolic identity assigned to the water separating the two figures.

- [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] - ruler credited with commissioning and using the pool.
- [[ChangAnHanCapital|长安]] - capital whose southwest water system and imperial landscape included the pool.
- [[CowherdAndWeaverGirl|牛郎织女]] - star figures represented by the poolside sculptures.
- [[QixiFestivalLayering]] - adjacent framework cautioning that one early star-and-landscape layer is not the whole later festival or romance.

## Contradictions

- The title calls the site the legend's “birthplace,” while the existing [[CowherdAndWeaverGirl|牛郎织女]] and [[QixiFestivalLayering]] evidence treats the familiar story as layered and historically unstable. The stone figures are therefore retained as evidence of early monumentalization and celestial pairing, not proof that the complete modern narrative originated only at this site.
- The supplied file is a compact structured summary rather than a transcript, archaeological report, or primary-source edition. The exact construction date and dimensions, “first large artificial lake” label, channel system, statue dating and find context, measurements, temple history, Qianlong imitation, and disappearance chronology remain episode-attributed.
- The interpretation that Han Wudi made himself analogous to the Jade Emperor is explicitly presented as the host's inference, not a demonstrated imperial statement.
- The summary mentions military, hydraulic, productive, ritual, and leisure benefits but does not provide a cost account; the project should not be classified as net-beneficial without evidence on labor, finance, ecological effects, and competing uses.
