# 117. Alexander the Great part 2

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]] from his refusal of a western settlement with [[DariusIII|Darius III]] through the [[BattleOfGaugamela]], the capture and burning of [[Persepolis]], the pursuit of Darius and Bessus, campaigns in Central Asia and India, the army's refusal to continue at the Hyphasis, and Alexander's death in Babylon. It presents continued conquest as a tension between strategic succession to the [[AchaemenidEmpire]] and a personal inability to stop.

The episode's strongest synthesis is [[AlexanderImperialSuccessionStrategy]]: Alexander could not rule the Persian imperial space through Macedonian victory alone, so he retained local elites, adopted royal forms, married into conquered dynasties, incorporated Persian troops, and represented himself as Darius's avenger and heir. Those choices generated Macedonian resistance, while [[AlexanderLegendFormation]] later transformed an uncertain historical record into Roman exemplarity, religious legend, romance, and arguments over whether conquest deserves the name greatness.

## Key Claims

- Alexander rejects Darius's offer of the western empire and defeats a larger Persian force at Gaugamela by exploiting a gap in its line.
- His relatively restrained entry into Babylon and Susa, retention of Persian officials, and later adoption of court forms show conquest becoming a bid to inherit the Achaemenid governing system.
- The burning of Persepolis is secure at event level but disputed in motive: revenge for Xerxes, dynastic rupture, political theater, and the drunken Thaïs story remain competing explanations.
- Darius's death allows Alexander to claim the role of avenger and legitimate successor, making pursuit of Bessus both strategic consolidation and continued expansion.
- Persian dress, proskynesis, marriage, mass weddings, and Persian recruitment broaden the regime while provoking Macedonian fears of displacement and betrayal.
- The Indian campaign, Hyphasis refusal, and disastrous desert return expose the limits of an army and empire organized around continuing conquest.
- Alexander's sudden death without a secure succession settlement helps explain the successor wars, but the episode does not treat the empire's size alone as proof that it could never have survived.
- Late and derivative sources blur history with romance, while Roman emulation and Jewish, Christian, and Islamic traditions make Alexander's afterlife part of his historical significance.

## Key Quotes

> "Alexander would win a fight, but Augustus would win the peace." - the episode's contrast between charismatic conquest and durable political settlement.

> "history and romance" - the hosts' shorthand for the evidentiary problem surrounding Alexander's life and afterlife.

## Connections

- [[AlexanderTheGreat]], [[DariusIII]], and [[BattleOfGaugamela]] - conqueror, defeated king, and decisive battle in the transfer of imperial power.
- [[AchaemenidEmpire]], [[Persepolis]], and [[AlexanderImperialSuccessionStrategy]] - inherited state, symbolically destroyed capital, and integration strategy.
- [[AlexanderLegendFormation]] - transformation of uncertain campaign history into Roman, religious, and romance traditions.
- [[SogdianRock]] - eastern fortress episode connecting this broader campaign account to Alexander's tactical profile.
- [[PtolemyI]] and [[Alexandria]] - successor and city through which Alexander's body and memory later supported dynastic legitimacy.
- [[HistoricalInquirySourceCriticism]] and [[MythAsHistoricalEvidence]] - methods for separating campaign reconstruction from later narrative elaboration.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. The episode qualifies [[154-the-most-disastrous-party-in-history-glt6506112996]] by retaining the drunken Thaïs story as only one explanation for the burning of Persepolis, alongside deliberate revenge and political rupture.
- Army sizes, reported speeches, oversleeping before Gaugamela, the circumstances of Darius's death, court plots, the Hyphasis mutiny, Alexander's final illness, the location of his body, and many religious afterlife identifications remain disputed or source-scoped.
- Claims that Alexander made Christianity or Islamic civilization possible identify broad cultural consequences rather than a single sufficient cause.
