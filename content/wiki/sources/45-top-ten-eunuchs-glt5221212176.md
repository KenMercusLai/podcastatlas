# 45. Top Ten Eunuchs

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode uses ten lives to compare eunuchs and other castrated people across Persian, Roman, Byzantine, Ottoman, European Christian, Russian sectarian, operatic, and Ming settings. Its central synthesis is double-edged: castration was commonly produced through slavery, captivity, punishment, family ambition, imperial desire, or religious extremism, yet exclusion from ordinary descent could also make some survivors unusually trusted courtiers, administrators, commanders, performers, or religious symbols.

The cases resist a single career model. [[Bagoas]] and [[Sporus]] expose court intimacy and unequal power; [[SamsonRowley|Samson Rowley / Hassan Aga]], [[Narses]], and [[ZhengHe|Zheng He]] show violent displacement followed by exceptional imperial authority; [[Farinelli]] reveals the family and institutional economics of castrato music; [[PeterAbelard|Peter Abelard]] shows punitive mutilation reshaping an established intellectual life; and [[BostonCorbett]] and [[KondratiSelivanov|Kondrati Selivanov]] place literalist or sectarian self-castration at the religious edge.

## Key Claims

- Rulers often valued eunuchs because inability to found a biological dynasty or impregnate women could be interpreted as reduced succession risk, making intimate palace access politically useful without eliminating suspicion.
- Eunuch status did not itself create one social role: the episode presents lovers, slaves, courtiers, treasurers, generals, singers, theologians, sect leaders, and maritime commanders.
- [[CastrationCoercionAndMobility]] names the episode's recurring paradox: bodily violence and constrained choice could coexist with later mobility, but advancement does not retroactively make the violence voluntary or benign.
- [[EunuchCourtPower]] depended on institutional design and ruler trust rather than a supposedly uniform eunuch character; Bagoas, Narses, Hassan Aga, and Zheng He operated in different political systems.
- [[Castrati]] joined musical demand, family poverty, childhood surgery, exceptional training, celebrity, and large earnings in a highly unequal winner-take-most system.
- [[ReligiousSelfCastration]] ranged from disputed stories and individual literalism to organized sectarian practice, so Origen, Corbett, and the Skoptsy should not be flattened into one tradition.
- The modern decline of institutional eunuch-making is presented as historically unusual, but the episode's closing comparison with modern gender-affirming surgery is too compressed to erase differences in consent, age, purpose, and medical context.

## Key Quotes

> "trusted insiders and targets of suspicion" — the episode's recurring court paradox, summarized from the discussion.

> "the modern world is unusual" — the hosts' concluding contrast with societies in which eunuch-making was institutionalized.

## Contradictions

- The episode itself doubts the report that Origen castrated himself and notes his later rejection of literal self-castration; the claim remains a contested ancient tradition, not a settled fact.
- Sporus's story substantially qualifies the earlier comic use in [[206-historical-love-island-glt8073929093]] by restoring coercion, post-Neronian transfer, threatened spectacle, and suicide to the profile.
- Annual castration totals in Italy, Skoptsy membership, the identification of particular “last” eunuchs or castrati, reported dialogue, motives, intimate relationships, and detailed career chronology remain episode-attributed or source-scoped.
- The comic countdown format acknowledges suffering but sometimes compresses slavery, child mutilation, sexual violence, and coercion into entertainment; category labels and rankings should not be mistaken for equivalent experiences.
