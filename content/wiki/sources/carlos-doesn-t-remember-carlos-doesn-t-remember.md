# Carlos Doesn’t Remember

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode follows [[CarlosStudent|Carlos]], a gifted low-income student from Lennox whose path into private schooling depended on [[EricEisner]] and the [[YESProgram|YES program]]. Academic ability and scholarships repeatedly proved insufficient against material deprivation, family-care duties, foster placement, school disruption, and the need for a continuing advocate.

Research attributed to [[CarolineHoxby]] and [[ChristopherAvery]] broadens the case beyond one student. The episode argues that many high-achieving low-income students do not apply to selective colleges and that college-stage outreach misses children whose confidence or educational trajectory has already been damaged. It develops [[TalentFragilityUnderDisadvantage]], [[EarlyOpportunityIntervention]], [[CollegeApplicationUndermatch]], and [[SecondChancePrivilege]] as connected mechanisms rather than an inspirational resilience story.

## Key Claims

- Gladwell defines social “capitalization” as the share of people able to realize their potential and uses Carlos to argue that talent realization depends on protection and navigation as well as ability.
- The YES program identified Carlos in fifth grade, provided tutoring and advocacy, and used relationships to place him in a private elementary school.
- A full scholarship to an elite boarding school did not solve Carlos's constraints because his mother expected him to remain home and help care for his younger sister.
- Carlos and his sister later became wards of Los Angeles County, moved through approximately four foster homes, were separated temporarily, and lost contact with Eisner and Alina Barof for about eighteen months.
- Hoxby and Avery are reported to have estimated that roughly 35,000 U.S. students per year scored at or above the 90th percentile while living in families earning less than $40,000.
- Many such students reportedly did not apply to selective colleges even when those colleges might cost less than local alternatives.
- Gladwell argues that privilege supplies recovery capacity: interruption is less likely to become permanently disqualifying when families can provide money, stability, information, advocacy, and repeated chances.
