# 414. The Peasants’ Revolt: London’s Burning (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows the [[PeasantsRevolt1381|Peasants’ Revolt of 1381]] from the rebels’ entry into London through the Mile End concessions, the Tower executions, [[WatTyler|Wat Tyler’s]] death at Smithfield, and the wider suppression. It presents the rising as a [[PopularRevoltCoalition|coalition]] rather than a single-class program: opposition to serfdom and taxation combined with [[RebelRoyalism|loyalty to the young king]], religious radicalism, attacks on records and monopolies, urban commercial rivalries, and xenophobic murder.

The episode makes [[RichardIIOfEngland|Richard II’s]] conduct after Tyler’s fall its central political scene. His decision to ride toward armed rebels, claim their allegiance, and lead them away helped prevent immediate bloodshed, but his concessions were later revoked and the revolt’s formal program failed. Its clearest institutional legacy was narrower: poll taxes became politically toxic, and the experience may have strengthened Richard’s belief in royal charisma while warning government that the commons could not be pressed without limit.

## Key Claims

- The revolt attacked servitude, forced labor, poll taxation, legal records, monopolies, tolls, corrupt officials, and parts of the church hierarchy without forming one coherent modern ideology.
- Rebels could destroy symbols and machinery of lordship while remaining royalists who preferred direct dependence on Richard to rule through magnates, abbeys, and ministers.
- The burning of charters, court rolls, and chancery records was [[PoliticalRecordDestruction|political action]] against the documents through which debt, status, and authority were enforced.
- London participation broadened the rising but also enabled commercial rivals and political elites to use popular disorder for targeted score-settling.
- Richard granted major concessions at Mile End, then used personal courage and royal authority to defuse the Smithfield crisis after William Walworth struck Tyler.
- The uprising also involved prison-breaking, summary executions, anti-Flemish violence, and attacks on sanctuary, complicating heroic or progressive memory.
- Although serfdom, monopolies, church property, and official structures survived, the disappearance of poll taxes gave the failed revolt a durable policy legacy.

## Key Quotes

> “They shall have no captain but him.” — the episode’s account of Richard claiming leadership of the rebels after Tyler fell.

> “Lovers of truth and justice rather than thieves.” — the episode’s description of the rebels’ moral self-presentation.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with the later Richard II source was found; this episode supplies the detailed 1381 basis for that source’s claim that the revolt shaped Richard’s fear of disorder and confidence in sacred kingship.
- Richard’s sincerity at Mile End, the planning behind Smithfield, the rebels’ entry into the Tower, the influence of Wycliffite ideas, and the role of London insiders remain uncertain or interpretive.
- The revolt’s later radical, Protestant, American, free-market, and Thatcherite associations are retained as contested memory and analogy, not established direct lines of descent.
