# 389. The Fall of the Aztecs: The Night of Tears (Part 6)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode follows [[HernanCortes|Hernan Cortes]] from the catastrophic [[NocheTriste|Noche Triste]] retreat through recovery in [[Tlaxcala]], the smallpox epidemic, and preparations for the [[SiegeOfTenochtitlan|siege of Tenochtitlan]]. It explains the renewed campaign through Indigenous coalition politics, reinforcements, strategic terror, disease, and control of towns and supply routes rather than Spanish arms alone. The result is a transition from failed escape to an attritional war whose violence falls heavily on civilians.

## Key Claims

- The June 30-July 1, 1520 escape collapsed when the alarm was raised, Mexica forces attacked from canoes, and a makeshift bridge failed under people, horses, cannon, and treasure.
- Cortes survived the loss of hundreds of Spaniards, many Indigenous allies, artillery, horses, and most of the gold because Tlaxcalan leaders chose to renew a mutually instrumental alliance.
- The campaign then became a deliberate war of terror, enslavement, destruction, and supply denial intended both to weaken Tenochtitlan and to preserve Spanish leverage within a predominantly Indigenous coalition.
- Smallpox, probably introduced through Narvaez's expedition, spread from the coast through Tlaxcala and Tenochtitlan, killing [[Cuitlahuac]] and compounding mortality through hunger and social disruption.
- [[Cuauhtemoc]] succeeded Cuitlahuac as Mexica ruler while Cortes seized strategic towns, accepted reinforcements, occupied Texcoco, and prepared brigantines for Lake Texcoco.
- Cortes's later narrative benefited from the deaths of eyewitnesses and downplayed the scale and agency of Tlaxcalan, Texcocan, and other Indigenous allies.

## Key Quotes

> "night of tears" - the episode's English rendering of the remembered Spanish disaster known as the Noche Triste.

> "we lack nothing" - the reported answer that turns the survival of shipbuilder Martin Lopez into a symbol of Cortes's renewed campaign.

## Connections

- [[NocheTriste]] - failed Spanish-led withdrawal whose losses transformed but did not end the campaign.
- [[HernanCortes]] - commander who turned military collapse into a renewed coalition war.
- [[Tlaxcala]] - Indigenous polity whose continued alliance made recovery possible.
- [[Cuitlahuac]] - Mexica successor to Moctezuma who opposed the alliance and died during the smallpox epidemic.
- [[Cuauhtemoc]] - successor who inherited the defense as the siege approached.
- [[Malinche]] - interpreter who survived the retreat and remained part of the campaign.
- [[MexicaEmpire]] - polity that defeated the escape but then faced epidemic disease and a reorganized coalition.
- [[SpanishConquestOfMexico]] - broader process joining coalition politics, terror, disease, logistics, and narrative control.
- [[ColumbianExchange]] - biological process represented by smallpox's spread and social effects.
- [[SiegeOfTenochtitlan]] - coming campaign prepared through isolation, strategic towns, and the construction of brigantines.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with [[390-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-war-to-the-death-part-7-glt1796642529|Part 7]] or [[391-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-last-emperor-part-8-glt2984940365|Part 8]] was found; this episode supplies the retreat, epidemic, coalition recovery, and campaign preparation that precede them.
- The estimate of roughly six hundred Spanish deaths, the one-third mortality claim for Tlaxcala, the water-fetcher's alarm, the weeping-tree scene, speeches, and remembered dialogue remain source-scoped.
- The episode attributes smallpox's arrival to Narvaez's party as the probable route, not a demonstrated certainty, and does not supply an independent epidemiological reconstruction.
- Claims about ritualized Mexica war, Cortes's inner motives, sacrifice, cannibalism, and the deliberate shaping of later memory are interpretations or mediated reports rather than uncontested facts.
