# The Tipping Point Revisited: An Excerpt

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] excerpt previews the opening chapter of [[MalcolmGladwell]]'s *Revenge of the Tipping Point* through Los Angeles's bank-robbery surge. The Yankee Bandit, takeover crews, and the organization built by [[RobertSheldonBrown|Robert Sheldon Brown (“Casper”)]] and [[DonzellThompson|Donzell Thompson (“Sea Dog”)]] support an account in which a small number of repeat offenders changed, scaled, and transmitted a criminal practice.

The comparison with New York then turns the story into a boundary problem. [[JohnWennberg]]'s research on geographically clustered medical practice and low vaccination rates around Waldorf schools support [[SmallAreaVariation]] and [[BoundedSocialContagion]]: behavior can spread intensely within a local professional or social community while stopping sharply at its edge.

## Key Claims

- Los Angeles bank robberies rose from simple note-passing toward coordinated armed takeovers, with successive offenders copying and modifying visible methods.
- [[RobertSheldonBrown|Casper]] and [[DonzellThompson|Sea Dog]] separated planning from execution, recruited replaceable crews, and reportedly organized 175 robberies in four years.
- The decline after their arrests is consistent with a small number of organizers sustaining the outbreak, but the episode does not isolate arrests from other changes affecting robbery.
- New York's failure to develop a comparable epidemic despite Willie Sutton's fame suggests that exposure to an influential example is insufficient without receptive local conditions.
- [[JohnWennberg]] found large, stable differences in procedure rates among nearby medical-service areas that could not be explained only by illness prevalence or patient demand.
- Waldorf-school vaccination clusters illustrate how a belief can be uncommon across a state yet concentrated within particular institutions and communities.
- [[BoundedSocialContagion]] joins influential actors to local opportunity and group norms; none of those elements alone explains where a social epidemic begins, scales, or stops.
