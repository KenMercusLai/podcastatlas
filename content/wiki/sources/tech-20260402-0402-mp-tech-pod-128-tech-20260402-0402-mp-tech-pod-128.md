## Summary
This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode has [[MeganMcCartyCorino|Megan McCarty-Carino]] interview [[AmirRashidi]] of [[MianGroup]] about [[Iran]]'s wartime internet blackout and the [[NationalInformationNetwork|National Information Network]]. Rashidi argues that the blackout is not a total connectivity failure: domestic services on the state-controlled network can still work, while global internet access, independent news, and social media are cut off.

The episode's main synthesis is that [[DomesticNetworkSovereignty]] can preserve selected local services while isolating people from outside information. It also shows how [[DomesticServiceCensorship]] and [[InternetBlackoutPublicSafetyRisk]] turn censorship from an online speech issue into a problem for war alerts, medical access, police checks, and civil coordination.

## Key Claims
- [[Iran]] is described as experiencing the longest internet blackout in its history while U.S. and Israeli airstrikes continue into their fifth week.
- [[AmirRashidi]] says Iranians still have access to the [[NationalInformationNetwork|National Information Network]], or NIN, a domestic network controlled by the regime.
- The NIN allows some domestic services to function, including local delivery-style apps, but blocks access to [[Google]], social media, and international news sites such as [[BBC]] and [[CNN]].
- Rashidi says Iran has domestic equivalents for services such as email and search, but a search for "war" on the national search engine returns no results.
- The episode uses [[DomesticServiceCensorship]] to show that censorship is embedded inside approved domestic platforms, not only at the border between Iran and the global internet.
- Rashidi says Ukrainian-style strike reporting and alert apps are not technically possible on the NIN because the government is sensitive about bombing, explosions, and war information.
- [[MahsaAlert]] was developed by the internet freedom community outside Iran, but it still needs internet access, so the blackout itself limits the workaround.
- Rashidi cites medical-access problems and a police license-plate check failure as examples of [[InternetBlackoutPublicSafetyRisk]].
- VPNs, satellite internet, Starlink, and local communication tools can help some people, but Rashidi says the resources needed to support roughly 90 million people would be much larger.
- Rashidi says the government would frame the shutdown as protecting people, but he interprets it as an attempt to control the wartime narrative and prevent mobilization.
