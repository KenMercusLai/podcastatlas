# 373-培根是不是在四百年前已经预想出了硅谷的技术乌托邦？《新大西岛》

## Summary

This [[DushuBuchenglin|独树不成林]] episode reads [[FrancisBacon|弗朗西斯·培根]]'s [[NewAtlantis|《新大西岛》]] less as a catalogue of technological predictions than as an institutional thought experiment. [[SalomonsHouse|所罗门之宫]] organizes specialized research, global knowledge collection, experimentation, invention, secrecy, and public advice, making science a durable collective enterprise rather than the work of isolated geniuses.

The same design creates a governance problem. The institution can choose research priorities, decide which discoveries to publish or conceal, authenticate religious and empirical claims, and sometimes withhold information from government. The episode connects that [[EpistemicInfrastructurePower]] to modern technology firms and uses [[ScientificInstitutionSelfGovernance]] to ask whether internal ethics can substitute for external scrutiny when governments and publics depend on expertise and infrastructure they cannot readily reproduce.

## Key Claims

- Bacon rejects authority-centered scholastic reasoning in favor of observation, experiment, comparison, error exclusion, and induction, while warning that habit, language, and bias distort inquiry.
- *New Atlantis* combines political closure with scientific openness: Bensalem restricts ordinary foreign contact but periodically sends knowledge collectors abroad.
- Salomon's House anticipates [[OrganizedInstitutionalScience]] through specialized roles, laboratories, cumulative experimentation, analysis, invention, and public forecasting.
- The institution's control over research agendas, disclosure, secrecy, and verification turns knowledge and infrastructure into political power.
- Scientific and technical advancement coexists with religious, familial, and social conservatism; innovation in one domain does not imply general social liberalization.
- Internal moral commitments against deception are an incomplete safeguard when the same experts can manufacture illusions and determine what counts as authentic.
- Modern governments may regulate technology firms while also depending on their cloud, AI, cybersecurity, and defense capacities, creating asymmetric interdependence rather than a simple regulator–regulated relationship.
