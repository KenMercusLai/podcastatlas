# 72. The Vietnam War

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] conversation with [[AndrewPreston|Andrew Preston]] places the Vietnam War inside French colonialism, decolonization, Cold War alliance logic, and a major intra-Vietnamese civil war. It argues that familiar American images and political memories become more accurate when Vietnamese agency is restored: the famous Saigon rooftop was a CIA facility rather than the U.S. Embassy, and the napalm strike that injured Kim Phuc was carried out by South Vietnamese forces.

The episode also assesses intervention and withdrawal. It describes U.S. conduct as a form of [[AmericanImperialSelfDenial|“anti-imperial imperialism”]], judges the intervention illegitimate and unwinnable on acceptable terms, and connects [[LyndonBJohnson|Lyndon B. Johnson]]'s reluctant escalation, [[RichardNixon|Richard Nixon]]'s withdrawal problem, anti-war politics, unusually open press access, and film into a single argument about [[VietnamWarCivilWarAgency]], [[PhotographicWitness]], and [[VietnamWarAmericanMemory]].

## Key Claims

- French recolonization after 1945 and U.S. support for France connected decolonization to the emerging Cold War before direct American intervention.
- Wartime cooperation between [[HoChiMinh|Ho Chi Minh]]'s Viet Minh and the American OSS did not survive the hardening anti-communism of 1947–1948.
- The [[FirstIndochinaWar]] ended with the French defeat at Dien Bien Phu and a divided Vietnam, after which the United States backed the non-communist South.
- The conflict had a major civil-war dimension: North Vietnamese, South Vietnamese, and Viet Cong actors pursued their own projects rather than serving only as proxies for outside powers.
- American officials thought they could avoid French mistakes by drawing on South Korea, Taiwan, West Germany, Malaya, and the Philippines, but underestimated communist-nationalist commitment and capability.
- Preston argues that the United States could not win on acceptable terms; even very large deployments promised long war and uncertain results, while decisive destruction of North Vietnam would have imposed unacceptable costs.
- Johnson chose escalation while feeling cornered and fearing damage to the Great Society; Nixon inherited a withdrawal in which leaving was strategically justified but endangered South Vietnamese allies.
- Draft exposure, class inequality, racial disproportionality, and the distance between many student protesters and working-class soldiers complicated the anti-war movement's moral and social profile.
- Broad reporter access and limited censorship helped photographs and field reporting expose gaps between official claims and visible events, although iconic images often lost essential context.
- Films and photographs made Vietnam exceptionally powerful in American memory while often keeping Americans, rather than Vietnamese people, at the center.

## Key Quotes

> “anti-imperial imperialism” - Preston's phrase for U.S. officials who rejected an imperial identity while supporting policies with imperial effects.

> “poison chalice” - the episode's description of the withdrawal problem inherited by Nixon.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. The episode extends the later-war accounts in [[324-fall-of-saigon-the-nightmare-begins-part-1-glt6047251362]] and [[325-fall-of-saigon-apocalypse-now-part-2-glt1698435670]] backward into colonial origins, escalation, and wartime media.
- Its Vietnamese-agency argument qualifies narratives that treat the war only as American aggression or Cold War proxy conflict, but it does not erase the scale or moral significance of U.S. intervention.
- Claims about bombing tonnage, force requirements, draft and casualty disproportionality, press access, official motives, hypothetical victory, and individual films remain episode-attributed or source-scoped.
- The source itself remains largely structured through American and British questions, so its call to center Vietnamese actors is stronger than the range of Vietnamese voices directly represented.
