# 194. The First Fascist

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode with [[LucyHughesHallett]] presents [[GabrieleDAnnunzio|Gabriele D'Annunzio]] as a writer, erotic celebrity, media manipulator, nationalist, and man of action who converted literary style into political power. His interventionist speeches, wartime exploits, and occupation of [[Fiume]] joined blood sacrifice, modern technology, elitism, virility, and public spectacle before Mussolini made those elements part of a durable regime.

The episode nevertheless distinguishes precursor from dictator. D'Annunzio's Fiume used balcony dialogue, uniforms, salutes, songs, processions, and leader mythology that became central to [[FascistPoliticalTheatre]], but he preferred dramatic arrival and permanent excitement to taxation, administration, or sustained government. The source therefore argues that D'Annunzio was not straightforwardly a fascist even though [[ItalianFascism|fascism was deeply D'Annunzian]].

## Key Claims

- D'Annunzio deliberately built mass celebrity from adolescence, using journalism, erotic notoriety, literary ambition, and even a false report of his own death to control attention.
- His nationalism grew from the post-Risorgimento ambition to turn a recently unified state into a culturally and emotionally unified people.
- His treatment of women included exploitation and source-reported sexual violence; artistic brilliance, talented partners, and later political importance do not mitigate that conduct.
- His alertness to Nietzsche, futurism, aviation, weapons, speed, and new media helped him turn modernity into a political style rather than a consistent doctrine.
- His 1915 intervention speeches framed war as a purifying sacrifice, but the Treaty of London had already committed the government to an Allied bargain; his clearer role was mobilizing and intimidating public opinion.
- Genuine wartime courage, aviation injury, and the Vienna leaflet flight strengthened his authority as a performer who fused words with physical risk.
- The 1919 occupation of [[Fiume]] became an unstable revolutionary stage joining Italian nationalism, aesthetic utopianism, free-love and anti-money currents, military mutiny, hunger, and ethnic coercion.
- Balcony speeches, call-and-response slogans, black uniforms, straight-armed salutes, songs, and processions made Fiume a laboratory for fascist political theatre.
- [[BenitoMussolini|Mussolini]] feared D'Annunzio's following and borrowed emotional grammar and ritual from him, but built a political machine that D'Annunzio showed little interest in administering.

## Key Quotes

> "a poet, a seducer and a preacher of war" - Hughes-Hallett's compact description of D'Annunzio's linked literary, sexual, and political identities.

> "fascism was definitely D'Annunzio" - the episode's distinction between his personal classification and his influence on fascist style.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode deepens the existing account of D'Annunzio's interventionism and Fiume rather than overturning it.
- Calling D'Annunzio the “first fascist” is treated as a question, not a settled classification: the source separates his innovations in ritual, myth, violence, and mass performance from Mussolini's party organization and governing ambition.
- The episode says his influence on Italy's entry into war looked larger than it was because secret diplomacy preceded his return, while still assigning him a role in public pressure and intimidation.
- Claims about sexual violence, Lenin's reported admiration, precise crowd influence, literary standing, and the counterfactual possibility of a D'Annunzio dictatorship remain attributed to the episode and guest.
