# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》1135丨曹操为什么没有一举歼灭马超？

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode explains why [[CaoCao|曹操]] stopped pursuing [[MaChao|马超]] after the [[WeinanBattleLateHan|渭南之战]]. When Tian Yin and Su Bo rebelled in Hejian, Cao Cao treated disorder near his Hebei political base as more urgent than finishing a distant western enemy, accepting the risk that Ma Chao could recover; the choice extends [[CoreBeforePeripheryStrategy|先内后外式战略顺序]] from simple geography into protection of the regime's center.

The episode then follows the predicted cost. After [[YangFuLateHan|杨阜]] warns that an unfortified Liangzhou will be vulnerable, Ma Chao combines personal military ability, the local standing inherited from [[MaTeng|马腾]], Qiang and Hu support, and reinforcement from [[ZhangLuFivePecks|张鲁]] to regain Longyou and besiege Ji. A parallel institutional branch places Cao Cao's Duke of Wei title, state altars, ancestral temple, and daughters' palace appointments inside a gradual near-sovereign transition while preserving that Wei remained a vassal state within Han at this point.

## Key Claims

- The opening resilience lesson distinguishes calm, problem-oriented action after failure from blind optimism, but its use of “antifragility” remains motivational rather than a demonstrated historical mechanism.
- In 213 CE, Cao Cao's Wei polity establishes altars to soil and grain and an ancestral temple, making territory, sacrifice, lineage, and hereditary continuity publicly legible after his elevation as Duke of Wei.
- The episode treats those institutions and the placement of three Cao daughters in [[EmperorXianOfHan|汉献帝]]'s palace as steps toward a later transfer, while stressing that Wei was still formally a Han vassal state rather than an independent sovereign dynasty.
- The *Zizhi Tongjian* passage looks back from that institutional moment to Cao Cao's pursuit of Ma Chao, Yang Qiu's surrender, and the Tian Yin-Su Bo rebellion in Hejian.
- Cao Cao abandons the near-complete western pursuit because rebellion near Hebei, Ji Province, and Ye threatens the political center more directly and must be suppressed quickly enough to deter wider disorder.
- Yang Fu warns that Ma Chao's combat ability and standing among Qiang and Hu groups will endanger the territories west of Longshan if Cao Cao withdraws without strengthening the frontier.
- After the withdrawal, Ma Chao regains forces, takes control across Longyou, and besieges the Liangzhou seat at Ji, setting up the eight-month siege and failed relief examined in [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1136-ta-yong-ru-hanxin-que-yin-yige-jueding-quanjia-bei-sha-lky3lnnzjqlehbyyxhqfv73xpmtb|Hanji 1136]].
- The episode attributes Ma Chao's recovery jointly to personal command skill, Ma-family regional roots, local ethnic support, and more than ten thousand reinforcements reportedly sent by Zhang Lu under Yang Ang.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The source supplies the strategic prehistory for Hanji 1136: Cao Cao's withdrawal and Ma Chao's recovery precede the later multi-constraint failure to relieve Ji.
- The priority decision is not presented as costless or as proof that the frontier did not matter. Yang Fu's fulfilled warning shows the predictable territorial price of choosing the political center first.
- The supplied file is a structured popular-history summary rather than a transcript, primary-source collation, institutional history, or military study. Dates, offices, force totals, the scale of Qiang and Hu support, Yang Ang's identity and command, exact territorial control, siege duration, ritual sequencing, and Cao Cao's private intentions require broader verification.
- The host's claim that Cao Cao was deliberately preparing every later abdication step is interpretive. The evidence here more securely supports cumulative institutional elevation while Wei remained formally inside the Han order.
- The opening claims about emotional control, optimism, struggle, reconciliation, and antifragility are retained as motivational framing rather than universal causal evidence.
