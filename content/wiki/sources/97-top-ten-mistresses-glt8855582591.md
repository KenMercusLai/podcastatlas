# 97. Top Ten Mistresses

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] countdown compares ten people conventionally remembered as mistresses or royal favourites, using influence rather than romance alone as its loose ranking criterion. From [[NightingaleTuti|the Nightingale / Tuti]] and [[CleopatraVII|Cleopatra VII]] to [[Aspasia]] and [[Malinche]], the episode argues that intimacy, translation, celebrity, patronage, and proximity could give people outside formal office consequential access to rulers and commanders.

The list is deliberately playful rather than definitive. Its strongest historical thread is [[IntimateAccessInformalPower]]: private access can affect dynastic choices, court factions, diplomacy, war, cultural fashion, or public memory, but surviving evidence is often hostile, anecdotal, sexualized, or unable to recover the relationship itself.

## Key Claims

- The ranking favors people who rose from vulnerable or non-royal positions and then gained exceptional historical influence, which pushes Cleopatra down the list because she was already a sovereign.
- Intimate access can become political access without becoming formal authority; the effect ranges from patronage and dynastic recognition to factional conflict, diplomacy, and war.
- “Mistress” is an unstable category: the list includes a reigning queen, a male royal favourite, a performer linked to an admiral, a hetaira, an enslaved interpreter, and women whose sexual relationships ended while their political or cultural influence continued.
- [[Malinche]] ranks first because the episode treats her Nahuatl-Maya-Spanish interpretation and alliance mediation as indispensable to the coalition that overthrew the Mexica Empire.
- [[Aspasia]] ranks highly because ancient traditions give her extraordinary intellectual and political significance, although hostile comedy and later attribution make the scale of that influence uncertain.
- [[EmmaHamilton]], [[NellGwyn]], and [[LillieLangtry]] show different relationships between celebrity and security: visibility could produce cultural influence, popularity, or a career without guaranteeing durable protection.
- [[CleopatraVII]] is better understood as a dynastic strategist pursuing her throne and kingdom through alliances with Caesar and Antony than as the promiscuous seductress of hostile reception.

## Key Quotes

> "came from nowhere" - the episode's shorthand for the upward-mobility criterion behind the ranking.

> "people outside normal power structures" - the recurring frame for influence grounded in personal access rather than office.

## Connections

- [[NightingaleTuti]] - Persian court figure included mainly as a case of affection and memorial notice rather than demonstrated state influence.
- [[CleopatraVII]] - sovereign whose Roman relationships were strategic and dynastic, complicating the category of mistress.
- [[PiersGaveston]] - male royal favourite whose closeness to Edward II intensified baronial conflict.
- [[EmmaHamilton]] - performer and celebrity whose relationship with Nelson brought fame but not lasting financial security.
- [[NellGwyn]] - popular Restoration actress whose sons entered the aristocracy through Charles II.
- [[LillieLangtry]] - royal mistress who converted notoriety and beauty into a transatlantic acting career.
- [[MadameDePompadour]] - favourite who retained political and cultural influence after her sexual relationship with Louis XV ended.
- [[Aspasia]] - intellectually influential partner of Pericles whose surviving reputation is filtered through hostile and speculative traditions.
- [[MarieWalewska]] - Polish noblewoman whose relationship with Napoleon joined national lobbying, affection, and dynastic consequence.
- [[Malinche]] - enslaved interpreter and diplomat whose constrained role in conquest gives her the episode's highest ranking.
- [[IntimateAccessInformalPower]] - synthesis of how proximity, communication, patronage, and public image can create influence outside office.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode's Cleopatra and Malinche sections broadly agree with the wiki's fuller source-critical profiles while compressing their lives into a ranking format.
- Calling every listed person a mistress can obscure sovereignty, enslavement, coercion, uncertain sexuality, intellectual partnership, and forms of influence that continued beyond sex.
- Aspasia's authorship or teaching, Gaveston's sexual relationship with Edward II, Pompadour's responsibility for major policy, Walewska's effect on Napoleon, and Malinche's indispensability remain uncertain, debated, or source-scoped.
- The countdown is an argumentative entertainment format, not a comprehensive comparison of historical causal weight.
