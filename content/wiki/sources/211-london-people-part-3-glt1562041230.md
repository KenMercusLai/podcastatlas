# 211. London: People (Part 3)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode pairs two people born outside [[London]] who became part of its history. [[HenryFielding]] connects satire, fiction, magistracy, urban growth, and the small [[BowStreetRunners]] experiment, while [[CLRJames]] connects Marxism, Caribbean migration, cricket, post-colonial writing, and late-life residence above *Race Today* in [[Brixton]].

The pairing supports [[AdoptedLondonIdentity]]: London belonging is presented as something made through residence, work, institutions, writing, politics, sport, and local memory rather than birthplace alone. It also develops [[EarlyProfessionalPolicing]] as an incremental response to a growing city whose volunteer constables and private thief-takers were inadequate or corrupt.

## Key Claims

- Fielding moved from anti-Walpole theatrical satire and novels including *Shamela*, *Joseph Andrews*, and *Tom Jones* into government-aligned magistracy after writing against the 1745 Jacobite rising.
- Eighteenth-century London's growth, poverty, disorder, volunteer constables, and corrupt thief-takers created pressure for more regular criminal investigation.
- Fielding's six paid Bow Street men pursued suspects, raided gaming houses, recovered property, and carried standardized equipment, making them a small precursor rather than a complete modern police force.
- Fielding also proposed a workhouse combining housing, paid work, training, lessons, and rehabilitation, but the episode says the scheme was ignored.
- James joined cricket journalism to Marxist and anti-colonial thought; *The Black Jacobins* and *Beyond a Boundary* linked Caribbean history, empire, literature, politics, and sport.
- Brixton's affordable housing and proximity to Windrush-era arrival infrastructure helped Caribbean settlement, while entertainment venues and later *Race Today* activism made the district a cultural and political center.
- James's final residence above the *Race Today* offices on Railton Road joined his transnational intellectual life to Brixton's postwar Black British history and local memory.
