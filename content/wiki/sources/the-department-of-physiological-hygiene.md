# The Department of Physiological Hygiene

## Summary

[[RevisionistHistory]] reconstructs the [[MinnesotaStarvationExperiment]], in which [[AncelKeys]] subjected 36 healthy conscientious objectors to stabilization, six months of severe calorie restriction, and controlled rehabilitation to study starvation and recovery during World War II. Oral histories preserve the volunteers' weakness, food obsession, irritability, withdrawal, binge eating, and loss of self-control; [[SamLegge]]'s finger injury and uncertainty about whether it was accidental make impaired judgment especially concrete.

The study produced enduring knowledge about malnutrition, rehabilitation, and eating disorders, but [[ArtCaplan]] argues that modern oversight would not approve a comparable protocol. The episode therefore frames the experiment as both scientifically consequential and ethically unresolved: survival and public benefit do not by themselves determine whether researchers were justified in imposing profound physical and psychological harm.

## Key Claims

- Keys designed a year-long protocol with stabilization, six months of restriction, and rehabilitation, aiming for each volunteer to lose 25 percent of ideal body weight while continuing substantial weekly walking.
- Starvation transformed attention, mood, relationships, and self-control: volunteers became preoccupied with food, socially withdrawn, irritable, and vulnerable to compulsive eating or possible self-harm.
- Ending restriction did not immediately end hunger; several men ate beyond stomach fullness, showing that physiological refeeding and subjective satiety can diverge.
- The study sought practical answers about whether rehabilitation depended more on food quantity or composition and tested different calorie, protein, and vitamin regimens.
- *The Biology of Human Starvation* became an influential account of prolonged deprivation and recovery, but later usefulness does not settle whether the original harms were justified.
- Earlier human research operated without modern federal oversight, disclosure standards, consent procedures, or a guaranteed right to withdraw.
- Caplan's modern-ethics judgment is categorical at the protocol level: deliberately starving volunteers amid severe psychological stress would not receive contemporary approval.
