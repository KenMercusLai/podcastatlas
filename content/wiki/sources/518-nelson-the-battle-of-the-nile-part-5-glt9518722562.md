# 518. Nelson: The Battle of the Nile (Part 5)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode reconstructs the [[BattleOfTheNile|Battle of the Nile]] on 1 August 1798, from [[HoratioNelson|Horatio Nelson]]'s decision to attack before nightfall through [[ThomasFoley|Thomas Foley]]'s landward flanking maneuver and the explosion of *L'Orient*. It presents the victory as a combination of audacity, shared command intent, practiced ship handling, and close-range gunnery that destroyed French maritime support for the [[NapoleonEgyptCampaign|Egyptian expedition]], while repeatedly acknowledging French courage and the battle's extreme human cost.

## Key Claims

- Nelson attacked with little daylight remaining in unfamiliar, shoal-filled water because his captains understood the desired objective and he feared losing the chance for decisive action.
- British preparation before contact—clearing decks, wetting timber, spreading sand, readying guns and surgery, and preparing stern anchors—made tactical risk executable rather than merely heroic.
- The French line assumed its landward side was protected by shoals, but Foley used an older chart and the logic that anchored French ships required sufficient depth to identify a passage between them and shore.
- Five British ships passed inside the French van while Nelson attacked from seaward, concentrating double-sided fire on the front and center before the French rear could provide effective support.
- *Culloden* grounded, and *Bellerophon* suffered severe losses against *L'Orient*, showing that initiative and preparation reduced but did not remove navigational or combat risk.
- The fire and explosion aboard *L'Orient* became the battle's dramatic symbol, but the episode leaves its precise ignition uncertain and records British rescue of French survivors.
- French commanders and crews fought with conspicuous courage; the scale of defeat is explained through position, readiness, coordination, and British execution rather than a simple moral contrast.
- Destruction or capture of most of the French fleet trapped Napoleon's army in Egypt, ended French Mediterranean predominance, encouraged renewed European resistance, and converted a naval action into a wider strategic turning point.
