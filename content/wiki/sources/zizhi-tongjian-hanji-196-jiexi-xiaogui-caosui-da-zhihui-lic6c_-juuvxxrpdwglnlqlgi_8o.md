# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》196｜解析“萧规曹随”大智慧

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode follows [[CaoShen|曹参]] from his earlier service as chancellor of [[QiKingdomEarlyHan|齐国]] to his succession after [[XiaoHe|萧何]] as Han chancellor. Its central claim is that “萧规曹随” is not mere inactivity: Cao Shen judges the founding settlement sufficiently complete, selects cautious officials, limits punitive scrutiny and policy churn, and uses [[HuangLaoQuietGovernance|黄老式清静治理]] to protect postwar recovery.

The episode traces that choice to [[GaiGongEarlyHan|盖公]], whose counsel in Linzi joins quietness, non-disturbance, responsiveness to the people, legal regularity, and non-forcing. The durable synthesis is conditional: [[XiaoGuiCaoSuiInstitutionalContinuity|萧规曹随式制度连续]] can preserve a sound inherited order when society needs rest, but the source does not establish that visible inactivity, tolerance of error, or policy continuity is always wise.

## Key Claims

- After Xiao He's death, Cao Shen expects to be summoned and then leaves Xiao He's laws and institutions substantially unchanged, despite the two men's reportedly strained public relationship after promotion.
- Cao Shen favors cautious, plain-spoken, substantial officials and excludes sharp-tongued, textually exacting, fame-seeking personnel, making personnel selection part of his continuity policy.
- His drinking, refusal to hear would-be remonstrants, and concealment of minor mistakes look like neglect but are interpreted by the host as deliberate resistance to bureaucratic overactivity and punitive fault-finding.
- When [[LiuYing|汉惠帝刘盈]] questions this style, Cao Shen argues that neither ruler nor chancellor surpasses Liu Bang and Xiao He, so their responsibility is to preserve the established order carefully and avoid error.
- The episode connects Cao Shen's judgment to his Qin legal experience, long military service, and knowledge of the social damage caused by extended warfare.
- As Qi chancellor, Cao Shen consults many elders and thinkers, then seeks out Gai Gong, houses him in the chancellor's residence, and reportedly uses his Huang-Lao teaching to produce order in Qi.
- The source presents Huang-Lao as a practical synthesis of Dao, law, balance, adaptation, and non-forcing suited to recovery after the Qin collapse, rather than as simple passivity.
- Cao Shen's three-year chancellorship and the popular praise attached to it make institutional continuity an achievement of restraint: fewer visible interventions are judged by whether ordinary people remain settled.
- The episode extends this Qi-tested governing style into the early Han court and retrospectively connects it to the later Wen-Jing recovery, while leaving the precise duration and causal scale source-scoped.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content was found. The episode deepens Cao Shen's earlier military and Qi-command profile by explaining the governing doctrine he later carries from Qi into the Han chancellorship.
- Hanji 194 part 1 attributes continued measured government under Xiao He and Cao Shen to Liu Ying's reign; this episode supplies a ministerial mechanism for that continuity without resolving how much policy authorship belonged to emperor, chancellor, or broader court.
- The document labels the five-part Huang-Lao outline “守道、一法、均衡、知变、求为” but immediately describes “无为” as the goal. That wording is preserved as an internal terminological ambiguity rather than silently harmonized.
- The claim that Huang-Lao governed early Han politics for more than sixty years and produced the Wen-Jing settlement is a broad retrospective causal judgment, not demonstrated by this single episode.
- The exact relationship between Cao Shen and Xiao He, Cao Shen's expectation of appointment, dialogue, personnel criteria, drinking strategy, handling of errors, Gai Gong's words, chronology, and Qi governance results remain episode-attributed.
- Avoiding unnecessary change can reduce disruption, but this source does not show how the same approach handles corrupt inherited rules, major external shocks, or errors that are not minor.
