# 556. 1066: The Battle of Hastings (Part 3)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[WilliamTheConqueror|William the Conqueror]] from his weather-delayed Channel crossing to victory over [[HaroldGodwinson|Harold Godwinson]] at the [[BattleOfHastings|Battle of Hastings]] on 14 October 1066. Its central interpretation is that William accepted a dangerous coastal position and ravaged Harold's Sussex lands to force the decisive battle he needed, while Harold's best path was to hold defensively until night, reinforcement, or naval pressure made the Norman foothold untenable.

The episode also uses the Carmen, William of Poitiers, and the [[BayeuxTapestry|Bayeux Tapestry]] to distinguish vivid narrative from secure knowledge. English deployment, the balance of infantry and cavalry, the deliberate character of Norman retreats, and the precise manner of Harold's death remain contested; the durable conclusion is that the English line broke, Harold died, and William's invasion acquired the battlefield victory required for the later conquest.

## Key Claims

- William's delayed crossing depended on weather, fleet cohesion, and the temporary dispersal of Harold's fyrd, making the invasion a logistical gamble before battle began.
- Pevensey offered a landing place, but Hastings offered access to the London road while also leaving William vulnerable to blockade from land and sea.
- William's ravaging and exposed base may have served as strategic bait because an inconclusive campaign favored Harold, whereas William needed to defeat and kill the reigning king.
- Harold marched south quickly and fought before every summoned force arrived, plausibly because failure to defend Wessex and his own Sussex lands would damage both resources and kingship.
- The English defensive position was difficult to outflank and needed chiefly to remain intact; Norman archers, infantry, cavalry, rout, rally, and reported feigned retreats eventually pulled fighters out of formation.
- Harold's famous arrow-in-the-eye death is not settled: the tapestry is ambiguous and restored, the earliest written sources omit it, and the Carmen gives a different collective killing account.
- Harold's death, rather than the exact weapon or killer, is the decisive event because it ends organized English resistance on the field and allows William's gamble to succeed.

## Key Quotes

> "William had gambled everything and won." - the episode's closing judgment on the invasion and battle.

> "hold until night" - the hosts' concise statement of the English defensive objective.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction found. The episode strengthens the existing claim that Hastings enabled but did not itself complete the Norman Conquest.
- The arrow-in-the-eye tradition remains contested rather than rejected: the tapestry image, restoration history, earlier copies, and silence of the Carmen and William of Poitiers do not support certainty.
- William's bait strategy, Harold's motives, battlefield layout, shield-wall mechanics, cavalry emphasis, intentional feigned retreats, heroic episodes, and the Carmen's killing account remain source-scoped reconstructions.
