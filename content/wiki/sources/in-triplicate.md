# In Triplicate

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode traces [[TriplicatePrescriptionMonitoring]] from [[PaulMadden]]'s California system to [[SidneyWolfe]]'s unsuccessful campaign for national adoption. It argues that serialized three-part prescription forms made oversight visible to physicians and that [[PurduePharma]]'s own pre-launch research identified a prescribing “chilling effect,” leading the company to concentrate OxyContin marketing outside the five remaining triplicate states.

The resulting state variation is presented as a natural experiment: non-triplicate states reportedly had almost twice the per-capita OxyContin use and faster overdose-death growth, while the divergence persisted for two decades. [[MalcolmGladwell]] uses his own earlier dismissal of Wolfe to connect [[MessengerBasedEvidenceDiscounting]], [[InstitutionalInnovationAdoptionResistance]], and [[PublicIntellectualErrorCorrection]], while acknowledging that the supplied summary does not independently establish every causal estimate or fully weigh undertreated pain and administrative burden.

## Key Claims

- [[PaulMadden]]'s triplicate system placed a serialized controlled-drug prescription record with the physician, pharmacist, and state, making later inspection possible in three locations.
- The episode argues that monitoring worked partly through psychological salience: every relevant prescription reminded a physician that government scrutiny was possible.
- A 1995 Purdue focus group reportedly found that triplicate rules made physicians anxious and materially reduced their willingness to prescribe opioids.
- [[PurduePharma]] largely avoided the five remaining triplicate states when launching OxyContin, creating uneven marketing exposure across otherwise comparable jurisdictions.
- Economists using Purdue litigation documents reportedly found nearly twice as much per-capita OxyContin use in non-triplicate states and a persistent divergence in overdose deaths.
- [[SidneyWolfe]] advocated national monitoring before OxyContin's launch, but opposition from pain specialists and industry joined skepticism toward Wolfe's confrontational style.
- Gladwell treats his own failure to investigate Wolfe's warning as a journalistic error and argues that evidence should be assessed independently of its messenger.
