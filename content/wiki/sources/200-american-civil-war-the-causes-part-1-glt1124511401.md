# 200: American Civil War: The Causes (Part 1)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] conversation with historian [[AdamIPSmith|Adam I. P. Smith]] traces the [[AmericanCivilWar|American Civil War]] from the Constitution's slavery compromises through cotton expansion, the Mexican-American War, the [[FugitiveSlaveAct1850|Fugitive Slave Act]], Bleeding Kansas, [[DredScott|Dred Scott]], Lincoln's election, secession, and [[FortSumter|Fort Sumter]]. Its central distinction is between naming slavery as the cause and explaining the mechanisms by which slavery destabilized territory, federalism, party competition, constitutional legitimacy, and everyday liberty.

The episode rejects both inevitability and the idea that ordinary compromise could indefinitely contain the conflict. Cotton made enslaved labor more profitable, western expansion repeatedly reopened its legal geography, federal slave-catching radicalized Northern opinion, and Southern defenses hardened into a positive-good ideology. [[AbrahamLincoln|Abraham Lincoln]]'s sectional Electoral College victory then triggered secession by states whose new Confederacy explicitly protected slavery; the attack on Fort Sumter converted constitutional rupture into war.

## Key Claims

- The Constitution secured a stronger national government through compromises that protected slavery in the states, postponing rather than resolving a recognized sectional danger.
- Cotton demand, the cotton gin, and territorial acquisition made slavery more profitable and turned its western expansion into a recurring national crisis.
- Northern fear of a “slave power” grew when federal law made alleged fugitive recovery visible inside free states, especially through the [[AnthonyBurns|Anthony Burns]] rendition.
- Southern proslavery argument hardened from regrettable necessity toward positive good, while racial caste gave many non-slaveholding white Southerners a status interest in the system.
- Antislavery did not imply racial equality: many white Northerners opposed slavery while supporting exclusion, colonization, or a white republic.
- Kansas, [[JohnBrown|John Brown]], congressional violence, and Dred Scott showed political restraint and territorial compromise breaking down.
- Lincoln's 1860 victory produced secession before inauguration; the Confederate constitution protected slavery, and Fort Sumter turned the secession crisis into armed conflict.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode makes slavery central while distinguishing structural pressure from a claim that the exact war, date, or sequence was predetermined.
- “States' rights” does not supply a rival cause in this account: Southern state-right claims protected slavery, while Northern states invoked local authority against federal fugitive-slave enforcement.
- Antislavery belief is not treated as proof of racial egalitarianism; Lincoln, abolitionists, and white Northern voters held different and changing positions on colonization, Black migration, citizenship, and equality.
- Cotton-import shares, slaveholding proportions, desertion patterns, private motives, religious characterizations, alternate-war scenarios, and the claim that one statute was the single most important trigger remain approximate, interpretive, or source-scoped.
