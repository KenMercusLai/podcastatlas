## Summary
This [[PlanetMoney]] episode follows the [[SquamishNation|Squamish Nation]] as it turns [[SinakVillageSite|a returned village site near downtown Vancouver]] into an 11-tower, roughly 6,000-unit rental-housing development. The source connects historical dispossession, sovereign land control, and [[SovereignHousingDevelopment]] to show how ordinary municipal zoning and permitting can shape urban housing supply. It also treats neighborhood opposition, Indigenous cultural visibility, and long-term nation-building as part of the same economic case rather than separate political side issues.

## Key Claims
- In 1913, British Columbia officials forced Squamish residents from the village site the source names as Sinak; after a 1977 lawsuit, the [[SquamishNation|Squamish Nation]] regained about 10.5 acres in 2003.
- [[ChiefGibby]] initially wanted a signature project that could create recurring income for the nation, with early plans closer to a few mid-rise buildings and about 1,500 units.
- [[WilsonWilliams]] argued that the first plan was too modest for a seven-generations horizon and did not sufficiently express the place's beauty or Squamish presence.
- Because the land is sovereign Squamish territory, normal [[Vancouver]] zoning limits did not bind the project the way they would on ordinary municipal land.
- The later plan expanded into roughly 11 towers, 6,000 apartments, shops, restaurants, parks, and Squamish design elements, with Squamish members voting to move forward in 2019.
- [[JacobLewisIII]] presents the architecture as both commercial housing and [[IndigenousDevelopmentVisibility|cultural visibility]], because visitors should understand whose land they are on.
- Neighbor objections over traffic, parks, birds, and skyline change make the project a case in [[LocalVetoHousingPolitics]], where concentrated costs can dominate diffuse housing benefits.
- The source treats some opposition as more than neutral planning concern: Jacob says criticism that Indigenous people should not build modern high-rises carried racist assumptions.
- Public coordination still mattered. The Squamish agreed to fund traffic and transit-related upgrades, [[Vancouver]] supported utility and road connections, and the Canadian government provided a C$1.4 billion low-interest loan.
- The source links the project to [[PermittingDelayCost]]: normal development fights, lawsuits, and approval delays can raise costs before construction begins.
- The first phase is expected to add about 1,400 apartments, and the full 11-tower project is expected to finish by 2033 in the source's account.
