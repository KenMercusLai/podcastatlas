# Under Secretary of State Sarah B. Rogers on dismantling the Censorship Industrial Complex

## Summary
This [[AllIn|All-In]] episode interviews [[SarahBRogers|Sarah B. Rogers]] of the [[USDepartmentOfState|U.S. Department of State]] on public diplomacy, free speech, and foreign regulation of American platforms. Rogers argues that the [[UKOnlineSafetyAct|UK Online Safety Act]] and [[EuropeanUnionDigitalServicesAct|EU Digital Services Act]] create [[CrossBorderPlatformSpeechRegulation]] pressure when foreign governments fine or threaten U.S. platforms over speech standards that differ from American First Amendment norms.

The discussion then broadens into AI deepfakes, disinformation NGOs, trusted flaggers, debanking, demonetization, labels, [[CommunityNotes|Community Notes]], and [[Grok]]. Its central source-scoped claim is that existing legal remedies and open counterspeech should usually be preferred to broad bureaucratic speech-control systems, especially when regulators can pressure risk-averse intermediaries.

## Key Claims
- Rogers frames [[USDepartmentOfState|State Department]] public diplomacy as the relationship between the U.S. government and foreign publics, a portfolio made more important by the internet and disputes over "disinformation."
- She says the [[UKOnlineSafetyAct|UK Online Safety Act]] imposes age-gating and risk-assessment burdens and can require removal of content considered illegal under UK law.
- She says the [[EuropeanUnionDigitalServicesAct|EU Digital Services Act]] contains content rules, hate-speech standards, and [[TrustedFlaggerSystem|trusted flagger]] structures that can pressure platforms through vague or risk-averse enforcement.
- Rogers argues that Europeans can set rules inside Europe, but that foreign fines against American platforms for applying U.S. speech norms create [[CrossBorderPlatformSpeechRegulation]] and [[PlatformFirstAmendmentDefense]] concerns.
- The source treats UK speech-arrest examples as warning signs that online-safety rules can amplify older speech restrictions, even when specific arrests are not directly under the UK Online Safety Act.
- Rogers and [[DavidSacks|David Sacks]] describe EU digital fines against [[Twitter|X]] and other American platforms as potentially functioning like a de facto "censorship tariff."
- On AI, Rogers says existing defamation, fraud, child-protection, and cybercrime laws already cover many harmful uses, while [[AIDeepfakeParodyBoundary]] problems depend on whether a reasonable viewer understands a depiction as parody.
- The episode presents the [[CensorshipIndustrialComplex]] as a network of governments, regulators, NGOs, and platforms that can suppress lawful but disfavored speech indirectly.
- Rogers says some disinformation organizations can define disinformation broadly enough to include true information that supports an "adverse narrative," using COVID transmission and lab-leak debates as source-scoped examples.
- The episode uses the [[CenterForCounteringDigitalHate]] and [[TrustedFlaggerSystem|trusted flaggers]] to explain why NGO-platform-government channels can become more coercive than ordinary public criticism.
- Rogers connects [[NRAVullo|NRA v. Vullo]] to [[IndirectRegulatoryCoercion]] and [[ViewpointDebanking]], arguing that governments may use banks, payment processors, advertisers, cloud providers, and platforms as intermediaries when direct censorship is constitutionally constrained.
- The hosts and Rogers present [[CommunityNotes|Community Notes]] and user-facing AI tools such as [[Grok]] as more transparent truth-checking mechanisms than regulator-imposed labels, because the original post remains visible and disagreement is exposed.

## Key Quotes
> "censorship tariff" - host framing for foreign digital fines against American platforms.

> "trusted flaggers" - DSA mechanism Rogers says gives selected NGOs privileged reporting channels.

> "adverse narrative" - phrase Rogers uses when criticizing broad disinformation definitions.

## Contradictions
- No direct contradiction found with existing wiki content.
- The source qualifies [[PoliticalDeepfakeRegulation]] and [[AIContentProvenance]] rather than rejecting them outright: Rogers accepts that narrow disclosure or watermarking can be useful, but warns that broad AI-specific speech rules can become censorship tools.
- The source aligns with [[SocialMediaAgeGateSpeechBurden]] on age-verification burdens, while shifting the legal frame from U.S. state laws to the UK Online Safety Act and foreign regulation of American platforms.
- The episode is one-sided by design. European child-safety, hate-speech, and platform-accountability rationales are mentioned but not developed, so claims about foreign motives, arrest comparability, and NGO conduct should remain source-attributed.
