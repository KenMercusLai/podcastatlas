# 155. Ukraine and Russia

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode uses [[VladimirPutin]]'s claim that modern [[Ukraine]] was created by Russia and the Bolsheviks as the entry point for a survey from the steppe and [[KievanRus|Kievan Rus]] through Polish-Lithuanian rule, the [[CossackHetmanate]], Russian imperial absorption, Soviet catastrophe, and the 1991 independence referendum. [[TomHolland]] and [[DominicSandbrook]] argue that shared history, shifting borders, and Russian-language use do not erase [[UkrainianNationalIdentity|Ukrainian national identity]], which they trace through religion, print, Cossack autonomy, literature, political organization, and repeated demands for self-rule.

The episode's main interpretive contribution is [[HistoricalNarrativeTerritorialLegitimation]]: medieval inheritance, the 1654 Cossack agreement, Novorossiya, Crimea's transfer, and Soviet federal borders acquire present political force when selected as evidence for territorial ownership. Its century-spanning format is introductory rather than specialist, so demographic totals, treaty meanings, casualty figures, motives, and compressed period judgments remain source-scoped.

## Key Claims

- Ukrainian and Russian histories are deeply entangled, but entanglement does not establish that Ukraine lacks a distinct identity or legitimate sovereignty.
- [[KievanRus|Kievan Rus]] is a shared medieval inheritance whose later Ukrainian and Russian appropriations should not be projected backward as exclusive modern ownership.
- Long periods under Poland-Lithuania, Austria-Hungary, the [[RussianEmpire]], and the [[SovietUnion]] produced regional, religious, linguistic, and political variation within Ukraine.
- The [[CossackHetmanate]] supplied a durable autonomy tradition; the 1654 agreement with Moscow remains contested between submission and alliance interpretations.
- Nineteenth-century language suppression coexisted with literary and political revival, while independence demands and competing Ukrainian states predated Bolshevik rule.
- Soviet recognition of a Ukrainian republic did not create national consciousness from nothing; collectivization, famine, purges, Russification, Nazi occupation, the Holocaust, and wartime destruction made the twentieth century catastrophic.
- The 1991 referendum's broad territorial support is presented as the strongest modern democratic basis for Ukrainian independence, while Russian-speaking identity is not treated as equivalent to pro-Russian allegiance.
- Putin's historical argument is selective because it emphasizes incorporation and common origin while minimizing autonomy, cultural suppression, independent organization, and democratic statehood.
