# The Dog Will See You Now

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode has [[MalcolmGladwell]] follow working-dog selection at [[ThreeSixtyCanineGroup|360 Canine Group]], prostate-cancer demonstrations associated with [[AndreasMershin]] and [[ClaireGuest]], and [[WilliamSchneider|William Schneider's]] synthetic scent-training work. It argues that disease-related metabolic changes can create odor patterns dogs learn across people, making [[CanineDiseaseDetectionScreening]] a plausible rapid first-line screen rather than a standalone diagnosis.

The episode extends that premise into [[FrequencyFirstOutbreakScreening]]: inexpensive, repeated screening could interrupt enough transmission to matter even without perfect detection, provided alerts lead to confirmatory testing. Its accuracy, cost, disease-generality, and comparative-performance claims are interview reports rather than a comprehensive validation record, so the proposed national canine reserve remains a policy thought experiment rather than an established clinical or pandemic-response program.

## Key Claims

- Working detection dogs are selected for search drive, persistence, confidence, environmental composure, and willingness to keep working, not breed or appearance alone.
- [[AndreasMershin]] explains disease odor as a pattern of volatile metabolic byproducts found in bodily emissions rather than a single uniform molecule.
- A dog can reportedly generalize that pattern across patients, but prostate-specific antigen screening, biopsy, and canine alerts all have different error modes and cannot be compared responsibly through anecdotes alone.
- [[WilliamSchneider]] describes using pathogen information to produce inert, stable scent-training aids that can be shipped without infectious clinical material.
- The episode reports strong performance by COVID-trained dogs across masks, socks, sweat, and saliva, but supplies no underlying protocol, sample size, blinding, prevalence, confidence interval, or independent replication.
- A school or venue workflow would use dogs for rapid triage and route an alert to a secondary rapid test rather than treating the canine indication as a diagnosis.
- The public-health case depends on frequency, speed, access, and cost reducing transmission opportunities, not on canine screening achieving laboratory perfection.
- A strategic reserve would require standardized training, handlers, quality control, animal-welfare safeguards, surge logistics, public acceptance, and continuing validation across settings and disease variants.
