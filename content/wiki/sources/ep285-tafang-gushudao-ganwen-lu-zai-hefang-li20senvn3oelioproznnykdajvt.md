# EP285 踏访古蜀道：敢问路在何方？

## Summary
This [[TalkSanlian|Talk三联]] episode has [[XuePeng|薛鹏]], [[AiJiangtao|艾江涛]], and [[ZhangXingyun|张星云]] compare field reporting along [[JinniuRoad|金牛道]], [[BaoxieRoad|褒斜道]], and [[LitchiRoad|荔枝道]]. It reframes the [[ShuRoadNetwork|古蜀道]] from a single notoriously difficult path into a changing network that linked political centers, military movement, migration, trade, and local life across the Qinling and Daba mountains. Its core synthesis is methodological as well as historical: [[RoadArchaeology|道路考古]] must combine texts, maps, surface traces, bridges, oral memory, and walking, while [[TerrainBoundTransportEvolution|交通技术与地形关系]] explains the shift from river valleys and ridge paths to trestles, cliff roads, highways, tunnels, and high-speed rail.

## Key Claims
- The [[ShuRoadNetwork|古蜀道]] is better understood as a route system between the Guanzhong, [[Hanzhong|汉中]], [[BaShu|巴蜀]], and upper Yangtze regions than as one fixed road.
- [[JinniuRoad|金牛道]] had the longest continuous official importance of the three featured routes, linking Hanzhong through Guangyuan, Jianmen, Zitong, Mianyang, and Deyang toward [[Chengdu|成都]]; many walkable remains now visible are Ming-Qing forms rather than untouched ancient surfaces.
- [[BaoxieRoad|褒斜道]] was a strategically important Qinling corridor whose trestles were repeatedly built, damaged by floods, burned in war, and recorded by cliff inscriptions such as 《石门颂》.
- [[LitchiRoad|荔枝道]] has fewer continuous physical remains and a shorter period as an official Tang road, so claims about its course, Yang Guifei's fruit supply, and military purpose require stronger separation between evidence, local memory, and hypothesis.
- [[Hanzhong|汉中]] is the network hinge: it joins routes north across the Qinling with routes south into Ba-Shu, and its political, ecological, and cultural transition character helps explain why successive regimes competed for it.
- Road technology changed with material and maintenance constraints. Timber trestles could traverse sheer river gorges but were vulnerable to rot, fire, flood, and sabotage; later cliff-side or ridge roads traded directness for durability.
- Political geography changed route importance. Roads rose or declined as capitals moved, water transport expanded, military priorities shifted, and state investment selected different corridors.
- Ming-Qing movement of salt and other goods along the Litchi Road shows that a route could outlive its original official purpose and become a civilian trade corridor.
- Modern roads, railways, viaducts, and tunnels compress distance and escape older terrain logic, but they can also cover ancient alignments and weaken travelers' perception of the landscapes and settlements through which movement once passed.
- [[RoadArchaeology|Road archaeology]] differs from ordinary excavation because long, partly vanished routes must be reconstructed from gazetteers, historical maps, waterways, old bridges, surface survey, GPS, aerial imagery, local recollection, and repeated walking.

## Key Quotes
> “天然交通博物馆” — the episode's description of Mingyue Gorge, where several transport eras overlap.

> “军转民” — the speakers' shorthand for a route shifting from a primarily military function toward ordinary travel and trade.

> “石牛粪金” — the legend used to show how conquest memory, place names, and tourism storytelling accumulate around the Jinniu Road.

## Contradictions
- No settled contradiction found.
- The episode qualifies a literal reading of [[LiBai|李白]]'s 《蜀道难》: its place names span different routes, and the expert view reported here is that Li Bai may have left Sichuan by water rather than personally traversing the Qin-Shu roads he evokes.
- The source preserves rather than resolves the dispute over whether [[YangGuifei|杨贵妃]]'s lychees came from Ba-Shu or Lingnan.
- The alleged Wanyuan inscription about Tang tribute-fruit transport is treated as probably unreliable because the original is unavailable, early gazetteers omit it, and the rubbing reportedly resembles the discoverer's handwriting.
- Early route origins, ancient-tree ages, exact battle itineraries, climate-based lychee range, and the Litchi Road's possible role in Tang-Nanzhao strategy remain source-scoped or hypothetical.
