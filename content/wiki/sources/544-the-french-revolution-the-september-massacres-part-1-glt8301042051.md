# 544. The French Revolution: The September Massacres (Part 1)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode reconstructs the [[SeptemberMassacres|September Massacres]] as organized prison killings produced by Prussian invasion, the fall of Verdun, rumors of internal conspiracy, radical incitement, and official non-intervention. It argues that the violence was neither simply a spontaneous mob frenzy nor a centrally commanded state operation: local teams used improvised tribunals, killed selected prisoners, spared many others, and presented the result as preventive [[RevolutionaryPopularJustice|popular justice]].

The episode also separates the documented violence from the atrocity stories that gathered around [[PrincesseDeLamballe|the Princesse de Lamballe]]. Revolutionary claims of harsh necessity and counter-revolutionary accounts of sexualized demonic brutality formed a [[RevolutionaryAtrocityNarrativeContest|narrative contest]] whose exaggerations do not erase the killings' methodical cruelty.

## Key Claims

- Prussian invasion, the Brunswick threat, the fall of Longwy and Verdun, alarm bells, recruitment, raids, and rumors of prison breakouts made enemies within Paris appear as urgent as the army outside it.
- Radical journalists and political writers, including [[JeanPaulMarat|Jean-Paul Marat]], publicly discussed killing prisoners before the massacres began.
- The killings started on 2 September 1792 and spread through prisons including the Abbaye, Carmes, La Force, Saint-Firmin, Bicêtre, and the Salpêtrière before ending on 6 September.
- Organized teams and improvised tribunals make “death squads” more accurate than an undifferentiated mob: prisoners were questioned, some were acquitted, and more than half of detainees in the targeted prisons survived.
- Victim categories widened from suspected political enemies and priests to petty criminals, forgers, young detainees, and sex workers, showing how preventive security logic absorbed socially marginal people.
- [[GeorgesDanton|Danton]] mobilized Paris against invasion, helped intensify internal-enemy fear, declined to protect prisoners, and later called the deaths an indispensable sacrifice, although the source does not claim he directly commanded the killings.
- [[StanislasMaillard]] is one identifiable organizer, but the episode locates coordination mainly in sections and local revolutionary networks rather than a proven order from Danton or [[MaximilienRobespierre|Robespierre]].
- [[FrancoisJourgniacSaintMeard|François Jourgniac Saint-Méard]] survived the Abbaye tribunal and later described the silent, selective, quasi-judicial procedure in *My Agony of 38 Hours*.
- [[PrincesseDeLamballe|The Princesse de Lamballe]] was killed after refusing to swear hatred of the royal family; later claims of mutilation, cannibalism, and sexual violence range from plausible but unverified to contradicted by surviving records.
- Contemporary approval framed the killings as an atrocious necessity, public duty, or popular justice, while later historical debate asks how to explain wartime panic without turning context into excuse.

## Key Quotes

> “Atrocious necessity” - Rosalie Jullien's reported description of the massacres.

> “Indispensable sacrifice” - Danton's reported characterization of the deaths as a means of appeasing Paris.

## Contradictions

- The episode's estimate of around one thousand deaths is lower than the roughly 1,500 reported by [[707-the-terror-an-assassination-in-paris-part-1-glt6580043397]]; both remain source-level estimates rather than an audited wiki-wide total.
- The source complicates earlier shorthand that Marat “encouraged the militants”: it documents his incitement but locates operational organization mainly in local sections and networks.
- Danton's and Robespierre's responsibility remains differentiated from direct command. The episode presents knowledge, rhetoric, non-intervention, and political approval, but no proven top-down order.
- Lamballe's decapitation and delivery of her body are treated as established within the episode; the head-on-a-pike procession is plausible but uncertain, while many sexualized mutilation and cannibalism stories are unsupported or contradicted by section records.
- Survivor testimony, private motives, exact perpetrator numbers, destroyed section documents, tribunal procedures, and claims about public approval remain source-scoped reconstructions.
