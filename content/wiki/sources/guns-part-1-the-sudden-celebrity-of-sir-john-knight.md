# Guns Part 1: The Sudden Celebrity of Sir John Knight

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode traces how [[SirJohnKnight]]'s 1686 acquittal became a modern American gun-rights precedent and figured in [[NewYorkStateRiflePistolAssociationVBruen|New York State Rifle & Pistol Association v. Bruen]]. A compressed later legal account made the case appear to show that English common law tolerated peaceful public carry despite the Statute of Northampton, an interpretation developed by [[JoyceMalcolm]] and used within the Court's history-and-tradition inquiry.

The episode then uses decoded newsletters by [[RogerMorrice]] to reconstruct a different context: Knight reportedly left his party's arms at Bristol's boundary, left his own gun and sword outside St. Michael's, and faced prosecution amid anti-Catholic agitation rather than a straightforward test of a right to carry. The resulting dispute strengthens [[GunRightsHistoricalPathDependence]], [[HistoryAndTraditionConstitutionalTest]], and [[ContextualLegalHistory]] while remaining limited by the supplied file's status as a structured episode summary rather than the newsletters, briefs, judicial opinions, or complete scholarly debate.

## Key Claims

- Knight's acquittal acquired far greater importance in modern American Second Amendment argument than it retained in English legal memory.
- A short later digest made the case look like evidence that the Statute of Northampton did not broadly prohibit peaceful firearm carrying.
- Malcolm treats the acquittal and a reported judicial statement about ordinary arms as support for a historical right of armed self-protection.
- Morrice's contemporary newsletters reportedly show that Knight and his companions complied with Bristol weapons restrictions and that Knight denied carrying arms into the church.
- The fuller account places anti-Catholic speech, local politics, and the weakness of the prosecution at the center of the acquittal.
- *Bruen* made remote historical analogies legally consequential by asking whether modern firearm restrictions fit the nation's historical tradition.
- The episode argues that choosing among plausible historical readings by reference to the Second Amendment conclusion risks circularity.
