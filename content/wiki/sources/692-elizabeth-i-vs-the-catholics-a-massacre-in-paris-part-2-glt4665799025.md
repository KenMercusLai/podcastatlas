# 692. Elizabeth I vs The Catholics: A Massacre in Paris (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode backfills the origin layer before [[693-elizabeth-i-vs-the-catholics-englands-greatest-hero-part-3-glt5069845329|episode 693]] by following the 1572 [[StBartholomewsDayMassacre]] and its English afterlife. It argues that Paris taught [[FrancisWalsingham]], [[WilliamCecil|William Cecil / Lord Burleigh]], and other Elizabethan Protestants to read Catholic power as a possible existential threat rather than only as religious difference.

The second half turns from atrocity memory to security practice. [[WilliamAllen]], [[RobertPersons]], and [[EdmundCampion]] try to sustain English Catholicism through seminaries and undercover priests, while Walsingham reads the same network through [[ElizabethanRecusancySecurityState|recusancy security politics]], informants such as [[CharlesSled]], and the unresolved [[PastoralMissionSubversionAmbiguity|pastoral mission versus subversion]] problem.

## Key Claims

- [[ChristopherMarlowe]]'s play about the massacre shows how the Paris killings became part of English Protestant memory as well as French history.
- [[GaspardColigny]]'s murder by [[HenryDukeOfGuise|Henry, Duke of Guise]]'s men helped turn a tense royal wedding settlement into wider anti-Huguenot violence.
- The episode treats the massacre as a convergence of aristocratic vendetta, royal opportunism, mob violence, and local grievances rather than a single-cause event.
- Papal and Spanish celebration of the massacre sharpened English Protestant fear, while Catholic rescue of some Huguenots keeps the source from flattening all Catholics into one behavior.
- The Pope's 1570 excommunication of [[ElizabethI|Elizabeth I]], [[MaryQueenOfScots|Mary, Queen of Scots]]' claim, and Paris together made Catholic loyalty look politically dangerous to Elizabethan ministers.
- [[FrancisWalsingham]]'s Paris experience is presented as formative for his later spy system, including foreign agents, informants, and hard intelligence targets in Spain and the papacy.
- Elizabethan laws against calling Elizabeth a heretic, schismatic, tyrant, infidel, or usurper, plus imprisonment pressure on recusants, turned religious nonconformity into a security category.
- [[WilliamAllen]]'s seminaries at Douai and Reims trained English Catholic priests for return missions, creating the [[SeminaryPriestMission|seminary priest mission]] that Walsingham saw as inseparable from foreign-backed rebellion risk.
- [[RobertPersons]] and [[EdmundCampion]] sharpened the Jesuit branch: the mission claimed pastoral purpose, but its papal loyalty, exile planning, and Spanish-Irish context made Protestant authorities read it as subversion.
- [[CharlesSled]]'s May 1580 disclosure of names, pensions, and descriptions gave Walsingham actionable Catholic-network intelligence before Campion's capture.
- Campion's denial of treason in [[CampionsBrag|Campion's Brag]] failed to settle the source's central ambiguity because his preaching success made him both spiritually inspiring and politically frightening.
- Campion's capture, torture, treason conviction, and execution at Tyburn on 1 December 1581 turned the pastoral-versus-treason question into a state verdict, while [[RobertPersons]] escaped and the priest mission continued.
