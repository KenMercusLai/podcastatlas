# As space launches increase, there aren't enough spaceports

## Summary
This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode explains why U.S. commercial space growth is running into [[SpaceLaunchCapacityBottleneck|launch-capacity bottlenecks]] rather than only rocket-design limits. The discussion connects [[CommercialSatelliteConstellations]], crowded launch pads around the [[FloridaSpaceCoast]], scarce site geography, and an FAA proposal to reduce environmental review for launches and new spaceports.

The source's main contribution is to put launch infrastructure inside a policy tradeoff. [[MichelleHanlon]], [[DanielAckerman]], and [[WendyWhitmanCobb]] frame capacity as strategically important for the [[UnitedStates]] space industry, while [[BrettHartle]] and the [[CenterForBiologicalDiversity]] warn that the proposed [[SpaceLaunchEnvironmentalReview]] waiver could weaken habitat protections and may especially benefit [[SpaceX]].

## Key Claims
- The episode says the [[FloridaSpaceCoast]] set a new record with more than 100 orbital launches last year, but the launch pads are crowded and many need upgrades for newer rockets.
- [[CommercialSatelliteConstellations]] are driving launch demand because companies are deploying many smaller satellites for internet service, GPS, and Earth imaging rather than relying on a few expensive long-lived satellites.
- [[Starlink]] is described as the main driver of this trend, and the episode says more than 15,000 satellites are currently orbiting Earth.
- Existing U.S. launch sites named in the episode include the [[CapeCanaveral]] area, [[VandenbergSpaceForceBase]] in [[California]], and [[SpaceX]]'s Gulf Coast site in [[Texas]].
- [[DanielAckerman]] identifies three key spaceport factors: distance from population centers, access to a deep water port, and geography that supports eastward launches near the equator.
- Launches can scatter debris, create loud noise, and interrupt commercial air traffic, so [[SpaceportSitingConstraint|spaceport siting]] is constrained by safety, logistics, and social tolerance.
- [[WendyWhitmanCobb]] says very few areas in the United States still satisfy the combined spaceport requirements.
- The proposed [[FederalAviationAdministration|FAA]] rule could reduce or waive parts of environmental review for rocket launches and spaceports, including review tied to endangered species, air, and water laws.
- The FAA frames the proposal as a way to streamline launch licensing and reduce regulatory burdens for space companies.
- The proposal remained out for public comment in the source, and the episode says an early beneficiary could be [[SpaceX]] if it acquires land for a possible southern [[Louisiana]] Gulf spaceport.
- [[BrettHartle]] argues that the rule could harm southern Louisiana wetlands and federally protected species and that Congress has not authorized the FAA to make such a broad change.
- The episode frames launch capacity as an economic and strategic issue because the private sector now drives much of the space industry, while [[China]], the [[DominicanRepublic]], and countries on Africa's east coast are exploring launch capacity.
- Ocean platforms are presented as another possible way companies may bypass scarce terrestrial launch sites.
