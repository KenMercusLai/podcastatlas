# 47. The Seven Years' War

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] conversation with historian [[DanSnow|Dan Snow]] presents the [[SevenYearsWar|Seven Years' War]] as an overlapping global conflict whose North American opening, European alliances, Indian company warfare, Atlantic campaigns, and naval blockade jointly transformed the balance of empire. It argues that Britain's advantage came not from sea power alone but from a [[FiscalMilitaryState|fiscal-military state]] able to borrow cheaply, subsidize Prussia, sustain fleets, and mount expeditions while continental allies kept French resources engaged.

Victory nevertheless created new liabilities. Britain acquired territory and commercial reach connected to slavery and corporate corruption, then faced the cost of governing and defending its enlarged empire; France's effort to recover and Britain's attempt to tax its colonies helped make the war a precursor to both the [[AmericanRevolution|American]] and [[FrenchRevolution|French Revolutions]].

## Key Claims

- Fighting began in North America before the formal 1756 declaration, with [[GeorgeWashington|George Washington]] involved in the Ohio Valley and Britain attacking French reinforcements in 1755.
- The conflict was a collection of connected but partly autonomous wars: European alliance and balance-of-power fighting, Anglo-French imperial rivalry, colonial frontier war, Atlantic operations, and [[EastIndiaCompany|East India Company]] expansion in Bengal.
- Britain's overseas strategy depended on Prussia's survival and allied warfare in Germany, which prevented France from concentrating its resources or trading European gains for colonial concessions.
- Naval victory, blockade, public credit, low-interest borrowing, subsidies, and expedition finance formed a [[FiscalMilitaryState|fiscal-military system]] as important as battlefield command.
- [[JamesCook|James Cook]]'s St Lawrence survey work helped the British fleet and army reach Quebec, showing how cartography and seamanship became operational imperial capacity.
- The settlement enlarged the [[BritishEmpire|British Empire]] through Canada, Caribbean sugar wealth, West African slave-trading positions, and Company power in India, while Plassey depended on bribery and Indian elite collaboration rather than British battlefield valor alone.
- Victory removed the French threat to the colonies but left Britain with debt, frontier resistance, a standing military burden, and an empire difficult to govern, helping produce the American rupture; French naval rebuilding after defeat added to the fiscal crisis preceding 1789.
