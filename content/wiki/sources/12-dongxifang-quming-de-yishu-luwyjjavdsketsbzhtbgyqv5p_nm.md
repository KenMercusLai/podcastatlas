# 12 东西方取名的艺术

## Summary

This [[WeirdHistoryPodcast|怪东西 Weird History]] episode treats personal names as changing social institutions rather than natural, timeless labels. It moves from the disputed pre-Qin distinction between 姓 and 氏 through Chinese 名、字、号、排行、字辈 and [[ImperialNameTaboo|避讳]], then compares Roman multi-part names, European hereditary surnames, Russian patronymics, Icelandic patronymics and matronymics, Arabic descent chains, and Spanish paternal-maternal surnames.

Its central synthesis is [[NamingSystemsAsSocialInstitutions|姓名制度作为社会制度]]: naming systems distribute information about kinship, rank, religion, place, occupation, political authority, and self-presentation in different ways. The episode also distinguishes [[StateSurnameStandardization|国家推动的固定姓氏标准化]] from older lineage practice and uses [[KinshipEncodingInPersonalNames|姓名中的亲属关系编码]] to show that a component resembling a Western surname may instead identify a parent, clan branch, marriage connection, or generation.

## Key Claims

- The familiar account in which 姓 marks a larger marriage group and 氏 marks a branch, rank, fief, office, or ancestor is useful for some Zhou-period material but remains disputed; the episode also presents the alternative claim that 氏 may predate a Western Zhou reorganization of 姓.
- As the Zhou order weakened, 姓 and 氏 converged toward later surname usage, while compound surnames emerged from places, offices, migration, and ethnic interaction and could later contract into single-character surnames.
- Chinese 名、字、号、排行 and 字辈 performed different social tasks: personal distinction, ritual address, semantic expansion, family order, lineage continuity, and chosen self-presentation.
- [[ImperialNameTaboo|Name taboo]] made rulers' and elders' names constraints on other people's speech, writing, examinations, and names; the episode condemns the practice while noting that some rulers chose rare characters or renamed themselves to reduce the burden.
- Fixed hereditary surnames appeared late in many societies and could be imposed through registration, reform, assimilation, or decolonization, making [[StateSurnameStandardization|surname standardization]] a tool of governance as well as family memory.
- Occupational, geographical, physical, religious, and ancestral references supply recurring name material, but different systems arrange and inherit those elements differently.
- Russian father-names, Icelandic patronymics or matronymics, Arabic ibn/bin/bint chains, Spanish paternal-maternal surnames, Western compound surnames, and Chinese generation names encode family relationships without sharing one universal surname model.
- Name order, capitalization, translation, shortening, marriage, fashion, class association, and chosen names can change how identity is read across languages and across a person's life.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode broadens [[PreQinSurnameClanIdentity|先秦姓氏身份]] beyond the earlier Changping Jun case while explicitly preserving scholarly disagreement about whether 姓 or 氏 came first and how consistently later definitions apply to early periods.
- The episode reinforces [[ImperialNameTaboo|避讳]] as a burden on language and status while adding the broader naming-system context and an explicit normative rejection of the practice.
- Etymologies, surname counts, legal dates, national adoption timelines, historical anecdotes, and claims about the contemporary class or generational feel of particular names remain episode-attributed pending specialist verification.
- Cross-cultural resemblance does not establish institutional equivalence: Roman clan names, Chinese 姓氏, hereditary European surnames, Russian father-names, Icelandic patronymics, Arabic descent chains, and Spanish double surnames follow different rules.
