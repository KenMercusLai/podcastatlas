# A Blockbuster-era law could define modern online privacy rules

## Summary
This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode has [[EricGoldman]] explain how the 1988 [[VideoPrivacyProtectionAct|Video Privacy Protection Act]] moved from the disclosure of [[RobertBork]]'s video-rental history to Supreme Court litigation over website analytics and targeted advertising. The core dispute is whether a statute written around videotape-service transactions can govern routine data flows on modern sites that contain video.

The episode frames a broad reading as [[LegacyPrivacyStatuteInternetSpillover]]: consent duties and statutory damages could reach a wide range of websites, while a narrow reading could leave invasive data practices outside this particular law. Its closing discussion introduces [[DelegatedAIAgentConsent]], asking whether an agent can consent for a user and whether websites may restrict agents acting on users' behalf.

## Key Claims
- Congress enacted the Video Privacy Protection Act in 1988 after a reporter published Supreme Court nominee Robert Bork's video-rental history.
- Plaintiffs are using the law to challenge analytics and targeted-advertising data flows on websites that also provide video.
- Goldman says courts lack a clear, broadly accepted method for translating the statute's older consumer relationship into the modern internet context.
- Consent may reduce legal exposure, but prompts can create access friction and additional compliance questions.
- Goldman warns that a broad interpretation could make almost any website containing video a potential defendant and support statutory-damages claims for technical violations.
- The dispute develops amid the absence of a comprehensive federal data-privacy law and alongside attempts to apply other older computer-crime or eavesdropping statutes online.
- Even a limited plaintiff victory could revive many cases, while the Supreme Court could instead return the matter to lower courts.
- Analytics and targeted advertising can feel invasive while also supporting personalization and results users expect, so the source does not treat all data-dependent functionality as valueless.
- The closing exchange asks whether AI agents can consent for users and notes that some businesses, including Amazon and Williams-Sonoma, already restrict agent access.

## Key Quotes
> "dinosaur law" - Goldman's description of applying the 1988 statute to standard internet practices.

> "perpetual motion machine" - Goldman's warning about repeat statutory-damages litigation over technical violations.
