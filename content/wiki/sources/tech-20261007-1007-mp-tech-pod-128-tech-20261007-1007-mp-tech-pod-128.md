# AI spending takes center stage ahead of midterms

## Summary

This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode has [[BrendanGlavin]] of [[OpenSecrets]] explain how AI-linked super PAC networks are intervening directly in U.S. primaries and general-election politics. It contrasts [[LeadingTheFuture]], connected in the episode to [[OpenAI]] and a less-regulatory position, with [[PublicFirstAction]], connected to [[Anthropic]] and a more-regulatory position, while treating those affiliations and policy labels as source descriptions rather than proof of company control.

The episode's core contribution is [[AIIndustrySuperPACPolitics]]: AI interests are borrowing the cryptocurrency sector's candidate-by-candidate spending model, operating across party lines, and turning regulation and [[DataCenterBacklash]] into electoral issues. New York and South Dakota examples show how outside money can overshadow candidates or local donors, but the source does not independently establish spending totals, donor control, advertising effects, or voter response.

## Key Claims

- AI-focused political groups are spending directly to support or oppose candidates whose positions may affect AI and data-center regulation.
- [[LeadingTheFuture]] is described as connected to OpenAI and less regulation-oriented, while [[PublicFirstAction]] is described as connected to Anthropic and more regulation-oriented.
- [[GregBrockman]] and his wife reportedly withdrew a planned $25 million Leading the Future donation after an earlier $25 million contribution, with Brockman saying the PAC had become a distraction at OpenAI.
- In a New York congressional primary, Leading the Future-linked groups reportedly opposed [[AlexBores]] after his state-level AI-regulation work, while Public First supported him.
- In a South Dakota Senate race, the episode says AI super-PAC spending exceeded contributions from state residents, illustrating how outside money can nationalize a local contest.
- AI groups are described as copying the cryptocurrency industry's Fairshake-style network of affiliated super PACs and direct candidate intervention rather than relying mainly on party committees.
- The groups operate in both Democratic and Republican races, but Glavin says the politics are becoming more polarized and the pro-AI position is beginning to lean Republican.
- Data-center power use, utility costs, and public attention can turn AI-industry support from an asset into a candidate liability.
- Glavin places AI interests alongside AIPAC-related groups and cryptocurrency interests as leading primary-season outside spenders, while expecting party-linked spending to grow nearer the general election.

## Key Quotes

> "Jobs and Democracy PAC" - example Glavin uses to show that a PAC name may conceal its AI-policy role from voters.

> "a distraction at OpenAI" - Brockman's reported explanation for withdrawing a planned second donation.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode extends prior [[AIBacklashPolitics]] evidence from voter anxiety and AI-linked spending into a more concrete super-PAC network and candidate-intervention mechanism.
- The account preserves a live policy split inside the AI industry: one network is presented as less regulation-oriented and another as more regulation-oriented, so “AI interests” should not be treated as a single regulatory coalition.
- PAC affiliations, donation amounts, race spending, local-donor comparisons, policy labels, partisan movement, and claims about the largest outside-spending blocs remain episode-attributed pending campaign-finance records and group disclosures.
