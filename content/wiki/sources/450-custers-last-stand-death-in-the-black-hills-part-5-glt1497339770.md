# 450. Custer's Last Stand: Death in the Black Hills (Part 5)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[GeorgeArmstrongCuster]] from post-Washita drift and political weakness through effective fighting on the 1873 Yellowstone expedition and his celebrity-making 1874 survey of the [[BlackHills]]. It places his admiration for Plains life beside a racial and civilizational worldview that accepted its destruction, while connecting railroads, bison killing, gold publicity, and the Panic of 1873 to mounting pressure on [[LakotaPeople|Lakota]] land.

The Black Hills expedition turned a small gold find into a national political crisis. Miner intrusion outpaced attempted enforcement, [[SittingBull]] and [[CrazyHorse]] helped organize resistance, and a federal commission failed to buy the hills on terms Lakota leaders could accept; the episode ends with President Grant summoning senior officials to decide between peace, coercion, and war.

## Key Claims

- Custer combined genuine affection for Plains life, scientific curiosity, racial hierarchy, literary ambition, and acceptance of Indigenous displacement.
- His weak Reconstruction performance and Democratic sympathies damaged his relationship with Grant and Sheridan, but battlefield skill and press appeal restored his usefulness.
- Lakota and Cheyenne resistance to railroad survey parties in 1872 preceded Custer's disciplined defense and dramatic counterattack during the 1873 Yellowstone expedition.
- Railroad expansion and large-scale bison killing undermined Plains independence while U.S. policy described forced reservation life, schooling, and cultural change as assimilation.
- The 1874 Black Hills expedition mixed military force, science, journalism, and spectacle; publicity around a small gold discovery accelerated an uncontrollable influx of miners.
- Lakota attachment to the Black Hills joined conquest, resources, livelihood, political authority, and spiritual significance rather than a simple timeless-ownership claim.
- Failed 1875 purchase talks converted the gold rush into a choice among continued treaty enforcement, economic coercion, and war.

## Key Quotes

> “I am not surprised that they prefer the wild, free life they lead.” - the episode's quotation from Custer before his appeal to “civilization.”

> “I would not sell even a pinch of soil.” - the episode's rendering of Sitting Bull's refusal to sell the Black Hills.

## Connections

- [[GeorgeArmstrongCuster]] - ambitious soldier, writer, press figure, and expedition commander whose competence coexisted with destructive assumptions.
- [[BlackHills]] - contested region where military survey, gold publicity, miner intrusion, and failed purchase talks pushed policy toward war.
- [[SittingBull]] - Lakota leader associated with survey resistance, wider coordination, and refusal to sell the hills.
- [[CrazyHorse]] - Lakota leader associated with survey resistance, mourning during the 1874 expedition, and refusal to join the sale talks.
- [[PhilipSheridan]] - senior commander who sponsored the Yellowstone and Black Hills expeditions while initially trying to exclude miners.
- [[BloodyKnife]] - Arikara scout whose friendship and warning helped Custer during the Yellowstone clashes.
- [[LakotaPeople]] - people whose land, livelihood, and political authority came under growing railroad, mining, and military pressure.
- [[CoerciveReservationAssimilation]] - framework for the episode's account of confinement, schooling, dependency, and cultural suppression.
- [[ManufacturedWarPretext]] - later coercive sequence whose economic and territorial preconditions are established here.
- [[HeroicLastStandMyth]] - posthumous legend preceded by Custer's deliberate frontier self-fashioning and newspaper celebrity.

## Contradictions

- Custer expressed sympathy for Native preference for free Plains life while treating removal before “civilization” as inevitable and legitimate.
- The episode distinguishes assimilationist intent from racial extermination, but the described policy still depended on military pressure, bison destruction, confinement, and suppression of Native autonomy.
- Sheridan initially tried to keep miners out of the Black Hills even though his expedition and Custer's publicity helped make the rush difficult to stop.
- The hosts qualify claims that the Black Hills were timelessly sacred to the Lakota, while still presenting the hills as politically, materially, and spiritually important; the depth and chronology of sacred association require Native and specialist sources.
- Private motives, reported dialogue, force and bison totals, the scale of the gold find, battlefield details, and the proposed purchase prices remain source-scoped in a popular-history reconstruction.
