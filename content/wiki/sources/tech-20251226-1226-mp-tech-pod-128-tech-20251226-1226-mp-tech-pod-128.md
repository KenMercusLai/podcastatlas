## Summary
This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode has [[MeganMcCartyCorino|Megan McCarty-Corino]] interview [[DrewHarwell]] of the [[WashingtonPost|Washington Post]] about [[OnlineAgeVerification]] becoming mainstream in 2025. The discussion links adult-content laws, a [[SupremeCourt]] decision involving [[Texas]], UK and [[Australia]] rules, and the spread of state-level requirements into a broader [[AgeVerificationPatchwork]].

The episode's main wiki contribution is to treat age verification as infrastructure, not only as a website pop-up. ID scans, face matching, webcam-based [[PlatformAgeEstimation]], [[BehavioralAgeInference]], compliance contractors, data breaches, false positives, VPN shifts, and repeated site-level checks make child-safety policy a privacy, cost, and responsibility-allocation problem.

## Key Claims
- About half of U.S. states require some form of online age verification, usually for pornography or broader adult content, and some laws reach toward social media.
- Harwell says 25 U.S. states have passed their own age-verification laws, creating a state-by-state compliance surface.
- A Texas explicit-content age-verification law reached the [[SupremeCourt]], and the episode says the ruling made lawmakers more willing to require websites to check user age.
- [[AgeVerificationComplianceIndustry]] vendors now offer ID-and-face matching, webcam age estimation, and other technical checks for platforms.
- Contractor costs can hit smaller platforms harder because vendors may charge per scan.
- The UK and [[Australia]] are also adding age-verification rules, making the issue international rather than only U.S. state policy.
- [[Bluesky]] stopped service for users in [[Mississippi]] because of legal-exposure concerns, illustrating how compliance patchwork can change access by state.
- Age checks may require sensitive identity data or face prints, which creates [[CivilLibertiesSurveillanceRisk]] and data-breach exposure.
- [[Google]] and [[OpenAI]] are cited as companies discussing age inference from user behavior, such as follows, searches, and watched videos.
- Evidence of child-safety benefit remains uncertain: traffic can drop at compliant adult sites while users move toward noncompliant sites or VPNs.
- The episode ends with an unresolved responsibility question: age checks could sit at website, app-store, device-platform, or federal-regulator level, but each option creates cost, liability, privacy, and family-device complications.

## Key Quotes
> "cottage industry" - Harwell's shorthand for age-verification contractors.

> "face print" - privacy-sensitive data that age checks may ask users to provide.

> "patchwork" - the state-by-state legal structure motivating possible federal attention.
