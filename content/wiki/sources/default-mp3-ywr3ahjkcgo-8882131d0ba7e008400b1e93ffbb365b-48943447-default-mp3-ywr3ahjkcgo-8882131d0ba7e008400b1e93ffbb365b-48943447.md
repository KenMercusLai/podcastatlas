# How Rude!

## Summary
This [[HiddenBrain]] episode has behavioral scientist [[ChristinePorath|Christine Porath]] explain how experiencing or witnessing disrespect can divert attention, impair memory and problem-solving, narrow creativity, weaken trust, and reduce information sharing. [[IncivilityCognitiveAndSocialSpillover]] joins these individual and network effects: one encounter can affect targets, witnesses, teams, customers, patients, and later interactions even when the carrier does not intend to pass the negativity forward.

The practical response has both cultural and personal branches. [[CivilityFeedbackCulture]] combines self-awareness, caring directness, behavioral feedback, trust, and psychological safety, while [[SelfDistancingRecovery]] helps a target regain perspective and attention without making recovery a substitute for offender or institutional accountability.

## Key Claims
- Incivility can trigger threat-related arousal and rumination that capture attention after the encounter has ended.
- The episode reports selective-attention, calculation, memory, problem-solving, response-speed, and creativity effects among both targets and witnesses, including roughly 30 percent lower cognitive performance across cited studies.
- Rudeness can weaken trust and information sharing, making employees less likely to offer ideas, identify errors, or ask for help; the episode connects this mechanism to degraded performance in surgical teams.
- [[IncivilityCognitiveAndSocialSpillover]] extends beyond the original dyad because witnesses may unintentionally carry negativity into later interactions with colleagues, customers, patients, friends, or family.
- Much incivility may be unintentional because people systematically overestimate their self-awareness and can remain blind to habits such as interruption, dismissiveness, or hostile email tone.
- [[CivilityFeedbackCulture]] requires clear but caring challenge, trusted feedback, shared behavioral expectations, and enough safety to surface mistakes and uncertainty.
- [[SelfDistancingRecovery]], exercise, relationships, volunteering, sleep, nutrition, and meaningful activity can strengthen recovery and agency, but they do not remove organizational responsibility for repeated harm.
