# 480. The French Revolution: The Rights of Man (Part 6)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode places the [[DeclarationOfRightsOfManAndCitizen|Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen]] inside the panic, hunger, fiscal collapse, and rural violence of summer 1789. The Declaration broke with privilege and absolutism by grounding liberty, property, security, resistance to oppression, expression, and sovereignty in equal citizens and the nation, yet its universal language left practical exclusions and the relation between national power and universal rights unresolved.

The second half traces how abolition of tithes, seizure of Church property, dissolution of monasteries, and the [[CivilConstitutionOfTheClergy|Civil Constitution of the Clergy]] turned reform into a religious fracture. The required clerical oath divided priests and communities, helped form a counterrevolutionary constituency, and made the conflict look less like a simple contest between reason and religion than a struggle between rival universal moral and political authorities.

## Key Claims

- The [[GreatFear1789|Great Fear]] joined real rural deprivation to rumors of aristocratic starvation plots and attacks on records of feudal obligation.
- The night of 4 August abolished feudal obligations and privilege in an effort to answer rural disorder with a new legal and social foundation.
- The Declaration drew on American and Enlightenment precedents but went further by presenting a list of natural and sacred rights as universal claims for mankind.
- National sovereignty displaced the king, Church, estates, and corporations as the ultimate source of political authority, creating a [[UniversalRightsNationalSovereigntyTension|tension between universal rights and national power]].
- Property protection preserved much noble wealth even as abolition of tithes and nationalization of Church property shifted the Revolution's burden toward the clergy.
- [[Talleyrand]] proposed using Church land to address the fiscal crisis while turning clergy into state-paid functionaries.
- The [[CivilConstitutionOfTheClergy|Civil Constitution]] and its loyalty oath split clergy roughly between oath-takers and non-jurors, with sharp regional variation.
- Anti-clerical Enlightenment thought mattered, but popular Catholic attachment and the [[ChristianInheritanceOfEnlightenmentRights|Christian inheritance claimed for universal morality]] qualify a simple secular-versus-religious story.
- Revolutionary use of tablets, sacred language, and biblical imagery suggests both appropriation of religious forms and competition between rival claims to universal truth.

## Key Quotes

> "Men are born and remain free and equal in rights." - Article 1 of the Declaration as presented by the episode.

> "You my brothers were called to be free." - Pauline text used by the Archbishop of Paris after the fall of the Bastille, as presented by the episode.
