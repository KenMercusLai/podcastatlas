# 456. Fall of the Sioux: The Massacre at Wounded Knee (Part 3)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode closes its Plains Wars sequence by connecting [[SittingBull]]'s death, the suppression of the [[GhostDance]], and the [[WoundedKneeMassacre]] to reservation control, settler expansion, military panic, racial ideology, and divided [[LakotaPeople|Lakota]] communities. It argues that Wounded Knee is better understood as a massacre of a surrounded, frightened, and substantially disarmed group than as the heroic battle presented by early press and official accounts.

The episode also follows the violence into memory and entertainment. Sitting Bull's cabin and Ghost Dance performers became attractions, while later books, music, museums, tourism, and public controversy shifted sympathy toward Native perspectives without restoring land or power. That sequence develops [[FrontierViolenceSpectacle]] and [[AtrocityNarrativeReframing]] as linked problems: conquest can be commercialized almost immediately, and later moral correction can remain symbolic or extractive.

## Key Claims

- The Ghost Dance panic provided authorities and the press with a frame for treating Sitting Bull as the leader of an uprising even though the episode does not present him as directing a coordinated rebellion.
- Sitting Bull's attempted arrest became lethal through institutional hostility, older personal conflict, family pressure, humiliation, and a crowd confrontation rather than a planned battlefield encounter.
- Agency police occupied a divided position as Lakota men, reservation officials, Christian converts, relatives, and rivals, making the arrest an intimate consequence of coercive assimilation as well as federal power.
- At Wounded Knee, the Seventh Cavalry surrounded Big Foot's hungry and ill band, attempted disarmament, and opened fire after Black Coyote's gun discharged during a struggle.
- The scale, weapons, victims, pursuit of people fleeing into a ravine, and friendly fire support the episode's classification of Wounded Knee as a massacre rather than a conventional battle.
- Early press and official praise converted the killing into a heroic victory despite General Nelson Miles's condemnation of it as a criminal military blunder and massacre.
- Cabins, staged villages, Wild West shows, tourism, and modern representation disputes show Native defeat being turned into marketable spectacle before and after public sympathy changed.

## Key Quotes

> "an abominable criminal military blunder" - the episode's quotation from General Nelson Miles's judgment of Wounded Knee.

> "woke Buffalo Bill" - the hosts' deliberately provocative phrase for sympathetic but still outsider-shaped cultural tourism.

## Connections

- [[SittingBull]] - Lakota leader whose arrest and killing form the episode's first tragic climax.
- [[LakotaPeople]] - the people whose internal divisions, military defeat, survival, and representation organize the episode.
- [[GhostDance]] - millenarian movement treated as hope under social collapse and as the object of official panic.
- [[WoundedKneeMassacre]] - final mass killing in the sequence and central dispute over battle versus massacre language.
- [[FrontierViolenceSpectacle]] - conversion of conquered people, places, and ceremonies into entertainment and tourism.
- [[AtrocityNarrativeReframing]] - movement from heroic official reporting toward victim-centered historical memory.

## Contradictions

- The episode rejects early press and official descriptions of Wounded Knee as a heroic battle, emphasizing that the surrounded group included starving refugees, women, children, and many people without weapons.
- General Miles's condemnation conflicts with the Secretary of War's praise of the Seventh Cavalry and with Colonel Forsyth's exoneration; the disagreement is preserved rather than harmonized.
- Reports about Sitting Bull's dancing horse, the exact sequence of individual shots, Kicking Bear's untranslated Glasgow speech, motives attributed to participants, and casualty totals remain uncertain or source-scoped.
- Later sympathy for Native people changes the moral narrative but does not contradict the episode's account of persistent material dispossession and structural harm.
