# The Companies Changing Warfare Forever: Palantir & Anduril Execs on Drones, AI & the Future of War

## Summary
This [[AllIn|All-In]] episode interviews [[TreyStephens|Trey Stephens]] of [[Anduril]] and [[ShyamSankar|Shyam Sankar]] of [[Palantir]] on defense technology, deterrence, procurement reform, and U.S. industrial capacity. The guests argue that war is bad, but credible deterrence requires fast software, autonomous systems, factories, replenishable supply chains, and cultural legitimacy for defense work. The episode's strongest contribution is [[FactoryBasedDeterrence]]: stockpiles alone do not deter if adversaries doubt that the [[UnitedStates]] can replace drones, munitions, ships, and components at wartime tempo.

## Key Claims
- [[TreyStephens|Trey Stephens]] says defense technology should aim at deterrence: making adversaries believe challenging the United States is not worth the cost.
- [[ShyamSankar|Shyam Sankar]] says Silicon Valley's anti-defense posture was partly a product of a long post-Cold War peace, while [[Russia]]'s invasion of [[Ukraine]] changed many views of defense work.
- The guests argue that the old American industrial base included dual-purpose companies such as Chrysler, General Mills, and Ford, whereas today's defense base is narrower and more specialized.
- Sankar argues that Ukraine consuming years of production in weeks should have made factories and replenishment capacity central to deterrence.
- Stephens describes [[ArsenalOne|Arsenal One]] as [[Anduril]]'s five-million-square-foot Columbus, Ohio factory campus built for modular defense production.
- The episode frames [[Anduril]] as a [[PrivateCapitalDefensePrime]]: it invests private R&D first, tries to sell products rather than cost-plus projects, and wants manufacturing capacity before the government has fully specified demand.
- Sankar says defense is a monopsony, so [[DefenseMonopsonyInnovation]] often starts as heresy and is validated backward from exercises, deployed users, or combat.
- The guests argue that low-cost, attritable autonomous systems are needed because high-end platforms and munitions can be too expensive, scarce, and slow to replace.
- Stephens treats autonomous weapons as compatible with accountability when a responsible human remains answerable for operating the system.
- Sankar says [[Palantir]] does not collect data and argues its cell-level security and auditability can make misuse easier to detect, while critics' surveillance concerns remain a live governance boundary.
- Both guests say illegal government use should be reported through inspector general channels.
- The episode closes by making industrial renewal part of U.S.-China competition: if the United States fails to rebuild capacity, the guests argue, [[China]] may set more of the global order by 2040.

## Key Quotes
> "factories, not stockpiles" - Sankar's deterrence lesson from Ukraine-style consumption.

> "software-defined, hardware-enabled" - Stephens's description of the next-generation prime thesis.

> "not morally neutral" - Stephens's argument against abstaining from national-security technology.
