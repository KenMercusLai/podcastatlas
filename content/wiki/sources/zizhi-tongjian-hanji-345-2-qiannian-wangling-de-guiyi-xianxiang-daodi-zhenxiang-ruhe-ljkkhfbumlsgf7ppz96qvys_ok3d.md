# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》345-2｜千年王陵的诡异现象到底真相如何？！

## Summary

This short [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode introduces the [[SanzhuangHanTombComplex|三庄汉墓群]] as the burial landscape associated with the Western Han [[SishuiKingdomWesternHan|泗水国]]. It moves from the discovery of large mounds and grave goods to the reported north-south planning of the cemetery, then follows the decision to examine the smaller [[ChendunHanTomb|陈墩汉墓]] before the much larger [[DaqingdunHanTomb|大青墩汉墓]].

Chendun complicates rather than resolves the royal-cemetery hypothesis. Its wooden-chamber form and furnishings indicate an elite burial, while the episode reports a roughly thirty-year-old woman classified as Xiongnu and a copper seal reading “张廷意.” Those observations do not by themselves show that the woman was a Sishui royal, that the seal named the principal occupant, or that every tomb in the larger cemetery belonged to the Liu ruling line. The episode therefore extends [[ArchaeologicalInterpretationUnderSparseEvidence]] by separating cemetery attribution, individual tomb rank, biological classification, portable-object ownership, and personal identity.

## Key Claims

- The episode says Sishui was founded under Emperor Wu, passed through five generations and six kings, and ended after Wang Mang's takeover, but its exact duration and territorial description remain source-scoped.
- Large mounds around Sanzhuang were recognized as ancient burials after land leveling exposed coffins, human remains, coins, iron, and jade.
- A remote-sensing survey reportedly found tombs arranged along a north-south axis, leading excavators to consider a planned family cemetery.
- The smaller Chendun mound was examined before Daqingdun so that its structure could inform work on the suspected royal tomb.
- Chendun is described as an early Western Han pit-and-wooden-chamber tomb with one coffin, one outer chamber, one side compartment, and elite grave goods.
- The episode reports that physical-anthropological study reclassified the skeleton from an initial male impression to a roughly thirty-year-old woman of Xiongnu origin.
- A copper seal normalized here as “张廷意” creates an identity question but does not independently establish that the woman, a spouse, an attendant, or the principal tomb owner bore that name.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-346-qiannian-wangling-beihou-yincang-de-jingtian-da-mimi-lophz5ccj4jalgmohvozcol5nelx|Hanji 346]] is adopted. This episode supplies the cemetery and Chendun setup; Hanji 346 supplies Daqingdun's stronger “泗水王冢” evidence.
- Name normalization: the supplied note's “四水国,” “四阳县,” “陈堆,” and “张庭义” are normalized to 泗水国, 泗阳县, 陈墩, and 张廷意. They are treated as transcript variants, not separate identities.
- The supplied note calls the instrument a roughly 1.6-meter “骨瑟.” Contemporary reporting describes a roughly 1.2-meter lacquered wooden se; material, dimensions, and any superlative therefore remain unresolved rather than combined into a false composite.
- The episode's excavation chronology differs from accessible summaries that place the Chendun rescue excavation in 2002. The 1998 survey, 2002-2003 excavation sequence, precise mound areas, and stated planning rationale require direct report comparison.
- The woman's age, Xiongnu classification, the first on-site sex assessment, exact seal reading and findspot, social rank, and relationship to the Sishui ruling house remain episode-attributed. A named seal found in a coffin is evidence in context, not an automatic identity card.
