# Zootopia Exposed! (Part One)

## Summary
This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode reconstructs screenwriter [[GaryGoldman]]'s unsuccessful allegation that Disney used a Zootopia pitch he submitted years before releasing its 2016 film. [[MalcolmGladwell]] and [[AngusFletcher]] then argue that [[Zootopia2Film|Zootopia 2]] unusually echoes the dispute through Gary De'Snake, a stolen family invention, powerful “fat cats,” and a Louisiana-associated setting. The pattern motivates several theories—coincidence, mockery, apology, or an internal creative maneuver—but the episode provides no confirmation from [[TheWaltDisneyCompany|Disney]] and leaves its preferred explanation for Part Two.

## Key Claims
- Goldman says that around 2000 he devised an animal world called Zootopia and in 2009 gave Disney an eight-page *Looney* outline featuring that world, cynical hyena Roscoe, optimistic squirrel Mimi, and a challenge to the promise that children can become anything.
- After Disney passed, its 2016 *Zootopia* used the same world name, an animal-only society, a closely related aspirational theme, and a cynical fox–optimistic rabbit pairing that Goldman believed resembled his characters.
- Goldman lost in federal court, on appeal, and in a later state-court effort after seven years of litigation; the episode says the courts found no copyright infringement and did not resolve whether Disney had used his pitch.
- The dispute illustrates the [[CopyrightIdeaExpressionBoundary]]: thematic premise, title, character types, and underlying ideas may feel appropriated without meeting the law's requirements for protectable expression.
- *Zootopia 2* gives Gary De'Snake a family invention allegedly stolen by the wealthy Lynxleys, making the sequel's plot a conspicuous parallel to Goldman's own allegation about the invention underlying Zootopia.
- Gary's name, appearance, gentle manner, Louisiana-associated setting, and stolen-invention grievance are interpreted as a possible composite allusion to Goldman, but the source contains no production evidence establishing that intent.
- Disney's prior legal victory makes the sequel's apparent allegory puzzling; the speakers consider mockery, corporate acknowledgment, encoded apology, coincidence, and an unauthorized or deniable creative act.
- Sealed filings and corporate silence limit verification, while Goldman's family response shows how a prolonged authorship grievance can become emotionally distributed across relatives.

## Key Quotes
> “Anybody can be anything.” — wording the episode associates with both Goldman's pitch and the released film's central promise.

> “Transactive anger.” — Gladwell's phrase for the way Goldman's family members carried different parts of the grievance.

## Connections
- [[GaryGoldman]] - screenwriter whose pitch, litigation, and family experience anchor the investigation.
- [[Zootopia2Film|Zootopia 2]] - sequel whose name, character design, setting, and stolen-invention plot generate the episode's mystery.
- [[AngusFletcher]] - narrative scholar who brings Goldman's account to Gladwell and interprets the sequel as an allegory of the dispute.
- [[CopyrightIdeaExpressionBoundary]] - legal distinction used to explain why perceived conceptual appropriation may not constitute infringement.
- [[SymbolicCreativeRestitution]] - possible acknowledgment theory introduced here and developed more fully in Part Two.
- [[TheWaltDisneyCompany|The Walt Disney Company]] - company that rejected Goldman's pitch, prevailed in the litigation, and declined to explain the alleged parallels.

## Contradictions
- No settled factual contradiction is adopted. Part One supplies the litigation and emotional background for Part Two's symbolic-restitution theory; Part Two adds a proposed explanation rather than reversing this episode's open ending.
- The shared title, premise, theme, character pairing, Gary resemblance, Louisiana references, sequel allegory, corporate knowledge, sealed-record implications, and internal-review pathway remain episode-reported or interpretive. The supplied note is not the pitch, films, screenplay, court record, sealed evidence, production archive, or testimony from Disney personnel.
