# The Road to Damascus

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode reconstructs how an unnamed former terrorist voluntarily became a highly valued [[CIA]] source, helped locate [[CarlosTheJackal|Carlos the Jackal]], and was later killed after a [[NewYorkTimes|New York Times]] article made his distinctive operational achievement public. [[MalcolmGladwell]] uses [[JohnRizzo]]'s memoir and interviews with journalist [[TimWeiner]] and former CIA general counsel Jeffrey Smith to hold two democratic obligations together: intelligence agencies require external scrutiny, but disclosure can become lethal when contextual details identify a protected source. The asset's claimed service as atonement also extends [[SecondChanceRecognitionFailure]] by asking whether institutions can preserve accountability for past violence while recognizing genuine change.

## Key Claims

- Rizzo describes the unnamed asset as a former terrorist who approached the United States voluntarily, supplied reliable intelligence without seeking substantial payment, and treated cooperation as atonement.
- The asset reportedly had direct access to a terrorist organization and supplied the decisive CIA tip that helped French intelligence capture [[CarlosTheJackal|Carlos the Jackal]] in Sudan in 1994.
- After [[AldrichAmes|Aldrich Ames]] was arrested, CIA director John Deutch ordered a source review that found the asset had participated in two European bombings intended to kill Americans and had wounded some of them.
- The CIA had recruited and used the asset without first consulting the [[FederalBureauOfInvestigation|FBI]] or [[USDepartmentOfJustice|Justice Department]], creating a legitimate accountability issue independent of his intelligence value.
- Weiner says he corroborated the concealed relationship through several independent sources, while CIA officials warned that publication could identify and endanger the asset.
- The newspaper withheld nationality, affiliation, attack locations, and other details, but retained the asset's role in finding Carlos; CIA officials considered that distinctive fact identifying.
- After advance warning, the asset rejected evacuation, broke with the agency, and was later identified and killed by former associates.
- The episode does not settle exclusive blame: Rizzo's account implies publication caused the death, while Weiner argues the CIA created the danger through procedural failure and broad official disclosure.
