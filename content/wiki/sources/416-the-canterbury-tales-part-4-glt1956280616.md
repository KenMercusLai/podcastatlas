# 416. The Canterbury Tales (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode presents [[GeoffreyChaucer]] and [[TheCanterburyTales]] as guides to a 14th-century England shaped by plague, war, commerce, revolt, and changing social rank. Chaucer's movement from a London wine-trade family into royal, diplomatic, and customs service becomes a biographical case for [[PostPlagueSocialMobility]], while the pilgrims' competing voices make the poem an example of [[LiteratureAsSocialHistory]].

The episode also argues that Chaucer's English writing was cosmopolitan rather than isolationist. Italian and French influence, multilingual London, and continental travel feed [[VernacularLiteraryLegitimacy]], while [[WifeOfBath]] gives the poem's social range a gendered edge through wealth, work, marriage, abuse, mobility, and demands for female sovereignty.

## Key Claims

- Chaucer's childhood overlapped with the 1348 Black Death; family deaths and inherited property connect catastrophe to unequal redistribution and later opportunity.
- Recurrent plague appears inside the poem through death imagery and the Pardoner's tale of greed, betrayal, and mortality.
- The pilgrimage frame can be read as social reconnection after isolation: strangers meet at the Tabard Inn and travel together toward Thomas Becket's shrine.
- Chaucer's court, military, diplomatic, Italian, and customs careers joined elite service to cosmopolitan London commerce.
- The poem acknowledges the traditional orders of fighters, clergy, and laborers but lets figures such as the drunken Miller disrupt expected speaking rank.
- Chaucer's comic self-portrait and use of the real innkeeper Harry Bailey blur biography, fiction, and self-parody.
- The episode treats the Cecily Champagne documents as a Statute of Labourers wage-and-service dispute rather than proof of rape, but that legal reinterpretation remains source-scoped here.
- The [[WifeOfBath]] connects female speech and marital sovereignty to cloth wealth, widowhood, pilgrimage, and post-plague labor conditions.
- Chaucer's adoption and development of literary English drew on continental vernacular models and helped increase English literary prestige.
- [[AliceChaucer]]'s wealth and dynastic ascent extend the episode's social-mobility theme beyond Chaucer's own lifetime.

## Key Quotes

> "newfangledness" - the episode's shorthand for Chaucer's association with novelty and change.

> "sovereignty" - the Wife of Bath's answer to what women most desire in marriage.
