## Summary
This [[HowIBuiltThis]] episode follows [[BrianRudolph]] and [[ScottRudolph]] from apartment-kitchen chickpea experiments to building [[Banza]] into a nationally distributed pasta brand. Its strongest contribution is a scale-up case: retail demand arrived before the founders had a reliable industrial process, and a transfer from a small adaptive plant to a faster but bean-inexperienced manufacturer produced pasta that dissolved during cooking. The later glyphosate allegation adds a second operating lesson about [[ConsumerBrandEvidenceResponse]]: testing, traceability, published results, and visible interim communication all matter when a food brand's safety promise is challenged.

## Key Claims
- Brian's recurring sinus symptoms and self-directed wheat elimination led him to experiment with chickpea-based foods, although his physician did not confirm wheat as the cause.
- Early product development used chickpea flour, eggs, chia, tapioca, xanthan gum, a wine bottle, and later a hand-cranked pasta machine before the formulation moved toward a vegan product.
- Brian recruited his older brother Scott as co-founder; crowdfunding, Venture for America support, a television appearance, and pitch competitions created early capital and retailer access.
- A Meijer buyer requested a 190-store launch before Banza had finished packaging or reliable high-volume production.
- The larger California manufacturer could produce about 1,000 pounds per hour but lacked bean-pasta experience; repeated 8,000-15,000-pound failed runs consumed scarce capital, and operators reportedly believed they were making corn pasta.
- To fulfill the first major order, the team shipped about 20,000 boxes with manually applied instructions telling customers to steep the pasta in hot water with the heat off, then returned to the smaller Michigan plant for shorter learning cycles.
- The orange box, protein-and-fiber framing, television exposure, retail displays, creator outreach, and later [[Target]] expansion helped make an unfamiliar product visible and broadly relevant rather than only gluten-free.
- In 2024, Moms Across America published a glyphosate test allegation; Brian says weekly sales immediately fell about 30%, while Banza's independent results took roughly three weeks and reported a level 98% below the original finding but not zero.
- Banza subsequently tightened farmer-level traceability or testing, pursued third-party pesticide certifications, published results through a package QR code, and concluded that it should have communicated more visibly before definitive results arrived.
- Brian's final operating judgment is that founders can increase their exposure to luck by repeatedly entering competitions, accepting uncertain media opportunities, contacting creators, and placing the company where favorable surprises can occur.

## Key Quotes
> "steep it like tea" - emergency cooking instruction applied to the first large shipment after the pasta proved unstable in boiling water.

> "okay" - Brian's boss's modest reaction to an early batch, which Brian nevertheless treated as encouragement.

> "increase the likelihood of luck" - Brian's retrospective description of Banza's opportunity-seeking approach.

## Contradictions
- No settled contradiction is adopted. The source presents Brian's wheat-symptom connection as a personal observation that his physician could not confirm, so it does not establish a general medical claim about wheat or sinus inflammation.
- The episode reports materially different glyphosate test results but does not independently compare sampling, laboratory methods, chain of custody, or toxicological interpretation. The wiki therefore treats the dispute as a brand-response case, not a definitive pesticide-risk adjudication.
