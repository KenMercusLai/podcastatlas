# 4. We’re all so 17th Century

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode asks whether pandemic restrictions, political polarization, statue disputes, Scottish nationalism, and moralized public conflict make the present resemble seventeenth-century Britain. [[TomHolland]] and [[DominicSandbrook]] use the English Civil War, the Great Plague, the Restoration, religious fragmentation, pamphlet culture, and later political memory to identify real parallels while emphasizing that seventeenth-century mortality and violence were vastly more severe.

Its strongest contribution is methodological. A historical comparison can isolate recurring mechanisms—epidemic fear, collapsing information control, inflammatory media, confessional identity, scapegoating, or inherited political geography—without making the periods equivalent. The episode therefore combines playful analogy with an explicit scale correction.

## Key Claims

- Plague fears can produce recognizable anxieties about contaminated objects, movement, public gatherings, and elite flight even when diseases, medicine, and mortality differ sharply.
- The Little Ice Age and Geoffrey Parker’s global-crisis thesis place British conflict inside a wider seventeenth-century pattern of climate stress, war, revolution, and population loss, but the episode does not establish a direct modern equivalence.
- Civil-war and plague mortality make contemporary comparisons useful partly as contrast: present divisions do not approach the violence and demographic shock of the 1640s–1660s.
- The weakening of Crown licensing and the growth of pamphlets and early newspapers widened argument while accelerating atrocity stories, religious polemic, and anxiety over whether expression should be regulated.
- [[JohnMilton]] connects the Commonwealth pamphlet world to arguments about free expression, although his defense of open print coexisted with service to [[OliverCromwell]] and support for Charles I’s execution.
- Religious fragmentation helped make liberty of conscience thinkable for some groups, but seventeenth-century actors also treated national religious uniformity as necessary to collective safety.
- Cavaliers, Roundheads, Whigs, Tories, Cromwell, the Levellers, Brexit maps, and Scottish nationalism show later movements repeatedly constructing political ancestry from the century without preserving a simple line of descent.
- Witch-hunt comparison can identify scapegoating and moral denunciation, but differences in voice, law, theology, punishment, and social power prevent a direct equation with online criticism.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. The episode’s strongest claim is that genuine structural parallels do not make modern Britain equivalent to the seventeenth century.
- Civil War and Brexit geographic overlap, the religious inheritance of Scottish nationalism, climate-crisis comparison, population-loss figures, plague mortality, and lines of descent from Cavaliers, Roundheads, Whigs, Tories, Cromwell, and the Levellers remain compressed or source-scoped.
- The J. K. Rowling discussion is an exploratory analogy rather than evidence that online denunciation reproduces early-modern witch prosecution.
