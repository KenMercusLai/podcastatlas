# 545. The French Revolution: The First Feminist (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode uses [[Marianne]] and [[OlympeDeGouges|Olympe de Gouges]] to expose a central contradiction in the [[FrenchRevolution|French Revolution]]: women could embody Liberty and the Republic, march in revolutionary crowds, influence salons, and be praised as mothers of the nation while remaining excluded from suffrage, formal deliberation, and military citizenship. De Gouges's [[DeclarationOfTheRightsOfWoman|Declaration of the Rights of Woman and of the Female Citizen]] makes that gap explicit by applying universal rights language to women.

The episode also distinguishes women's political positions rather than treating them as one movement. [[MadameRoland]] exercised major informal influence while defending a domestic ideal of female politics, [[TheroigneDeMericourt|Théroigne de Méricourt]] pursued clubs and armed participation, and [[NicolasDeCondorcet|Condorcet]] and [[SophieDeGrouchy]] supported broader female public participation.

## Key Claims

- The Republic's female allegory was politically useful because she could appear universal, classical, classless, and abstract without granting actual women citizenship.
- Women participated materially in the Revolution as marchers, sans-culottes, spectators, salon organizers, petitioners, club founders, and would-be soldiers, but collective praise as “mothers of the nation” rarely produced equal formal power.
- [[MadameRoland]] demonstrates the instability of informal influence: she shaped Girondin politics while arguing that women should inspire action privately, yet opponents attacked her through the same hidden-female-influence language used against Marie Antoinette.
- [[TheroigneDeMericourt|Théroigne de Méricourt]], the Fernig sisters, and [[PaulineLeon|Pauline Léon]] show that some women sought clubs, weapons, and military service even though women-only and mixed club projects struggled and the Convention barred women from battle in April 1793.
- [[OlympeDeGouges|Olympe de Gouges]] connected female political equality to abolitionism and a broader compassion for subordinated people, while retaining politically risky sympathy for Louis XVI.
- Her 1791 declaration exposed the contradiction between supposedly universal rights and male-only citizenship, arguing that exposure to punishment should entail access to political speech and judgment.
- [[NicolasDeCondorcet|Condorcet]] and [[SophieDeGrouchy]] supplied a minority pro-suffrage and salon context, while the Girondins were presented as relatively more open to female public participation than the Montagnards.
- Rousseauian domestic roles, classical republican models, and masculine virtue helped make women's political exclusion appear natural even to some women.

## Key Quotes

> “Woman has the right to mount the scaffold; she must equally have the right to mount the rostrum.” - Olympe de Gouges's argument as presented by the episode.

> “Mothers of the nation” - collective honor that the episode contrasts with women's exclusion as individual citizens.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with the existing French Revolution branch. The episode adds the gender boundary within the same universal-rights, factional, and republican-founding politics covered by later episodes.
- Calling Olympe de Gouges “the first feminist” is an episode title and interpretive framing, not a settled priority claim.
- The episode's contrast between relatively receptive Girondins and hostile Montagnards is directional rather than absolute; neither camp is presented as a disciplined party or a uniform program for women.
- Claims about why many women accepted exclusion, women's appetite for political clubs, individual influence on Condorcet, private motives, dialogue, and the exact reach of Rousseauian influence remain source-scoped.
