# 605. Greek Myths: Jason & The Quest for the Golden Fleece (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode uses the 1963 film *Jason and the Argonauts* to recover [[ApolloniusOfRhodes|Apollonius of Rhodes]]' [[Argonautica]], the layered traditions behind [[Jason]], [[MedeaCharacter|Medea]], and the [[GoldenFleece|Golden Fleece]], and the Hellenistic world in which the poem was composed. The film's Talos, harpies, Clashing Rocks, fire-breathing bulls, dragon's teeth, and skeleton warriors are presented not as arbitrary Hollywood inventions but as adaptations of an already adaptive ancient epic.

The episode's central synthesis is [[MythToMythologyTransition]]: in post-Alexander [[Alexandria]], poets and scholars preserved inherited stories by comparing variants, recognizing intertexts, relocating wonder, and reorganizing myth as learned literature. This did not mean ritual or supernatural belief simply disappeared. Ruler cult, philosophical criticism, astrology, fortune, and traditional festivals coexisted while the Olympian stories became increasingly available as a self-conscious literary system.

## Key Claims

- The 1963 film is closer to the *Argonautica* than its camp reputation suggests; many of its signature monsters and trials come from Apollonius, although the adaptation rearranges them.
- Apollonius wrote around 250 BCE in the Hellenistic world after [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]], reusing Homer, Hesiod, Pindar, Euripides, and local variants rather than inventing the Argonautic story from nothing.
- The poem turns the Golden Fleece cycle into a recognizable shared story world: Achilles appears as a baby, while Circe, the Sirens, Cadmus's dragon's teeth, and other inherited materials are repositioned around Jason's voyage.
- [[MedeaCharacter|Medea]] is both indispensable helper and latent threat. Apollonius gives her erotic compulsion and magic a central role while holding back the full horror familiar from [[Euripides]]' [[MedeaPlay|Medea]].
- The voyage preserves an older geography of Greek exploration around the Black Sea, while Apollonius shifts marvels toward less familiar routes through the Danube, Rhône, and western Mediterranean.
- Alexandria's lack of ancient local myth, its immigrant court culture, and its library scholarship made inherited Greek stories resources for cultural legitimacy and learned competition.
- Skepticism about anthropomorphic Olympians coexisted with ruler cult, astrology, fortune, philosophical theology, and continued festivals; the episode does not support a simple replacement of religion by rationalism.
- Greek stories endured because literary and scholarly transmission made them reusable by Virgil, Ovid, Shakespeare, Titian, cinema, and modern children's fiction even when the original ritual imagination became difficult to recover.

## Key Quotes

> "from myth to mythology" - the episode's formulation for the transition from lived sacred narrative to self-conscious literary and scholarly inheritance.

> "a kind of blockbuster prequel to Homer" - the hosts' analogy for Apollonius recombining familiar figures and motifs into an adventure epic.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode extends the earlier Medea source by moving backward from Euripidean tragedy to Apollonius's adventure epic; the two works select different moments and emphases from a variable tradition.
- The traditions about Apollonius's quarrel with Callimachus, his birthplace, his tenure as chief librarian, and the precise dating of the poem remain source-scoped rather than settled biography.
- The episode's broad movement from myth to mythology is retained as an interpretive model, not a claim that traditional ritual abruptly ended or that Hellenistic people ceased believing in gods.
- Euhemerus, divine kingship, astrology, Tyche, Epicurean theology, ancient route geography, and the poem's later influence are episode-level syntheses that would require specialist evidence for stronger historical claims.
