# 354: Paris 1968: The Return of De Gaulle (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode follows [[CharlesDeGaulle|Charles de Gaulle]] from apparent paralysis during the French general strike to his secret consultation with the army at Baden-Baden, return to Paris, dissolution of the National Assembly, and overwhelming electoral victory. It interprets [[May1968France|May 1968 in France]] as a political defeat for the protest movement but leaves open a longer cultural and intellectual legacy.

The episode's governing distinction is [[PoliticalDefeatCulturalAfterlife]]: failing to take or durably reorganize state power does not settle whether a movement changed language, institutions, or later thought. Its discussion of [[MichelFoucault|Michel Foucault]], surveillance, prisons, repression, and “docile bodies” illustrates that possible afterlife, while its skeptical tone and comparisons with Prague, Mexico City, and the United States keep the claimed influence qualified.

## Key Claims

- De Gaulle's near-monarchical conception of the Fifth Republic made the May crisis a challenge to his personal authority and to a paternal, hierarchical image of France.
- His initial dismissal of student unrest, failed 24 May speech, and despair on 28-29 May reveal genuine uncertainty about whether the state could still command obedience.
- The secret Baden-Baden trip tested military loyalty; General Jacques Massu's reassurance restored de Gaulle's confidence while exposing how seriously the regime contemplated collapse.
- Georges Pompidou's advice to dissolve the National Assembly converted an uncontrolled street and strike crisis into an electoral contest over order and communism.
- De Gaulle's short radio address and the large Gaullist counter-demonstration demonstrated a constituency for order that the protest movement had underestimated.
- The June election made May 1968 a clear political defeat for the left in the episode's account, although de Gaulle resigned the following year.
- The movement's cultural legacy is harder to measure: the hosts connect it to later analysis of discipline and institutional power, especially through Foucault, but acknowledge a skeptical and retrospective interpretive frame.
- International comparison limits Parisian exceptionalism: Prague, Mexico City, and American racial unrest involved different stakes, violence, and political consequences.
