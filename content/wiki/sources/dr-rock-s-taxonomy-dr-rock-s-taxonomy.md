# Dr. Rock’s Taxonomy

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode profiles [[JohnRock]], the Catholic fertility physician who helped develop the birth-control pill and then argued that it could fit Catholic teaching. His clinical work among poor women, lifelong emphasis on conscience, collaboration with Gregory Pincus, and refusal either to abandon the pill or leave the [[CatholicChurch]] turn a medical history into a case about doctrine and lived responsibility.

The episode uses casuistry to connect Rock's argument with [[ParticularizedMoralDiscernment]]. Rather than treating every pregnancy-preventing intervention as morally identical, Rock classified the pill with the permitted rhythm method because it used endogenous hormones and created a pregnancy-like state. A speculative mTOR fertility-preservation treatment extends the same [[ReproductiveTechnologyMoralClassification]] problem: purpose, mechanism, temporality, and effects may place one technology in more than one moral category.

## Key Claims

- Rock's work in Boston's Irish tenements and his treatment of poor women made repeated pregnancy and maternal hardship concrete clinical realities rather than abstract cases.
- Gregory Pincus led the laboratory work on hormone-based contraception while Rock conducted clinical trials and pressed the FDA to review their evidence.
- Rock treated personal conscience as a durable obligation: the episode connects his defense of Jamaican workers as a young man with his later refusal to renounce either Catholicism or the pill.
- Catholic doctrine rejected deliberate frustration of procreation but permitted periodic abstinence through the rhythm method, creating the categorical distinction Rock tried to use.
- Rock argued that the pill was closer to rhythm than to condoms or diaphragms because its hormones occur in the body and its physiological state resembles pregnancy.
- A 72-member Vatican commission reportedly voted 68–4 to permit the pill and emphasized married couples' conscience, but Pope Paul VI rejected the recommendation in *Humanae Vitae* because he believed prior papal teaching could not be overturned.
- A proposed mTOR-pathway treatment that might pause follicle development could both preserve fertility and prevent ovulation temporarily, showing why a technology's purpose and classification can be morally consequential.
