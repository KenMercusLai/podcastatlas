## Summary

This sponsored [[RevisionistHistory]] episode has [[MalcolmGladwell]] discuss the inaugural [[LasVegasGrandPrix]] with [[CallieField]], [[UlfEwaldsson]], and [[EmilyPrazer]]. The guests present [[TMobile]]'s dense-crowd 5G deployment as infrastructure for ticketing, navigation, payments, event operations, live video, and race telemetry, then extend the model to sailing, baseball, golf, marathons, and school sports. The episode supports [[ConnectedSportsExperience]] and a live-event branch of [[ConnectivityEnabledWorkflowRedesign]], but it provides no independent comparison of performance, cost, privacy, reliability, or alternative network architectures.

## Key Claims

- The nearly four-mile Las Vegas street circuit makes a race difficult to follow from one seat, so spectators need information about action occurring elsewhere.
- [[TMobile]] reportedly installed about 50 sites around the circuit and supported average speeds of 200–300 megabits per second for a crowd of roughly 150,000; these figures are episode-reported rather than independently audited.
- The event application combines changing walking routes, e-ticketing, communications, point-of-sale functions, and F1 TV video with timing and telemetry.
- Real-time race data can include car position, speed, gaps, overtaking, wind, and other conditions, turning a phone into a personalized second screen.
- [[FiveG|5G]] capacity and low latency are presented as allowing sensors, cameras, cloud processing, and audience interfaces to participate in a live event rather than only support later analysis.
- SailGP is used as a comparison in which hundreds of onboard sensors reportedly generate about 300,000 data points for athletes, coaches, and viewers.
- Live conditions and historical analysis can support coaching decisions, while AI may help select useful signals from high-volume telemetry.
- Personalized feeds could let families follow an individual marathon runner or create remote views of school sports through sensors, drones, and 360-degree cameras.
- Formula One's local production is described as feeding a London operation that distributes footage to 170 broadcast partners worldwide.
- The episode treats major sports as demanding network test environments whose operational lessons may transfer to enterprise settings.
