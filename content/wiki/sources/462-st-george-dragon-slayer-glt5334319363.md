# 462. St George: Dragon-Slayer

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode treats [[StGeorge|St George]] as a historically uncertain martyr whose cultural force grew through accumulated stories, images, institutions, and political reuse. It moves from the possible setting of [[Diocletian]]'s persecution through late martyr legends and the medieval dragon narrative, then follows George into crusading memory, the [[OrderOfTheGarter]], English royal patronage, Protestant allegory, national identity, and modern sport.

The episode's central synthesis is that weak biographical evidence did not prevent durable symbolic authority. [[SaintLegendAccretion]] explains how martyr, soldier, horseman, and dragon-slayer layers accumulated; [[DragonSlayerMotifLayering]] keeps Christian and classical imagery distinct without forcing one origin; and [[PatronSaintSymbolicPlasticity]] explains how George could survive reform and remain usable by religious, royal, national, sporting, and political communities.

## Key Claims

- No contemporary evidence for George's life survives, and the earliest martyr stories appear roughly two centuries after the period in which he is supposed to have lived.
- If George had a historical core, the episode places it most plausibly in [[Diocletian]]'s early-fourth-century persecution and keeps proposed identification with an unnamed martyr in Eusebius explicitly uncertain.
- The late martyr legend presents George as an imperial servant or soldier who refuses sacrifice to Apollo, survives repeated tortures, and dies on 23 April; learned Christian skepticism toward such stories is itself old.
- Dragon-slaying evidence appears much later than the proposed martyr: early images show a mounted saint spearing serpents, while narrative rescue-and-conversion accounts emerge in the 11th and 12th centuries.
- [[Bellerophon]] and older mounted-serpent imagery provide visual and mythic parallels, but the episode does not reduce George to a single classical source.
- Crusading and Norman traditions made George a military helper, but the episode rejects the simple claim that Richard the Lionheart brought the red cross back to England.
- [[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]] made George central to the [[OrderOfTheGarter]], and later English rulers used the saint and his cross in warfare, court ritual, relic culture, armor, coinage, and pageantry.
- George survived Protestant reform partly because his distant, uncertain biography lacked the concentrated shrine and relic infrastructure that made other saint cults easier targets.
- Protestant literature could recode him: Spenser's Red Cross Knight turns the dragon story into an allegory of the Church of England against Catholic error.
- George's modern uses remain plural. The episode acknowledges far-right appropriation while also retaining royal, sporting, ironic, Maltese, transnational Christian, and Muslim forms of veneration.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content found. The source extends the medieval and national-symbol branches by showing that a saint can gain institutional and political durability even when the underlying biography remains uncertain.
- The source explicitly rejects several overstatements: George's existence and identity are unverified, the dragon story is late, Georgia is not named after him, he was not a crusader, and the English red cross cannot be traced through a simple Richard-the-Lionheart origin story.
