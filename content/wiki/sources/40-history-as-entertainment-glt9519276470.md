# 40. History as Entertainment

## Summary

This episode of [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] follows historical entertainment from Roman spectacles and medieval tournaments through theme parks, board games, and video games. [[TomHolland]] and [[DominicSandbrook]] argue that entertainment has always been part of historical storytelling, while stressing that reenactments and games select, simplify, and politicize the past through roles, rules, victories, villains, and omissions.

## Key Claims

- Historical entertainment answers a desire to see, inhabit, or replay otherwise unreachable worlds; narrative pleasure is therefore integral to public engagement with history rather than an accidental supplement.
- Reenactments combine performance, sport, commemoration, and politics: from Roman staged victories and Victorian medievalism to [[PuyDuFou|Puy du Fou]], the past can legitimize national, imperial, or ideological stories.
- [[MonopolyGame|Monopoly]] shows that a game's political meaning can reverse over time: a Georgist critique of landlord accumulation became a mass-market competition centered on acquiring property and bankrupting rivals.
- Strategy games can make constraints, tradeoffs, betrayal, logistics, and statecraft experiential, but their rule systems also compress contingency into legible variables and reward structures.
- [[CivilizationGameSeries|Civilization]] and similar grand-strategy games can make industrialization, colonization, territorial expansion, and European-style development appear like a universal progression rather than one contested historical path.
- Detailed reconstruction and plausible texture can coexist with deliberate counterfactual play; historical games attract players partly by letting familiar actors and settings produce unfamiliar outcomes.
- Historical entertainment becomes ethically fraught when violence, slavery, Nazism, Confederate memory, or atrocity is aestheticized while victims, coercion, and political meaning are obscured.
