# You 2.0: Did That Really Happen?

## Summary
This [[HiddenBrain]] episode has psychologist [[AyannaThomas|Ayanna Thomas]] explain [[ReconstructiveMemory]] through the forgetting curve, schema-shaped recall, family stories, manipulated photographs, leading questions, and conflicting accounts of remote events. Its central warning is that confidence, vividness, and sincerity do not certify accuracy because later information and repeated imagination can produce [[MemoryContaminationRisk]] and [[ImaginationInflationAndSourceConfusion]]. The practical branch turns the same associative machinery toward [[SelfTestingMemoryPractice]], spacing, sleep, memory palaces, and distinctive interactive imagery.

## Key Claims
- Ebbinghaus's forgetting work presents retention as a steep early decline with residual learning that can make relearning faster than first acquisition.
- Bartlett's “War of the Ghosts” work illustrates that recall is systematically reconstructed through familiar schemas rather than degraded at random.
- Family stories, photographs, conversations, leading questions, and news or investigative language can supply details that later feel personally remembered.
- [[ImaginationInflationAndSourceConfusion]] makes repeatedly imagined actions more likely to be misidentified as actions that were actually performed.
- Confidence and vividness cannot by themselves distinguish a more accurate memory from a convincing reconstruction; high-stakes testimony needs corroboration and careful interviewing.
- Different participants can sincerely remember the same event differently because salience, attention, and later reconstruction differ; one person's nonmemory does not prove the event did not occur.
- [[SelfTestingMemoryPractice]], spaced study, sleep, familiar routes, and vivid interactive imagery are presented as ways to improve retrieval without making memory perfectly faithful.
- [[CognitiveAging]] can involve encoding and source-monitoring changes, while anxiety about age-linked decline can itself impair test performance through stereotype threat.
