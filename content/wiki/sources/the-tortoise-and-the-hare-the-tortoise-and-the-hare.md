# The Tortoise and the Hare

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode argues that legal education and hiring confuse useful selection proxies with the capabilities required for legal work. [[MalcolmGladwell]] contrasts the timed [[LawSchoolAdmissionTest|LSAT]] and elite-school pedigree with the slow, sustained reading demanded by difficult cases, using [[JeffreySutton]] as a counterexample to [[AntoninScalia]]'s prestige-based clerkship filter.

The episode does not reject speed or testing categorically. It argues that law needs both fast “hares” and deliberate “tortoises,” while [[EvanParker]]'s performance analysis suggests that school prestige and conventional interview ratings may add less predictive value than employers assume. Its proposed “don't ask, don't tell” rule for school names is a deliberately strong version of [[ObjectiveHiringAssessment]].

## Key Claims

- [[AntoninScalia]] says he usually selected Supreme Court clerks from the hardest law schools to enter because elite admission reduced his perceived risk of a hiring error.
- [[JeffreySutton]], whom Scalia later described as one of his finest clerks, attended Ohio State after Michigan rejected him and therefore contradicts a rule that equates elite-school attendance with exceptional legal ability.
- The [[LawSchoolAdmissionTest|LSAT]] asks candidates to solve demanding questions under fixed section limits, so its score reflects reasoning under time pressure as well as correctness.
- Supreme Court clerkship work can require months of slow reading, research, and analysis, creating a validity problem when access is heavily influenced by a speeded admissions test.
- Uniform timing improves standardization and score separation, but it prevents candidates from reallocating time among sections and may systematically favor faster readers.
- The episode argues that longer test time could change the ranking curve and enlarge the pool above elite schools' score thresholds, while acknowledging that some legal work genuinely rewards speed.
- Elite-school scarcity magnifies small selection differences; the episode proposes expanding prestigious institutions as one way to weaken that bottleneck.
- [[EvanParker]] reports that law-school prestige repeatedly failed to predict law-firm success in his analyses and that conventional interview ratings could be weak or even negatively associated with retention.
- Parker's account suggests that work history, later improvement, and demonstrated performance can reveal talent that pedigree-based screening misses.
- Gladwell proposes barring employers from asking where candidates studied and candidates from disclosing it, forcing hiring decisions toward ability, experience, and fit.
