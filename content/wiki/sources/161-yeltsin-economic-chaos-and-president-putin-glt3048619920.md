# 161. Yeltsin, Economic Chaos and President Putin

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode presents [[BorisYeltsin]]'s 1990s presidency as the crisis setting from which [[VladimirPutin]] emerged. [[TomHolland]] and [[DominicSandbrook]] connect [[PostSovietShockTherapy]], [[OligarchicPrivatizationCapture]], institutional collapse, the 1993 constitutional crisis, the Chechen wars, and national humiliation to a later demand for order and strength.

The episode also places domestic collapse inside the post-Cold War security settlement. It treats NATO enlargement as simultaneously understandable protection for Central and Eastern European states and a source of Russian grievance, then closes with Putin's appointment as prime minister, the Second Chechen War, and Yeltsin's resignation on December 31, 1999. Its main contribution is retrospective: [[AuthoritarianStabilityLegitimation]] becomes easier to understand when the preceding disorder is specified rather than invoked abstractly.

## Key Claims

- Rapid price liberalization under [[YegorGaidar]] is presented as an attempt to create a functioning market after Soviet controls, but the episode says inflation devastated pensioners and other people on fixed incomes.
- Soviet workplaces had supplied employment, services, and social structure, so enterprise collapse removed institutions of everyday life as well as production.
- Voucher privatization and later loans-for-shares deals allowed managers, criminal actors, and politically connected financiers to acquire valuable assets cheaply, creating [[OligarchicPrivatizationCapture]].
- The 1993 confrontation ended with Yeltsin using military force against parliament and adopting a constitution with a stronger presidency; [[AnatolySobchak]] is identified as a contributor to that constitution.
- The episode presents [[VladimirPutin]]'s St. Petersburg role under Sobchak, security-service connections, and usefulness to the Yeltsin circle as foundations for his later selection as successor.
- NATO enlargement is framed as a two-sided security problem: former Warsaw Pact and Baltic states sought protection, while Russia experienced the alliance's eastward movement as humiliation and threat.
- The First Chechen War exposed military weakness and produced mass death; the Second Chechen War then became part of Putin's emergence as a leader promising restored state capacity.
- Yeltsin's resignation and Putin's succession are interpreted as an elite bargain protecting Yeltsin, his family, and beneficiaries of the 1990s order, though the episode does not independently establish the terms of such a deal.

## Key Quotes

> "young boys in pink shorts and yellow boots" - the episode's quotation of Alexander Rutskoi's attack on westernized young reformers.

> "Weimar Russia" - the episode's shorthand for lost empire, economic collapse, weak democratic institutions, grievance, and receptiveness to strongman politics.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode's account of NATO enlargement preserves both Eastern European demand for protection and Russian perceptions of provocation rather than treating either perspective as a complete causal explanation.
- Claims that Western officials promised NATO would not expand require distinctions among verbal statements, the German reunification context, later policy, and legally binding commitments; the episode's compressed account remains source-scoped.
- Claims that the 1999 apartment bombings were an FSB false flag are disputed and are not adopted as established fact. The quoted poverty, mortality, inflation, election, casualty, and privatization figures also remain source-scoped pending external verification.
