# 413. The Peasants' Revolt: England Erupts (Part 1)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode reconstructs the origins and early spread of the [[PeasantsRevolt1381|Peasants' Revolt of 1381]]. It argues that the label “peasants' revolt” obscures a movement concentrated in commercially connected, relatively prosperous regions and driven by ambitious rural communities as well as poorer laborers. The immediate trigger was enforcement of the third [[PollTax|poll tax]], but failed war finance, post-plague labor scarcity, wage controls, serfdom, local lordship, hostile officials, and documentary enforcement supplied the deeper conflict.

The episode follows resistance from Brentwood and the Bocking oath through coordinated attacks across Essex and Kent, the capture of Rochester and Canterbury, and the march toward London under [[WatTyler|Wat Tyler]]. [[JohnBall|John Ball's]] Christian egalitarianism gave a radical religious language to demands for free labor, an end to servitude, and redistribution of church wealth, while systematic attacks on records show that rebels understood written archives as practical instruments of coercion.

## Key Claims

- The 1381 rising was not simply an uprising of the poorest peasants; prosperous, mobile, and assertive rural people played a leading role in the wealthier southeast.
- The failing [[HundredYearsWar|Hundred Years' War]] and three poll taxes connected military disappointment to resentment of Parliament, ministers, commissioners, and local officeholders.
- Black Death mortality tightened labor supply and raised wages, while the Statute of Labourers and surviving servile obligations attempted to preserve pre-plague control.
- The third poll tax and intrusive enforcement converted long-running labor, lordship, and legal grievances into open resistance at Brentwood.
- Horse-borne organizers, inter-village coordination, and the Bocking oath show a networked political movement rather than spontaneous local disorder.
- Rebels systematically attacked legal records because documents enforced labor service, dues, wage limits, and manorial authority.
- [[JohnBall|John Ball's]] preaching and [[WatTyler|Wat Tyler's]] leadership joined material demands to egalitarian, anticlerical, and organizational currents.

## Key Quotes

> “When Adam delved, and Eve span, who then was a gentleman?” - Thomas Walsingham's hostile report of the egalitarian argument associated with John Ball.

> “ambitious and assertive” - Nigel Saul's characterization, as reported by the episode, of many participants in the rising.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with Part 2 was found; this episode supplies the pre-London origins, organization, and regional escalation for that account of the capital and suppression.
- The episode challenges a bottom-up poverty-only interpretation without denying deprivation, servitude, or coercion; “peasants” remains a conventional label rather than a complete social description.
- Walsingham's Blackheath sermon scene is treated as chronologically doubtful, and Wat Tyler's earlier biography or military experience remains speculative.
- Population totals, the exact degree of preplanning, reported oaths and speeches, official motives, and broad claims about Robin Hood culture remain source-scoped.
