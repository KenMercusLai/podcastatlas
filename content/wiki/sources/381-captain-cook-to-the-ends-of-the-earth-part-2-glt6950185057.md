# 381. Captain Cook: To the Ends of the Earth (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[JamesCook]] and the [[Endeavour]] from the transit of Venus at [[Tahiti]] through the charting and British claiming of [[NewZealand]] and the eastern coast of [[Australia]]. It uses a Star Trek “prime directive” analogy to ask whether scientific observation can remain non-interfering once trade, sex, disease, theft, translation, firearms, and imperial rivalry enter an encounter.

The episode's strongest synthesis is that Cook cannot be reduced either to an innocent explorer or a flat colonial villain. His discipline, scientific curiosity, and growing respect for Pacific peoples coexist with coercive threats, lethal violence, possession ceremonies, and consequences reconstructed mainly through European journals. [[Tupia]]'s agency and translation work, [[Maori|Māori]] resistance, and [[AboriginalAustralians|Aboriginal Australian]] indifference and refusal complicate a story centered on European discovery.

## Key Claims

- Cook's public mission was to observe the transit of Venus at Tahiti, while sealed Admiralty instructions also directed him to search for Terra Australis and claim suitable lands for Britain.
- The Royal Society's instruction to avoid harm or interference could not survive contact unchanged: trade, sexual exchange, disease risk, theft, punishment, and unequal firepower altered relations even when the stated purpose was observation.
- [[JosephBanks]] combined serious curiosity about Pacific language, ritual, surfing, tattooing, flora, and fauna with European assumptions that sometimes misread local behavior.
- Tupia chose to join the voyage and became an essential translator in New Zealand, revealing linguistic connections across Polynesia and helping Cook appreciate long-distance [[PolynesianNavigation]].
- Early New Zealand encounters included multiple Māori deaths at British hands, failed coercive attempts at diplomacy, and remorse recorded by Cook and Banks.
- Cook charted both main islands of New Zealand and performed a British possession ceremony despite minimal local participation or consent.
- At Botany Bay and during later repairs, Aboriginal Australians did not behave as expectant recipients of European trade; refusal, resistance, limited accommodation, and British collection of objects made the encounter negotiated and coercive rather than a simple discovery scene.
- Cook's first voyage joined scientific achievement and extraordinary seamanship to imperial knowledge and territorial claims whose later demographic and geopolitical consequences greatly exceeded the expedition's immediate actions.

## Key Quotes

> "as a curiosity" - Banks's troubling description of possibly taking Tupia to Britain, qualified by the episode's emphasis on Tupia's own agency.

> "naturally of good disposition" - Cook's source-described judgment of Māori despite conflict and his horror at cannibalism.
