# 104. Macbeth

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode separates [[MacbethHistoricalKing|the historical Macbeth]] from [[MacbethPlay|Shakespeare's Macbeth]]. It reconstructs 11th-century [[Scotland]] as a changing field of Pictish, Gaelic, Brittonic, Northumbrian, Viking, Alba, and Moray power in which succession admitted several adult royal claimants; Macbeth kills Duncan in battle, rules for about seventeen years, travels to Rome with [[Gruoch]], and is later displaced by Duncan's line.

The second half follows the making of Macbeth's tyrant image through later dynastic history, Holinshed, [[WilliamShakespeare]], and the Jacobean court of [[JamesVIAndI|James VI and I]]. Banquo, equivocation after the [[GunpowderPlot]], royal witchcraft fears, regicide, Lady Macbeth, later productions, and the late theatrical curse tradition show a historical reputation repeatedly reshaped for political and performance needs.

## Key Claims

- Early medieval Scotland was not a settled modern nation-state: Alba, Moray, Strathclyde, Northumbrian, Pictish, Gaelic, and Viking power overlapped while surviving evidence remained sparse and politically rewritten.
- [[MedievalScottishSuccession|Medieval Scottish succession]] did not operate through simple primogeniture, so several adult members of the royal kin could present plausible claims and conflict was structurally recurrent.
- Duncan attacks Macbeth in Moray and dies in battle in 1040; the source therefore distinguishes the historical succession war from Shakespeare's murder of an elderly guest-king.
- Macbeth's long reign, pilgrimage to Rome with Gruoch, continued rule after the 1054 invasion, and honorable burial on Iona complicate the later image of a brief, universally hated usurper.
- Later restoration of Duncan's line encouraged a cleaner continuous genealogy in which Macbeth could be made an illegitimate interruption.
- Shakespeare drew on chronicle tradition but reshaped it for James: Banquo becomes virtuous, witchcraft becomes central, and the play absorbs post-Gunpowder-Plot language about equivocation and hidden treason.
- The play's later force comes through regicide, ambition, gendered suspicion, adaptation, and theatrical superstition; the curse story may itself be a late-19th-century invention rather than an early performance tradition.

## Key Quotes

> "effective and probably popular king" - the episode's contrast between the historical ruler and the theatrical tyrant.

## Contradictions

- The source directly corrects the Shakespeare-shaped image of Macbeth as a brief, murderous usurper: in its historical account Duncan dies in battle after attacking Moray, and Macbeth rules successfully for about seventeen years.
- Macbeth's role in Gilla Comgain's death, the location and sequence of the 1054 fighting, Malcolm's movements, the strength of popular support, the Oxford-nymph influence, textual shortening, and the origin of the theatrical curse remain uncertain or source-scoped.
