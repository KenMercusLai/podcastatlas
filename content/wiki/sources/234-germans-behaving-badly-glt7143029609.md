# 234. Germans Behaving Badly

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode with [[AndreaWulf|Andrea Wulf]] uses the philosophers, poets, critics, translators, and partners gathered in 1790s Jena to explain [[JenaRomanticism|early German Romanticism]] as an attempt to place the active self at the center of knowledge, freedom, morality, art, science, and nature. It treats [[JohannGottliebFichte|Johann Gottlieb Fichte]] as the intellectual catalyst, [[CarolineSchlegelSchelling|Caroline Schlegel Schelling]] as an indispensable but under-credited critic and collaborator, and [[GWFHegel|G. W. F. Hegel]] as a late arrival who helps mark the movement's end.

The episode also keeps ideas inseparable from institutions and lives. Fragmented German sovereignty and Jena's divided governance weakened censorship; shared households, translation, magazines, lectures, affairs, feuds, and gendered publishing constraints made the movement socially productive and personally unstable; and Napoleon's victory at Jena supplied a military ending to its local world.

## Key Claims

- The Jena circle helped redefine modern individuality by treating the self as an active organizer of experience rather than a passive receiver of a fully given world.
- Fichte's freedom was not intended as permission for narcissism: self-determination carried moral duty and responsibility toward others, although later readers could detach liberty from obligation.
- German Romanticism sought to reunite reason and imagination, art and science, and self and nature rather than simply reject rational inquiry for emotion or fantasy.
- Jena's political fragmentation, university culture, and incomplete censorship created an unusually open intellectual setting in which older figures such as Goethe and Schiller met a younger radical generation.
- Caroline's editing, reviewing, translating, criticism, and household labor were central to the group, but gender and scandal restricted public attribution of that work.
- The same emphasis on rebellious individuality that energized collaboration also intensified rivalry, jealousy, literary combat, and the circle's eventual breakup.
- Hegel's arrival and Napoleon's 1806 victory are presented as intellectual and political endpoints, while the movement's selfhood and nature ideas continued through English Romanticism and American transcendentalism.
