# Project Dillard

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode uses [[DillardUniversity]] to argue that college rankings can reward institutional wealth, affluent and conventionally prepared students, selectivity, and inherited reputation more readily than educational contribution. [[MalcolmGladwell]] and Dillard president [[WalterKimbrough]] contrast the ranking incentives with Dillard's access mission, supportive academic culture, and reported production of Black physics graduates. A deliberately exaggerated simulation makes the central conflict concrete: Dillard could rise by becoming richer, smaller, and more exclusionary while changing little about teaching and abandoning many students it exists to serve.

## Key Claims

- [[CollegeRankingPrivilege]] arises when correlated measures of wealth, student advantage, selectivity, completion, and reputation are treated as independent signs of institutional quality.
- The episode's Reed College analysis reports that endowment alone predicted about half of peer-assessment variation and that a larger institutional model predicted 91.3%, suggesting that reputation judgments may substantially reproduce visible institutional characteristics.
- Six-year graduation rates are difficult to interpret without student context because housing instability, family illness, job loss, and low income can interrupt study independently of teaching quality.
- The episode's simulation moves Dillard from 161st to 103rd by substituting Williams College's financial profile, to 43rd after adding stronger test scores, and eventually to third after changing remaining ranked inputs.
- The simulated climb requires Dillard to shrink, reject more applicants, recruit affluent high-scoring students, and redirect resources toward prestige-enhancing conditions rather than widen access.
- Dillard's reported Black-physics outcomes and close faculty, adviser, peer, and presidential support illustrate [[PotentialAsGrowthRate|developmental value]] that input-heavy ranking measures can miss.
- Colleges, applicants, parents, and ranking publishers collectively sustain the system when they submit data, optimize behavior, and treat a rank as a general measure of educational worth.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. The episode extends the wiki's existing ranking critique from visibility and data integrity to construct validity and incentive design.
- Graduation, completion, spending, and selectivity can contain useful information; the episode shows that they are confounded and incomplete, not that they are always irrelevant.
- Rankings can facilitate comparison, but a single ordinal score cannot establish institutional fit, causal teaching quality, or value added for a particular student.
