## Summary
This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode interviews [[SapnaMaheshwari]] of the [[NewYorkTimes|New York Times]] about [[AIBabyMonitors]], subscription parenting features, and the spread of AI advice into nursery products. The episode uses [[Nanit]], [[HatchBaby|Hatch]], sleep scores, parent-facing chatbots, and general tools such as [[ChatGPT]] and [[Claude]] to show how baby tech is moving from safety monitoring into guidance, data interpretation, and paid service layers.

The source's main contribution is a new parenting-tech branch linking [[AIParentingAdvice]], [[QuantifiedParenting]], [[ParentalJudgmentOutsourcing]], [[ChildBedroomDataPrivacy]], and [[ParentingDataHypervigilance]]. It treats the products as genuinely useful for some families while keeping the risks explicit: parents may over-defer to systems, children's bedroom data may outlive infancy, and sleep metrics can either calm or intensify anxiety.

## Key Claims
- Modern nursery products can combine HD cameras, biometric sensors, and AI analysis.
- Some monitor features target acute safety risks, such as hazardous position changes or a blanket covering a baby's face.
- Products are expanding from urgent monitoring into sleep optimization, sleep plans, parent chatbots, and recurring subscriptions.
- Maheshwari used Nanit with both of her children, and the system suggested sleep timing and put-down routines.
- Other products she used, including Hatch and a baby-tracking app, began adding paid upgrades around sleep tips, sleep plans, and chatbot features.
- Baby sleep scores borrow language and behavior from adult wearables such as [[OuraRing|Oura Ring]] and [[Whoop]].
- Some parents compare their own wearable sleep scores with their baby's Nanit score on social media.
- The episode warns that parents may dull instincts by outsourcing ordinary observations, such as noticing when a baby is tired.
- Some parents also use [[ChatGPT]] or [[Claude]] to create baby sleep plans.
- Nanit says it makes money from hardware, apps, and subscriptions rather than selling or marketing against data, but the source still raises privacy concerns.
- Nanit's CEO is described as wanting the product to remain part of children's bedrooms until the child gets a first [[AppleWatch|Apple Watch]], often around age 10.
- The anxiety effect varies: some parents avoid breathing bands or heart monitors because they know the data would make them spiral, while others find specific visibility calming or useful.
