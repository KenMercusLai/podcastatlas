## Summary
This [[HowIBuiltThis]] episode features [[GuyRaz]] interviewing [[RothMartin]] about building [[Rothys|Rothy's]] with [[StephenHawthornthwaite]] from an athleisure-era footwear hypothesis into a profitable shoe brand with proprietary manufacturing. The account connects recycled polyester, programmable flat knitting, failed U.S. development, a defective first production run, a six-month sales shutdown, technical-team internalization, owned Chinese manufacturing, direct customer learning, and cautious retail expansion. Its central synthesis is that a sustainability story became defensible only when [[KnitToShapeFootwearManufacturing]], [[VerticalIntegrationForQualityControl]], healthy first-sale economics, and [[ProductLedBrandLongevity]] reinforced one another.

## Key Claims
- Martin moved from commodities trading into collectible European furniture, applying research and arbitrage judgment before concluding that his taste- and relationship-dependent Hedge Gallery was difficult to scale.
- Martin and Hawthornthwaite identified a gap around 2011-2012 between fashionable but uncomfortable ballet flats, occasion-limited sneakers, and comfortable but less versatile flip-flops.
- Their first China prototype placed a sock-like upper over a shoe last and injected a rubber outsole; it did not fit securely but established the basic construction path.
- Circular knitting could not provide enough control over different structural zones, so the team shifted to flat knitting and selected polyester for strength, rigidity, and color performance.
- The founders worked with Unifi on recycled-polyester yarn and programmed shaped uppers directly, reducing the cutting waste created by conventional sheet-based footwear construction.
- A roughly 3,000-square-foot development facility in Lewiston, Maine exposed inconsistent machinery, variable yarn behavior, dye differences, and millimeter-level sizing demands. The founders invested about $1 million each and traveled there nearly weekly for about 18 months.
- In 2015, a sweater-factory programmer in Fujian improved the upper rapidly enough that Martin told Hawthornthwaite to close the Maine operation; knitting and assembly initially remained split between Fujian and the Guangzhou area.
- Rothy's launched online in December 2015 with two flat styles and several thousand pairs, reportedly selling about $100,000 in its first month.
- The outside assembly factory then changed construction, substituted materials, and removed processes under economics that gave it little incentive to preserve quality. Rothy's stopped selling for about six months in early 2016.
- The company hired its own pattern maker and knit programmer, bought knitting machines, supervised assembly closely, and later brought full production into its own Chinese factory.
- Rothy's reported just under $3 million of revenue in the second half of 2016. Martin estimated early material and machine-production cost near $10 per pair against launch prices around $120-$140, while noting that this was not a full landed-cost or audited margin account.
- Word of mouth joined comfort, washability, frequent wear, and the recycled-bottle explanation; farmers-market selling also let the company observe try-on and customer questions directly.
- In 2017 the owned factory grew from about nine workers to roughly 200. By the pandemic it reportedly employed about 900, creating substantial fixed overhead when normal wearing occasions disappeared.
- Brazilian company Alpargatas acquired a 49.9% stake at the end of 2021 in a transaction described as valuing Rothy's at $1 billion; Martin saw manufacturing diversification as part of the strategic fit.
- The first San Francisco store helped customers test an unfamiliar recycled-plastic shoe. One roughly 300-square-foot location reportedly produced about $3.5 million before broader retail expansion.
- Martin attributes durability to innovative product, proprietary production, strong margins, repeat purchase, cautious channel testing, operating-cash-flow funding, culture, and patience rather than growth at any cost.

## Contradictions
- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content is adopted. The source reinforces existing cases in which vertical integration is justified by quality-sensitive process knowledge, while showing that ownership also creates fixed-cost and geographic-concentration exposure.
- Revenue, cost, margin, staffing, valuation, profitability, investment, store-productivity, wage, waste, and certification figures remain source-scoped to Martin and the host framing rather than independently audited here.
- The episode is a retrospective co-founder account with limited supplier, employee, customer, co-founder, investor, and environmental-life-cycle evidence. Recycled input and reduced cutting waste do not by themselves establish total environmental impact.
