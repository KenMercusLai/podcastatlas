## Summary
This [[PlanetMoney]] episode uses a July 2026 [[Cyclospora]] outbreak potentially linked to shredded lettuce from [[TaylorFarms]] and eaten by many [[TacoBell]] customers to explain modern lettuce economics and produce safety. It argues that [[BaggedSaladIndustrialization]] turned lettuce from a commodity into a standardized, value-added product, while [[ProduceContaminationAmplification]] makes a small farm or processing problem capable of spreading across many states. The regulatory story centers on the [[FoodSafetyModernizationAct]], [[FoodAndDrugAdministration|FDA]] farm-safety rules, inspection capacity, imported produce, and unresolved [[FoodbornePathogenUncertainty]].

## Key Claims
- The episode says more than 1,600 people had been infected in the current [[Cyclospora]] outbreak, with many cases connected to [[TacoBell]] meals and a 27-state [[TaylorFarms]] lettuce recall.
- [[TaylorFarms]] is presented as the leading U.S. salad processor, supplying restaurants and retailers including [[TacoBell]], [[McDonalds|McDonald's]], [[Costco]], [[WholeFoodsMarket|Whole Foods]], Aldi, and Safeway.
- Lettuce became a national year-round product first because iceberg lettuce tolerated long-distance shipping, then because breathable plastic films made chopped and mixed salads shelf-stable enough for grocery and restaurant distribution.
- Bagged lettuce shifted the market from whole-head commodity selling toward manufactured salad kits and value-added processing, which requires capital-intensive plants and concentrates the industry among a few processors.
- [[TaylorFarms]] acts as a processor and middleman, buying lettuce mostly from [[California]], [[Arizona]], and increasingly [[Mexico]], washing and mixing it in more than two dozen facilities, then distributing it nationally.
- The episode argues that large-scale processing can increase outbreak reach because products from multiple farms can be mixed, processed, and sent to many states before contamination is detected.
- The 2006 spinach E. coli outbreak exposed a regulatory blind spot: the [[FoodAndDrugAdministration|FDA]] had facility rules for processors but lacked official safety rules for farms growing fruits and vegetables.
- Congress passed the [[FoodSafetyModernizationAct]] in 2010, giving the [[FoodAndDrugAdministration|FDA]] authority to inspect farms and enforce produce-safety standards around hygiene, water, manure, compost, and animal feces in fields.
- Routine farm-safety inspections did not begin until 2019, and the episode says some water standards were finalized only recently, with some farms not required to comply until 2027.
- The source identifies limited money, limited inspector staffing, and the absence of a reliable regular farm-inspection schedule as practical constraints on [[FarmSafetyInspectionCapacity]].
- Imports complicate oversight: the episode says 60% of U.S. fruit and about one-third of fresh vegetables are imported, and foreign inspections require permission, money, and reliance on importers to verify supplier compliance.
- The episode keeps the current outbreak source-scoped because, at the time of recording on July 22, 2026, the [[FoodAndDrugAdministration|FDA]] believed the tainted lettuce came from a farm in [[Mexico]] but the final cause remained unresolved.
- Scientific uncertainty remains a safety constraint: the source says investigators could not fully explain how salmonella reached peaches in one outbreak, and that [[Cyclospora]] transmission and testing remain poorly understood.
- The episode's risk framing is balanced: raw food can never be risk-free, but Americans eat billions of pounds of lettuce safely each year, so the public-health problem is rare, distributed, and sometimes severe rather than constant.
