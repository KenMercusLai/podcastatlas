## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode uses [[JimBalsillie]]'s account of [[BlackBerry]]'s 2011 strategic crisis to argue that analytical correctness does not implement itself. Balsillie wanted the company to leave declining handset hardware and turn [[BlackBerryMessenger|BlackBerry Messenger]] into a carrier-bundled, cross-platform service, but the board rejected the plan after he had done too little preparation, repeated explanation, trust-building, and coalition work.

The episode treats the failure as both [[SelfDisruptionDiscipline]] and [[FounderBoardRelationshipMaintenance]]: an incumbent must recognize when its current business is dying, while the executive advocating a radical pivot must still make the proposal legible and supportable to the people who hold formal decision power.

## Key Claims

- [[JimBalsillie]] joined [[MikeLazaridis]] at Research in Motion and helped turn [[BlackBerry]] into a multibillion-dollar smartphone company.
- By 2011, Balsillie believed [[Apple]] and [[Google]] had made BlackBerry's handset path untenable while its messaging service remained a valuable strategic asset.
- He spent roughly eighteen months acquiring companies and developing [[BlackBerryMessenger|BBM]] into an open service with storage, rich media, voice-over-IP, groups, and delivery-status features.
- BBM reportedly had about 80 million users, more than the emerging [[WhatsApp]], and Balsillie says two of the three major US carriers had committed to bundle the service with mobile plans.
- The board hired consultants, showed limited interest in the proposal, and cancelled the initiative shortly before its planned launch; Balsillie then resigned from the board and sold his shares.
- Balsillie retrospectively says he assumed evidence and his strategic record would carry the decision, underestimating the board's authority and the need for preparation, conversation, reassurance, and trust.
- [[MikeLazaridis]] and new CEO Thorsten Heins remained committed to hardware and believed the services pivot would destroy that business, while Balsillie argued that the hardware business was already dead.
- Balsillie's failed attempts to buy and relocate a [[NationalHockeyLeague|National Hockey League]] franchise are used as a parallel case in which a strong market argument did not overcome institutional and relational resistance.

## Key Quotes

> “The hardware business was already dead.” — Balsillie's retrospective answer to the objection that the services plan would destroy BlackBerry's device business.

> “Heretic” — Balsillie's description of his tendency to challenge accepted thinking without accommodating others' sensitivities.

## Contradictions

- No settled factual contradiction was adopted. The episode challenges product-only explanations of BlackBerry's decline by adding governance, persuasion, and board-power mechanisms rather than disproving competition or execution explanations.
- Balsillie's claim that an open BBM strategy had a 100 percent chance of major success is a participant counterfactual, not an observable result. User counts and carrier commitments do not establish retention, monetization, execution quality, or competitive response.
- The supplied file is a structured summary rather than a transcript, board record, consultant report, launch plan, carrier agreement, or market study. Timing, commitments, internal motives, and remembered dialogue remain episode-attributed.
- The board, Lazaridis, Heins, carrier executives, employees, and other directors do not provide full accounts, so the episode cannot establish that relational failure was the only or decisive reason for rejection.
