# 547. The French Revolution: The Execution of the King (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[LouisXVI|Louis XVI]] through indictment, defence, voting, farewell, execution, burial, and immediate political afterlife. It treats the proceedings not only as punishment of an accused traitor but as an attempt by the [[NationalConvention|National Convention]] and the [[FrenchRevolution|French Revolution]] to strip kingship of sacred exception and make the Republic real through a public act.

The episode also preserves the unresolved struggle around that act. The [[Girondins]] sought a referendum and later a reprieve without ceasing to be republicans, the execution joined ordinary criminal procedure to extraordinary military spectacle, and royalist martyrdom competed immediately with republican accounts of a defeated tyrant.

## Key Claims

- Louis was prosecuted both for alleged treason and as the living symbol of monarchy, making legal judgment inseparable from a wider attack on sacred kingship.
- Addressing him as “Louis Capet,” withholding courtly deference, dressing him as a private citizen, and using ordinary execution procedure performed his removal from royal status before death.
- Louis rejected a defence based chiefly on constitutional inviolability and instead answered the indictment point by point, although the hosts judge that flight, foreign contact, and counterrevolutionary plotting made factual acquittal implausible.
- The Girondin appeal to a popular referendum and later reprieve showed that republican disagreement concerned punishment, political legitimacy, and procedure as well as guilt.
- Public voting, petition pressure, correspondence with the king, and suspicion of concealed treason made the Convention's decision a test of factional loyalty.
- The guillotine presented execution as equal, mechanical state procedure, but the armed occupation, renamed square, controlled crowd, drums, and display of the head made it an exceptional founding spectacle.
- The hosts interpret Louis's death as a sacrificial moment through which the Republic acquired symbolic existence, while keeping that interpretation distinct from the official claim that he died like any other condemned person.
- Unmarked burial and hostile stories sought to deny royal martyrdom, but royalists immediately treated Louis as a martyr and his son as Louis XVII.
- Competing accounts of dignity, panic, innocence, tyranny, sacrifice, and national joy left the execution politically unsettled and pointed toward deeper civil conflict.

## Key Quotes

> “Serene joy” - Marat's reported description of the public mood after the execution, as presented by the episode.

> “Only after the tyrant's head had fallen did he truly believe in the Republic.” - the episode's paraphrase of Marat's political interpretation.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with the existing French Revolution branch. This episode supplies the January 1793 regicide layer that precedes the later crisis, Girondin purge, Terror, and Marat martyrdom already represented in the wiki.
- The claim that the death sentence passed “by one vote” is retained as the episode's formulation; the source does not reconstruct the disputed counting conventions in enough detail to make it an unqualified wiki-wide judgment.
- Louis's final words, reported conversations, exact crowd mood, private motives, symbolic intent, military and vote totals, and hostile posthumous anecdotes remain source-scoped reconstructions.
- The interpretation of the execution as the Republic's founding sacrifice is the hosts' historical synthesis, not a claim that all contemporaries shared one meaning.
