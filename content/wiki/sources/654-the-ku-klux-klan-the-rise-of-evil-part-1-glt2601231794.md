# 654. The Ku Klux Klan: The Rise of Evil (Part 1)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode traces the first [[KuKluxKlan|Ku Klux Klan]] from a small Confederate veterans' social club founded in Pulaski, Tennessee, in 1866 into a decentralized white-supremacist paramilitary movement. Emancipation, Black institution-building and voting, congressional Reconstruction, and [[AndrewJohnson|Andrew Johnson]]'s conflict with Radical Republicans form the political setting in which costumes, invented ranks, secrecy, and public parades became instruments of intimidation.

The episode follows [[GeorgeWGordon|George W. Gordon]]'s organizational Prescript, [[NathanBedfordForrest|Nathan Bedford Forrest]]'s reported position as Grand Wizard, and [[WilliamGannawayBrownlow|William Gannaway Brownlow]]'s effort to protect Tennessee's Reconstruction government. Its central claim is that the group's absurd theatricality must not obscure its political function: by 1868, night raids, whipping, rape, hanging, shooting, and murder targeted Black citizens, Union veterans, and Republican organizers while sympathetic or passive local institutions made punishment unlikely.

## Key Claims

- The Civil War and Reconstruction setting is indispensable: emancipation created schools, churches, Union Leagues, voting power, and Republican organization that white ex-Confederates saw as threats to racial and political control.
- The Klan began around late May 1866 among six educated Confederate veterans in Pulaski as a fraternal club with mock-secret ritual, costumes, and invented titles.
- Political conflict in late 1866 and 1867 converted the club into a militant movement opposing Black equality, Republican rule, and congressional Reconstruction.
- [[GeorgeWGordon|George W. Gordon]]'s Prescript supplied elaborate ranks and membership questions, but formal hierarchy did not produce reliable centralized command.
- [[NathanBedfordForrest|Nathan Bedford Forrest]] became the reported Grand Wizard while local dens retained substantial autonomy.
- [[WilliamGannawayBrownlow|Brownlow]]'s defense of Black voting, threats of militia or federal force, and conflict with former Confederates made Tennessee Reconstruction a focal point for resistance.
- Costumes, ghost performances, songs, notices, merchandise, and comic titles were part of coercion because targets understood that refusal or resistance could be followed by violence.
- By summer 1868, beatings, shootings, hangings, rape, and murder were the movement's core activity, aimed especially at Black political participation, Union loyalty, and Republican organization.
- Sympathetic sheriffs, attorneys, grand juries, jurors, and local officials created a protection gap in which formal law existed without dependable enforcement.

## Contradictions

- The estimate of roughly 150,000 members, the claim that dens existed in about one quarter of former Confederate counties, dates, quotations, motives, local affiliations, and individual atrocity details remain episode-attributed.
- Claims about Tennessee's exceptional social structure, the geographic conditions favoring Klan activity, and the causal effect of Brownlow's threats describe the hosts' synthesis rather than independently verified comparisons.
