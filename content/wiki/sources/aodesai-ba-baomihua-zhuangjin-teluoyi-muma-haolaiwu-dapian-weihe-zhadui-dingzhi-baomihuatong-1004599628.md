## Summary
This [[ShengdongZaokafei|声动早咖啡]] episode opens with short business updates on [[LuckinCoffee|瑞幸咖啡]], [[Amazon]], [[DinTaiFung|鼎泰丰]], and [[MarriottInternational|Marriott]], then uses blockbuster popcorn buckets to explain a new theatrical merchandise loop. Its central argument is that [[CustomMoviePopcornBuckets|custom movie popcorn buckets]] extend [[ThemeParkAsMediaPlatform|theme-park souvenir logic]] into cinemas: licensed objects can raise concession-margin revenue, create social-media moments, mark fandom identity, and become collectible scarcity goods. The source also stresses the operating risk: theaters must order months before a release, so a weak film, wrong character choice, or overestimated fan demand can turn high-margin merchandise into [[InventoryWriteDownRisk|inventory risk]].

## Key Claims
- [[LuckinCoffee|瑞幸咖啡]] is described as adding nearly 2,700 China stores in the second quarter and exceeding 36,000 stores, while facing same-store pressure from delivery-subsidy comparisons, tea-drink competitors, and Starbucks' China-market push.
- [[Amazon]] is described as crossing a $3 trillion market-cap threshold after strong cloud growth, while [[WholeFoodsMarket|Whole Foods]] becomes more important as Amazon lowers prices, pushes private labels, and retreats from Amazon Fresh supermarkets.
- [[DinTaiFung|鼎泰丰]] is described as having only 14 mainland China stores after another Shanghai closure, with high-end dining pressure tied to value-for-money expectations, rent, labor, and service-fee changes.
- [[MarriottInternational|Marriott]] is described as facing Middle East travel-demand pressure after regional revenue per available room fell sharply, while North America saw a lift from World Cup and summer-travel demand.
- The episode says [[TheOdyssey|《奥德赛》]] marketed special popcorn buckets shaped like a Trojan horse and an [[IMAX]] film camera, making the bucket itself part of the film's promotional surface.
- Custom buckets have moved beyond printed containers into physical replicas of characters, props, vehicles, bags, and scenes; the product is now closer to collectible merchandise than ordinary concession packaging.
- The source traces the format to [[TheWaltDisneyCompany|Disney]], [[Disneyland]], [[TokyoDisneyResort]], and Universal-style theme-park souvenirs, where food containers also carry memory, character attachment, and repeat-visit value.
- Design vendors such as [[ZincPopcornBuckets|Zinc]], [[SnapCreative]], and [[GoldenLink]] are presented as an industry layer that seeks rights-holder approval before selling licensed buckets to cinemas.
- [[AMCTheatres|AMC Theatres]] helped validate the category when a 2019 [[StarWars|Star Wars]] R2-D2 bucket designed by Zinc sold out quickly at a high price point.
- The 2024 Dune sandworm bucket turned the category into a social-media marketing contest, with later films designing buckets partly for discussion, humor, and shareability.
- Because theaters keep more of concessions and merchandise than of split ticket revenue, a $50 or $80 bucket can matter to theater economics even when the film ticket itself has lower retained margin.
- Limited release windows, fan identity, and resale premiums connect movie buckets to [[CollectibleDropScarcity]], but the same short cycle creates [[RetailInventoryVelocity]] and [[InventoryWriteDownRisk]] problems.
- China-side examples such as Nezha 2 and Zootopia 2 show the format moving into domestic theaters, where low supply and fan demand can create secondary-market premiums.
