# 329: Coronations: Chaos, Ceremony and Empire (Part 3)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode surveys British coronations from George I to the eve of [[CharlesIII|Charles III's]] ceremony. It treats [[BritishCoronationRitual|coronation]] as a durable sacred rite repeatedly reshaped by succession politics, marital conflict, administrative failure, popular crowds, imperial display, and changing ideas of national inclusion.

The episode's strongest through-line is the coexistence of consecration and theatre. Hanoverian riots, forgotten ceremonial equipment, picnicking spectators, George IV's extravagance, Victoria's railway crowds, imperial homage, radio, and television show that solemn meaning has never eliminated disorder or mediation; [[CoronationMassMediaTransformation|mass media]] instead changed who could witness the ritual and how monarchy became a shared public event.

## Key Claims

- George I's contested Protestant succession and coronation-day riots made coronation a test of Hanoverian legitimacy rather than an uncomplicated display of unity.
- George II's ceremony paired comparatively effective organization with Handel's immediately admired "Zadok the Priest," despite a mistimed choral entrance.
- [[GeorgeIII|George III's]] coronation exposed the fragility of sacred theatre when congestion, missing equipment, lack of rehearsal, eating spectators, and banquet disorder overwhelmed ceremony.
- [[GeorgeIV|George IV]] revived archaizing costume and magnificent display, but exclusion of Caroline of Brunswick, oppressive heat, extreme cost, wax, awkward ritual, and post-banquet looting made the event both splendid and cruelly farcical.
- William IV reduced expense and abolished major elements such as the banquet, yet reported awe when crowned, showing that pared-down ritual could retain personal sacral force.
- [[QueenVictoria|Victoria's]] coronation became a railway-enabled mass event while remaining under-rehearsed; Edward VII and George V then made imperial representation increasingly explicit, including the Delhi Durbar.
- George VI's radio-age ceremony and [[ElizabethII|Elizabeth II's]] television coronation moved participation beyond Westminster Abbey and made communication technology part of monarchical legitimacy.
- The debate over [[CharlesIII|Charles III]] turns on whether Christian anointing can remain publicly meaningful in a more secular and sceptical Britain, or whether spectacle now carries most of the event's popular appeal.

## Key Quotes

> "read the riot act" - the enduring phrase connected in the episode to legislation passed after early Hanoverian unrest.

> "shame" - the crowd's reported response when Caroline of Brunswick was refused entry to Westminster Abbey.

> "another kingdom" - George IV's reported measure of what it would take to make him endure the coronation ordeal again.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode qualifies any story of coronation becoming steadily more polished: competence improved unevenly, while the balance among worship, politics, entertainment, empire, and media changed.
- The hosts disagree in emphasis rather than fact about modern relevance: one treats the Christian service as the ceremony's indispensable core, while the other stresses that many viewers value spectacle without sharing that theology.
- Crowd sizes, costs converted into modern GDP terms, riot totals, television audiences, motives, reported dialogue, and judgments such as “vulgar” or “dull” remain source-scoped.
