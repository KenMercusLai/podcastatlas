# How to Change the World

## Summary

This [[HiddenBrain]] episode has political scientist [[EricaChenoweth|Erica Chenoweth]] describe how a challenge from [[MariaStephan|Maria Stephan]] led them to compare maximalist violent and nonviolent campaigns systematically. Their episode-reported result is that nonviolent campaigns succeeded about twice as often because [[NonviolentResistanceStrategy]] can lower participation barriers, widen coalitions, induce defections from an opponent's [[PillarsOfSupport]], support tactical adaptation, and preserve organizational resilience.

[[Otpor]]'s humor and [[DilemmaActions]] in Serbia, mass noncooperation after lethal repression in Sudan, and falling public support after protester violence in Spain make the mechanisms concrete. The episode also qualifies its headline findings: nonviolence does not always work, the [[MovementParticipationThreshold|3.5 percent threshold]] is a descriptive rule of thumb rather than a guarantee, and Sudan shows that removing a ruler or winning a transition does not establish durable democracy.

## Key Claims

- In the campaigns Chenoweth and Stephan compared, nonviolent resistance succeeded about twice as often as armed resistance under a strict maximalist-campaign success definition.
- Broader and more diverse participation gives a movement more social ties into business, civil service, media, police, military, and political institutions whose cooperation sustains a ruler.
- [[PillarsOfSupport]] can be weakened through strikes, boycotts, stay-at-home actions, parallel institutions, and other forms of noncooperation rather than by persuading the ruler personally.
- [[DilemmaActions]] use humor, social norms, and asymmetric response costs to make repression, tolerance, or arrest politically awkward for authorities.
- Tactical variety, organizational resilience, and nonviolent discipline help campaigns adapt under repression and avoid shrinking the coalition they need to win.
- The episode reports a sharp loss of support after protester violence in Spain and argues that regimes may provoke violence because it legitimizes repression and strengthens security-force cohesion.
- Mobilizing about 3.5 percent of a population was associated with no failures in one set of 323 maximalist campaigns, but active participation is demanding and the threshold is not an iron law.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode's comparative claim extends the existing Memphis account by showing why discipline and coalition breadth matter across campaigns, while the Memphis case shows that organizers can bear reputational costs for disorder they do not fully control.
- Sudan's later turmoil qualifies the study's one-year success definition: achieving a stated near-term outcome is not equivalent to durable civilian democracy.
- Campaign coding, selection, success rates, the Spanish support change, casualty totals, participation estimates, and the 3.5 percent pattern remain episode-scoped because the supplied file does not include the underlying dataset, coding decisions, surveys, or later outcome audit.
- The episode does not establish that nonviolent resistance always succeeds, that armed campaigns never succeed, or that every polity offers equal room for mass organization and defections.
