## Summary
This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode extends the show's "Robots Ate My Taxes" reporting through [[MeganMcCartyCorino|Megan McCarty Carino]], [[DavidBrancaccio]], and legal scholar [[RyanAbbott]]. It argues that AI disruption is not only a labor-market issue: because the [[UnitedStates|U.S.]] tax system relies heavily on human labor income and payroll contributions, large-scale substitution of people with software could weaken public budgets.

The episode's distinctive contribution is [[AIPayrollTaxNeutrality]]: a business replacing a worker with an AI system can avoid the employer share of Social Security and Medicare payroll taxes, which the source gives as about 7.65% for many workers. Abbott proposes eliminating payroll taxes on human labor as one way to make the tax system neutral between work done by people and work done by machines, while Brancaccio lists sales taxes, value-added taxes, direct AI-usage taxes, and [[AIProfitTaxSubstitution|corporate-profit taxation]] as possible replacement bases.

## Key Claims
- The episode says federal, state, and some local budgets could take a serious hit if AI reduces wage income and therefore labor-tax revenue.
- Brancaccio says 66%, or by another calculation about three quarters, of federal revenue comes from taxing human labor.
- AI systems can receive a tax advantage over employees because employers pay Social Security and Medicare taxes on human workers but not on software doing comparable work.
- [[RyanAbbott]] argues for tax neutrality between human and machine work, including the "edgy" idea of eliminating payroll taxes on human labor.
- Possible replacement tax bases include a national sales tax, a value-added tax, a direct tax on AI usage, or stronger taxation of corporate profits.
- The optimistic scenario is conditional: if AI sharply raises productivity and the tax system adapts, public revenue could eventually rise after an initial disruptive period.
