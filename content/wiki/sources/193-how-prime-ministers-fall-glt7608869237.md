# 193. How Prime Ministers Fall

## Summary

Recorded just after [[BorisJohnson|Boris Johnson]] won the Conservative confidence vote of 6 June 2022 by 211 votes to 148, this [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode argues that formal survival can expose fatal political weakness. It develops [[PrimeMinisterialRegicide|prime ministerial regicide]] through cases ranging from [[RobertWalpole|Robert Walpole]] to [[TheresaMay|Theresa May]], showing how war failure, party division, rival colleagues, economic pressure, and exhausted authority can turn a narrow defeat or even a ballot victory into a delayed exit.

The survey distinguishes mechanisms rather than proposing one universal rule. Foreign-policy humiliation helped end Lord North and Neville Chamberlain; principle and party fracture brought down Robert Peel; David Lloyd George displaced H. H. Asquith through wartime coalition intrigue; and [[MargaretThatcher|Margaret Thatcher]] and May demonstrate that winning an internal vote does not necessarily restore authority. Harold Wilson supplies the counterexample: a weakened leader can survive when rivals cannot coordinate.

## Key Claims

- Johnson's 211-148 victory meant that 41% of Conservative MPs opposed him, making the result a warning about eroding authority rather than a renewed mandate.
- Prime ministers usually leave through election defeat or retirement, but internal party revolt, parliamentary loss, foreign-policy failure, assassination, illness, and exhaustion of political capital create distinct routes out of office.
- [[RobertWalpole|Walpole]] established an early pattern in which wartime failure and narrow Commons reverses revealed that a long premiership had become untenable.
- Lord North, H. H. Asquith, Neville Chamberlain, and Robert Peel show that national crisis becomes personally fatal when party or coalition support no longer absorbs blame and disagreement.
- [[MargaretThatcher|Thatcher]] and [[TheresaMay|May]] show that a leader can win a confidence ballot yet emerge weaker because the scale of dissent becomes public and follow-on events continue draining authority.
- Harold Wilson's survival amid plots suggests that weakness does not guarantee removal when plausible successors divide one another.
- The episode predicts that worsening by-election and economic conditions could make Johnson a convenient party scapegoat, but this was a contemporary forecast rather than knowledge of his July 2022 resignation.

## Key Quotes

> "regicide" - the episode's metaphor for political removal from within rather than ordinary electoral defeat or retirement.

> "Brexit means Brexit" - the slogan associated with Theresa May's initially strong post-accession authority.
