# 18. The North South Divide

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode with [[DanJackson]] interprets the [[EnglishNorthSouthDivide|English North-South divide]] through physical geography, medieval boundaries, industrialization, language, class, sport, politics, and local rivalry. It proposes the Mersey-Humber line as a useful historical boundary while stressing that the Midlands, Wales, east-west divisions, and differences between northern cities and towns prevent a simple binary map.

The episode gives [[London]] unusual explanatory weight. Roman foundations, the Thames, access to continental Europe, accumulated political and cultural power, and the defeat or absorption of regional challenges underpin [[LondonMetropolitanDominance|London's durable metropolitan dominance]], while coal and manufacturing created northern industrial power without permanently displacing the capital.

## Key Claims

- The Mersey-Humber line aligns physical obstacles with several older ecclesiastical, political, and administrative divisions, making geography a starting condition rather than a complete cause.
- London has enjoyed durable structural advantages from its Thames crossing, estuary, proximity to Europe's prosperous core, and centuries of accumulated political and cultural centrality.
- Northern industrial strength between roughly 1700 and 1914 rested on coal, geology, manufacturing, and urban concentration but did not overturn London's national dominance.
- [[EnglishRegionalIdentityFormation|English regional identities]] are repeatedly produced through remoteness, work, transport, religion, class, accent, media, sport, and relations with the center.
- Received pronunciation, boarding schools, and the BBC helped associate elite southern speech with national authority, while northern accents retained both stigma and claims to authenticity.
- Wales and northern England shared important industrial and Labour political affinities, but Brexit exposed a divide between many Remain-voting northern cities and more disaffected smaller towns.
- Distinct identities around Newcastle, Sunderland, and Teesside show that rivers, railways, watersheds, accents, and neighboring rivalries can matter at a much smaller scale than North versus South.
