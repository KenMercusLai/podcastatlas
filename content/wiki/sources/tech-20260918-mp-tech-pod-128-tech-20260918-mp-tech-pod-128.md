# Trump rejects AI's "effective altruism" movement

## Summary
This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode links three technology-trust disputes: political conflict over slowing advanced AI, bipartisan efforts to limit [[FlockSafety|Flock Safety]] and other license-plate readers, and [[Apple]]'s optional system for authenticating photos captured on forthcoming iPhones. Its strongest synthesis is that trust depends on more than company assurances: AI governance needs public legitimacy, surveillance needs enforceable access and security controls, and photo provenance needs a protected capture-time reference while preserving the distinction between authenticated and merely unauthenticated images.

The AI segment shows safety concern cutting across conventional party lines while industry positions remain divided by competition and open-model interests. The surveillance segment adds alleged police misuse, an exposed encryption key, supply-chain ambiguity, and a proposed federal funding lever; the photo segment adds [[DeviceOriginPhotoAuthentication]] as a narrower response to synthetic-media uncertainty.

## Key Claims
- [[DarioAmodei|Dario Amodei]] called for slowing AI development, with [[SamAltman|Sam Altman]] and [[ElonMusk|Elon Musk]] described as agreeing, while [[DonaldTrump|Donald Trump]] rejected rogue-AI fears and attacked AI "doomers" and [[EffectiveAltruism|effective altruism]].
- The episode presents AI governance as a substantive dispute over catastrophic risk, competition, open development, and who gets to make decisions, not as a stable left-right split.
- Rules favored by large frontier labs could entrench incumbents, while compliance burdens may fall more heavily on smaller developers and [[OpenSourceAIModels|open-source AI]].
- A bipartisan bill would use federal transportation funding to pressure states to limit automatic license-plate readers to specified purposes such as felony investigations and missing-person searches.
- Reports of officers allegedly tracking former partners, unclear access and retention practices, and an unencrypted device key strengthen the case that surveillance controls must cover misuse and technical compromise.
- Questions about where Flock devices are assembled and whether Chinese-made components create security risk remain unresolved in the episode.
- [[Apple]] says forthcoming iPhone models can cryptographically sign sensor data and retain a protected reference image, allowing later verification that a photo was captured on an iPhone without proving that every unauthenticated image is fake.
- The photo-authentication feature is optional, consumes extra storage, is designed to avoid disclosing identity or exact location, and is initially unavailable in the [[EuropeanUnion|European Union]] and [[China]].

## Contradictions
- The episode sharpens tension with [[FlockSafety|Flock Safety]]'s earlier company-side governance account: mandatory auditing and shorter retention may mitigate misuse, but the new source reports alleged officer abuse and a device-level key-management weakness. The accounts are retained as source-scoped evidence rather than collapsed into a settled verdict.
- The AI segment records opposed interpretations of safety advocacy: it can express genuine catastrophic-risk concern while also creating incumbent-friendly compliance barriers. The episode does not establish company motives or a specific regulatory design.
- Apple's system verifies a positive capture claim for participating devices; failure to authenticate is not evidence that an image is synthetic. Model availability, independent technical testing, exact supported devices, and regional rollout remain source-scoped.
