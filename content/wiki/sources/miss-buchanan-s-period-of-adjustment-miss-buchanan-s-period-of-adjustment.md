# Miss Buchanan’s Period of Adjustment

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode reexamines [[BrownVBoardOfEducation|Brown v. Board of Education]] through the experiences of [[LeolaBrown]], [[LindaBrown]], and dismissed teacher [[DarlaBuchanan]]. It argues that the Brown family valued Monroe School and challenged a racially imposed denial of choice, while the Supreme Court's emphasis on psychological injury gave less attention to institutional power and the strengths of Black educators.

The implementation history connects [[TeacherRepresentationStudentOpportunity]] to [[DesegregationEducatorDisplacement]]. Teachers exercise authority through recognition, recommendation, discipline, and care, yet desegregation often moved Black pupils into white schools while closing Black schools and dismissing Black teachers. The episode therefore distinguishes the legal defeat of segregation from the unequal distribution of integration's authority and costs.

## Key Claims

- The Brown family opposed the school board's power to deny nearby enrollment solely by race; [[LeolaBrown]] did not present Monroe School or its Black teachers as educationally inferior.
- The Court's psychological-harm rationale condemned segregation but obscured an alternative account centered on power, connection, opportunity, and public control.
- Episode-reported research associates Black teachers with higher gifted-program identification for high-achieving Black pupils and with test-score, disciplinary, suspension, and dropout differences.
- Teachers are institutional gatekeepers because discretionary attention, referrals, discipline, and support can shape a student's educational path.
- [[CelestinePorter]] argued that teachers and administrators should have been integrated alongside or before pupils so that institutional authority, rather than only student placement, changed.
- Moberly's dismissal of eleven Black teachers and Topeka's termination of [[DarlaBuchanan]] show how subjective criteria and anticipated white parental resistance preserved exclusion after formal desegregation.
- The episode reports that roughly half of about 82,000 Black teachers in the South were dismissed within a decade of Brown, producing a loss of professional authority whose representational effects persisted.

## Key Quotes

> “period of adjustment” — the superintendent's euphemism for Buchanan's dismissal and for the unequal human cost concealed by administrative reassurance.

> “best for everyone concerned” — closing assurance whose universal language contrasts with the episode's account of who actually bore the loss.

## Connections

- [[BrownVBoardOfEducation]], [[LindaBrown]], and [[LeolaBrown]] - case and family perspectives separating equal choice from a deficit account of Black education.
- [[DarlaBuchanan]] and [[CelestinePorter]] - dismissed teacher and educator testimony centering professional authority, care, and displacement.
- [[TeacherRepresentationStudentOpportunity]] - mechanism connecting teacher identity and discretion to recognition, discipline, and access.
- [[DesegregationEducatorDisplacement]] - implementation pattern through which Black schools closed and Black professionals lost positions.
- [[FormalEqualityStructuralDiscrimination]] - framework for understanding why formally integrated enrollment could coexist with unequal institutional power.
- [[MalcolmGladwell]] and [[RevisionistHistory]] - host and show reframing a familiar civil-rights victory through neglected implementation costs.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. The episode affirms the necessity of ending state-enforced segregation while challenging the claim that reassignment of pupils was sufficient integration.
- The Brown family's praise for Monroe qualifies any inference that their lawsuit depended on the inherent inferiority of Black schools.
