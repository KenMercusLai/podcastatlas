# 375. Hitler and the Mitford Sisters

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[UnityMitford|Unity Mitford]] from an antisemitic aristocratic family culture and rivalry with [[DianaMitford|Diana Mitford]] through [[BritishFascism|British fascism]], Munich, friendship with [[JuliusStreicher|Julius Streicher]], and extraordinary personal access to [[AdolfHitler]]. Its central judgment is that performance, thrill-seeking, status, and infatuation do not excuse explicit political commitment: Unity publicly promoted antisemitism, brokered fascist connections, benefited from Jewish dispossession, and remained in Germany until Britain declared war.

The episode also examines [[FascistGlamourAndMemory|fascist glamour and memory]]. Nancy Mitford's novels, the sisters' celebrity, and later sensational rumors helped recast a history of fascism as aristocratic eccentricity, while [[OswaldMosley|Oswald Mosley]] and Diana's postwar careers show that British fascism remained electorally marginal without disappearing.

## Key Claims

- Unity's fascist trajectory combined inherited antisemitism, sibling rivalry, attention-seeking, admiration for powerful men, and a search for a cause; the episode rejects any attempt to make those motives exculpatory.
- After joining Mosley's movement and selling Blackshirt material, Unity moved to Munich, pursued Hitler for months, and reportedly met him more than 140 times after gaining access.
- Unity cultivated Streicher and publicly used antisemitic language in *Der Stürmer*, increasing both her notoriety and her value within Hitler's social orbit.
- Hitler appears to have treated Unity less as a mistress than as a younger-sister figure, jester, symbolic talisman, and informal Anglo-German social connection.
- Her fantasy of a British-German alliance linked personal access to a wider interwar elite fascination with Germany, but it produced no durable diplomatic bridge.
- A Munich flat taken from a Jewish couple made Unity a direct beneficiary of persecution rather than merely a distant admirer of the regime.
- The shooting on 3 September 1939, survival, return to Britain, later illness, and implausible Hitler-child rumor show how sensational biography can displace political responsibility in public memory.
- Mosley's postwar Union Movement and anti-immigration politics persisted, but the episode presents British fascism as electorally marginal within a resilient parliamentary order.

## Key Quotes

> “The Frivolity of Evil” - the subtitle used for David Pryce-Jones's biography and the episode's central moral problem.

> “no one she would rather meet” - Unity's reported response after seeing Hitler in 1933.
