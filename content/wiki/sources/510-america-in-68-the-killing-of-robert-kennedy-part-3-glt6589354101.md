# 510. America in '68: The Killing of Robert Kennedy (Part 3)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[RobertFKennedy|Robert F. Kennedy]] from grief after his brother's murder through his Senate career, anti-poverty advocacy, changing Vietnam position, and late entry into the 1968 Democratic nomination contest. It presents his campaign as an unstable combination of moral rhetoric, celebrity, support among Black, Hispanic, and poorer voters, ideological ambiguity, and an attempt to convert primary victories into pressure on a party apparatus still inclined toward [[HubertHumphrey|Hubert Humphrey]].

The episode ends with Kennedy's California primary victory and assassination by [[SirhanSirhan|Sirhan Sirhan]], then separates the tragedy and durable martyr image from the counterfactual claim that Kennedy was destined to become president. Its central qualification is that the primary calendar did not determine most delegates, Humphrey remained ahead, and a general election against [[RichardNixon|Richard Nixon]] would have occurred in a strong law-and-order environment.

## Key Claims

- Kennedy's post-1963 public identity joined intense grief, classical and existential reading, moral seriousness, celebrity, family mythology, and direct encounters with poverty and racial exclusion.
- His political profile remained ambiguous: advisers spanned Democratic factions, his Vietnam position moved away from hawkish Cold War assumptions, and hostility toward [[LyndonBJohnson|Lyndon B. Johnson]] complicated judgments of motive.
- [[EugeneMcCarthy|Eugene McCarthy]] took the initial political risk against Johnson; Kennedy's later entry therefore looked opportunistic to McCarthy supporters even after Johnson withdrew.
- Kennedy's primary strategy depended on wins, crowds, publicity, and emotional pressure compensating for Humphrey's strength among unpledged delegates and party insiders.
- Indiana revealed Kennedy's strength among Black voters and weakness among many white voters, while Oregon exposed the appeal of McCarthy's more cerebral anti-war campaign to educated white liberals.
- Kennedy narrowly won California after an exhausting, security-conscious campaign, but victory did not erase Humphrey's delegate advantage.
- Sirhan shot Kennedy at the Ambassador Hotel after the victory speech; the episode accepts Sirhan's stated hostility to Kennedy's support for Israel while rejecting CIA-brainwashing conspiracy claims.
- Kennedy's assassination created a powerful liberal martyr and “once and future king” memory, but the episode treats nomination and presidential victory as uncertain counterfactuals rather than lost certainties.

## Key Quotes

> "On to Chicago." - Kennedy's final line after declaring victory in the California primary.

> "The once and future king." - graffiti near the assassination scene, cited as an early expression of Kennedy's posthumous political myth.

> "Oh no, don't lift me." - words the episode identifies as Kennedy's last.

## Contradictions

- The episode directly challenges the popular claim that California victory put Kennedy on a clear path to the Democratic nomination or presidency; it treats Humphrey's delegate lead, party-insider support, white-voter weakness, and the national demand for order as substantial obstacles.
- Kennedy's anti-war and anti-poverty positions are presented as meaningful, but the source resists turning them into a fully coherent ideology or separating them cleanly from rivalry with Johnson and campaign strategy.
- Sirhan's responsibility and stated political motive are accepted, while CIA brainwashing and related conspiracy explanations are rejected by the hosts; detailed forensic disputes are not independently adjudicated here.
- Vote shares, delegate projections, private motives, death threats, personal-life judgments, quoted private remarks, and hypothetical 1968 or 1972 outcomes remain source-scoped.
