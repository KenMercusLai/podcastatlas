## Summary
This [[LuoyonghaosCrossroads|罗永浩的十字路口]] interview has [[LuoYonghao|罗永浩]] and [[YangLiStandup|杨笠]] discuss a completed stand-up tour, stage anxiety, writing and performance craft, rural family life, career formation, public controversy, online abuse, grief, intimacy, and long-term creative plans. Its durable synthesis joins [[StandupComedyIteration]] and [[PerformerAudienceStateCoupling]] to [[ComedyPublicReceptionBoundary]]: a comedian needs live audience response to know what a routine does, but neither applause, outrage, supporters, nor opponents fully determine the work or the person. Yang's account also makes creative continuity concrete—public conflict changes caution and format choices, while grief and a stable internal standard help clarify that creation remains her intended lifelong work.

## Key Claims
- [[YangLiStandup|杨笠]] describes a long tour as changing from repetition fatigue into performance craft: once the text became familiar, she could attend more closely to pacing, pauses, control, and the room.
- [[StandupComedyIteration]] is inseparable from live response because a cold room can turn delivery faster and make failure feel like direct rejection, while the same material may land differently with another audience or performer state.
- Stage ease is not the absence of fear; Yang and [[LuoYonghao|罗永浩]] both describe anticipatory anxiety, while preparation and a barely used teleprompter function as support for flexibility rather than proof of weak craft.
- [[ComedyPublicReceptionBoundary]] separates comic intent, actual audience effects, and public projection: Yang says controversy is not simply a misunderstanding, because different groups can genuinely experience the same public joke differently.
- Serving a comic standard can require naming an obvious tension in the room, but later amplification changes the ethical calculation; Yang says past controversy made her more attentive to avoidable harm, younger audiences, and people she directly jokes about.
- Public visibility brings privacy loss, harassment, family exposure, and distorted intimacy with fans; Yang therefore prefers to understand fans as audiences whose attachment may change rather than as people who know her private self.
- Her career path—from low-paid theater work and unstable design work into clubs, writing, television, and touring—supports a source-scoped account of confidence emerging from practice and professional recognition rather than childhood certainty.
- Her father's illness and death coincided with fame and controversy, making public backlash a work difficulty rather than the defining event of that period; his early death strengthened her commitment to act on creative plans while time is available.
