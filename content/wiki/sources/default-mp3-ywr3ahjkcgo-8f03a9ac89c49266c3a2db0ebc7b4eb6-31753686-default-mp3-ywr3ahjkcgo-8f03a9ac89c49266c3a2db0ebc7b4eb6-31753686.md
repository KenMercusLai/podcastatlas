## Summary

This [[PlanetMoney]] update uses historical polling failures and a 2022 field experiment with the [[MaristPoll|Marist Poll]] to explain why sample composition matters more than sample size and why falling response rates threaten representative election surveys. It compares random telephone sampling, text outreach, demographic weighting, and [[CrowdWisdomPolling|crowd-wisdom questions]], finding that Marist's conventional 2022 national House estimate closely matched the reported result while the experimental questions substantially overstated the Republican lead.

## Key Claims

- Election polling depends on representative selection, but unfamiliar-call avoidance, unequal response rates, and manual cell-phone dialing make random telephone samples increasingly costly and incomplete.
- A large biased sample can perform worse than a smaller representative sample; interviewer-filled demographic quotas can also reintroduce selection bias.
- Statistical weighting can correct known demographic imbalances only when pollsters identify characteristics that distinguish respondents from nonrespondents.
- Asking respondents who will win or how their social circles will vote did not improve Marist's 2022 national House estimate, although the questions may still illuminate expectations, turnout, or bandwagon and underdog effects.
- Public polling supplies information beyond campaign-controlled claims and therefore matters as civic measurement, not only as election forecasting.

## Key Quotes

> "The composition of a sample matters more than its sheer size." — episode summary of the 1936 polling failure

> "Let’s try a few and see how it goes." — interviewer training language intended to reduce survey refusal
