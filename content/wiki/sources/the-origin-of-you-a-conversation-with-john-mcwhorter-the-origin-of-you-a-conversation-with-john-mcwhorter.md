# The Origin of “You”: A Conversation with John McWhorter

## Summary

In this [[RevisionistHistory]] conversation, [[MalcolmGladwell]] and linguist [[JohnMcWhorter]] connect historical recordings, family speech, public voice, pronouns, word meanings, and email punctuation to the social history carried by everyday language. McWhorter treats Black English as a rule-governed variety rather than failed standard English, while his own inability to shift into it illustrates how speech repertoire and perceived identity do not always align.

The episode's strongest synthesis is that language repairs its own gaps while social meaning constrains which repairs become standard. English speakers compensate for singular/plural “you” through “y'all,” “yous,” “yins,” and “you guys”; speakers also repurpose exclamation points and periods to manage friendliness and distance. These changes improve local coordination without making any one form socially neutral.

## Key Claims

- [[BlackEnglishSystematicity]] treats constructions such as repeated narrative “had” as patterned grammar rather than error.
- [[RegisterSwitchingSocialIdentity]] includes pronunciation, vocabulary, volume, manner, and relationship stance, not merely substituting words between codes.
- Public expectations once strongly rewarded a prestigious standard dialect, but the episode argues that this constraint weakened after the 1960s.
- [[SecondPersonNumberRepair]] describes how English speakers build plural alternatives after “you” absorbed the earlier singular/plural distinction between “thou” and “you.”
- “Y'all” is presented as fundamentally plural even when addressed to one visible listener because it may include companions or soften direct address.
- Writing slows some forms of language change, but recorded speech makes changes in accent and colloquial grammar historically audible.
- [[DigitalPunctuationTone]] shows punctuation acquiring new interpersonal functions: an exclamation point can mark ordinary warmth, while a bare period can appear cold in informal digital writing.
- Borrowing from Black American English through popular culture is framed as linguistic diffusion, although the episode does not systematically test its social distribution or contested appropriation boundaries.
