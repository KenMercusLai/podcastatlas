# The internet’s next big domain expansion is underway

## Summary
This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode has [[PeteMillspaugh|Pete Millspaugh]] explain [[ICANN]]'s first application round for new generic top-level domains since 2012. The discussion connects costly registry applications and auctions to [[TopLevelDomainRegistryEconomics]], country-code windfalls such as Anguilla's `.ai`, proprietary brand extensions, community projects such as [[DotMeowFoundation|dotMeow]], and competition among [[Google]], [[OpenAI]], and [[Meta]] for `.agent`.

The episode treats the text to the right of a web address's final dot as infrastructure, a commercial asset, a governance choice, and a medium for identity or wordplay. It also keeps the payoff uncertain: `.com` retains credibility and scarcity value, while the expensive `.web` extension remains unavailable after years of legal dispute.

## Key Claims
- The source says ICANN received more than 1,000 applications in its first new generic top-level-domain round since 2012.
- Applying costs roughly $250,000 before operating expense or auction cost, so financial and technical capacity strongly shape participation.
- A registry can sell second-level registrations, keep an extension private, organize a community namespace, or use it defensively to control a brand.
- Anguilla's `.ai`, Montenegro's `.me`, Tuvalu's `.tv`, and Colombia's `.co` show how semantic demand can turn a country-code domain into an export-like revenue asset.
- Google, OpenAI, and Meta are among the applicants for `.agent`; the episode speculates that future AI agents may need distinct online identities.
- [[DotMeowFoundation|dotMeow Foundation]] proposes `.meow` as queer community infrastructure whose registration proceeds would support queer projects and businesses.
- Alternative extensions can increase linguistic and branding flexibility, but `.com` still signals credibility and benefits from scarcity.
- Auction prices do not guarantee usable or profitable registries: the source contrasts Google's $25 million `.app` purchase with the still-unreleased, legally disputed $135 million `.web` result.
