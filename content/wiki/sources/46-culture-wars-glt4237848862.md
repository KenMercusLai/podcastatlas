# 46. Culture Wars

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode asks whether culture wars are perennial conflicts over identity and tradition or a more specific modern form of moral-political struggle. [[DominicSandbrook]] emphasizes recurring disputes from late Rome and the Reformation to Whigs and Tories, patriotism, universities, sexuality, and football; [[TomHolland]] argues more narrowly that modern culture war is [[CultureWarHistoricalGenealogy|sublimated theology]] shaped by Christian ideas of progress, moral purification, universalism, and inherited sin.

The productive synthesis is neither timeless sameness nor exclusive Christian causation. Older conflicts supply recurring mechanisms—symbolic objects, generational replacement, institutional power, local belonging, and moral legitimacy—while the modern Western form gains distinctive force from secularized claims about progress and universal justice. The Altar of Victory, Reformation iconoclasm, civil rights, gay rights, statues, patriotism, and the European Super League therefore illuminate one another without becoming identical cases.

## Key Claims

- A broad definition makes culture war a recurring struggle over identity, inherited values, public symbols, and the story a society tells about itself; a narrow definition ties its modern Western form to Bismarck's Kulturkampf and post-Christian theological conflict.
- The Altar of Victory dispute provides a proto-culture-war case: [[Symmachus]] defended toleration of inherited civic religion, while [[AmbroseOfMilan|Ambrose]] treated sacrifice as an evil with which Christians could not compromise.
- Modern progressive arguments may retain Christian moral assumptions about victims, slavery, conquest, sin, abolition, and universal justice even when their advocates reject Christian institutions or identity.
- Political culture wars become durable when parties, universities, law, state power, media, sport, and generational socialization carry rival moral frameworks beyond a single controversy.
- Symbols such as statues, flags, football teams, and local clubs matter because they make abstract disputes over belonging and legitimacy emotionally concrete.
- Institutional capture can accelerate change, but it does not guarantee victory: the English Reformation depended on state power and generational succession, changing attitudes toward homosexuality moved through culture and education, and communism's institutional control could not overcome practical failure.
- Parliamentary and party competition can contain conflict by allowing rival traditions to coexist and continue arguing without requiring total moral victory.

## Key Quotes

## Contradictions

- The episode preserves rather than resolves a definitional disagreement: Sandbrook's broad continuity thesis includes many premodern conflicts, while Holland argues that calling every clash over culture a culture war empties the category and obscures its Christian-modern genealogy.
- The Christian-inheritance claim is interpretive rather than exclusive. It does not establish that progress, moral universalism, civil rights, abolitionism, or concern for victims have only Christian sources.
- The episode is heavily British, American, Roman, and Christian in scope; it cannot establish a universal history of cultural conflict, and its claims about universities, voters, generations, communism, and how attitudes change remain conversational or source-scoped.
