# 16.时尚受害者：惊！绿色或为最毒颜色

## Summary
This [[MihuanChishu|蜜獾吃书]] episode uses [[FashionVictimsBook|《时尚受害者》]] to reread fashion history as a history of bodies exposed to infection, poison, fire, machinery, labor exploitation, and ecological damage. Its strongest contribution is [[ToxicFashionMaterialRisk]]: beautiful clothing, cosmetics, dyes, hats, shoes, accessories, artificial fibers, and dry-cleaning practices can hide mercury, arsenic, lead, aniline dyes, radioactive metal, solvents, and other hazards. The discussion also turns fashion into [[MaterialHistoryNarrative]] by connecting consumer desire to [[FashionSupplyChainExternality]], [[GenderedFashionSafetyBlame]], [[FemaleBodyFreedom]], and [[EnvironmentalTradeoffAccounting]].

## Key Claims
- Fashion is not only taste or status; the source treats clothing, cosmetics, accessories, and cleaning practices as material systems that can touch skin, lungs, workplaces, animals, and waste streams.
- The episode's opening claim that "clothes can kill" runs from mythic poisoned garments into historical cases of contaminated textiles, secondhand clothes, body lice, smallpox, cholera, typhus, trench fever, and wartime delousing.
- Industrial chemistry made fashion brighter and cheaper but moved new poisons into ordinary goods: mercury in hatmaking, arsenic in green dyes, lead in whitening cosmetics, aniline and nitrobenzene in dyes and shoe polish, and cobalt-60 in metal accessories.
- The episode repeatedly shifts attention from the wearer to the worker: hatters, flower makers, poor seamstresses, laundresses, ballet dancers, shoe-shine children, factory workers, and overseas laborers can absorb the cost before consumers see the object.
- "Green" becomes the episode's memorable color case because bright historical greens could depend on arsenic compounds even while consumers read the color as fresh or harmless.
- The fire section shows that dangerous fashion is not only chemical. Starched gauze, lace, cotton, crinolines, children's dresses, and ballet costumes could make ordinary proximity to flame deadly.
- The discussion rejects blaming women alone for dangerous fashion. Men designed, sold, watched, legislated, and profited from the same garments and spectacles that newspapers often framed as female vanity.
- Clothing can constrain movement even when it is not toxic: floor-length skirts, hobble skirts, scarves, long loose garments, high heels, and long hair around machines or vehicles make body freedom a safety question.
- Modern substitutions can solve one problem while creating another. Celluloid reduced some ivory and tortoiseshell demand but was highly flammable; artificial silk and dry cleaning improved convenience while adding chemical exposure questions.
- The final consumer advice is risk-aware rather than panic-driven: dose and exposure matter, but new clothes, dry cleaning, shoe glues, plastics, dyes, and unnecessary fashion turnover should not be treated as harmless by default.

## Key Quotes
> "衣服是可以杀人的" - the episode's opening frame for linking myth, fashion, and bodily danger.

> "时尚的姐妹是死神" - the source's memorable formulation of fashion's hidden deathliness.

> "抛开剂量谈毒性都是耍流氓" - the episode's warning against turning toxicology into pure panic.
