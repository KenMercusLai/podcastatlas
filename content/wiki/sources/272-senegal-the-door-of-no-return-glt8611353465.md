# 272: Senegal: The Door of No Return

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode uses [[Goree|Goree Island]] and its [[HouseOfSlavesGoree|House of Slaves]] to examine the tension between documentary history and commemorative meaning. It says the building was not the major embarkation depot promoted by its museum narrative and that Gorée was statistically minor within the Atlantic slave trade, while explaining how [[BoubacarJosephNdiaye|Boubacar Joseph Ndiaye]], UNESCO recognition, tourism, and African American pilgrimage made its “Door of No Return” a globally powerful symbol.

The episode's synthesis is [[ContestedHeritagePilgrimage]]: factual scrutiny need not deny grief or symbolic power, but emotional importance cannot convert unsupported numbers or functions into established history. [[MemorialAudienceFraming]] adds a second qualification: a site shaped around visitors from the United States can foreground North American ancestry while obscuring the larger Brazilian and Caribbean branches of Atlantic slavery.

## Key Claims

- Gorée's offshore position, reduced disease exposure for Europeans, changing Portuguese, Dutch, English, and French control, and Franco-African trading families made it a commercial interface before its later preservation as a heritage site.
- Signares could act as agents, traders, and founders of wealthy Creole dynasties; the episode says a signare's son built the House of Slaves in 1776.
- [[BoubacarJosephNdiaye|Ndiaye]] promoted claims that one million captives passed through the door and 20 million left Gorée, helping make the museum an international pilgrimage site after Senegalese independence and UNESCO listing.
- The episode says documentary and logistical evidence does not support those claims: most captives exported from the region left river-mouth depots, the building was not an export warehouse, and rocks made the doorway an impractical embarkation point.
- [[PhilipDCurtin|Philip D. Curtin]] publicly attacked the museum narrative, and the episode presents the underlying historical correction as broadly accepted while acknowledging controversy over scholarly authority, race, and emotional ownership.
- A disputed site can still support mourning, ancestry, and public memory, but historians must remain free to test its numbers, material setting, and narrated function.
- The site's African American visitor frame risks shrinking a trade in which Brazil and the Caribbean were larger destinations than North America.

## Key Quotes

> “a human need for a sacred place” - an African American teacher quoted by the episode to explain why Gorée can matter independently of the literal embarkation claim.

> “myth is not a dirty word” - the hosts' formulation for studying a powerful historical story without treating it as documentary fact.
