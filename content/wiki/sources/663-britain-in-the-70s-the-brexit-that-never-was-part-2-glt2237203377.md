# 663. Britain in the 70s: The Brexit That Never Was (Part 2)

## Summary
This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode uses *Fawlty Towers* and the exhausted mood of 1975 to frame [[HaroldWilson|Harold Wilson]]'s fragile Labour government, near-25% inflation, union pay pressure, public borrowing, and Britain's first national referendum. Its central claim is that the [[BritishECReferendum1975|1975 European Community referendum]] was less a mass conversion to Europe than Wilson's tactical answer to a split [[LabourPartyUK|Labour Party]] during a frightening economic crisis.

The episode also deepens the wiki's Brexit prehistory. [[EdwardHeath|Ted Heath]] had taken Britain into the European Economic Community in 1973, but cultural distance, sovereignty anxiety, Commonwealth memory, and Labour-left anti-market arguments remained powerful. The 1975 Yes victory kept Britain inside the Community, yet the hosts stress that inflation, sterling weakness, and state-capacity doubts were still waiting immediately afterward.

## Key Claims
- [[HaroldWilson|Wilson]] returned to power with a tiny majority and used renegotiation plus a referendum to contain Labour's split over Europe.
- Britain's economic crisis was already severe in 1975: inflation neared 25%, public borrowing overshot, union wage settlements escalated, and ministers lacked confidence in available remedies.
- British European Community membership was culturally and constitutionally uneasy from the start, shaped by empire, Commonwealth ties, war memory, Protestant suspicion, sovereignty concerns, and dislike of continental habits.
- The 1975 campaign alignments differed sharply from 2016: [[MargaretThatcher|Margaret Thatcher]], Heath, most Conservatives, business, and most newspapers backed Yes, while the No side joined Labour leftists, communists, anarchists, right-wing Conservatives, and nationalist fringes.
- [[TonyBenn]] made the strongest democratic-sovereignty case against membership, arguing that European authority could block socialism and remove power from voters.
- Yes won decisively because remaining had become the safer status quo during economic fear, not because voters had become enthusiastic Europeans.
- The referendum settled Labour's immediate Europe problem but left the larger inflation, sterling, and public-finance crisis unresolved.
