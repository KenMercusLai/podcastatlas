# 171. The Falklands War: Battle for the Islands (Part 3)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows the [[FalklandsWar|Falklands War]] from Britain's recovery of South Georgia through the sinking of the [[GeneralBelgrano|General Belgrano]], the San Carlos landing, Goose Green, Bluff Cove, the hill battles outside Stanley, and Argentine surrender. It argues that the Belgrano attack was a legitimate and militarily consequential act against a naval threat, while keeping its 323 deaths and later controversy distinct from the legal and operational judgment.

The episode also treats media and diplomacy as part of the campaign. [[TheSunUK|The Sun]]'s triumphalism and attacks on the [[BBC]] sharpened [[WartimeMediaLoyaltyPressure|wartime loyalty pressure]], while [[MargaretThatcher|Margaret Thatcher]] accepted a Peru-backed peace proposal and authorized a final UN offer before approving the landings. The ground-war account emphasizes the contrast between British professional forces and cold, poorly supported Argentine conscripts without presenting combat as clean or bloodless.

## Key Claims

- Britain's Vulcan raids and naval planning were intended to reduce threats to the task force, even when the airfield was not fully disabled.
- The declared 200-mile exclusion zone was a warning boundary rather than a promise that threatening Argentine forces outside it could not be attacked.
- The General Belgrano and accompanying ships formed part of a potential attack on the British carriers; its sinking killed 323 people and caused Argentina's surface fleet plan to be abandoned.
- The hosts reject the claim that Thatcher ordered the sinking to destroy a Peruvian peace initiative, citing the Navy's request, the timing of ministerial knowledge, later British acceptance of a Peru-backed proposal, and Argentine naval judgments that the attack was legitimate.
- Tabloid celebration, especially The Sun's “Gotcha” treatment, intensified moral division, while criticism of detached BBC language turned journalistic verification into a test of national loyalty.
- Argentina rejected a mutual-withdrawal proposal and a later British offer involving UN administration before Britain authorized the San Carlos landing.
- The San Carlos landing achieved surprise, but Argentine air attacks inflicted serious ship losses; Goose Green, Bluff Cove, and the final hill battles then imposed further casualties.
- The account contrasts well-trained British forces with Argentine conscripts suffering cold, hunger, poor treatment, and collapsing morale, while acknowledging brutal battlefield behavior and disputed atrocity accounts.
- British forces took the heights around Stanley by 14 June, and Argentine command resistance collapsed before the formal surrender.

## Key Quotes

> "rejoice" - Thatcher's response after the recapture of South Georgia, used to frame her growing identification with the campaign.

> "Gotcha" - The Sun headline that became a lasting symbol of tabloid triumphalism after the Belgrano attack.

## Contradictions

- The source rejects the settled claim that the Belgrano sinking was a war crime or a deliberate effort to sabotage peace, but the ship's course, location outside the exclusion zone, casualty scale, and incomplete diplomacy explain why the act remained politically contested.
- The hosts' military-legitimacy judgment, reported Argentine naval views, casualty totals, peace-proposal chronology, commanders' motives, memoir accounts, and atrocity assessments remain source-scoped rather than independently adjudicated here.
