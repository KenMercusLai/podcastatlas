## Summary
This [[AllIn]] interview with [[BlakeScholl]] presents [[BoomSupersonic]] as a linked aviation, engine-manufacturing, regulatory, and data-center-power program. Scholl uses [[XB1SupersonicDemonstrator|XB-1]] as proof that a small private team can build and fly a supersonic jet, describes [[BoomlessCruise]] as an atmospheric-refraction technique for overland flight, and argues that the [[RollsRoyce|Rolls-Royce]] separation pushed Boom into [[CrisisForcedVerticalIntegration|engine vertical integration]]. The newest strategic claim is that adapting the same engine core for ground power could create a [[GroundPowerEngineValidationFlywheel|revenue and reliability-data flywheel]] before passengers fly behind it.

## Key Claims
- Scholl says commercial aviation stopped improving speed even as demand for shorter trips remained, making [[OvertureSupersonicAirliner|Overture]] an attempt to restore progress rather than merely revive Concorde.
- [[XB1SupersonicDemonstrator|XB-1]] became the first privately developed jet to break the sound barrier in 2025, according to the episode, and its roughly 50-person build team is presented as evidence that modern tools can compress aerospace development.
- [[BoomlessCruise]] does not eliminate the aircraft's sonic boom; it uses atmospheric conditions and a flight profile intended to refract the boom upward before it reaches the ground, with a claimed practical ceiling near Mach 1.3.
- Scholl says a June executive order ended the categorical U.S. overland restriction and that related legislation had advanced with unanimous votes, but the transcript also says full Senate action was still pending.
- After the [[RollsRoyce|Rolls-Royce]] relationship ended, Boom chose to design and manufacture its own engine and turbine components rather than depend on another established supplier.
- Boom proposes adapting the engine core into a water-free, trailerized, 42-megawatt behind-the-meter generator for data centers, with claimed demand in the tens of gigawatts but no confirmed customer identified in the episode.
- The [[GroundPowerEngineValidationFlywheel|ground-power strategy]] could fund aviation work and accumulate high-duty-cycle engine data, although it also adds manufacturing, market, fuel, emissions, certification, and execution risk.
- Scholl gives a roughly $3,500 break-even round-trip transatlantic fare and an aspirational passenger-service target around 2030, while explicitly declining to promise a date.

## Key Quotes
> "success is not guaranteed" — Scholl's qualification after describing the engine, power, and passenger-aircraft plan.

> "safe, comfortable, and affordable" — the three conditions Scholl says faster passenger flight must satisfy.

## Contradictions
- The source advances the earlier [[tsr-s5-blakescholl-v3-finalaudio-tsr-s5-blakescholl-v3-finalaudio]] account from planned proof to claimed XB-1 supersonic flight, engine-core assembly, turbine-factory output, and a more specific ground-power strategy; it does not overturn the earlier company-building narrative.
- Regulatory statements are time-sensitive and internally qualified: Scholl describes the categorical ban as ended while also saying legislation still awaited full Senate approval. The wiki therefore records policy progress without treating a complete durable statutory regime as settled.
- Fare, speed, demand, production, service-date, water-use, and reliability claims come from Boom's founder and remain source-scoped. The episode provides little independent analysis of certification, fuel burn, emissions, noise under varying weather, manufacturing scale, airline economics, or binding generator orders.
