# Waiting Games

## Summary

This [[HiddenBrain]] episode uses dangerous near misses, a passport crisis, an academic job search, medical-result studies, and pandemic uncertainty to distinguish [[ReliefResponseTypes|two forms of relief]] and explain [[WaitingUnderUncertainty]]. Psychologist [[KateSweeny]] argues that relief after narrowly escaping danger can preserve arousal, exhilaration, and counterfactual reflection, whereas relief after completing a prolonged ordeal more often produces exhaustion and closure.

The practical waiting toolkit is time-sensitive rather than uniformly optimistic or pessimistic. It combines optimism through much of a wait, late preparation for an adverse result, contingency planning, advance identification of possible benefits, and absorbing activities that create flow when an endpoint is remote or unknown.

## Key Claims

- [[ReliefResponseTypes|Near-miss relief]] can rapidly redirect residual high arousal from terror toward laughter or exhilaration while preserving awareness of how badly events could have ended.
- Task-completion relief is more likely to produce fatigue and closure after sustained effort, although the two types are presented as tendencies rather than universal categories.
- In the episode's singing experiment, participants spared at the last moment felt more elated and kept thinking about the avoided performance, while those who completed it moved on more quickly.
- [[WaitingUnderUncertainty|Waiting]] reportedly feels hardest near the beginning and end and somewhat easier in the middle.
- Optimism can make most of a wait more tolerable, while considering adverse outcomes near the moment of truth can reduce surprise and support preparation.
- Uncertainty becomes especially difficult when paired with low control; contingency plans and possible silver linings can restore limited agency without determining the result.
- Open-ended waiting removes a clear preparation horizon, making good distraction and flow-producing activity especially useful.
