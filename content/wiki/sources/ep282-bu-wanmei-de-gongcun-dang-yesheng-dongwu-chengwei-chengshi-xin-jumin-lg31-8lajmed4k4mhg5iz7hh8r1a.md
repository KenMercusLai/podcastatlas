## Summary
This [[TalkSanlian|Talk三联]] episode has [[XiaoChuzhou|楚洲]], [[QiuLian|邱连]], and [[BoJing|博靖]] discuss how urban wildlife has become newly visible through cases such as [[QianlingMountainMacaques|黔灵山猕猴]], [[NanjingWildBoars|南京野猪]], [[ShanghaiRaccoonDogs|上海貉]], balcony doves, birds, squirrels, bats, and snakes. The source treats [[UrbanWildlifeCoexistence|城市野生动物共存]] as an imperfect governance problem shaped by feeding, media attention, city expansion, missing predators, public fear, public affection, disease risk, and planning choices. Its core synthesis is that coexistence cannot be reduced to cuteness, expulsion, or hunting: [[WildlifeFeedingFeedbackLoop|投喂反馈回路]] and [[UrbanWildlifeConflictManagement|城市野生动物冲突治理]] require public norms, enforceable rules, habitat design, animal welfare, and human safety to be considered together.

## Key Claims
- Urban wildlife conflict often begins before obvious confrontation: routine feeding, garbage, cat food, tourism, greenbelts, and artificial spaces gradually lower the distance between animals and people.
- [[QianlingMountainMacaques|黔灵山猕猴]] show how long-term tourism interaction and unregulated feeding can push a population beyond estimated carrying capacity and make robbery, bites, vaccination, and relocation problems normal parts of park management.
- [[NanjingWildBoars|南京野猪]] show the gap between media image and ordinary visibility: the animals are not everywhere, but their crop damage, campus traces, urban movement, and culling controversies make them a persistent management case.
- [[ShanghaiRaccoonDogs|上海貉]] show a subtler residential form of coexistence, where building gaps, fake rockwork, garbage, and cat food bring nocturnal animals into old neighborhoods and create health, pet, and resident-anxiety questions.
- Feeding bans, fines, controlled official feeding, contraception, relocation, hunting, volunteer persuasion, cat-food platform design, tree-species choices, and ecological corridors all appear as partial tools rather than a single policy answer.
- The episode repeatedly qualifies wildlife protection as human protection too: close contact can injure people, create disease risk, and later make animals vulnerable to harsher control if conflict escalates.
