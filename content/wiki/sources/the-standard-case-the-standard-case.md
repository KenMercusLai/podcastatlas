# The Standard Case

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode introduces Jesuit casuistry as [[ParticularizedMoralDiscernment]]: begin with concrete standard cases, identify morally relevant similarities and differences, and revise a taxonomy when an unfamiliar problem does not fit inherited rules. [[MalcolmGladwell]] applies the method to [[AndyPettitte]]'s limited use of human growth hormone, placing it nearer [[TommyJohn]]'s restorative elbow surgery than [[BarryBonds]]'s transformative enhancement.

The episode then moves from sports ethics to [[JamesMartinJesuit]]'s response to the Pulse nightclub shooting and the treatment of LGBT Catholics. [[JamesKeenan]]'s account of sin as a failure to bother to love, consistent comparison across Church teachings, and attention to people's whole lives turn casuistry into a discipline of accompaniment as well as classification.

## Key Claims

- Casuistry begins from narrated cases and agreed standard cases rather than assuming that an inherited principle already resolves a novel situation.
- Maritime insurance is presented as closer to payment for protecting transported value than to an exploitative loan, showing how function can control classification.
- Restorative intervention aims to return an athlete toward a prior baseline, whereas transformative enhancement creates materially new capacity and may pressure competitors to accept comparable risks.
- Pettitte's account places his brief injury-recovery use nearer Tommy John surgery than Bonds's late-career transformation, provided the stated motive, limited use, and stable performance are accurate.
- Case-by-case comparison can produce practical agreement among people whose religious or philosophical foundations remain incompatible.
- Martin argues that selective enforcement becomes visible when LGBT Catholics are compared with Catholics who depart from other sexual, reproductive, or social teachings without losing employment or belonging.
- Accompaniment requires attention to a person's concrete life; abstract doctrine, platitude, or outrage cannot substitute for encounter with suffering and membership.
