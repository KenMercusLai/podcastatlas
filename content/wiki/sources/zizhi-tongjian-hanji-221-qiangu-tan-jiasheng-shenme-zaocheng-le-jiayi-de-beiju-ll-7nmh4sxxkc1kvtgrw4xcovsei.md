# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》221｜千古叹贾生，什么造成了贾谊的悲剧？

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode presents [[JiaYi|贾谊]] as both a far-seeing Western Han strategist and a court tutor whose political purpose became inseparable from [[LiuYiLiangKingWesternHan|梁怀王刘揖]]. After Liu Yi dies in a riding accident without an heir, Jia Yi argues that abolishing [[LiangKingdomWesternHan|西汉梁国]] would remove a useful eastern buffer; he proposes retaining or enlarging Liang and Huaiyang so controllable royal kingdoms can constrain Qi, Zhao, Wu, and Chu.

[[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] is said to adopt the strategic direction by moving [[LiuWuLiangKingWesternHan|刘武]] from Huaiyang to Liang, while leaving the northern Dai position unchanged because of Xiongnu pressure. The episode retrospectively treats Liang's resistance during the [[RebellionOfTheSevenStatesWesternHan|七国之乱]] as evidence that Jia Yi understood the geography of princely risk. It then explains Jia Yi's death at thirty-three through guilt over his pupil's accident, the collapse of a four-year educational project, and sustained grief; those psychological and causal claims remain source-attributed rather than medically established.

## Key Claims

- After Liu Yi dies childless in 169 BCE, customary abolition of his kingdom would, in Jia Yi's view, weaken the central court's strategic position.
- Jia Yi warns that second- and third-generation royal kingdoms are becoming harder to govern and may cease to respect central law as their power matures.
- His proposal is spatial as well as dynastic: strengthen Liang against Qi and Zhao, and Huaiyang against Wu and Chu, using more trusted royal houses to check stronger princes.
- Jia Yi frames the danger across generations, warning that a quiet present can still leave a new “six states” problem to a younger successor.
- Liu Heng reportedly accepts the direction, moves Liu Wu to Liang, and does not move the king of Dai because the northern frontier remains strategically exposed.
- The host reads Liang's later defense during the Seven States rebellion as retrospective support for Jia Yi's design, not proof that the exact proposal alone produced the outcome.
- The episode attributes Jia Yi's early death to intense responsibility for Liu Yi's fatal accident, grief over a failed tutorship, and more than a year of depression and crying.

## Key Quotes

> “即便陛下这一代不出祸患，也已经种下祸根。” - the episode's rendering of Jia Yi's intergenerational warning about princely power.

> “梁国可以抗拒齐国和赵国，淮阳国可以禁制吴国和楚国。” - the geographic logic attributed to Jia Yi's kingdom arrangement.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content was found. This episode complements Hanji 219: the earlier source emphasizes gradual territorial fragmentation, while this one emphasizes preserving and repositioning selected kingdoms as strategic buffers.
- The host's claim that Liang's later resistance validates Jia Yi is retrospective. It does not isolate the effects of the 169 BCE settlement from later leadership, resources, alliances, or battlefield decisions.
- Source-scope caution: the exact memorial wording, territorial transfers, identities and titles, implementation sequence, and relation between the proposal and later events require comparison with transmitted histories and historical geography.
- Medical and motive caution: prolonged grief and crying are reported, but the source does not establish a clinical diagnosis or prove that guilt and disappointment were the sole causes of Jia Yi's death.
- Reception caution: the Wang Anshi, Mao Zedong, and Changsha-folklore material shows later commemoration, not direct evidence for Jia Yi's inner state or policy effects.
