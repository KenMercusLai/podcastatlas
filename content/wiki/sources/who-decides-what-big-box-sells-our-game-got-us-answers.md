# Who decides what big box sells? Our GAME got us answers

## Summary
This [[PlanetMoney]] episode continues the [[SellMeASasquatch]] project after [[board-games-3-whats-in-a-name]], following the game from naming and shelf appeal into big-box retail approval, pricing, production, and launch. [[ExplodingKittens]] takes the [[MarketForLemons|The Market for Lemons]]-inspired game through [[RetailLineReview]], buyer questions about audience and price, [[PlanogramGatekeeping]], and a first round of roughly 100,000 units ordered by two major U.S. retailers.

The source's strongest contribution is to make physical retail gatekeeping concrete. [[ErikaBeras]] has only minutes to pitch the game, [[MattAdelman]] explains how buyers defend scarce shelf space internally, and the later manufacturing sequence shows how a $19.99 price point, royalties, packaging upgrades, a factory deadline, and quality control turn [[RetailShelfAppeal]] into an operational system.

## Key Claims
- Big-box buyers do not only ask whether a product is good; they ask whether it can outperform whatever loses shelf space inside a [[PlanogramGatekeeping|planogram]].
- [[RetailLineReview]] compresses product strategy into a short, high-stakes meeting where buyer confidence, audience clarity, price point, and support plan matter.
- [[AudienceBackedRetailPitch]] matters because the buyer may bet partly on the person or institution behind the product, not only on mechanics or box copy.
- [[RetailPricePointProductScoping]] turns pricing into design scope: a $9.99 card game and a $19.99 boxed game can support different components, packaging, and shelf signals.
- The source reports that two major retailers accepted [[SellMeASasquatch]] full chain throughout North America, with a first order around 100,000 units.
- The royalty deal is presented as industry-standard, roughly 3% to 7% of net revenue, with [[ExplodingKittens]] carrying most development, prototyping, and manufacturing risk.
- [[MassRetailProductionDeadline]] appears when big-box commitments force final card, box, instruction, logo, QR-code, finish, and color decisions before factory files are due.
- Packaging is tested as a shelf object: the team checks whether the Pantone purple box reads from distance, survives shadow, and feels adult enough after a matte-finish change.
- Listener playtesting and creature submissions become part of the product, with final listener-inspired cards marked inside the game.
