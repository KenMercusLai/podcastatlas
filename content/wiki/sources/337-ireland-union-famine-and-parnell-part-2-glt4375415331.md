# 337: Ireland: Union, Famine and Parnell (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode with [[PaulRouse]] follows [[Ireland]] from the failed promise of Catholic emancipation after the Act of Union through [[DanielOConnell]], the [[GreatIrishFamine]], the land struggle, [[CharlesStewartParnell]], and [[JohnRedmond]]. It asks why migration, imperial service, commerce, and political reform did not turn the United Kingdom of Great Britain and Ireland into a durable settlement.

Its central synthesis is that famine transformed demography and political memory while constitutional nationalism, rather than revolutionary republicanism, became the dominant route toward self-government. Redmond eventually brought [[IrishHomeRule|Home Rule]] onto the statute book, but nationalist underestimation of Ulster unionist attachment to the United Kingdom left Britain and Ireland approaching civil war before the First World War changed the sequence.

## Key Claims

- The post-Union refusal of Catholic emancipation left a major legitimacy gap until [[DanielOConnell]]'s mass organization and County Clare election helped force the 1829 settlement.
- Nineteenth-century Irish life combined grievances with migration, military service, commerce, education, and imperial participation; allegiance and nationalism were not clean social opposites.
- The [[GreatIrishFamine]] killed about one million people and drove more than one million to emigrate, reshaping Irish population, diaspora memory, and hostility toward British rule.
- British authorities knew the scale of suffering and operated large public-works and soup-kitchen programs, but withdrawal, ideological assumptions, and policy choices sustain a serious contemporary-standard judgment of culpability.
- Revolutionary republicanism persisted through the Irish Republican Brotherhood and diaspora networks, but the 1848 and 1867 rebellions failed and majority nationalism generally pursued constitutional Home Rule.
- [[CharlesStewartParnell]] linked parliamentary obstruction, land agitation, and Home Rule before forged allegations and the O'Shea scandal split his party and ended his leadership.
- Land purchase legislation gradually transferred landlord-held farms to tenants, showing that the constitutional route could produce major structural reform even before Home Rule.
- [[JohnRedmond]] reunited the Irish Parliamentary Party, supported the People's Budget and Parliament Act, and used the post-1910 balance at Westminster to make a third Home Rule bill legislatively viable.
- Home Rule meant limited Irish self-government under the monarchy rather than an independent republic, although some nationalists treated it as a step toward greater sovereignty.
- Liberal and nationalist leaders underestimated the depth of Ulster unionist resistance, so legislative success coexisted with armed mobilization and a plausible civil-war crisis by 1912-1914.

## Key Quotes

> "permanent good" from "transient evil" - Charles Trevelyan's providential framing of famine policy as presented in the episode.

> "policy of extermination" - the Lord Lieutenant's contemporary condemnation of continued policy amid mass suffering.

> "the decision of the Irish nation" - the post-1867 revolutionary threshold the episode contrasts with premature rebellion.
