# Love in the time of Palantir

## Summary
This [[PlanetMoney]] episode uses [[PalantirDatingEvent|Singles Dating Matching Using Palantir Data]], a satirical New York dating event organized by [[JamesSteinberg]], to make data surveillance concrete at a personal scale. [[MikeSteinberger]] distinguishes [[Palantir]]'s stated business model from its public reputation: the company is described as not owning, buying, or selling data, but as making clients' messy data easier to connect and use. The episode's durable synthesis is that [[DataOperationalization]] can be privacy-sensitive even without a secret corporate database, especially when [[CrossDatasetPrivacyLinkage]], facial search, public records, leaked data, and government-held datasets become easy to combine.

## Key Claims
- [[Palantir]] is framed less as a data owner than as a data-operationalization company: its software helps agencies and businesses connect data they already hold or can access.
- [[PalantirDatingEvent|Singles Dating Matching Using Palantir Data]] shows that invasive-feeling profiles can be assembled from names, phone numbers, public records, reverse lookup tools, facial recognition, bounty-hunter databases, and leaked data without using Palantir software.
- [[IdentityResolutionError]] matters because Jeannie Chan's common name, same-city professional double, and incorrect photos show that data-matching systems can expose or judge people even when they identify the wrong person.
- Zach's profile shows a consent gap around face-searchable public life: protest photos, concert photos, campaign work, travel, and employment history became searchable despite his not understanding himself as having consented to that searchability.
- Palantir's business and government examples diverge: the [[Airbus]] A350 case illustrates industrial integration value, while [[USImmigrationAndCustomsEnforcement|ICE]] and [[USDepartmentOfHomelandSecurity|DHS]] raise civil-liberties concerns about scale, speed, and downstream agency control.
- [[GovernmentDataSiloCollapse]] is the policy-level issue: post-9/11 data integration, the [[PrivacyAct1974|Privacy Act of 1974]], an anti-silo executive order, and an ICE-IRS data-sharing memorandum all appear as fights over when government-held data may be repurposed.
- [[TechnologyAssistedInteriorEnforcement]] is extended by the claim that Palantir software may help ICE enrich 30 to 40 datasets and search a database apparently containing 20 million names.

## Key Quotes
> "does not own data" - [[MikeSteinberger]]'s summary of Palantir's position in the episode.

> "not using Palantir data" - [[JamesSteinberg]]'s disclosure about the dating event.

> "20 million names" - scale attributed to the ICE database discussed near the end of the episode.
