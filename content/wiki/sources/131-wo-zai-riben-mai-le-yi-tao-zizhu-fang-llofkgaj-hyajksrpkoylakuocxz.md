# 131.我在日本买了一套自住房

## Summary
This [[QizhulouYanBinke|起朱楼宴宾客]] episode uses [[DavidWeng|大卫翁]]'s first self-use home purchase in [[Japan]] as a field report on [[JapaneseNewCondoBuying|Japanese new-build condo buying]]. The case is deliberately narrow: it covers a newly built apartment in [[Yokohama]], not resale homes, detached houses, investment property, or short-term rental loans. Its core synthesis is that Japanese housing can feel close to "housing for living" because presale payment, disclosure, mortgage review, maintenance reserves, taxes, insurance, and management turn ownership into an ongoing service-and-risk system rather than a pure appreciation bet. The same system still leaves core urban locations vulnerable to renewed investment logic when low rates, scarce central land, and city-center redevelopment push prices up.

## Key Claims
- The source is a personal process account, not a full Japanese property-buying guide; its strongest evidence is for a new-build condo purchased for self-occupation.
- [[Yokohama]] matters as a residence-choice case between Kamakura-style quieter life and Tokyo-scale convenience; location and daily fit outweighed tower amenities.
- Japanese condo categories separate lower-rise apartments, ordinary reinforced-concrete mansions, and tower mansions with larger shared facilities.
- [[JapaneseRailwayRealEstateDevelopment]] shows why Japanese housing cannot be separated from station access, commuter routes, and private railway operators' commercial-residential loops.
- [[JapaneseHousingPresaleProtection]] differs from the Chinese unfinished-apartment problem: the buyer paid a 10% deposit at booking and the rest at handover, while the lending bank also paid the developer only at handover.
- Japanese sale area uses wall-center measurement and excludes balconies, which remain common areas because they function as emergency escape space.
- [[JapaneseCondoMaintenanceReserve]] and management fees make ownership expensive, but they also support cleaning, administration, public-area repair, long-cycle replacement, and resale-value preservation.
- [[JapaneseHousingInformationDisclosure]] appears through important-matters documents, land history, legal restrictions, utilities, disaster information, urban plans, construction reports, handover procedures, and paper/electronic process redundancy.
- The source treats official customization options as a "pay for convenience" service: expensive, standardized, and useful when the buyer wants the developer to coordinate reliable work.
- [[JapaneseMortgageInsuranceBundle]] combines low-rate mortgage finance, borrower credit, tax deduction, foreign-resident friction, purpose-based loan categories, group credit life insurance, fire insurance, and earthquake insurance.
- The episode uses [[NegativeRealEstate]] to explain post-bubble Japan: in population-loss or weak-demand areas, property can become hard to sell and costly to hold.
- [[HousingAsConsumptionGood]] is the source's main market lesson: Japanese buildings often depreciate with age, while scarce land, location, management quality, and repair discipline carry more durable value.
- [[JapanesePropertyHoldingCost]] includes property tax, city planning tax, management fees, repair reserves, insurance, options, and services; the headline tax rate is less informative than the full carrying-cost stack.
- [[JapaneseUrbanCoreRedevelopment]] explains why central Tokyo, Osaka, Yokohama, and other core areas can become newly investable even inside a culture shaped by the real-estate bubble's collapse.

## Key Quotes
> "新建公寓" - the scope limit the host repeats so the episode is not mistaken for a whole Japanese property manual.

> "负动产" - the post-bubble warning that some property becomes an obligation rather than an asset.

> "房住不炒" - the source's shorthand for Japan's stronger self-use, holding-cost, and maintenance-oriented housing logic.
