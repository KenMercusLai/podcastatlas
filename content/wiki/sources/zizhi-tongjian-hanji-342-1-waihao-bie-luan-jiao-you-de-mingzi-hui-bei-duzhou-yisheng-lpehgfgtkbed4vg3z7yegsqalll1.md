# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》342-1｜外号别乱叫，有的名字会被毒咒一生

## Summary

This short [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode uses a maxim associated with the [[Huainanzi|《淮南子》]], disasters around 114 BCE, and a robbed and burned royal tomb to frame the Western Han title “King of Changshan” as apparently cursed. It strings together the early deaths or removals of Liu Buyi, Liu Hong, and Liu Bo, then makes [[LiuShunChangshanKingWesternHan|常山王刘顺]] the closing case: [[HanJingdi|汉景帝]]'s favored younger son ruled for thirty-two years, yet the episode identifies his burial with the violently disturbed [[GaozhuangHanTomb|高庄汉墓]].

The durable value is not evidence for a supernatural curse. It is a compact example of [[CursedTitleNarrative|封号诅咒叙事]]: repeated misfortune is organized around one inherited title, while different mechanisms—early death, dynastic killing, deposition, and posthumous tomb destruction—are compressed into a common pattern. The source introduces [[ChangshanKingdomWesternHan|西汉常山国]] and its royal line, but it does not supply the institutional history or direct archaeological evidence needed to prove either curse-like causation or the tomb's occupant.

## Key Claims

- A saying associated by the host with the Huainanzi links restrained desire to avoidance of harm; the episode uses it as a moral preface rather than as evidence about the Changshan kings.
- The episode places a fire at Han Jingdi's burial precinct, April hail, severe famine, and reports of cannibalism around 114 BCE as a disaster background.
- Real tomb protection is presented as reliance on closure and concealment rather than the poison, fire, sand, and mechanical traps common in tomb fiction; repeated robbery shafts show the limits of that protection.
- The episode describes Gaozhuang as a large Western Han royal tomb that robbers emptied and then burned so intensely that stone elements collapsed.
- Liu Buyi dies two years after becoming King of Changshan, and Liu Hong is killed under the claim that he was not Han Huidi's biological son; these distinct deaths are grouped under the same title pattern.
- Liu Shun is described as indulged, repeatedly offending rules, and repeatedly forgiven because he was Han Jingdi's favored youngest son; he remains king for thirty-two years.
- Liu Shun's son Liu Bo is soon deposed after succeeding him, after which the episode says the title is not used again until the Eastern Han.

## Key Quotes

> “谁受封，谁都没好下场。” - the host's suspense formula for the Changshan title pattern.

> “诅咒” - a narrative label for repeated misfortune, not a demonstrated causal force.

## Connections

- [[ChangshanKingdomWesternHan|西汉常山国]] - vassal kingdom and title line around which the episode organizes several different royal outcomes.
- [[LiuShunChangshanKingWesternHan|常山王刘顺]] - long-reigning exception in life whose robbed and burned burial supplies the episode's posthumous reversal.
- [[GaozhuangHanTomb|高庄汉墓]] - disturbed royal tomb attributed by the episode to Liu Shun.
- [[CursedTitleNarrative|封号诅咒叙事]] - pattern-making device that compresses heterogeneous misfortunes into one ominous title history.
- [[HanJingdi|汉景帝]] - father whose favor is used to explain Liu Shun's repeated pardons and unusually long reign.
- [[Huainanzi|《淮南子》]] - philosophical text loosely invoked for the opening warning about desire and harm.
- [[ArchaeologicalInterpretationUnderSparseEvidence]] - evidentiary boundary relevant to identifying a robbed tomb's rank, dynasty, polity, and particular occupant.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing canonical pages was found; the Changshan royal line and Gaozhuang tomb were not previously represented.
- “Cursed” is an explicit storytelling metaphor. The episode combines unrelated causal categories and does not establish that the title itself affected anyone's fate.
- The supplied note says the 114 BCE fire affected Han Jingdi's burial precinct, but it does not identify the structure or provide a primary-text citation; the date, site, hail, famine, and cannibalism claims remain episode-attributed.
- The claim that thirty-two people held the Changshan title across dynasties, the exact early royal genealogy, the grounds for Liu Hong's killing, Liu Shun's offenses, Liu Bo's deposition, and the title's period of disuse require direct comparison with received histories.
- The source says archaeologists judged Gaozhuang to be Liu Shun's tomb but does not provide the excavation report, inscriptions, grave inventory, dating argument, or alternative candidates. Royal rank and personal occupant identity therefore remain different claims.
- Fictional tomb mechanisms, robber motives for burning the chamber, fire intensity, and structural collapse are presented in popular-history form and are not independently verified here.
