# 12 Days: The Battle of Wakefield and Emperor Karl I

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode pairs two anniversaries of failed monarchical projects. The first follows [[RichardOfYork|Richard, Duke of York]] from protector and rival claimant to his death at the [[BattleOfWakefield]] in 1460, placing the disaster inside a [[WarsOfTheRoses|Wars of the Roses]] crisis driven by disputed succession, weak kingship, magnate armies, and the aftermath of English defeat in France.

The second follows [[KarlI|Karl I]] from his unexpected succession and wartime coronation with the [[HolyCrownOfHungary]] to reform and peace efforts, the collapse of [[AustriaHungary]], and two failed Hungarian restoration attempts. Across both halves, sacred legitimacy matters but cannot replace military strength, political coalition, or control of institutions.

## Key Claims

- The Wars of the Roses is presented primarily as a dynastic struggle intensified by Henry VI's incapacity, English defeat in France, returning military manpower, and aristocratic rivalry rather than by a deep ideological divide.
- Richard of York's descent from Edward III, service as lord protector, and reputation for competence made a crown claim plausible, but even his allies resisted displacing an anointed king outright.
- The settlement that left Henry VI on the throne while disinheriting his son converted a temporary compromise into an immediate threat to Margaret of Anjou and the Lancastrian succession.
- Richard's sortie from Sandal Castle at Wakefield remains poorly explained; his defeat, the death of his son Edmund, and the display of his paper-crowned head turned military failure into dynastic humiliation.
- Shakespeare's treatment of Wakefield helped shape later emotional memory, especially the dramatic image of Margaret of Anjou, without supplying a neutral battlefield record.
- Karl inherited an empire under military and political exhaustion, pursued limited federal reform and peace feelers, but remained constrained by German dominance and the First World War.
- His Hungarian coronation preserved medieval sacred ritual inside total war, while the empire's collapse and Horthy's refusal to restore him showed that coronation alone could not recreate governing power.
