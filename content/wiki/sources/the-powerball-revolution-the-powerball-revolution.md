# The Powerball Revolution

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode follows [[AdamCronkite]]'s experiments using [[Sortition]] to select student councils in Bolivia, where volunteering without campaigning drew in students who said they would not have stood for election and produced socially broader teams. [[MalcolmGladwell]] uses those councils, [[MichaelLauer]]'s analysis of [[NationalInstitutesOfHealth|NIH]] grant-review scores, and a workshop at [[LawrencevilleSchool]] to argue that campaign performance and expert rankings can be weak predictors of later leadership or research impact.

The episode's practical proposal is not unrestricted chance. [[QualifiedPoolLottery]] first removes clearly unsuitable options or identifies candidates who meet a threshold, then uses a lottery among the acceptable remainder. This design acknowledges [[PredictiveSelectionLimits]] while retaining minimum standards, but the supplied summary does not establish long-term council performance, causal effects, or superiority across every selection setting.

## Key Claims

- In the Bolivian school experiments, lowering the entry cost from campaigning to volunteering for a lottery attracted students who would not have entered a conventional election.
- Lottery-selected councils reportedly mixed social circles more broadly and completed practical projects including first-aid kits, field trips, competitions, a library, and student identification cards.
- Cronkite and teachers often misjudged future contributors: confidence, charisma, popularity, and initial engagement did not reliably identify who would collaborate or follow through.
- Lauer reportedly found no strong relationship between NIH peer-review scores within the funded range and the later citation impact of funded work.
- Elections, grant review, and admissions share a prediction problem when polished presentation is treated as evidence of future performance.
- A [[QualifiedPoolLottery]] preserves a competence threshold while refusing to manufacture a precise ranking where evidence cannot support one.
- Legitimacy can come from participation opportunities and process design as well as from winning a familiar competitive ritual.
