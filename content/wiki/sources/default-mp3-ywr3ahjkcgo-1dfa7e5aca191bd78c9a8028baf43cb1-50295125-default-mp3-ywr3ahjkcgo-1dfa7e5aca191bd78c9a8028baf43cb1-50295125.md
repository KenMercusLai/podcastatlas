# Mind Reading 2.0: How Others See You

## Summary
This [[HiddenBrain]] episode has psychologist [[EricaBoothby]] explain why people often infer harsher social judgments than their conversation partners actually report. [[LikingGap]] names the recurring underestimation of how much another person enjoyed an interaction or liked the speaker, while [[SocialAttentionAsymmetry]] joins several related errors produced by unequal access to one's own thoughts, intentions, flaws, and attention.

The practical response is information-seeking rather than blanket optimism. Outside observers, careful direct questions, curiosity about the other person, and sincere compliments can reveal evidence that guarded self-monitoring conceals, but privacy, safety, power, culture, and genuine rejection still limit how directly people should disclose or approach.

## Key Claims
- [[LikingGap]] appears when people like a conversation partner more than they believe that partner likes them; the episode reports persistence among new college roommates for several months.
- Self-critical thoughts are highly available to the self but usually hidden from the other person, encouraging people to project insecurity onto an observer whose attention is broader and often more charitable.
- [[SocialAttentionAsymmetry]] includes the illusion of transparency, overestimation of how much an embarrassing feature is noticed, the feeling of being the observer rather than the observed, and underestimation of how long one's words remain in another person's thoughts.
- Indirect signaling leaves conversation length, attraction, reconciliation, and other preferences underdetermined because participants often hide the information needed for accurate interpretation.
- The episode reports no liking gap among four-year-olds, an effect emerging around age five and increasing through age eleven, and a larger gap among shy participants; broader lifespan and social-anxiety patterns remain hypotheses.
- Outside perspectives and respectful direct questions can replace speculation with information, while curiosity shifts attention from performance monitoring toward learning about the other person.
- Expressing sincere positive judgments gives others evidence that is otherwise invisible and can counter imagined rejection.
