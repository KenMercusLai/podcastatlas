# 262: Tunisia: Dido of Carthage

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode compares [[Dido|Dido / Elissa]] as the resourceful Tyrian founder of [[Carthage]] with the tragic queen reshaped by [[Virgil]] in the [[Aeneid]]. The contrast makes Dido a case of politically consequential literary transformation: older traditions emphasize exile, foundation, cunning, and fidelity to her murdered husband, while Virgil joins her abandonment and death to [[Aeneas]]'s Roman destiny, the Punic conflict, and the human cost of empire.

The episode treats neither version as secure biography. Phoenician names, Carthage's Tyrian origin, possible coin imagery, and ritual parallels may preserve older material, but dates, motives, deaths, and direct historical identifications remain uncertain. Virgil's Dido may echo [[CleopatraVII|Cleopatra VII]] and Augustan anxiety about foreign queens, yet the poem gives Dido dignity and makes Roman duty costly rather than functioning as simple propaganda.

## Key Claims

- Older or parallel traditions call Dido Elissa, make her a Tyrian princess fleeing her brother Pygmalion after her husband Acherbas's murder, and credit her with preserving treasure through deception before founding Carthage.
- The oxhide story makes Dido's intelligence part of the foundation legend: she cuts the hide into strips to enclose the Byrsa hill rather than accepting the apparent limit literally.
- In the traditions associated with Timaeus and Trogus, Dido kills herself rather than marry the local ruler Iarbas, making fidelity to her dead husband central; Virgil instead makes love for Aeneas and abandonment the immediate tragic frame.
- The chronology prevents the Aeneas relationship from functioning as straightforward history, and the episode presents Virgil as transforming an inherited founder tradition for Augustan Rome.
- Virgil's Dido is not a simple anti-Carthaginian villain: her welcome, competence, suffering, curse, and death expose the personal and historical costs imposed by Aeneas's divinely authorized destiny.
- The comparison with Cleopatra is interpretive rather than conclusive. Both could signify a powerful eastern queen in Roman political imagination, but the episode does not establish a one-to-one portrait.
- Phoenician names, Tyrian settlement history, possible founder imagery on coins, and Carthaginian self-immolation traditions may preserve context without verifying the legend's individual events.

## Contradictions

- The episode preserves a direct conflict between traditions: Virgil makes Dido die after Aeneas leaves, while the Elissa tradition makes her die to avoid marriage to Iarbas and a later Greek epigram has her deny ever meeting Aeneas.
- This is treated as literary transformation rather than a contradiction the wiki can resolve historically. The Aeneas chronology, Dido's biography, the oxhide episode, the taking of Cypriot women, Iarbas's proposal, founder-coin identification, the name's meaning, ritual parallels, and exact foundation date remain source-scoped or contested.
- The child-sacrifice and self-immolation parallels do not prove that Dido's death preserves a real Carthaginian rite. The Cleopatra analogy likewise remains an interpretive possibility, not an identified historical model.
