# Wolfgang's Law - Part Two

## Summary

In this [[RevisionistHistory]] episode, [[MalcolmGladwell]] compares [[JohnPuskas]]'s minimally invasive coronary-bypass work with [[DavidKennedyCriminologist|David Kennedy]]'s [[FocusedDeterrence|focused-deterrence]] strategy. Both cases support the episode's central claim: a promising method can remain marginal when it demands new skills, coordinated teams, shared authority, durable institutional support, and a changed professional identity rather than a simple technical substitution.

The policy branch follows [[OperationCeasefireBoston|Boston's Operation Ceasefire]] and Baltimore's delayed adoption under [[BrandonScott]]. It presents focused deterrence as a combination of network analysis, direct communication, practical help, community legitimacy, and predictable enforcement—not punishment alone—and uses Baltimore's intensive work around an at-risk young man to illustrate [[TeamBasedServiceDelivery]]. The cross-domain synthesis is captured by [[InstitutionalInnovationAdoptionResistance]]: evidence of benefit does not by itself remove the organizational cost of implementation.

## Key Claims

- [[CardiacSurgeryModalitySelection|minimally invasive coronary bypass]] can reduce incision burden, hospitalization, bleeding, infection, and recovery time for selected patients, but the episode says it remains only a small share of US bypass surgery after roughly three decades.
- [[FocusedDeterrence]] concentrates communication, support, community pressure, and credible enforcement on the small connected network driving a disproportionate share of serious violence.
- The episode reports a large homicide decline after [[OperationCeasefireBoston|Operation Ceasefire]] began in Boston, while noting that early programs elsewhere often faded after outside experts left.
- Baltimore waited more than two decades after Kennedy's 1997 presentation to implement a comprehensive version, and an earlier punishment-heavy attempt omitted the strategy's supportive half.
- Both minimally invasive bypass and focused deterrence replace heroic individual command with [[TeamBasedServiceDelivery|team-based delivery]] across specialized roles.
- [[InstitutionalInnovationAdoptionResistance]] can arise from retraining burdens, lost autonomy, professional identity, budget and coordination needs, political leadership, and weak implementation fidelity even when decision-makers recognize the underlying problem.
- The episode argues that generational turnover may help new methods gain authority when established practitioners remain attached to prior models, but this is an interpretive conclusion rather than a universal law.

- The generational explanation is retained as an interpretation. Funding, organizational turnover, competing evidence, local capacity, political incentives, and implementation fidelity may also explain slow adoption.
