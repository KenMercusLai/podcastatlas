# 543. Death in the Amazon: Aguirre, the Wrath of God

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode uses Werner Herzog's film *Aguirre, the Wrath of God* to reconstruct the 1560-1561 [[AguirreExpedition|Ursua-Aguirre expedition]] from Peru through the Amazon and into Venezuela. It follows [[LopeDeAguirre|Lope de Aguirre]] from mutineer to rebel commander: he helps overthrow [[PedroDeUrsua|Pedro de Ursua]], abandons the search for [[ElDorado]], renounces [[PhilipII|Philip II]], occupies Margarita Island, and attempts to redirect a failed conquest toward an invasion of Peru.

The episode's strongest interpretation joins individual violence to [[ColonialFrontierGovernanceFragility|fragile colonial rule]]. Aguirre is presented as brutally paranoid yet sometimes strategically lucid, while the surviving accounts pose a [[ComplicitWitnessScapegoatNarrative|complicit-witness problem]] because followers who participated in the rebellion had reasons to make him uniquely demonic.

## Key Claims

- In late-1550s Peru, weak royal control, unemployed soldiers, feuds, inflation, and conquest expectations made a treasure expedition useful both as an imperial venture and as a way to remove dangerous manpower.
- Viceroy Andres Hurtado de Mendoza appointed [[PedroDeUrsua|Pedro de Ursua]] to lead roughly 400 Spaniards and thousands of Indigenous Peruvians toward [[ElDorado]], but poor vessels, hunger, rain, and leadership grievances quickly damaged the expedition.
- On 1 January 1561, Aguirre's faction murdered Ursua while invoking liberty, loyalty to the king, and resistance to tyranny; Aguirre then exposed the fiction by signing himself "the traitor."
- Aguirre rejected El Dorado as fantasy, installed Fernando de Guzman as prince, renounced Philip II, and proposed sailing to the Atlantic before taking Margarita, Panama, the treasure fleet, and Peru.
- After eliminating Guzman and other rivals, Aguirre used surveillance, a Basque guard, movement controls, and repeated killings to hold the shrinking force together.
- The expedition reached the Atlantic after about nine months and occupied Margarita, where burning royal account books and targeting officials made its challenge to crown authority explicit.
- Aguirre's letter to Philip II attacked royal ingratitude, corrupt colonial officials, and religious orders, showing that the rebellion had a political argument even though its practice remained murderous and coercive.
- Royal amnesty induced desertion at Barquisimeto; Aguirre killed his daughter Elvira before former followers killed him on 27 October 1561.
- Later portrayals of Aguirre as uniquely mad may preserve real brutality while also narrowing responsibility away from the many participants who later needed reconciliation with royal authority.

## Key Quotes

> "Lope de Aguirre, the traitor" - the episode's account of Aguirre signing the mutineers' document after insisting that killing the king's governor made them all traitors.

> "war of fire and blood" - Aguirre's proclaimed war against the king of Castile and his vassals after landing in Venezuela.
