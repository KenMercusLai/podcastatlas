## Summary
This [[PlanetMoney]] episode uses the 1974 recombinant-DNA moratorium and 1975 Asilomar conference to explain why AI developers can warn about catastrophic risk while continuing to race. [[JanetMertz]] and [[PaulBerg]] anchor a case in which scientists briefly paused risky work, negotiated risk-tiered containment rules, and relied on the [[NationalInstitutesOfHealth]]'s control of research funding to make restraint apply beyond the conference room.

The episode's strongest lesson is institutional rather than analogical: coordination becomes more credible when the agenda is narrow, liability turns diffuse danger into personal exposure, and enforcement is attached to a resource participants cannot easily replace. It also adds a distributional warning through Mertz, whose early-career lead was lost even though she accepted the pause as socially worthwhile.

## Key Claims
- Frontier AI safety is a collective-action problem because unilateral restraint can transfer advantage to competitors without removing the shared risk.
- Recombinant-DNA researchers called a temporary moratorium after concern that modified *E. coli* carrying tumor-virus material might escape a laboratory and spread harm.
- The Asilomar conference classified experiments by risk and paired physical containment with weakened organisms unable to survive in the wild.
- Personal liability and the prospect of externally imposed rules helped shift scientists from abstract concern toward concrete negotiation.
- NIH funding made scientist-designed guidance enforceable across U.S. research institutions, including laboratories not represented at Asilomar.
- AI is a harder coordination case because development is faster, competition is international, and leading firms can rely on private capital rather than public research grants.
- Collective restraint creates unequal individual losses; Mertz says the moratorium erased a substantial research lead and damaged her career.

## Key Quotes
> "The moratorium was worth it." - Mertz's retrospective judgment despite the career cost attributed to the pause.

> "Regulation was inevitable." - the legal intervention as summarized by the episode.

## Contradictions
- No settled contradiction is recorded.
- Asilomar is a qualified analogy rather than proof that the same design will govern AI: the episode itself emphasizes private capital, development speed, international rivalry, and weaker dependence on a single funder.
- The feared recombinant-DNA escape was a low-probability scenario, not a documented catastrophe, and the source does not establish the counterfactual outcome without the pause.
- Proposed AI taxes, hardware kill switches, and independent release audits are discussion options rather than evaluated or enacted solutions in this source.
