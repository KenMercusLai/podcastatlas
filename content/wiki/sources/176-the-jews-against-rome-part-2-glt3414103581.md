# 176. The Jews Against Rome (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode reconstructs the long political and religious background to the [[JudeanRevolt66To73|Judean revolt]], from Temple destruction, Persian restoration, scripture, Hellenistic rule, and Judean expansion to Pompey's conquest, [[HerodTheGreat|Herod's]] client kingdom, and unstable Roman administration. Its distinction between “Jews” and “Judeans” emphasizes a people tied to Judea and Jerusalem but also dispersed across Babylon, Alexandria, and Rome, qualifying any account of the war as a timeless religious binary.

The opening campaign shows [[RomanProvincialRevoltEscalation]] as a compound process rather than a single-cause explosion. Taxation and Florus's extraction, cultural resistance, apocalyptic and messianic expectation, garrison violence, Roman prestige, strategic geography, and divisions within Judean society all mattered. After Cestius Gallus's defeat at Beth-Horon, [[Vespasian]] entered Galilee; the priestly commander later known as [[Josephus]] survived Jotapata, surrendered, and linked Vespasian to a Judean prophecy of world rule before the Roman civil war made that prediction politically useful.

## Key Claims

- Temple, Torah, scripture, diaspora, and Jerusalem-centered identity preserved Judean peoplehood across conquest and dispersion.
- Translating *Ioudaioi* as “Judeans” can recover territorial and political dimensions obscured by a purely religious reading, although no single translation fits every context.
- Pompey's settlement and Herod's client kingship show Rome governing through tribute, local worship, dynastic brokerage, construction, and selective autonomy before moving toward direct rule.
- The revolt of 66 CE arose from converging fiscal, administrative, cultural, political, and eschatological pressures rather than one uniform national or religious motive.
- Judean society was divided by region, class, priestly status, and attitude toward Rome; Agrippa, Berenice, and many other Judeans opposed rebellion.
- Cestius Gallus's failed Jerusalem expedition and defeat at Beth-Horon transformed unrest into a prestige crisis Rome believed it had to reverse.
- Josephus's Jotapata survival and prophecy became entangled with Vespasian's victory in the Year of Four Emperors and later Flavian legitimacy.
