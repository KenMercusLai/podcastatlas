# Meta's new Muse AI agent gives investors the warm-'n-fuzzies

## Summary
This [[MarketplaceTech]] week-in-review episode has [[MeganMcCartyCorino|Megan McCarty-Corino]] interview [[AnitaRamaswamy]] of [[TheInformation]] about [[MusePersonalAgent|Muse]], AI-linked cybersecurity valuations, and [[Discord]]'s age-assurance rollout. The shared problem is trust under expanded access: a consumer agent becomes useful by acting through sensitive accounts, security vendors gain attention by promising protection against AI threats, and child-safety systems classify users through behavioral data or identity documents.

The source strengthens [[PlatformAgentAccessConflict]] and [[BehavioralAgeInference]] while adding [[AICybersecurityValuationGap]]. Its durable synthesis is that real demand does not prove a particular implementation: convenience, security, and compliance claims still require evidence about errors, product performance, permissions, disclosure, data handling, and recoverability.

## Key Claims
- [[MusePersonalAgent|Muse]] is presented as a consumer agent that can schedule appointments, complete forms, manage a fantasy-football lineup, shop, and perform other routine tasks rather than only answer questions.
- A customizable furry avatar and a Tamagotchi-like wearable give Muse a playful consumer identity that may make agents more legible to less technical users.
- The episode says Muse reached early download and popularity levels comparable with [[ChatGPT]], but does not provide audited retention or usage evidence.
- Consequential action requires sensitive permissions, potentially including access to medical information, financial accounts, credentials, calendars, and purchases.
- Early reports include Muse booking two hotel rooms instead of one, showing that action errors can create real costs and correction work.
- Some calls attributed to Muse are reportedly completed by people behind the scenes, and the source raises disclosure questions about whether users and call recipients know when a human is involved.
- [[Amazon]] has blocked Muse bots while [[Shopify]] allows Muse to shop through its storefronts, making platform access a practical limit on general-purpose agents.
- Amazon's stated credential and scraping concerns coexist with a commercial interest in retaining customer profiles, personalization, traffic, and transaction control.
- AI-related security risk has supported investor enthusiasm for [[CrowdStrike]], [[Okta]], and [[PaloAltoNetworks|Palo Alto Networks]], but the source argues that vendors should not be treated as interchangeable beneficiaries.
- The cited cybersecurity index gain of more than 40% versus roughly 11% for the S&P 500 is a source-dated market comparison, not proof that current products justify their valuations.
- The episode says some security vendors are only beginning to ship AI products and that product claims remain insufficiently tested at scale.
- Frontier-model providers such as [[Anthropic]] and [[OpenAI]] may compete with security companies in incident response, endpoint defense, and other categories rather than serving only as their technical substrate.
- [[Discord]] plans to estimate most users' ages in the background from account age and activity instead of asking everyone for ID or facial verification.
- Behavioral estimates necessarily create false-positive and false-negative risk, while fallback requests for driver's licenses, passports, or credit cards concentrate more sensitive data.
- The episode cites a breach at a Discord vendor affecting 70,000 users and a separate IDScan incident as evidence that age-verification infrastructure can become a high-value attack target.
- Laws and settlements are likely to make age assurance more common, so child-safety benefits must be weighed against data minimization, error correction, vendor security, and user notice.
