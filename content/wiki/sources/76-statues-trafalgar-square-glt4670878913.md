## Summary

This first on-location [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode walks through [[TrafalgarSquare]] to ask whether public statues should be retained, removed, reinterpreted, or balanced by new commissions. Charles I, James II, [[HoratioNelson|Nelson]], [[GeorgeIV]], Charles James Napier, Henry Havelock, [[GeorgeWashington]], and [[EdithCavell]] show that monuments can carry victory, empire, dynasty, friendship, martyrdom, moral restraint, and later indifference at the same time.

The episode resists both blanket preservation and blanket removal. Its strongest contribution to [[StatueCommemorationPolitics]] is that judgment should distinguish a monument's original claim, the subject's wider record, its present public meaning, the concerns of affected communities, and the practical question of what removal or addition would place in the civic landscape instead.

## Key Claims

- Charles I's restored statue and its orientation toward Whitehall make royalist restoration and unresolved Civil War memory visible in the city.
- James II's Roman dress links early modern British royal monuments to classical and Renaissance models, while public indifference shows that intended political meaning can decay into urban furniture.
- [[HoratioNelson|Nelson's]] column joins a triumphal Roman form to Trafalgar, naval victory, national survival, later imperial power, and present controversy over slavery and abolition.
- [[GeorgeIV]], Napier, and Havelock weaken any assumption that a statue certifies complete moral excellence: failure, self-criticism, imperial conquest, public subscription, and later audience judgment coexist.
- The earlier removal of Edward Jenner and the continuing fourth-plinth program show that commemorative landscapes already change through relocation, replacement, and new art.
- [[GeorgeWashington]] demonstrates how slaveholding, revolution against Britain, diplomatic gift-giving, and Anglo-American reconciliation can occupy the same monument.
- [[EdithCavell|Edith Cavell's]] memorial carries partially conflicting languages of patriotic sacrifice and moral restraint, making layered meaning explicit rather than accidental.

## Key Quotes

> "patriotism is not enough" - Cavell's final-night principle as discussed through the memorial inscription.

> "Peccavi" - the Latin pun popularly but incorrectly attributed to Napier after the conquest of Sindh.
