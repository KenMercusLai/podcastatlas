# 6. Troy

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode uses [[Troy]] to connect the basic Trojan War story—[[HelenOfTroy|Helen]], [[ParisOfTroy|Paris]], [[Achilles]], [[Hector]], and the [[TrojanHorse]]—to the qualified historical question of whether [[Homer]] preserves memory of a real Bronze Age conflict. [[HeinrichSchliemann]] and possible Hittite correspondences make a historical setting plausible without proving the epic plot or identifying its heroes.

The wider argument is about [[TrojanWarCulturalAfterlife|Troy's cultural afterlife]]. Ancient rulers, Greek historians and tragedians, Roman and British origin traditions, [[MehmedII]], modern war memory, and film repeatedly turn Troy into a story about East and West, ancestry, prestige, cunning, heroic glory, pointless suffering, and sympathy for the defeated.

## Key Claims

- Hisarlik is widely treated as archaeological Troy, but that does not establish that Homer's war happened as narrated.
- Possible links among Wilusa and Ilium, Alexandu and Alexander/Paris, and Ahhiyawa and Achaeans are suggestive Hittite-era correspondences rather than conclusive identifications.
- [[Herodotus]] and [[Thucydides]] already offered rival causal accounts: reciprocal abductions and disproportionate retaliation versus power, wealth, and strategic interest.
- The [[TheIliad|Iliad]] gains force from the gap between aristocratic heroic ideals and petty behavior, grief, destruction, and the defeated side's suffering.
- [[GreekTragedy]] makes Trojan women and other defeated people central, preventing Greek victory from becoming an uncomplicated triumph story.
- Greeks, Romans, medieval Britons, and Ottomans could claim or visit Troy to authorize geopolitical, ancestral, or imperial identities.
- Modern comparison with the First and Second World Wars shows how Troy can support both moral-heroic and futile-catastrophic models of war memory.
- Film can keep Troy culturally alive but may also flatten ancient religious and moral difference when it removes the gods in pursuit of modern realism.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode reinforces the wiki's existing middle position: Bronze Age language, sites, and diplomatic names can preserve historical residue without authenticating Homer's characters or narrative.
- Hisarlik's identification as Troy is presented as broad archaeological consensus, while the exact historical conflict, Hittite name equivalences, trade-route explanation, and continuity from Bronze Age events to Homer remain qualified or source-scoped.
- The episode's claims about ancient, medieval, Ottoman, Christian, and modern responses describe influential readings, not uniform beliefs within any period or culture.
