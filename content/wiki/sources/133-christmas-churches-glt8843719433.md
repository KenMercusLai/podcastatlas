# 133. Christmas churches

## Summary

This festive [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode invites [[RachelMorley]] of [[FriendsOfFriendlessChurches]] to build a deliberately tenuous top ten of British churches connected to Christmas. The route moves through sacred music, parish charity, [[MRJames|M. R. James]] ghost stories, [[CharlesDickens|Dickens]], self-raising flour, [[SaintNicholas]], Scottish bans on Christmas, medieval calendar calculation, *Sir Gawain and the Green Knight*, nativity paintings, and seasonal folk customs.

Its strongest synthesis is [[ChurchCulturalMemory]]: churches preserve social history through buildings, furnishings, inscriptions, graves, art, ritual, literature, film locations, and local stories as well as through worship. The episode's humor depends on weak or sideways Christmas connections, so individual anecdotes and attributions are better treated as prompts for heritage interpretation than as independently verified historical findings.

## Key Claims

- Parish churches preserve charitable history through benefaction boards and alms boxes, including Christmas food, clothing, and poor relief.
- Church manuscripts, stained glass, graves, and antiquarian settings shaped [[MRJames|M. R. James]]'s Christmas ghost-story tradition.
- [[CharlesDickens]]'s *A Christmas Carol* acquired a material afterlife through churches and screen adaptation, including the Scrooge prop gravestone retained at St Chad's, Shrewsbury.
- The Winchester Cathedral font uses [[SaintNicholas]] miracle scenes to preserve a medieval saint tradition later connected linguistically and culturally to Santa Claus.
- Scottish and English attempts to suppress Christmas show that seasonal observance was contested by church and state rather than continuously or uniformly practiced.
- A medieval Sunday-letter dial, possible Green Chapel landscapes, and early nativity wall paintings show churches and church-adjacent sites preserving calendar science, literary geography, and sacred visual culture.
- Mari Lwyd, hoodening, animal-head customs, concealed horse skulls, and tinsel broaden the episode from formal worship to regional folklore and material custom.
