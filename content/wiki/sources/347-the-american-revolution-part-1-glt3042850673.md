# 347: The American Revolution (Part 1)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode opens its [[AmericanRevolution|American Revolution]] sequence with historian [[AdamIPSmith|Adam I. P. Smith]], tracing the crisis from prosperous, locally self-governing colonies through the [[SevenYearsWar|Seven Years' War]], western-settlement conflict, postwar debt, new customs enforcement, and the [[BostonMassacre|Boston Massacre]]. It argues that independence was not inevitable in 1770: colonists still sought practical self-government and English liberties within the empire, while British ministers treated taxation, troops, and administrative reform as reasonable consequences of victory and imperial defense.

The episode's central explanation is a collision between [[PostwarImperialFiscalSecurityBind|postwar imperial fiscal and security pressures]] and [[ImperialConstitutionalMismatch|incompatible constitutional assumptions]]. Britain defended parliamentary sovereignty and expected under-taxed colonies to help fund defense; many colonists treated taxation without their own consent, juryless customs proceedings, and a standing military presence as violations of inherited rights. Economic stress, political suspicion, boycotts, press networks, and crowd violence then made each side's logic look illegitimate to the other.

## Key Claims

- The thirteen mainland colonies were prosperous, culturally diverse, and accustomed to extensive local participation before post-1763 imperial administration became more visible.
- The [[SevenYearsWar|Seven Years' War]] removed French power from much of North America but left Britain with debt, new territories, frontier conflict, and a larger military establishment to finance.
- The [[ProclamationLine1763|Proclamation Line of 1763]] exposed opposing strategic lessons: London wanted to restrain settlement and preserve Native alliances, while colonists including [[GeorgeWashington|George Washington]] saw western land as opportunity.
- British officials viewed the Sugar and Stamp Acts as ordinary revenue and enforcement measures, but colonists experienced customs courts, paperwork, taxation, and troops as infringements of English rights.
- [[ImperialConstitutionalMismatch|Imperial constitutional mismatch]] made compromise difficult because Parliament regarded its supremacy as non-negotiable while colonial petitions asked the king to override a legislature he could not constitutionally displace.
- Consumer boycotts, homespun production, press circulation, and intimidation made resistance socially broad and politically effective even when non-importation was leaky.
- The [[BostonMassacre|Boston Massacre]] condensed competing accounts of crowd violence, military self-defense, race, and propaganda; [[JohnAdams|John Adams]] nevertheless defended the soldiers through ordinary legal process.
- Independence remained contingent in 1770 because colonists generally sought self-taxation and practical autonomy inside the British Empire rather than a republic outside it.

## Key Quotes

> “talking past each other” - Smith's description of British and colonial argument from incompatible premises.

> “rights as Englishmen” - the constitutional language through which colonists opposed customs enforcement, taxation, and juryless proceedings.

> “not inevitable” - the episode's judgment on independence as of 1770.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode deepens the existing contingency account by showing that British policies could be administratively intelligible while still violating colonial expectations of consent, local government, and English legal rights.
- Colonial liberty rhetoric is qualified by Richard Henry Lee's use of enslaved people in anti-Stamp Act theater and by racialized descriptions of [[CrispusAttucks|Crispus Attucks]] during Adams's defense of the soldiers.
- The Great Awakening's causal weight, population and debt totals, regional political attitudes, individual motives, customs-court practice, boycott effectiveness, mob composition, and accounts of the Boston shooting remain source-scoped.
