# 3 如何称呼东西方的帝王：从“恺撒”到“官家”

## Summary

This [[WeirdHistoryPodcast|怪东西 Weird History]] episode compares sovereign titles, forms of address, and rulers' self-reference across Chinese, Roman, European, Persian, and Islamic settings. Its central synthesis is [[MonarchicalTitulatureAndAddress|monarchical titulature and address]]: 皇帝, 天子, Caesar, Augustus, shah, king, regent, 陛下, 官家, 朕, and the royal “we” are not interchangeable labels but compressed claims about ritual, dynasty, territory, office, hierarchy, and political legitimacy.

The episode also grounds [[InstitutionalTitleTranslation|institutional-title translation]]. Similar functions can justify a readable translation, but a familiar Chinese or European term can also conceal different constitutional histories. The source therefore compares function before vocabulary: who rules, through which office or territory, under what succession and ritual order, and how contemporaries actually address that authority.

## Key Claims

- “天子” and “皇帝” could emphasize different dimensions of Chinese rulership: Heaven-facing sacred and external order versus dynastic succession and administrative rule, without becoming two separate sovereign offices.
- Temple names, posthumous names, era names, and personal or reign identifiers formed overlapping Chinese naming systems; their prevalence and practical use changed over time.
- [[EraNameSystem|era names]] joined chronology to political authority, but repeated names, frequent changes, and retrospective naming complicate any simple ruler-to-label mapping.
- Caesar, Augustus, princeps, tsar, Charlemagne-derived king words, shah, sultan, emir, and bey show personal names, offices, ranks, and honorifics becoming sovereign titles through historical use rather than a universal designed hierarchy.
- European rulers could hold several territorial titles and different regnal numbers simultaneously, while Chinese sovereign naming more often combined one supreme office with temple, posthumous, and era-name systems.
- Indirect address such as 陛下, 殿下, Your Grace, Your Highness, and Your Majesty expresses hierarchy through place or dignity; the resemblance does not prove a shared origin or fixed one-to-one equivalence.
- Regency, retired-ruler status, female sovereignty, and heir titles show why translation must follow institutional function: regent need not mean 摄政王, a reigning queen is not a king's wife, and European abdication has no exact fixed equivalent to 太上皇.
- Rulers' self-reference—including 孤, 寡人, 朕, and the royal “we”—carried historically changing political and ritual implications rather than a timeless scale of humility or majesty.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode reinforces [[EraNameSystem|年号制度]] by distinguishing early retrospective naming, Wang Mang and Liu Xiu's compound usage, later one-era reigns, and the political meaning of chronological choice.
- Its broader regency and translation claims reinforce [[34-congxiongyali-zhanshen-dao-daqing-modai-jiepanxia-dongxifang-de-shezhengwang-lkuu3qwavvb1yrc81cxgwwoq5y9g|episode 34]] without adding a new judgment about whether formal title becomes effective governing capacity.
- The sacred-versus-administrative contrast between 天子 and 皇帝 is an analytical emphasis, not proof that either title had one stable function across every dynasty.
- Etymologies, seal uses, accession rites, regnal numbering, forms of address, private imperial self-reference, title chronologies, and individual examples remain episode-attributed pending specialist verification.
- The episode explicitly marks several claims as uncertain or disputed, including formal Qing address, Zhougong's royal status, the origin of 官家, and whether Chinese emperors always used 朕 in private.
- Cross-civilizational similarities in indirect address, sacred kingship, regency, or ruler plural do not establish common origin or institutional equivalence; translation may amplify the appearance of symmetry.
