# Essentials: Nicotine's Effects on the Brain & Body & How to Quit Smoking or Vaping

## Summary
This condensed [[HubermanLab]] episode has [[AndrewHuberman]] separate nicotine's receptor, attention, arousal, reward, appetite, cardiovascular, dependence, and withdrawal effects from the additional harms of smoking, vaping, dipping, and snuffing. It restates [[NicotineNeurobiologyAndDependence]], [[ElectronicCigaretteRisk]], and [[SmokingCessationSupport]] from the longer [[nicotines-effects-on-the-brain-body-how-to-quit-smoking-or-vaping-scim2598262635|full episode]]: rapid delivery strengthens reinforcement, brief cognitive effects do not make nicotine a generally safe enhancer, and quitting may require structured behavioral and clinical support. Because this is an Essentials edit of the same material, it is overlapping provenance rather than independent confirmation.

## Key Claims
- Nicotine activates nicotinic acetylcholine receptors and recruits dopamine, acetylcholine, and norepinephrine systems associated with transient motivation, focus, alertness, and reinforcement.
- Nicotine can suppress appetite, modestly raise metabolism, increase heart rate and blood pressure, and constrict blood vessels; these effects complicate any cognitive-enhancement framing.
- Faster nicotine and dopamine delivery can strengthen reinforcement, making high-speed delivery through vaping especially concerning for dependence.
- Smoking, vaping, dipping, and snuffing add route-specific toxicant, vascular, pulmonary, oral, cardiovascular, and cancer risks beyond nicotine's own pharmacology.
- Repeated exposure can lower the compensated baseline and produce craving and withdrawal when nicotine is removed, with the first week presented as a particularly vulnerable relapse period.
- Clinical hypnosis, bupropion, nicotine-replacement products, exercise, cold exposure, and positive social contact are discussed as cessation supports, but medication choice, contraindications, dosing, and combinations require qualified care.
