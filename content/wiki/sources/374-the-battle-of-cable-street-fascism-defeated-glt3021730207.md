# 374. The Battle of Cable Street: Fascism Defeated

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode treats the [[BattleOfCableStreet|Battle of Cable Street]] as both a successful blockade and a simplified political memory. A broad anti-fascist coalition prevented [[OswaldMosley|Oswald Mosley]] and the [[BritishUnionOfFascists|British Union of Fascists]] from marching through the Jewish East End, but the main street fighting was between demonstrators and police, while BUF membership, meetings, and antisemitic violence did not immediately collapse.

The episode places the confrontation inside a longer account of [[BritishFascism|British fascism]]. Mosley turned from failed predictions of democratic and capitalist collapse toward small-trader resentment, protectionism, explicit antisemitism, and later ultra-appeasement; wartime Regulation 18B detention, rather than Cable Street alone, finally broke the movement's momentum.

## Key Claims

- Cable Street blocked the planned 4 October 1936 march, but the episode rejects the retrospective claim that it immediately defeated British fascism.
- Police separated the opposing groups, so most direct fighting involved police and anti-fascist demonstrators rather than fascists and anti-fascists meeting one another in battle.
- Mosley adopted explicit antisemitism opportunistically while directing economic resentment against chain stores, Jewish businesses, immigrants, and alleged international capitalism.
- Local BUF activity and estimated London membership rose after Cable Street, and Blackshirts attacked Jewish businesses in Stepney the following weekend.
- Late-1930s fascist campaigning recast the movement as an anti-war and ultra-appeasement force, showing how extremist politics could travel through peace, anti-establishment, and anti-elite rhetoric.
- [[WilliamJoyce|William Joyce]] left for Germany and broadcast as Lord Haw-Haw, whereas Mosley stayed in Britain, opposed war over Poland, and was interned in May 1940 under expanded emergency powers.
- Britain's democratic resilience, uneven interwar economy, and public distaste for continental fascist violence constrained the BUF even when localized support and street violence remained serious.

## Key Quotes

> “They shall not pass” - the anti-fascist slogan through which Cable Street entered public memory.

> “the peace party” - the hosts' description of the BUF's late-1930s effort to present ultra-appeasement as opposition to war.

## Contradictions

- The episode qualifies the heroic public memory that Cable Street itself stopped British fascism: the march was blocked, but BUF activity and antisemitic violence persisted and may have grown locally afterward.
- This does not contradict the wiki's judgment that British fascism remained electorally marginal; it distinguishes national electoral weakness from temporary membership growth, neighborhood organization, and capacity for harm.
- Crowd sizes, membership estimates, Mosley's motive for adopting antisemitism, Edward VIII's sympathies, Regulation 18B's fairness, hypothetical occupation governments, and claims about Hitler's private view of Mosley remain source-scoped.
