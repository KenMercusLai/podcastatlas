## Summary
This [[HiddenBrain]] episode has Stanford economist [[NickBloom]] use the randomized [[Ctrip]] home-working experiment and pandemic-era evidence to replace the binary office-versus-home question with a task-, worker-, and outcome-specific frame. Remote call-center employees produced more, quit less often, and cost less, yet they also experienced isolation and received fewer promotions after performance was controlled.

The durable conclusion is conditional rather than universal. Routine individual work can benefit from home, while creativity, mentoring, relationship-building, and complex coordination often benefit from physical proximity; choice, suitable space, reliable connectivity, fair evaluation, and a limited hybrid schedule shape whether flexibility works.

## Key Claims
- Before COVID-19, remote work remained culturally suspect even though broadband, laptops, and video calling had made it technically feasible; physical presence often substituted for meaningful performance measurement.
- [[Ctrip]] randomly assigned volunteers either to work from home four days per week for nine months or to remain in the office, with remote staff returning for a fifth day of meetings and training.
- Home-based Ctrip employees were reported as 13% more productive; roughly one-third of the gain came from greater output per minute, while the rest reflected more minutes worked.
- The source reports about $2,000 in annual company savings per remote employee and a quit-rate decline from roughly 50% to 25%.
- Roughly half of the remote experimental group later chose to return to the office, with loneliness, depression, distraction, and an overly intensive four-day schedule offered as explanations.
- After performance was controlled, remote workers were promoted at about half the rate of office workers, suggesting that visibility, informal learning, and managerial memory affect advancement separately from measured output.
- Bloom distinguishes routine work that can continue established processes remotely from creative, innovative, and large-team work whose coordination may weaken without in-person interaction.
- Worker preferences were widely dispersed, and the average preference of about two home days per week concealed meaningful differences in housing, household conditions, loneliness, and task needs.
- Remote-work access is unequal: the source reports that only 65% of surveyed Americans had internet suitable for a high-quality video call and 49% had a separate non-bedroom work room, while many retail, construction, and manufacturing jobs could not move online.
- Pandemic normalization, employer experience, home-office investment, and labor-market competition made some lasting increase in remote work likely, but forecasts about cities, migration, and property conversion remained more speculative.

## Key Quotes
> "choice and flexibility" - the two requirements Bloom emphasizes for sustainable remote-work policy.

> "three great enemies" - Bloom's label for the bed, refrigerator, and television as home-working distractions.

> "two remote days per week" - the approximate average preference reported in the episode, not a universal optimum.

## Contradictions
- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content. The episode strengthens the existing job-specific and hybrid interpretation while adding direct tensions between measured productivity, employee preference, career advancement, and social connection.
- The 13% productivity result comes from measurable Ctrip call-center work and should not be generalized to creative, collaborative, or poorly measured roles.
- Pandemic remote work with school closures and improvised workspaces is not equivalent to a designed hybrid arrangement with equipment, childcare, and voluntary participation.
- Productivity, savings, quit rates, promotion rates, survey percentages, newspaper counts, and forecasts are episode-reported because the supplied document is a structured summary rather than the underlying studies or a verbatim transcript.
