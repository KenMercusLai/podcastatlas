## Summary
This [[WhatsNextKejiZaozhidao|What's Next｜科技早知道]] interview with [[HanShengXiaoying|韩晟]] of [[XiaoyingTechnology|小影科技]] distinguishes agents from tools by their responsibility for understanding a goal, making domain judgments, organizing execution, and delivering a reviewable result. Using consumer and small-business video as the main case, it argues that [[IterativeCreativeAgent|creative agents]] must clarify vague intent through editable drafts, while [[GeneralVerticalAgentCollaboration|general and vertical agents]] can divide context stewardship from professional execution. The episode also connects vertical opportunity to complex online-offline workflows, founder capability fit, rapid demand tests, model economics, and [[CulturalTrendLocalization|local cultural feedback]].

## Key Claims
- An agent product absorbs work that a tool leaves to the user: interpreting the objective, making professional choices, coordinating steps, checking the result, and escalating consequential actions for approval.
- General agents may serve as context-rich interfaces or “housekeepers,” while vertical agents behave more like specialist teams; frequency, attention cost, and task complexity determine whether users work through the general entrance or directly with a specialist.
- Consumer creative intent is often latent rather than fully verbalized, so a useful video agent should produce a first draft, observe reactions, and support local revisions instead of repeatedly regenerating the whole work.
- Product evaluation should measure the distance from an idea to a satisfactory artifact, including continuation after the first draft and revision behavior, rather than generation success alone.
- Building or post-training a proprietary model is most defensible when it controls a core experience or produces a meaningful cost advantage; the reported one-tenth compute result is a company claim without a disclosed external benchmark.
- Vertical opportunities are strongest where users cannot reliably decompose a complex task and where execution includes workflow, authorization, service operations, or physical-world follow-through.
- Localization requires current cultural taste, creators, and behavioral data in addition to language and static cultural knowledge; demand can be tested with demonstrations and explicit request signals before a complete product exists.
