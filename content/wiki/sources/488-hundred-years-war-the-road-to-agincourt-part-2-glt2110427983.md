# 488. Hundred Years' War: The Road to Agincourt (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[HenryVOfEngland|Henry V]] from the August 1415 landing and costly siege of [[Harfleur]] to the moment his depleted army sees the French force blocking the road near [[BattleOfAgincourt|Agincourt]]. It explains the campaign through [[CampaignLogisticsBattlefieldSelection|campaign logistics and battlefield selection]]: late arrival, siege delay, disease, limited food, guarded crossings, damaged causeways, weather, and the French attempt to concentrate beyond the Somme turned an intended eight-day march to Calais into a forced approach to battle.

The episode also presents Henry's restrictions on plunder as both religious conduct and political communication. Because he claimed to be France's rightful king, protection of churches, civilians, crops, and buildings supported [[ConquestDisciplineLegitimacy|conquest discipline as legitimacy]], even as Harfleur's bombardment, harsh surrender terms, expulsions, hunger, and a public hanging reveal the coercion and limits inside that claim.

## Key Claims

- Henry landed later in the campaigning season than was ideal, while French surprise allowed the English to complete an initially vulnerable three-day landing without opposition.
- Harfleur's weak defenses did not produce a quick victory: artillery damage, improvised repairs, marsh conditions, disease, and delay reduced the English army and compromised the wider campaign.
- Henry restricted pillage and attacks on churches, clergy, women, crops, and buildings because religious duty and a claim to rule France made disciplined conquest politically useful.
- Harfleur surrendered only after no relief army arrived; Henry gained a port but imposed imprisonment, expulsion, and other harsh terms on a town already damaged by siege.
- Henry chose an inland march toward Calais rather than return home with a costly but limited success, carrying only eight days of food and large arrow supplies while leaving cannon and sick soldiers behind.
- French commanders used the Somme as an operational barrier by guarding fords, destroying bridges and causeways, and forcing the English away from Calais and beyond their planned provisions.
- The English eventually crossed at two unguarded fords, but French concentration beyond the river and the army's hunger, exhaustion, and exposure brought the campaign toward Agincourt.
- French factional politics shaped attendance and command: the king and Dauphin stayed away, the Duke of Orléans represented royal authority, and [[JohnTheFearless|John the Fearless]] did not join the main force.

## Key Quotes

> "rightful king of France" - the political claim used to explain Henry's restrictions on plunder.

> "a major gamble" - the episode's framing of the march from Harfleur toward Calais.

> "forty miles from Calais" - the army's approximate distance from safety when the French blocked its route near Agincourt.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with the existing Agincourt or post-Agincourt pages was found. This source explains how the English army reached the battlefield in the weakened state described by part three.
- Reports of enormous French numbers in William Bardolph's warning are preserved as contemporary alarm, not accepted as reliable force totals.
- The suggestion that Henry wanted battle as divine validation, and the suggestion that John the Fearless stayed away hoping for an Armagnac defeat, remain interpretive rather than established motive.
- Garrison size, route distance, exact chronology, individual intentions, and some reported acts of discipline remain source-scoped podcast claims rather than independently audited conclusions.
