# 679. Germany: The Song Hitler Stole (Part 3)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode explains why the [[GermanNationalAnthem|German national anthem]] is both familiar and widely misunderstood. The hosts trace *Das Lied der Deutschen* from [[JosefHaydn]]'s Habsburg imperial tune and [[AugustHeinrichHoffmannVonFallersleben]]'s liberal nationalism through the [[WeimarRepublic]], [[NaziGermany]], [[WestGermany]], [[EastGermany]], reunification, and football culture.

The episode's strongest wiki contribution is the German case of [[SelectiveAnthemCanonization]]: modern Germany keeps Haydn's tune and Hoffmann's song but officially centers the third verse, "Einigkeit und Recht und Freiheit," while the first verse remains historically burdened by Nazi use and postwar convention. It deepens [[NationalAnthemPoliticalPlasticity]] and [[RegimeSymbolContinuity]] by showing that continuity can depend on narrowing what part of a symbol is publicly singable.

## Key Claims

- The modern [[GermanNationalAnthem]] is not the first verse beginning "Deutschland über alles" but the third verse of *Das Lied der Deutschen*, centered on unity, rights, and freedom.
- [[JosefHaydn]] composed the tune in 1797 as an Austrian imperial song after hearing "God Save the King" in London.
- [[AugustHeinrichHoffmannVonFallersleben]] wrote *Das Lied der Deutschen* in 1841 on Heligoland as a liberal-nationalist appeal for German unity above local state loyalties.
- The first verse's geographic language reflected a vague 1841 map of German-speaking regions, but later history made it easier to hear as expansionist.
- The [[WeimarRepublic]] made the song official in August 1922 under [[FriedrichEbert]], who emphasized the third verse as a liberal-democratic unity formula.
- [[NaziGermany]] kept the anthem but shifted political attention toward the first verse and paired public ceremony with the [[HorstWesselLied]].
- [[WestGermany]] restored *Das Lied der Deutschen* through a compromise associated with [[KonradAdenauer]] and [[TheodorHeuss]]: only the third verse would be sung, while the first two verses were not legally banned.
- The 1954 World Cup anthem incident and the 2006 World Cup singing debate show how [[FIFAWorldCup|World Cup]] football tested whether German patriotism could become publicly normal without reviving older associations.
- [[EastGermany]] adopted [[AuferstandenAusRuinen]], whose united-fatherland language later became awkward after the Berlin Wall and the acceptance of two German states.
- After reunification, Germany kept the third verse rather than combining Haydn's tune with East German lyrics or adopting [[Kinderhymne]].

## Key Quotes

> "Deutschland über alles" - first-verse phrase whose meaning shifted sharply across political contexts.

> "Einigkeit und Recht und Freiheit" - third-verse formula now central to the official anthem.

> "Auferstanden aus Ruinen" - East Germany's anthem and a later reunification alternative.
