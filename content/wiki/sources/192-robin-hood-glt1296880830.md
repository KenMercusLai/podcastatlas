# 192. Robin Hood

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode separates the medieval [[RobinHoodLegend|Robin Hood]] of ballad, play, devotion, trickery, and violence from the noble, romantic, patriotic, and philanthropic hero familiar from later books and screen adaptations. The earliest material presents a yeoman outlaw whose courtesy to selected people coexists with killing, mutilation, disguise, and hostility to corrupt officials and clerics; it does not securely contain the complete modern formula of a dispossessed aristocrat loyal to Richard the Lionheart who robs the rich to give to the poor.

The episode treats that difference as [[RobinHoodLegendAccretion|legend accretion]] rather than a simple true-versus-false split. May games, Anthony Munday, Joseph Ritson, Walter Scott, Thomas Love Peacock, Howard Pyle, children's publishing, Hollywood, television, and modern politics successively join Maid Marian, aristocratic birth, Saxon resistance, romantic love, philanthropy, visual costume, and ideological meaning to an older outlaw tradition. No single legal-record candidate is accepted as the historical Robin Hood; the strongest conclusion is that a widely circulating literary and folk hero may have absorbed names, incidents, and social meanings from broader outlaw culture.

## Key Claims

- The earliest surviving Robin Hood material discussed includes *Piers Plowman*, *Robin Hood and the Monk*, early plays, and *A Gest of Robin Hood*, placing a recognizable outlaw tradition in circulation by the late medieval period.
- Medieval Robin is a yeoman, trickster, Marian devotee, and violent outlaw rather than the consistently charitable nobleman of modern family versions.
- The *Gest* calls Robin a friend of poor men and includes aid to an exploited knight, but the clean maxim “rob from the rich and give to the poor” is not a secure medieval or Tudor program.
- Maid Marian, the Earl of Huntingdon identity, Loxley, Richard-the-Lionheart chronology, Saxon-versus-Norman politics, romantic love, and the familiar philanthropic mission entered or consolidated at different later stages.
- Joseph Ritson and Walter Scott gave the legend radical, antiquarian, aristocratic, and national frames; Howard Pyle and twentieth-century screen versions made the resulting package especially portable for children and American mass culture.
- Legal records contain possible candidates and show “Robin Hood” becoming a generic outlaw label, but none supplies a decisive biographical identification.
- French pastoral, courtly, and woodland traditions, English outlaw culture, Marian devotion, May ritual, and later political needs may all have contributed without proving one origin theory.
