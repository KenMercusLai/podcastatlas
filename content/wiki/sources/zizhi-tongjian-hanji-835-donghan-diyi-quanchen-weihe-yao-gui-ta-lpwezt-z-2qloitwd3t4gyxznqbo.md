# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》835丨东汉第一权臣，为何要跪他？

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode backfills the 151 CE [[EmperorHuanOfHan|汉桓帝]] court layer immediately before [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-836-xiongdi-fanmu-beihou-ruzhe-he-jianchen-de-jiaofeng-lq9wjn5c8mwt-onrtslum4fw53eq|Hanji 836]] and [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-838-tanlan-de-youhuo-rang-ta-kuangbiao-zai-zuosi-de-lushang-lrydilyni6o6yzhk1-e74m6aigwr|Hanji 838]]. Its core scene has [[ZhangLingLateHan|张陵]] publicly enforce court law against [[LiangJiLateHan|梁冀]] after Liang enters the palace audience wearing a sword; Liang kneels, apologizes, and loses one year's salary by imperial order.

The source then reads the incident through ambition, jealousy, exposed cleverness, and political retreat. It recalls Liang Ji's poisoning of [[EmperorZhiOfHan|汉质帝]] after the child emperor called him "跋扈将军", then uses [[FanLi|范蠡]], [[WenZhongYue|文种]], and [[ZhangLiang|张良]] as examples for knowing when to withdraw from dangerous height.

## Key Claims

- In 151 CE, Yuanjia first year, first day of the first month, Liang Ji enters the palace audience with a jeweled sword despite the ritual and legal expectations of the court setting.
- Zhang Ling, serving as shangshu, publicly rebukes Liang Ji, orders Huben and Yulin guards to remove the sword, and turns the scene into a visible check on Liang's status.
- Liang Ji kneels and apologizes immediately, showing that even an overmighty outer-relative can be constrained when an official controls the formal occasion and names a clear procedural breach.
- Zhang Ling memorializes an impeachment and asks that Liang be sent to the Tingwei; Emperor Huan instead orders Liang to forfeit one year's salary to redeem the offense.
- The source frames the episode's opening jealousy lesson as source-scoped moral interpretation, not a full explanation of late-Han institutional collapse.
- Liang Ji's earlier poisoning of Emperor Zhi is recalled as the darker background behind his current ambition and sensitivity to humiliation.
- Emperor Zhi's "跋扈将军" remark is treated as a case where visible intelligence and bluntness can become lethal under a threatening powerholder.
- Kangxi's comment on Emperor Zhi, and the examples of Fan Li, Wen Zhong, and Zhang Liang, turn the source toward a lesson about hiding brilliance, knowing limits, and retreating before merit or status becomes danger.
- Zhang Ling's later answer to Liang Buyi separates private recommendation from public office: he claims that enforcing court law is the real way to repay past patronage.
