# 703. Roman Civil War: Marching on the Senate (Part 3)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode continues the Roman Civil War sequence from [[702-roman-civil-war-the-barbarian-invasion-part-2-glt3336491089|Part 2]] by narrating [[LuciusCorneliusSulla]]'s [[FirstMarchOnRome|first march on Rome]] after [[GaiusMarius]] and [[PubliusSulpiciusRufus]] strip him of the Mithridatic command. It follows Sulla's army-backed seizure of Rome, the outlawing of Marius and Sulpicius, the unstable oath imposed on [[LuciusCorneliusCinna]], and Sulla's departure for the eastern war against [[MithridatesVI]].

The episode then tracks the parallel breakdown of Rome and the eastern Mediterranean: [[GnaeusPompeiusStrabo]] keeps his legions as a bargaining asset, Marius returns from exile to join Cinna, [[GnaeusOctaviusRomanConsul]] is killed in office, [[Athens]] revolts under [[AristionAthens]], and Sulla sacks Athens before making a pragmatic settlement with Mithridates. Its strongest wiki contribution is to move the crisis from command-transfer threshold to open precedent: republican legality still matters rhetorically, but armies, outlawry, revenge, and foreign command now determine what legality can mean.

## Key Claims

- Sulla's 88 BC march is framed as the first time a Roman commander leads an army against Rome itself, making it a major precedent on the path from Republic to Empire.
- The march is both personal and structural: Sulla's ambition, hatred of Marius, confidence in fortune, and desire for the eastern command interact with longer campaigns, soldier loyalty, street violence, and weakened norms.
- Sulla legitimizes his coup by asking the Senate to declare Marius, Sulpicius, and allies public enemies, showing force and legality becoming inseparable.
- Cinna's later expulsion and outlawry repeat the same logic: political disagreement over Italian enfranchisement escalates into public-enemy status, army recruitment, and siege.
- Mithridates turns Asian resentment of Roman exploitation into propaganda, massacre, and alliance-building, including the revolt of Athens under Aristion.
- Sulla's eastern campaign restores Roman dominance but leaves Athens broken, Asia punished, and Mithridates treated relatively generously because Sulla needs to return to Italy.
- The episode ends with Rome's first full civil war imminent because Cinna's regime has condemned Sulla while Sulla still controls a victorious army.

## Key Quotes

> "Felix" - Sulla's self-image as a man favored by fortune.

> "public enemies" - the legal category used to turn political opponents into killable outlaws.

> "respect for the dead" - the logic Sulla gives for sparing Athens itself after sacking the city.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction found. The source resolves Part 2's open threshold by narrating Sulla's response to the stripped command: he violates the taboo against marching on Rome, then claims to defend the Republic through Senate sanction.
- The episode qualifies any simple legality-versus-rebellion split. Sulla, Marius, Cinna, Octavius, and the Senate all use legal forms, but the operative force increasingly comes from armies, outlawry, and the capacity to intimidate or kill opponents.
- Source-scope caution: the reported 80,000 deaths in the Asian massacre are treated as inflated but politically powerful because Romans believed the scale.
