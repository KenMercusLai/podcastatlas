# Way to Go, Ohio

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode has [[MalcolmGladwell]] connect [[DavidMarine]]'s 1917 school experiment in [[AkronOhio|Akron, Ohio]] to the population-level success of [[IodizedSaltPopulationPrevention]]. Marine's carefully dosed iodine reportedly prevented goiter after earlier excessive dosing had caused serious harm; salt then became a cheap, stable, and nearly imperceptible vehicle for making small amounts of iodine broadly available.

Gladwell and [[SeemaSagayar]] use that history for an explicitly impossible “magic wand” experiment about COVID vaccination. Comparing injection, optional vaccine-fortified salt, and salt without a practical opt-out, they ask whether changing an intervention's form, label, convenience, and choice architecture could reduce resistance. [[InterventionDeliveryFraming]] preserves the episode's central limit: easier behavior may not represent informed acceptance, and concealment or near-unavoidability can replace needle resistance with stronger objections about autonomy, consent, and institutional trust.

## Key Claims

- Inland and formerly glaciated regions had iodine-poor soil and diets, contributing to endemic goiter and severe developmental harm in parts of the American Midwest.
- Earlier iodine studies reportedly used doses far above what was needed and caused hyperthyroidism, arrhythmias, and deaths, making dose and delivery central to public acceptance as well as medical safety.
- [[DavidMarine]]'s Akron school experiment is presented as decisive evidence that carefully administered iodine could prevent goiter.
- Salt was an effective population vehicle because it was widely consumed, required only a small iodine addition, and did not perceptibly change ordinary taste.
- [[SeemaSagayar]] says vaccine-hesitancy research had not adequately isolated whether delivery form, including injection, contributes to resistance.
- The episode hypothesizes that routine, convenient consumption and “supplement” framing could change behavior without first changing beliefs.
- The imagined three-arm comparison—no-choice fortified salt, choice between fortified and unfortified salt, and injection—would test form and choice together rather than form alone.
- The proposal is scientifically unavailable and ethically fraught: incomplete disclosure and practical inability to opt out could create coercion, backlash, and distrust even if uptake increased.
