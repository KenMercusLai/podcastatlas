# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》228｜周亚夫将文帝拒于营外，刘恒的表现令人意外

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode places the celebrated Xiliu inspection inside the 158 BCE [[Xiongnu|匈奴]] emergency. While the Bashang and Jimen camps admit the imperial party without resistance, [[ZhouYafu|周亚夫]]'s camp keeps weapons ready, initially refuses the emperor's advance party and then [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] himself, opens only after formal notification, prohibits galloping, and receives the ruler with military rather than kneeling court ritual.

Liu Heng responds by treating the restrictions as evidence of real preparedness rather than personal disrespect, calling Zhou a true general and later appointing him commandant of the capital. The episode then turns from frontier defense to drought and locust relief: suspended tribute, opened mountain-and-water access, reduced palace expenditure and staffing, granary relief, and renewed grain-for-rank exchange extend the ruler's burden-limiting profile. The durable synthesis is that [[WartimeCampCommandAuthority|战备营垒指挥权]] becomes credible when even the sovereign accepts its procedures; exact chronology, offices, dialogue, causal appointment claim, and relief effects remain source-scoped.

## Key Claims

- In 158 BCE, two Xiongnu forces reportedly enter Shang and Yunzhong commanderies, kill civilians, and push beacon warnings toward Ganquan and the capital.
- Liu Heng assigns generals to Feihu, Gouzhu, Beidi, Xiliu, Bashang, and Jimen as a layered response to frontier and capital-security risk.
- Bashang and Jimen admit the imperial convoy freely, while Xiliu remains armed, quiet, and closed even after the advance party announces the emperor.
- Xiliu officers say the army obeys the general's command rather than an unprocedural imperial announcement; Liu Heng enters only after sending a formal envoy with the imperial tally.
- The camp gate officer enforces the no-galloping rule, Zhou receives the emperor in armor using military ritual, and Liu Heng answers with solemn respect rather than punishment.
- Liu Heng contrasts Zhou with the more permeable camps, calls him a true general, and later appoints him 中尉 after the Xiongnu withdraw.
- During subsequent drought and locust damage, Liu Heng reportedly suspends princely tribute, opens restricted natural resources, reduces palace consumption and officials, releases grain, and again permits grain contributions in return for rank.

## Key Quotes

> “军中闻将军令，不闻天子之诏。” - the camp officer's reported boundary between field command and an unprocessed imperial arrival.

> “此真将军矣。” - Liu Heng's reported judgment after leaving Xiliu.

## Connections

- [[ZhouYafu|周亚夫]] - commander whose Xiliu defenses add the earliest operational layer to his current profile.
- [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] - ruler who accepts military procedure against his own immediate convenience and later uses burden-reduction measures during drought and locust damage.
- [[WartimeCampCommandAuthority|战备营垒指挥权]] - principle that a camp under active threat needs a controlled entry chain and internally credible command rules.
- [[Xiongnu|匈奴]] - external threat that makes the capital-area deployment and strict camp posture intelligible.
- [[WenJingProsperityTradeoff|文景之治的繁荣代价]] - recovery frame extended by a specific late-Wen disaster response that reduces extraction and court use.
- [[FrugalImperialBurialRestraint|节俭帝陵约束]] - later death-governance parallel to the same source's crisis-time reduction of court burden.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The Xiliu episode supplies an earlier basis for Zhou's military reputation before the Seven States campaign recorded in Hanji 234.
- The sequence qualifies, rather than overturns, Liu Heng's existing profile: willingness to respect military procedure and reduce disaster burdens coexists with his selective patronage of Deng Tong and earlier susceptibility to omen claims.
- The episode interprets the Xiliu restrictions as preparedness and the Bashang/Jimen reception as vulnerability, but it supplies no independent readiness audit or combat outcome for the three camps.
- Dates, place names, force and casualty numbers, quoted commands, military ritual, travel details, the causal link between the inspection and appointment, tribute scope, access rules, staffing reductions, relief quantities, and grain-for-rank implementation require comparison with transmitted and modern historical evidence.
