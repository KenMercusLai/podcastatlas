# 85. Sherlock Holmes

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode treats [[SherlockHolmes|Sherlock Holmes]] as both a fictional detective and a historical object that shaped later images of Victorian and Edwardian Britain. It connects [[ArthurConanDoyle|Arthur Conan Doyle]]'s medical training, literary ambition, public life, spiritualism, and justice campaigns to Holmes's scientific method, London atmosphere, imperial anxieties, serial readership, and visual afterlife.

Its central contribution is [[SherlockHolmesVictorianCulturalMyth]]: Holmes made modern urban disorder appear solvable through observation and deduction while turning Baker Street, fog, gaslight, empire, crime, and elite competence into a globally reusable image of Britain. The episode also preserves the tension between Holmes's rationalism and Doyle's willingness to accept spiritualist and fairy evidence.

## Key Claims

- [[JosephBell]]'s diagnostic observation at Edinburgh supplied an acknowledged model for Holmes, although Doyle transformed a medical teacher into a fictional detective rather than copying him directly.
- Doyle preferred historical novels such as *The White Company* and *Sir Nigel*, but mass literacy, short-story magazines, reader attachment, and the Strand market made Holmes his defining success.
- Holmes crystallized earlier detective traditions into a formula in which extraordinary observation and scientific deduction make urban mystery legible, often outclassing official authorities.
- [[SherlockHolmesVictorianCulturalMyth]] joins this rational competence to a mythic London of Baker Street, cabs, fog, gaslight, crime, class, and imperial reach.
- India, Afghanistan, American violence, stolen naval plans, and the approach of the First World War let the stories register fears that distant imperial or geopolitical dangers could return to metropolitan Britain.
- Doyle's campaigns for George Edalji and Oscar Slater extended his concern with wrongful conviction into public life, while his spiritualism and defense of the Cottingley fairy photographs exposed the limits of his own evidential judgment.
- [[SidneyPaget]]'s illustrations helped standardize Holmes's costume and silhouette, showing that serial art, adaptation, and fandom were central to the character's global recognizability.
- Moriarty's enlarged afterlife, the Baker Street Irregulars, parody, pastiche, stage and screen adaptation, and later Holmes-like characters show a fictional world becoming partly reader-owned.

## Key Quotes

> "You are yourself Sherlock Holmes" - the episode's account of Doyle acknowledging Joseph Bell as the detective's model.

> "the first superhero" - the listener prompt used to compare Holmes's exceptional intelligence with later non-supernatural heroes.

> "an east wind coming" - the episode's reference to Holmes anticipating the coming European war in “His Last Bow.”

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with the wiki was found. The episode strengthens the existing view that Holmes is simultaneously an evidence icon, publishing phenomenon, visual construction, and reusable cultural property.
- Claims about name origins, cricket influences, Holmes as the first superhero, imperial guilt, Doyle's motives, Piltdown involvement, and specific lines of influence remain conversational or source-scoped rather than established conclusions.
- Doyle's spiritualism does not invalidate the rational structure of the Holmes stories, while Holmes's fictional method does not establish that Doyle used the same evidential standards in every part of his life.
