# 684. Franklin: Revenge of the American Genius (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode presents [[BenjaminFranklin|Benjamin Franklin]] as the most famous 18th-century American and follows his movement from loyal English subject to revolutionary founder. It connects his Puritan family inheritance, self-made printing career, moral journalism, scientific celebrity, and London humiliation to the wider [[ColonialLoyaltyRupture|colonial loyalty rupture]] that produced the [[AmericanRevolution|American Revolution]].

The central synthesis is that Franklin's practical genius mattered most when image became statecraft. His calculated persona in [[France]], scientific reputation through the [[RoyalSociety]], and staged Enlightenment symbolism helped secure the [[FrancoAmericanAlliance1778|French alliance]], while his slaveholding, 1770 pro-slavery defense, 1790 abolition petition, and final satire make [[FoundingLibertySlaverySelfCorrection|founding liberty-slavery self-correction]] part of the same profile.

## Key Claims

- Franklin's 1770 lion-cub parable warned Britain that the colonies would not remain weak, prefiguring the political break he later helped make real.
- Franklin's self-made rise as printer, journalist, inventor, and moralist joined Puritan habits of discipline and improvement to [[PuritanEnlightenmentPracticalEthic|Enlightenment experimental usefulness]].
- His long attachment to Britain makes his later independence stance a case of [[ColonialLoyaltyRupture|colonial loyalty rupture]] rather than simple lifelong nationalism.
- His scientific reputation, including the lightning experiment and election to the [[RoyalSociety]], helped make America legible in Europe as inventive, practical, and modern.
- The [[ThomasHutchinson|Hutchinson letters]] and Franklin's public humiliation before the Privy Council's Cockpit accelerated his belief that Britain was moving toward repression.
- Franklin's work in the [[ContinentalCongress|Continental Congress]] and on the [[DeclarationOfIndependence|Declaration of Independence]], including the "self-evident" edit, made him a founding textual actor as well as a diplomat.
- His mission to France shows [[DiplomaticPersonaAsStrategicAsset|diplomatic persona as strategic asset]]: the fur cap, sage image, salon presence, and symbolic meeting with [[Voltaire]] helped make French support politically attractive.
- The French alliance and later [[TreatyOfParis1783|Treaty of Paris]] made Franklin indispensable to American victory and recognition.
- Franklin's record on slavery is contradictory: he owned enslaved people and defended slavery in 1770, then petitioned Congress against slavery in 1790 and published a final anti-slavery satire.

## Key Quotes
