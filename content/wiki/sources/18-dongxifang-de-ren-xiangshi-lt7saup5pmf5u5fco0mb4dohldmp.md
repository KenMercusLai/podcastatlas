# 18 东西方的人相食

## Summary

This [[WeirdHistoryPodcast|怪东西 Weird History]] episode treats cannibalism as a historically variable act rather than evidence of one people's inherent barbarity. War, famine, shipwreck, ritual mourning, revenge, intimidation, illness, medicinal belief, filial self-sacrifice, and literary metaphor can give superficially similar acts sharply different meanings. The source therefore grounds [[CannibalismContextualMeaning|the contextual meaning of cannibalism]] while rejecting unsupported rankings of civilizations, regions, or eras.

Its strongest synthesis is [[SurvivalCannibalismPowerInequality|survival cannibalism and power inequality]]. Famine chronicles and shipwreck law frame necessity as a problem of governance, morality, and criminal responsibility, but women, enslaved people, racialized minorities, children, the sick, and lower-status companions are often exposed first. A lottery can formalize equal risk only when participants have sufficiently equal standing and the procedure is genuine; crisis does not erase social hierarchy and may make it more violent. The closing reading of [[LuXun|鲁迅]]'s *Diary of a Madman* extends literal consumption into a critique of institutions and moral language that sacrifice vulnerable people while presenting the harm as necessary or noble.

## Key Claims

- Cannibalism has no single transhistorical meaning: survival, aggression, mourning, medicine, devotion, pathology, and political metaphor must be separated before comparison.
- Chinese chronicle phrases such as “人相食” can record extreme hunger while also assigning responsibility to failed relief, war, or disordered rule rather than merely condemning starving individuals.
- Siege and military narratives show that the moral praise attached to endurance or loyalty can obscure whose bodies were made available, especially those of women, dependents, enslaved people, and civilians.
- Stories of cutting flesh to heal or feed a parent developed from tension among bodily integrity, medicinal belief, Buddhist self-sacrifice, filial ethics, and official commendation rather than from one timeless custom.
- Fore mortuary practice, kuru, and disputed genetic interpretations of prehistoric consumption show why ritual meaning, disease transmission, and scientific inference must remain analytically distinct.
- Caribbean “cannibal” descriptions could be uncertain reports and also tools of [[CannibalismColonialJustification|colonial justification]], making evidentiary criticism inseparable from the label's political consequences.
- The *Mignonette* judgment rejects necessity as a defense to murder, while shipwreck examples show that lotteries and sacrifice rules can be distorted by rank, race, age, health, and force.
- [[LuXun|Lu Xun]]'s literary image of “eating people” turns physical consumption into a diagnosis of moralized social oppression and coerced participation.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode qualifies any account in which disaster simply strips away civilization: extreme scarcity can weaken ordinary restraint while preserving or intensifying rank, racialization, gender hierarchy, and control over procedure.
- The episode explicitly rejects claims that incomplete historical records can establish which region, people, or era practiced cannibalism most often.
- Particular ancient stories, reported customs, medical beliefs, dates, legal details, casualty patterns, and literary intentions remain episode-attributed pending specialist verification.
- The 2003 genetic argument for widespread prehistoric cannibalism and its 2008 defense are presented as disputed scientific interpretation, not as settled proof.
- Ritual mortuary practice, involuntary starvation, murder for survival, punitive consumption, medicinal use, and metaphor are not treated as morally or institutionally equivalent.
