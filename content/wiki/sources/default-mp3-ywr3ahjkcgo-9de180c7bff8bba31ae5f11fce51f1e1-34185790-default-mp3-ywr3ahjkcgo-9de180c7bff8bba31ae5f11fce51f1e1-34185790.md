# Inside the insurance industry’s destruction lab

## Summary
This [[PlanetMoney]] episode visits the [[InstituteForBusinessAndHomeSafety|Institute for Business and Home Safety]], where [[AnneCope]] and other researchers burn structures, generate hurricane-force winds, age roofs, and fire manufactured hailstones to test how buildings fail. It places the laboratory in a longer history of [[InsuranceFundedSafetyResearch]], from maritime underwriting and mutual fire insurance to product certification and automobile crash testing.

The strongest result is economic as well as technical: [[HomeFortificationEconomics]] can make relatively inexpensive construction changes prevent disproportionate damage, but [[CorrelatedCatastropheInsuranceRisk]] means a safer individual home may still face unaffordable premiums or unavailable coverage. [[JulieRochman]] presents the lab as a source of credible structural-resilience evidence, not a complete solution to climate exposure, neighborhood spillovers, insurer concentration, or household affordability.

## Key Claims
- The insurance industry funds IBHS because safer materials and construction can reduce claims, improve risk classification, inform prices and coverage, and reduce repeated rebuilding losses.
- In the lab's inaugural wind demonstration, a standard-code house was destroyed while a fortified house costing less than 5% more survived.
- Sealing a roof deck with a relatively inexpensive waterproof layer can prevent as much as 95% of wind-driven rain damage in the episode's account.
- Alabama combines fortification grants with required insurance discounts; the source says more than 50,000 residents have fortified roofs and reports better performance during Hurricane Sally.
- Wildfire protection requires a package of measures, including limiting flammable material near buildings, blocking embers at vents, and interrupting wooden-fence fire paths.
- Shared research can function as a semi-public good while remaining incomplete because member insurers receive fuller results and independently combine them with proprietary models.
- Physical resilience does not guarantee insurability when neighboring properties remain vulnerable or one insurer has too much geographically concentrated exposure.
