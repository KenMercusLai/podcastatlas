# 143. The Trial of Charles I Part 1

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode with [[TedVallance|Ted Vallance]] traces how religious fear, fiscal grievance, [[CharlesIOfEngland|Charles I]]'s resistance to criticism, military defeat, and his role in renewing civil war turned removal of a bad king into a revolutionary argument about monarchy itself. Charles's engagement with the Scots and the Second Civil War hardened army opinion, while the Remonstrance, Pride's Purge, and the Commons' declaration that the people were the source of just power created the coercive and ideological path to trial.

The episode presents legitimacy as the central unresolved problem. Parliament resisted the army's demand for prosecution, the Lords rejected the proposed court, the army claimed to embody the people after purging their representatives, and Charles refused to recognize any tribunal constituted without king, Lords, and an unpurged Commons. The public transformation of Westminster Hall therefore began as a contest over who could authorize judgment before the judges heard the case.

## Key Claims

- Religion was central to the Civil War: Charles's policies, episcopal enforcement, Catholic queen, punishments of critics, and European-style court culture made innovation appear to Puritan opponents as a route toward Catholic absolutism.
- Personal rule and ship money made apparently peaceful government look constitutionally aggressive, while Charles's intolerance of criticism and preference for managed harmony weakened compromise.
- After Naseby, Charles tried to recover authority by playing opponents against one another; his engagement with Scottish Covenanters helped produce the Second Civil War and convinced more army leaders that negotiated settlement was unsafe.
- Rank-and-file fears of prosecution and distrust of Parliament and senior officers helped radicalize the New Model Army, while the Putney debates opened questions of voting, political equality, and proceeding against the king.
- The Remonstrance demanded justice against Charles, Parliament resisted, and Pride's Purge used military force to remove or detain moderate MPs, making the route to trial effectively a coup.
- The Commons' declaration that the people were the origin of just power and that their representatives held supreme authority answered the Lords' veto but did not resolve whether a purged Commons or the army could credibly speak for the people.
- Charles's refusal to plead defended his theory of lawful monarchy and forced the prosecution to make jurisdiction, symbolism, and public staging central to the trial.

## Key Quotes

> "man of blood" - the biblical description Thomas Harrison applied to Charles when arguing that he should be proceeded against.

> "the people are, under God, the original of all just power" - the Commons' sovereignty claim as presented in the episode.
