# 634. Joan of Arc: Heroine in Chains (Part 3)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[JoanOfArc|Joan of Arc]] from the aftermath of Orléans through the Loire victories, [[CharlesVIIOfFrance|Charles VII's]] anointing at Reims, the failed assault on Paris, her independent campaigning, capture at Compiègne, and transfer toward English-controlled Rouen. It argues that Joan's sacred and chivalric image became a practical political-military force: reputation attracted men, weakened hostile resistance, damaged English morale, and made Charles's legitimacy publicly visible.

The episode also marks the limits of charisma. Charles lacked money and remained cautious, Paris did not fall, Burgundian diplomacy constrained the war, and Joan's completed mission left her without a secure court role. Her capture then allowed [[JohnOfLancasterDukeOfBedford|the Duke of Bedford]] and [[PierreCauchon|Pierre Cauchon]] to seek an ecclesiastical judgment capable of reversing the meaning of Reims.

## Key Claims

- Joan's white armor, banner, virgin identity, courage, and religious language combined martial and sacred authority in a form contemporaries found exceptional.
- The relief of Orléans made earlier claims credible enough to attract an army despite Charles's empty treasury and to authorize the Duke of Alençon to act on Joan's advice.
- Victories at Jargeau and Patay damaged the English reputation for invincibility, while Joan's reputation helped towns such as Troyes choose surrender over assault.
- Charles's anointing at Reims on 17 July 1429 converted military momentum into visible dynastic legitimacy, with Joan and her banner beside him.
- The Paris failure exposed a widening gap between Joan's continuing chivalric mission and Charles's fiscal, diplomatic, and strategic caution.
- Joan's independent company and failed sortie at Compiègne ended in Burgundian capture; Charles did not ransom her, while English authorities sought to turn captivity into a legitimacy weapon.
- Bedford and Cauchon joined English political need to ecclesiastical jurisdiction by arranging purchase, church trial, and charges including heresy, sorcery, and demonic influence.

## Key Quotes

> "Joan the Maid" - the identity by which the episode says Joan consistently described herself.

> "God's will was done" - the episode's summary of Joan's declaration after Charles's coronation at Reims.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. The episode qualifies structural explanations of English decline by treating Joan as an accelerator of a reversal enabled by France's deeper population and resources.
- Charles VII's relief at Joan's capture, Joan's enjoyment of command, Bedford's motives, and the archbishop of Reims's interpretation are presented as plausible readings rather than directly provable inner states.
- Troop and casualty figures, reported speeches and letters, the content of Joan's voices, the causes of town surrender, exact political motives, and the tower-fall details remain source-scoped.
