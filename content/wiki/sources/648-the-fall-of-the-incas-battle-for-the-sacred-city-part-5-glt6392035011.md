# 648. The Fall of the Incas: Battle for the Sacred City (Part 5)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[MancoInca]] from Spanish-backed ruler to leader of the 1536 uprising and [[SiegeOfCusco1536|siege of Cusco]]. It argues that encomienda expansion, plunder, sexual violence, factional rivalry, and repeated humiliation destroyed the possibility of accommodation, while Manco and [[VilacUmu]] used surviving Inca communications and mobilization capacity to assemble a vast army.

The assault nearly eliminates the Spanish position, but cavalry, steel, Indigenous auxiliaries, the recapture of Sacsayhuaman, seasonal dispersal, and reinforcements produce a stalemate. [[DiegoDeAlmagro]] then returns from Chile, fails to secure Manco's trust, takes Cusco from [[HernandoPizarro]] and [[GonzaloPizarro]], and helps drive Manco toward the independent [[NeoIncaState|Vilcabamba refuge]] developed in Part 6.

## Key Claims

- Manco's revolt followed cumulative Spanish extraction, sexual coercion, sacrilege, imprisonment, and public abuse rather than a sudden unexplained reversal.
- Inca institutions retained enough reach to transmit messages, stockpile supplies, and mobilize a reported force of about 100,000 people.
- The May 1536 assault brought Inca forces into Cusco and used incendiary sling stones effectively, but the defenders retained a small foothold and retook Sacsayhuaman.
- Spanish survival depended on armor, steel, cavalry, Indigenous allies, African labor, and overseas reinforcement rather than on a Spaniards-alone battlefield advantage.
- Manco's largely agrarian army could not sustain indefinite siege pressure, while mountainous terrain enabled [[QuisoYupanqui]] to destroy relief columns but favored Spanish cavalry near Lima.
- Almagro's return created a three-sided contest; his refusal to execute Spanish scouts convinced Manco that intra-Spanish rivalry did not make him a reliable ally.
- Withdrawal through the Sacred Valley toward Vilcabamba converted failed reconquest into a durable Inca state in exile.

## Key Quotes

> "greedy, violent occupiers" - the episode's synthesis of Manco's indictment of Spanish rule.

> "a final display of Inca organisational power" - the hosts' description of the mobilization at Lares.

## Connections

- [[MancoInca]], [[VilacUmu]], and [[QuisoYupanqui]] - political, religious, and military leadership of the uprising.
- [[SiegeOfCusco1536]], [[HernandoPizarro]], and [[GonzaloPizarro]] - assault, defense, and recapture of Sacsayhuaman.
- [[DiegoDeAlmagro]] and [[ConquistadorCivilWarPeru]] - returning Spanish faction that transformed the siege into a three-way conflict.
- [[SpanishConquestOfPeru]] and [[IndigenousAllianceInSpanishConquest]] - prolonged coalition war rather than a simple Spaniards-versus-Incas contest.
- [[EncomiendaColonialLaborSystem]] and [[NeoIncaState]] - extractive rupture and the political refuge that followed failed reconquest.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064|Part 6]] is adopted. Part 5 ends with the formation of Manco's Vilcabamba refuge; Part 6 follows its military, diplomatic, and dynastic afterlife.
- The episode's reported speeches, force totals, casualty numbers, exact motives, and reconstructed scenes remain source-scoped because the narrative relies heavily on Spanish and later chroniclers.
- The claim that African slaves, rather than divine intervention, saved the Spanish-held buildings is presented as the hosts' preferred account, not independently adjudicated here.
- Manco's idealization of pre-conquest Inca rule and the episode's emphasis on Spanish abuse do not erase earlier Inca coercion, internal rivalry, or Indigenous agency on both sides.
