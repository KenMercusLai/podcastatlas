# Burden of Proof

## Summary
This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode uses [[OwenThomas|Owen Thomas's]] death, posthumous CTE diagnosis, and [[ChrisNowinski|Chris Nowinski's]] explanation of the research to ask how much certainty institutions should require before acting against a potentially grave harm. [[MalcolmGladwell]] compares incomplete football evidence with Frederick Hoffman's 1918 coal-miner findings and argues that a demand for definitive causal proof can itself become a consequential policy choice. The resulting [[PrecautionaryActionUnderUncertainty]] case is forceful but bounded by [[ChronicTraumaticEncephalopathyEvidenceBoundary|selection bias, missing longitudinal comparison, and the inability to assign a single cause to an individual suicide]].

## Key Claims
- Owen was a highly regarded University of Pennsylvania football captain whose family observed a rapid deterioration before his 2010 death by suicide.
- Nowinski asked Owen's parents to preserve brain tissue, and the subsequent examination diagnosed CTE, giving the family a possible contributing factor without a complete causal answer.
- The episode associates CTE with repeated head impacts, tau accumulation, and cognitive, emotional, and behavioral symptoms, but acknowledges that donated-brain samples overrepresent families already concerned about impairment.
- Gladwell says a definitive comparison would require following similar football players and non-players over much of their lives, making timely certainty difficult to obtain.
- The coal-dust analogy argues that evidence can be decision-relevant before every causal question is settled, especially when the possible harm is severe and exposure continues.
- Penn continued football after Owen's diagnosis while adopting memorial gestures and later risk-reduction measures; Gladwell treats continued play as institutional moral indifference rather than an adequate precaution.
- Kyle Ambrose's earlier suicide and behavioral change add a disturbing parallel, but the absence of a brain examination means the episode does not establish that he had CTE.

## Key Quotes
> "We're not stopping football." - a Penn dean's reported response to Gladwell's challenge.

> "moral indifference" - Gladwell's description of continuing the football program despite unresolved danger.

## Connections
- [[OwenThomas]] - Penn captain whose death and posthumous diagnosis make the evidence question concrete.
- [[ChrisNowinski]] - researcher who requested tissue preservation and explains both the CTE concern and the limits of existing samples.
- [[ChronicTraumaticEncephalopathyEvidenceBoundary]] - separates repeated-impact association and postmortem pathology from representative prevalence and individual suicide causation.
- [[PrecautionaryActionUnderUncertainty]] - policy principle that severe credible risk can justify proportionate action before definitive proof.
- [[FootballPlayerSafetyTrustRisk]] - broader legitimacy and governance problem created when football institutions continue exposure amid disputed harm.
- [[MalcolmGladwell]] and [[RevisionistHistory]] - host and program advancing the coal-dust analogy and institutional critique.

## Contradictions
- No settled contradiction with the wiki was adopted. The episode strengthens [[FootballPlayerSafetyTrustRisk]] by moving from professional-league legitimacy to college-program duty and by distinguishing risk reduction from the question of whether play should continue.
- The 110-of-111 former-NFL-player brain-bank result is not a population prevalence estimate because donation was strongly selected by prior concern.
- Owen's diagnosis does not establish that CTE caused his depression or suicide, and Kyle's unexamined case cannot serve as medical evidence of CTE.
- The episode argues for stopping Penn football but does not compare the effectiveness of monitoring, reduced-contact practice, age restrictions, rule changes, or program closure.
- Medical descriptions, family recollections, research figures, institutional responses, and quoted dialogue remain episode-scoped because the supplied file is a structured summary rather than a transcript, autopsy report, cohort study, university record, or independent causal review.
