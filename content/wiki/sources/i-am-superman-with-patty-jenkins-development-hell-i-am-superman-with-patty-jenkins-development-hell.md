# I Am Superman with Patty Jenkins | Development Hell

## Summary

In this [[RevisionistHistory]] installment of *Development Hell*, [[MalcolmGladwell]] interviews [[PattyJenkins]] about [[IAmSupermanFilm|I Am Superman]], her unproduced R-rated film pairing an abused fighting dog with an incarcerated man in a prison-dog program. The project joins [[SecondChanceRecognitionFailure]] to an unconventional production design: Jenkins wanted a small crew, many shooting days, hidden digital cameras, and possibly trained incarcerated crew members so the dog could supply a genuine emotional performance.

Jenkins uses the film's failure to secure roughly $5 million after *Monster* to describe [[PrecedentLockedCreativeFinancing]]: the industry praised her execution of other people's projects while distrusting original work that lacked a familiar commercial precedent. She also connects her attraction to damaged outsiders, darkness, and romantic longing to childhood bereavement, while cautioning through her own range that biography informs rather than exhausts an artist's work.

## Key Claims

- *I Am Superman* follows Bandit, an abused fighting pit bull condemned after killing his trainer, and a man returned to prison who reluctantly takes responsibility for rehabilitating him.
- Jenkins rejects a conventional redemption arc in which the prisoner must first commit fresh violence; she treats the character as already changed but still trapped by other people's expectations.
- The prison's closure of the dog program and decision to kill Bandit make institutional refusal to recognize change, rather than individual moral conversion, the central conflict.
- Jenkins designed a patient, low-footprint production process around an authentic canine performance and considered filming in a real prison with selected incarcerated people trained for crew roles.
- Despite praise for the screenplay and the success of *Monster*, she could not secure the approximately $5 million she says the film required.
- Jenkins argues that backward-looking commercial precedents and sexism can let women direct established projects without granting equal trust in their original ideas.
- Her father's sudden death and another childhood loss shaped her interest in dangerous, misunderstood people and in filmmaking as a way to author outcomes unavailable in arbitrary life.
