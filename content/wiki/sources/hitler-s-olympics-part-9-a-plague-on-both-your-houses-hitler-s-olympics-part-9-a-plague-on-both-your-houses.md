# Hitler’s Olympics, Part 9: A Plague on Both Your Houses

## Summary

The finale of Revisionist History's *Hitler's Olympics* series compares the 1932 Los Angeles Games amid Jim Crow with the 1936 Berlin Games under Nazi rule. The [[ScottsboroBoys]] case makes the American injustice concrete, while [[BillyGarland]]'s commercially successful Los Angeles model shows how spectacle, development, and political legitimacy could coexist with oppression.

[[MalcolmGladwell]] and [[BenNaddaffHafrey]] disagree over whether refusing Berlin would have been meaningful resistance or symbolic action that displaced more consequential work. The episode resolves neither [[SportsBoycottEthics]] nor the wider institutional dilemma, but uses [[SydneyMcLaughlin]]'s running to argue that athletic excellence can retain value even when hosts, organizers, and political uses remain compromised.

## Key Claims

- The internationally publicized [[ScottsboroBoys]] prosecution exposed how disfranchisement, jury exclusion, rushed process, and racial hierarchy reinforced one another in Jim Crow Alabama.
- The lack of a serious Los Angeles boycott, despite international outrage over Scottsboro, suggests that entrenched American racism was normalized and that violence against Black people carried less international political weight.
- Berlin differed in the episode's account because Nazi rule was newer and more conspicuous and because the regime joined the Games more explicitly to its ideology and international image.
- The anti-Nazi World Labor Athletic Carnival preserved moral opposition but drew limited attendance, illustrating the reach problem faced by alternative protest institutions.
- [[BillyGarland]] treated Los Angeles 1932 as tourism, investment, urban promotion, and entertainment; its profitable spectacle became a practical model for Berlin.
- Boycott decisions divide symbolic legitimacy from institutional continuity: refusing participation may withhold prestige, while repeated refusal can weaken or end the institution through which athletes compete.
- [[SydneyMcLaughlin]] represents the episode's final distinction between compromised Olympic machinery and the human performance that gives the Games their strongest claim to preservation.
