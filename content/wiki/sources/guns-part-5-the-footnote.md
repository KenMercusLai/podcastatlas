# Guns Part 5: The Footnote

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode follows a footnote in [[PenelopeHanke]] and [[JamesGundlach]]'s 1995 paper “Damned Upon Arrival” to the death of [[BrandonYoung]]. It argues that homicide statistics reflect both serious violence and the medical system's ability to keep wounded people alive, making transport, trauma-center geography, and financing part of [[HomicideAsSurvivalSystemOutcome|which assaults become recorded homicides]].

[[BabakSarani]] explains the time sensitivity of uncontrolled bleeding, while [[ElizabethTung]] describes Chicago's racially unequal trauma deserts and the payer incentives that can pull expensive care away from communities with high need but weak reimbursement. Brandon's death makes the mechanism concrete: the episode reports that he bled to death after an ambulance took more than an hour to travel six miles to Gundlach's Macon County cabin.

## Key Claims

- [[HomicideAsSurvivalSystemOutcome]] separates the incidence of severe assault from the probability that the wounded person survives transport and treatment.
- [[BabakSarani]] describes level-one trauma care as an expensive, always-ready specialist system in which minutes can determine survival from uncontrolled bleeding.
- [[TraumaCareAccessInequality]] can arise when Black neighborhoods are farther from trauma centers and when hospitals have financial incentives to avoid poorly reimbursed gunshot care.
- [[ElizabethTung]]'s Chicago research, as presented, associates distance beyond five miles with higher gunshot mortality and locates majority-Black South Side tracts disproportionately in trauma deserts.
- [[PenelopeHanke]] and [[JamesGundlach]] reported that victims associated with Black women imprisoned for homicide were more likely to die during transport or after admission than victims associated with white women.
- Their estimate that nearly one-quarter of 468 African American women might not have been imprisoned for homicide under comparable transport and care is a consequential counterfactual, not a definitive causal result.
- [[BrandonYoung]]'s delayed ambulance response joins racial discrimination, rural emergency access, and the moral consequences of treating response speed as socially conditional.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. The episode strengthens the existing trauma-access mechanism without showing that medical care replaces violence prevention or that every geographic disparity is caused by profit alone.
- The Alabama study is described as suggestive rather than definitive. Its offender-based sample, victim comparability, case classification, transport and hospital controls, statistical uncertainty, and later replication are not supplied.
- The Chicago distance estimate, sevenfold South Side disparity, payer incentives, Brandon's response timeline, driver's statement, and counterfactual imprisonment estimate remain episode-scoped because the supplied file is a structured summary rather than a transcript, paper, patient-level dataset, dispatch record, or independent investigation.
- The source's closing correction gives August 23, 1994, rather than September 23, as the date of Brandon's death.
