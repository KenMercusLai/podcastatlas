# 219. Justinian: Making Rome Great Again (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode presents [[JustinianI]]'s effort to restore Roman territory, Christian unity, law, revenue, and imperial authority as one connected centralizing project. Persian pressure and plans for western reconquest made money and strategic timing essential, while taxation, hostility to religious dissent, weakened senatorial standing, and increasingly distant court ritual generated severe internal resentment.

The [[NikaRiots|Nika riots]] turn those pressures into an urban regime crisis. The Blues and Greens unite, Constantinople burns, Hypatius is proclaimed emperor, and [[Theodora]]'s refusal to flee precedes a massacre led by forces under [[Belisarius]] and Mundus. The rebuilding of [[HagiaSophia|Hagia Sophia]] then embodies the episode's central [[ImperialRestorationParadox|restoration paradox]]: Justinian renewed Roman engineering and grandeur through a Christian monument while the revolt's suppression diminished older civic and senatorial constraints.

## Key Claims

- Justinian joined territorial reconquest to a providential vision of Christian Roman order, treating law, doctrine, taxation, ceremony, and war as connected instruments of restoration.
- Peace payments to Sasanian Persia bought strategic room for western campaigns, while the episode leaves open whether resources or imperial destiny mattered more in his reconquest plans.
- John of Cappadocia's taxation, Tribonian's legal codification, religious coercion, and court prostration strengthened central authority but also widened opposition among ordinary residents, factions, religious minorities, and senators.
- The January 532 Nika crisis began with factional violence and a failed execution, then became a coalition revolt after Justinian refused clemency and the Blues and Greens united behind the chant “Nika.”
- Theodora's reported refusal to flee was politically decisive within the episode's narrative, but the exact speech survives through Procopius and should not be treated as a transcript.
- Belisarius and Mundus attacked the Hippodrome while Narses used money and factional contacts; the resulting death toll was enormous even if Procopius's figure of 50,000 is exaggerated.
- The destruction and crackdown enabled Justinian to execute Hypatius, weaken senatorial opposition, reshape central Constantinople, and build the new Hagia Sophia as Christian imperial architecture.

## Key Quotes

> “the purple” - the image in Theodora's reported argument that imperial death was preferable to flight.

> “surpassed Solomon” - the triumph attributed to Justinian upon entering the rebuilt Hagia Sophia.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content was found. The episode supplies the missing prehistory to Part 3 by showing how fiscal, religious, ceremonial, and urban centralization preceded western reconquest and plague.
- The episode's claim that the Nika crisis ultimately strengthened Justinian qualifies any simple account of the revolt as regime failure: near-collapse and durable consolidation occurred in sequence.
- Procopius's demonic stories, casualty estimate, reported speeches, motives attributed to Justinian, Theodora, Hypatius, and senators, and the suggestion that Hagia Sophia plans predated the fire remain hostile, interpretive, or source-scoped evidence.
