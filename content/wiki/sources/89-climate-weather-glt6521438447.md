# 89. Climate & Weather

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] countdown moves from the end of the last glacial period through the Little Ice Age, late-antique cooling, the Great Frost of 1709, typhoons, revolutionary France, Agincourt, D-Day, 1816, and Britain's Winter of Discontent. Its comparative argument supports [[ClimateHistoryCausalPluralism]]: weather and climate become historically powerful through food supply, military logistics, transport, political legitimacy, and cultural imagination rather than acting as complete explanations by themselves.

The cases also clarify [[ClimateShockInstitutionalResilience]]. Earlier subsistence societies were especially exposed to harvest failure, while modern forecasting, industrial systems, and state capacity can reduce some direct vulnerability without eliminating cascading risks. The [[JamesStagg]] and D-Day case is the constructive counterexample: environmental knowledge changed a high-stakes decision and may have prevented an invasion fleet from meeting much worse conditions.

## Key Claims

- The Holocene's warmer conditions enabled permanent settlement and early urban development after glacial retreat, though the episode presents this as an enabling condition rather than a sufficient cause of civilization.
- Little Ice Age and late-antique cooling cases connect environmental stress to harvest failure, famine, disease, war, migration, and political upheaval while cautioning against single-cause history.
- The Great Frost compounded scorched-earth logistics, hunger, and alliance failure during [[CharlesXII|Charles XII]]'s Russian campaign before Poltava.
- Typhoons disrupted both Mongol invasions of Japan, but later kamikaze mythology and wartime propaganda require source criticism.
- France's 1788 drought, hail, winter, floods, harvest losses, and bread-price surge intensified the fiscal, institutional, and legitimacy crisis preceding the [[FrenchRevolution|French Revolution]].
- Rain, ploughed clay, constrained frontage, archery, and formation pressure interacted at the [[BattleOfAgincourt|Battle of Agincourt]]; mud was consequential but not a standalone explanation.
- [[JamesStagg]]'s forecast supported Eisenhower's decision to delay and then launch D-Day on 6 June 1944, showing forecasting as an active form of risk management.
- Tambora-driven conditions in 1816 contributed to food and transport disruption while creating the constrained Lake Geneva setting associated with *Frankenstein*, Polidori's vampire, and Byron's darkness imagery.

## Key Quotes

> "weather mattered more further back in history" - the episode's shorthand for greater subsistence and harvest exposure in preindustrial societies.

> "a weather forecast changing history" - the D-Day case, where environmental knowledge informs action rather than merely explaining disaster afterward.
