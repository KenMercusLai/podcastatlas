# The Big Man Can’t Shoot

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode uses [[WiltChamberlain|Wilt Chamberlain’s]] underhand free throws and [[RickBarry|Rick Barry’s]] contrasting commitment to the same technique to ask why an effective practice can remain socially unacceptable. Chamberlain made 28 of 32 free throws during his 100-point game and improved substantially during the season in which he shot underhanded, yet abandoned the method because it felt silly and unmanly.

[[MarkGranovetter|Mark Granovetter’s]] [[GranovetterThresholdModel|threshold model]] separates belief in an action from willingness to perform it before enough peers do. Gladwell extends the distinction to basketball and to NFL draft and fourth-down strategy, then uses Barry to develop [[SituationalDisagreeableness]]: tolerating ridicule can protect performance, but habitual indifference to social feedback can also damage trust and relationships.

## Key Claims

- Chamberlain scored 100 points on March 2, 1962, while making an episode-reported 28 of 32 free throws with an underhand technique.
- The episode reports that Chamberlain’s free-throw percentage rose from roughly 40 percent earlier in his career to above 60 percent during the 1961–62 season, then declined after he abandoned the technique.
- Barry argues that the underhand motion is relaxed, mechanically simple, and more forgiving, but the episode supplies no controlled biomechanical comparison.
- Chamberlain, Shaquille O’Neal, college players, and members of Columbia’s women’s team illustrate how ridicule and learned convention can outweigh acknowledged effectiveness.
- Granovetter’s model defines an individual threshold as the number of other participants required before that person joins an action; threshold and private belief are therefore distinct.
- Research attributed to [[RichardThaler]] and Cade Massey on draft-pick value, and to David Romer on fourth-down decisions, supplies institutional analogies for convention-preserving behavior under reputational risk.
- Barry’s low dependence on approval supports unconventional performance while his reported abrasiveness shows that approval independence is not an unqualified social virtue.
