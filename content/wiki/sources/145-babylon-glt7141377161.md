# 145. Babylon

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[Babylon]] at its Neo-Babylonian height under [[NebuchadnezzarII]], through conquest by [[CyrusTheGreat]], continued importance under Persian and Greek rule, and eventual physical decline. Monumental walls, the Ishtar Gate, Etemenanki, commercial wealth, and the uncertain Hanging Gardens make the city both an archaeological place and an object of ancient fantasy.

Its strongest contribution is the connection between political catastrophe and symbolic afterlife. The destruction of Jerusalem and deportation of Judean elites sharpen [[BabylonianExileIdentityFormation]], while Jeremiah, Daniel, Revelation, Protestant polemic, Rastafarian usage, Saddam Hussein's reconstruction, and Iraq War damage turn Babylon into a reusable [[BabylonAsImperialMetaphor|metaphor for imperial wealth, coercion, corruption, exile, and expected judgment]].

## Key Claims

- Nebuchadnezzar's sixth-century-BC Babylon joined monumental rebuilding, dense commerce, cosmopolitan life, royal ritual, and claims to cosmic centrality.
- Herodotus remains useful but unreliable evidence: his city descriptions, customs, and sexual claims mix observation, inherited report, Greek assumptions, and fantasy.
- The Hanging Gardens may not have stood at Babylon; the episode presents a qualified argument that later tradition relocated an Assyrian garden from Nineveh.
- Babylon's conquest of Judah, destruction of Jerusalem and Solomon's Temple, and selective deportation of elites made exile a crisis through which Judean identity and monotheistic opposition to idolatry became sharper.
- [[Nabonidus]] weakened his position through prolonged absence and disrupted Marduk-centered ritual, while Cyrus presented Persian conquest as restoration rather than religious annihilation.
- Daniel and Revelation made Babylon an enduring political-theological image of luxury, hubris, oppressive empire, and imminent reversal, later applied to Rome, the Catholic Church, colonial power, policing, and modern capitals.
- Babylon remained important after 539 BC, including under Alexander and the Seleucids, but mud-brick decay and political relocation gradually turned the physical city into ruins while its textual afterlife expanded.

## Key Quotes

> “the center of the world” - the episode's shorthand for Babylon's sacred and cultural self-understanding.

> “numbered, weighed, divided” - the episode's paraphrase of Daniel's interpretation of the writing at Belshazzar's feast.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content was found. The episode extends the earlier Babylon source into the Neo-Babylonian, Persian, Greek, biblical, and modern afterlives.
- The episode dates Babylon's foundation to about 1900 BC, while [[181-the-birth-of-babylon-glt1570061216]] more cautiously places its emergence after 2000 BC; this is retained as a source-scope difference rather than resolved chronology.
- Herodotus's visit and customs, city population and measurements, the location and builder of the Hanging Gardens, Daniel's composition date and historical transfers, Nabonidus's motives, and many reported speeches and numbers remain uncertain or episode-attributed.
- The claim that exile intensified monotheism describes the episode's interpretation; it does not reduce Jewish identity formation to a single event or establish a simple before-and-after theological break.
