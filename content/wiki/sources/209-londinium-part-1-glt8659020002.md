# 209. Londinium (Part 1)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] walking tour reconstructs [[Londinium]] from the Thames crossing and port through the Walbrook, Mithraeum, forum and basilica, fort, city wall, and a late Roman burial. It argues that geography made the settlement useful as a bridge, road hub, commercial center, military access point, and eventually a provincial capital, while repeated destruction, stone reuse, abandonment, and later rebuilding left much of Roman [[London]] underground or embedded in mundane modern settings.

The route also makes evidentiary limits part of the history. Archaeological layers, writing tablets, organic deposits, masonry, artifacts, and burials can establish urban life and change, but skulls, fires, the London Stone, and the unknown girl's grave do not by themselves settle identity, motive, ritual, or belief.

## Key Claims

- The lowest practical Thames crossing, a navigable port, and converging roads made the site strategically and commercially valuable after the AD 43 conquest.
- A bridge probably existed by AD 47, an IOU dated 8 January AD 57 documents early commerce, and Londinium likely displaced Colchester as provincial capital by the end of the first century.
- A destruction layer and human remains at Number One Poultry connect the city materially to the [[BoudiccanRevolt]], although the identities and treatment of the dead remain uncertain.
- Wet Walbrook deposits preserved writing tablets, leather, wood, and other organic objects that rarely survive at dry sites.
- The [[Mithras|Mithraeum]], forum and basilica, fort, wall, roads, and port show religious, civic, military, administrative, and commercial functions within the same city.
- Roman fabric survives unevenly beneath offices, a market, a barber shop, Guildhall, and an underground car park, making interpretation and development-linked conservation central to public access.
- By the late fourth century the city's population and built intensity were declining; later stone reuse, interior abandonment, Anglo-Saxon settlement farther west, and a different street plan weakened visible continuity with modern London.
