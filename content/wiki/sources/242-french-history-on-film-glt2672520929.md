# 242. French History on Film

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode uses ten films, from Jacques Rivette's Joan the Maid to Jean-Luc Godard's [[LaChinoiseFilm|La Chinoise]], to examine how French cinema constructs, revises, and avoids national history. Its durable argument is that historical film works best when it reconstructs a past social or moral world rather than putting modern characters in period dress, while every screen history also participates in [[CinemaAsNationalMemory|cinema as national memory]].

The list moves through Joan of Arc, the Wars of Religion, Versailles, Napoleon, the Dreyfus affair, both world wars, Occupation and collaboration, postwar comedy, and the approach to [[May1968France|May 1968]]. [[RidiculeFilm|Ridicule]] and [[LesEnfantsDuParadisFilm|Les Enfants du Paradis]] become the strongest positive models; [[LeCorbeauFilm|Le Corbeau]] and La Chinoise show how allegory and formal tension can preserve conflicts a nation has difficulty stating directly.

## Key Claims

- French historical cinema does more than teach facts: it reinforces, contests, mythologizes, or evades national narratives already circulating through school, politics, and public memory.
- [[HistoricalWorldviewReconstruction|Historical worldview reconstruction]] matters more than costume accuracy alone; Ridicule succeeds by making access, wit, cruelty, and court ritual into the drama's governing social logic.
- Joan of Arc's repeated appropriation by republicans, monarchists, Vichy, communists, and the far right shows how a historical figure can remain politically available across incompatible traditions.
- Occupation-era films could use displacement and allegory both as escape and as diagnosis: Les Enfants du Paradis rebuilds a vanished Paris, while Le Corbeau turns informing and mistrust into a community pathology.
- Films change meaning with political context: La Grande Illusion's humane German officer was easier to accept in 1937 than on its postwar rerelease, and Roman Polanski's Dreyfus film became entangled with judgments about its director.
- La Grande Vadrouille shows comedy domesticating painful Occupation memory, whereas La Chinoise exposes the gap between bourgeois revolutionary talk and the labor sustaining it.
- The list's limited treatment of the Algerian War is itself evidence of selective cultural memory, not proof that French cinema lacks relevant works.

## Connections

- [[CinemaAsNationalMemory]] - framework joining film selection, historical narrative, political appropriation, changing reception, and cultural omission.
- [[HistoricalWorldviewReconstruction]] - criterion that favors social and moral intelligibility over decorative period detail.
- [[RidiculeFilm|Ridicule]] - Versailles case in which court access depends on wit, status, and verbal cruelty.
- [[LesEnfantsDuParadisFilm|Les Enfants du Paradis]] - Occupation-made reconstruction of nineteenth-century theatrical Paris and the episode's final recommendation.
- [[LeCorbeauFilm|Le Corbeau]] - poison-pen drama read as an indirect account of informing, mistrust, and compromised community under Occupation.
- [[LaChinoiseFilm|La Chinoise]] - Godard film connecting Maoist student politics, class contradiction, and the approach to May 1968.
- [[FrenchNewWave]] - movement context extended from Truffaut to Godard's politically self-questioning form.
- [[May1968France]] - historical crisis that La Chinoise is said to anticipate without explaining or causing.
- [[HistoricalMemoryContest]] and [[FictionAsHistoricalEvidence]] - neighboring frameworks for contested remembrance and guarded use of art as historical evidence.
