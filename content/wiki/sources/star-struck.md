# Star Struck

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode connects [[MargaretMitchell|Margaret Mitchell]]'s 1949 death after [[HughGravitt]] struck her with the deleted drunk-driving collision in [[AStarIsBorn1937|the 1937 *A Star Is Born*]]. [[DorothyParker]] and Alan Campbell's screenplay forced the intoxicated Norman Maine to confront a fatal crash, but producer [[DavidOSelznick]] removed the scene after junior readers argued that it left the character no plausible future beyond prison or degradation.

The episode uses that editorial decision as a counterfactual test of [[NarrativeMoralRehearsal]] and [[AccidentFramingAndResponsibility]]. It argues that popular stories can help a public recognize foreseeable wrongdoing before law, journalism, and ordinary language reliably name it, while preserving the qualification that one unproduced scene cannot be shown to have caused later reform.

## Key Claims

- Mitchell's exceptional fame did not turn her death into an early national reckoning with intoxicated driving, even though Gravitt had been drinking, was reportedly speeding, and had a substantial traffic-citation history.
- Calling a collision an “accident” can focus attention on lack of intent and chance while obscuring the earlier decision to drive while impaired.
- Parker and Campbell's original screenplay made Norman Maine cause a fatal collision and face its human consequences, offering a moral image that contemporary law and culture often withheld.
- Selznick's replacement preserved Norman as a sympathetic romantic figure by limiting his crash to a tree, brief punishment, and Vicki's promise to care for him.
- Contemporary institutional passivity joined weak prosecution expectations, permissive judges, limited testing, and media sympathy for drivers who did not intend to kill.
- The episode treats culture as one contributor to moral recognition rather than proving that a single film scene would have changed law or prevented Mitchell's death.
- The final film's widow-centered tribute shifts sympathy toward Norman and away from the wider harm his addiction could have caused.
