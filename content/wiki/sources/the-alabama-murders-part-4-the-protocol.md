# The Alabama Murders - Part 4: The Protocol

## Summary

This episode of [[TheAlabamaMurders]] follows [[JohnForrestParker]] from his 10-2 jury recommendation for life without parole through judicial override and his 2010 execution. [[TomPerryJr]]'s long friendship with Parker supplies a counterexample to institutional indifference: his [[MinistryOfPresence]] means returning, accompanying a condemned person through the final day, supporting the family, and preserving the person's memory without resolving guilt or erasing the victim.

The episode also traces lethal injection from [[AJChapman]]'s rapidly adopted, untested Oklahoma protocol to [[JoelZivot]]'s review of autopsies showing pulmonary edema and severely damaged lungs. It develops [[MedicalizedExecutionOpacity]] by arguing that sedation, restraint, and paralysis can create a peaceful outward scene while preventing witnesses from knowing whether the condemned person is burning, drowning in lung fluid, or otherwise suffering internally.

## Key Claims

- Parker's jury recommended life without parole by a 10-2 vote, but Judge Inge Johnson used Alabama's then-valid judicial-override law to impose death.
- Parker admitted participating in the assault on [[ElizabethSennett]] and expressed remorse while continuing to deny that he killed her.
- Perry visited Parker for years, attended his execution at Parker's request, supported his family, and treated reliable presence as more important than verbal ministry alone.
- Oklahoma adopted Chapman's three-drug lethal-injection sequence without experimental testing, and the procedure spread because it appeared more humane than electrocution or lethal gas.
- Zivot reports that nearly 80 percent of the execution autopsies he reviewed showed abnormally heavy, fluid-filled lungs consistent with pulmonary edema sustained while the prisoners were alive.
- In the episode's account, execution-sized pentobarbital doses can injure the lung barrier, while a paralytic can prevent movement or visible distress and potassium chloride then stops the heart.
- Parker's jaw movement and the procedure's duration did not establish his subjective experience; the protocol's restraint, sedation, and paralysis made Perry's sincere impression of a peaceful death evidentially inconclusive.
- The episode frames the protocol's persistence as a failure of testing, curiosity, publication, and care rather than a simple absence of available evidence.

## Contradictions

- Parker admitted participating in the assault but denied killing Sennett. The supplied episode summary does not independently resolve his precise conduct, the trial evidence, or the accounts of other participants.
- Perry experienced the execution as peaceful, while the episode argues that the protocol made visible calm unreliable evidence of Parker's internal experience. These positions concern observation and inference rather than Perry's sincerity.
- No settled contradiction is adopted between Parker's 10-2 life recommendation and Smith's 11-1 recommendation in later episodes; they concern different defendants whose trial judges separately imposed death.
