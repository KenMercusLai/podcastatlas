# You Don't Need a Crystal Ball

## Summary
This [[HiddenBrain]] episode separates two problems that are often collapsed: making an accurate forecast and getting an audience to act on a warning. The contrast between [[CassandraMythology|Cassandra]] and [[AndrewNatsios]] develops [[WarningReceptionFramework]]: clarity, messenger credibility, threat proximity, audience identity, and feasible action all affect whether evidence changes a decision.

The episode then has [[PhilipTetlock]] develop [[ProbabilisticForecastingDiscipline]]. Numerical probabilities, base rates, repeated scoring, fine-grained calibration, and frequent small updates make forecasts more accountable than elastic verbal confidence or vivid stories. [[ChrisHiemstra]]'s Alaskan fieldwork supplies a planetary warning case through [[PermafrostCarbonFeedback]], while the Challenger launch shows that technically grounded warnings can still lose to institutional momentum.

## Key Claims
- [[WarningReceptionFramework]] treats correctness as necessary but insufficient: warnings must also be comprehensible, credible, timely, and actionable within the audience's institutional setting.
- Cassandra's myth illustrates failure modes involving cryptic language, outsider status, long causal chains, and warnings that threaten the listener's identity or worldview.
- [[AndrewNatsios]] paired projected deaths and satellite evidence with insider trust, shared values, and politically legible options when warning the Bush administration about Darfur.
- [[ChrisHiemstra]] uses thawing Alaskan permafrost to show a slow, remote, hard-to-experience danger in which decomposition can release stored carbon and intensify warming.
- [[PhilipTetlock]] argues that forecasts become auditable when they use explicit probabilities and are recorded and scored across many questions.
- [[ProbabilisticForecastingDiscipline]] begins with comparable-case base rates, adjusts for case-specific evidence, and updates incrementally rather than waiting for a single decisive insight.
- Vivid narratives can make a narrow scenario feel more likely than a broader event class that logically contains it, so good communication and good probability judgment can pull in different directions.
- Forecasting tournaments can identify strong performance, but luck, selection effects, and an emphasis on narrow scoreable questions limit what “superforecaster” status proves.
