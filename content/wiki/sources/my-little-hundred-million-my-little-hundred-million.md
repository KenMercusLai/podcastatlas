# My Little Hundred Million

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode contrasts [[HankRowan|Hank Rowan]]'s 1992 gift of $100 million to a financially constrained New Jersey public college with later mega-gifts to already wealthy universities. The gift created an engineering school and helped transform the college into [[RowanUniversity]], leading [[MalcolmGladwell]] to argue that philanthropic value should be judged by marginal institutional and student impact rather than donation size or recipient prestige.

The episode develops [[WeakLinkStrongLinkSystems]] through soccer and basketball. When overall performance is constrained by the weakest participant or bottleneck, improving the bottom may outperform adding another star; when an exceptional performer can dominate the outcome, concentration may be rational. [[JohnHennessy]]'s defense of Stanford's highly endowed graduate scholarship program supplies the episode's strong-link counterposition, while [[PhilanthropicNeglectedness]] captures its broader allocation claim.

## Key Claims

- Rowan reportedly turned an initial scholarship solicitation into a $100 million engineering gift to Glassboro State College, whose endowment was then about $787,000.
- The gift created a practical engineering school that expanded beyond its planned capacity, charged relatively accessible in-state tuition, and reported high graduate employment.
- By spring 2016, the episode counted 87 higher-education gifts of at least $100 million and argued that most went to already wealthy, prestigious institutions.
- In a weak-link system such as soccer, upgrading the weakest contributor or bottleneck can improve the whole more than adding another elite performer; in a strong-link system such as basketball, a superstar can exert greater direct control.
- Gladwell extends weak-link logic to interdependent social systems, including air travel, industrial capability, and broad-access higher education.
- [[JohnHennessy]] defends a roughly $750–800 million Stanford endowment designed to support 100 new graduate scholars per year as an investment in exceptional future leaders.
- The episode calculates that a 5 percent return on an $800 million endowment would provide about $40 million annually, or roughly $133,000 per scholar per year once three cohorts were enrolled.
- Strong-link investment can be appropriate where top-level capacity is genuinely absent; Gladwell uses Canada's creation of a major science-and-technology university as his limiting case.
