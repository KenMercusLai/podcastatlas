# 153. God and the American Empire

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] conversation with [[AndrewPreston|Andrew Preston]] explains why religion remained unusually visible in modern American political culture and how it shaped foreign-policy language from the Second World War through the Cold War, 9/11, and [[DonaldTrump|Donald Trump]]. Its central synthesis joins [[AmericanReligiousPersistence]], [[JudeoChristianNationalIdentity]], [[AmericanExceptionalism]], and [[ReligiousMoralFramingOfUSForeignPolicy]]: church-state separation and religious competition helped preserve public faith, while changing coalitions repeatedly cast American power as a struggle among good, evil, freedom, tyranny, religion, and irreligion.

## Key Claims

- Preston argues that American modernization did not produce the degree of secularization expected by older modernization theory; church-state separation insulated churches from state failure, while a competitive religious marketplace encouraged innovation.
- The episode treats [[AmericanExceptionalism]] as a belief that the United States is different from, and often better than, other countries; religion supplies one important vocabulary for that national distinction.
- A public [[JudeoChristianNationalIdentity|Judeo-Christian national identity]] emerged only in the late 1930s and 1940s, when anti-Nazi and later anti-communist politics encouraged Catholics, Protestants, and Jews to be narrated as partners in a shared American cause.
- Preston rejects a simple isolationist-versus-interventionist divide. American religion supported both a warlike “sword of the spirit” and a reformist or restraint-oriented “shield of faith,” while the United States remained active abroad even when it avoided formal commitments.
- [[HarrySTruman|Harry S. Truman]]'s proposed world religious summit and recognition of [[Israel]] illustrate how biblical identification, religious pluralism, and strategic policy could overlap, although the episode does not reduce either decision to religion alone.
- [[RonaldReagan|Ronald Reagan]] revived moral and evangelical language without simply inventing it. His “evil empire” rhetoric represented confrontation, while the negotiated departure of the Siberian Seven and continuing pressure on [[MikhailGorbachev|Mikhail Gorbachev]] over religious freedom show a quieter confidence-building route.
- The hosts interpret [[FrancisFukuyama|Francis Fukuyama]]'s post-Cold War universalism as a secularized providential story in which liberal democracy spreads toward a final horizon; this is an episode-level interpretation rather than a complete account of Fukuyama's theory.
- [[GeorgeWBush|George W. Bush]]'s post-9/11 “crusade” slip drew on an available American vocabulary, but his insistence that al-Qaeda did not represent Islam also continued the pluralist strand of American religious politics.
- The closing discussion treats Trump's strong white-evangelical support and pro-Israel politics as evidence that religiously shaped coalitions can rally around a personally irreligious leader who performs their expected political commitments.

## Key Quotes

> “marketplace of faith” - Preston's explanation for how disestablishment could encourage religious competition and innovation.

> “sword of the spirit” and “shield of faith” - the episode's paired metaphors for militant and reformist religious impulses in foreign policy.

> “evil empire” - Reagan's moral-religious description of the Soviet Union, delivered to the National Association of Evangelicals.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. The episode broadens the existing [[AmericanExceptionalism]] page beyond rule-exception behavior in sport by adding a religious-national mission, but the two mechanisms are compatible rather than mutually exclusive.
- The “Judeo-Christian” dating, Truman's inner motives, religion's causal weight in recognizing Israel, Reagan's private belief, the Siberian Seven's diplomatic importance, Fukuyama's providential inheritance, and explanations for evangelical support of Trump remain episode-attributed or interpretive.
