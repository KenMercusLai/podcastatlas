# 422. Ancient Carthage: Rise of a Superpower (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode traces [[Carthage]] from its traditional Phoenician foundation in 814 BC to a wealthy, fortified western Mediterranean power on the eve of rivalry with the [[RomanRepublic]]. Geography, harbors, trade routes, an increasingly productive African hinterland, naval capacity, and shared Phoenician identity turn a colonial settlement into a [[CarthaginianCommercialToTerritorialEmpire|commercial and then territorial empire]] centered on western Sicily, Sardinia, and North Africa.

The episode also treats knowledge of Carthage as an archive problem. Most Carthaginian writing was destroyed or dispersed, leaving Greek and Roman rivals to shape surviving accounts of treachery, luxury, sacrifice, and strangeness. Its reconstruction therefore combines institutional evidence, archaeology, and hostile literary traditions while keeping vivid atrocity stories, motives, exact figures, and reported scenes source-scoped.

## Key Claims

- Carthage's defensible site near modern Tunis, large harbors, and position across east-west and north-south sea routes help explain its rapid growth.
- Phoenician descent and continuing ties to Tyre remained important elite identities even as Carthage became a cosmopolitan African and Mediterranean city.
- Babylonian pressure on Tyre and changing silver conditions helped Carthage become an independent western center whose early expansion relied on settlements, markets, sea control, and violence.
- Agricultural development in the African hinterland reduced external food dependence and joined maritime commerce to estates, irrigation, vines, olives, livestock, and technical expertise associated with Mago.
- Greek expansion made Sicily the main frontier; Himera and later campaigns show conflict driven by local alliances and imperial competition rather than a coordinated Persian-Carthaginian war against civilization.
- The [[CarthaginianConstitution|Carthaginian constitution]] combined wealthy aristocratic power with sufets, councils, courts, generals, and a popular assembly, while repeatedly resisting individual tyranny.
- By the fourth century BC, western Phoenician settlements and fortified bases such as Lilybaeum were increasingly incorporated into direct territorial rule.
- Agathocles' African invasion exposed Carthage to extreme danger, but the city's survival and renewed prosperity demonstrate institutional and material resilience.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction found. The episode gives the Carthaginian government, agriculture, Phoenician identity, and pre-Roman expansion that later parts only summarize.
- The source rejects the familiar claim that Romans sowed Carthage's ruins with salt as a modern invention.
- The claimed synchronization of Himera with Salamis and coordination between Carthage and Xerxes are treated as Greek propaganda rather than established strategy.
- Greek and Roman hostility, destroyed Carthaginian writing, translated institutional vocabulary, archaeological estimates, sacrifice reports, exact numbers, kinship claims, dialogue, motives, and dramatic deaths remain source-scoped or contested.
