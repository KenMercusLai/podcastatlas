# 449. Custer vs. Crazy Horse: Rise of Sitting Bull (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode traces the emergence of [[SittingBull]] from Hunkpapa childhood, warrior achievement, and visionary practice into leadership of Lakota people who rejected reservation life. It presents his authority as a combination of courage, humor, social presence, ritual endurance, spiritual reputation, and refusal to exchange independence for government supplies, while warning that translated interviews and later heroic stories do not preserve an unmediated voice.

The wider narrative moves from [[FettermanFight|the Fetterman Fight]] and [[RedCloud]]'s victory over the Bozeman Trail forts to the 1868 [[GreatSiouxReservation]] settlement. It treats Red Cloud's accommodation and Sitting Bull's refusal as constrained political choices under military, settler, railroad, and bison pressure, then ends with [[GeorgeArmstrongCuster]] escorting the 1873 Northern Pacific survey into the Yellowstone country.

## Key Claims

- Sitting Bull's remembered words and exploits are historically valuable but mediated by translation, journalism, retrospective narration, and legend.
- The Fetterman Fight was both a major Lakota victory and an event labeled a massacre in U.S. memory, showing how political viewpoint shapes frontier-war language.
- The 1868 settlement closed the Bozeman Trail forts and created the Great Sioux Reservation while leaving the status and permitted uses of unceded territory dangerously ambiguous.
- Red Cloud concluded after visiting Washington that reservation accommodation offered the most realistic path, while Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse remained outside that settlement.
- Sitting Bull's authority joined warrior honors, participation in warrior societies, ritual suffering, visions, charisma, humor, and a reputation for prediction.
- Lakota sacred life is presented as inseparable from ordinary relations among people, animals, dreams, healing, and power rather than a separate modern category of religion.
- Railroad surveys renewed direct pressure after a period of comparatively small-scale raiding and brought Custer back into conflict with Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse.

## Key Quotes

> “The life my people want is a life of freedom.” - the episode's translated and explicitly qualified rendering of Sitting Bull's 1882 interview.

> “We do not want white men here.” - the episode's rendering of Sitting Bull's refusal of reservation dependency and territorial intrusion.

## Connections

- [[SittingBull]] - Hunkpapa leader whose warrior, spiritual, social, and political authority forms the episode's center.
- [[RedCloud]] - leader whose military success and later accommodation provide the principal contrast with Sitting Bull.
- [[CrazyHorse]] - war leader associated with the Fetterman decoy and later refusal of reservation life.
- [[LakotaPeople]] - people whose dynamic territorial history, sacred world, and divided political responses organize the narrative.
- [[FettermanFight]] - 1866 Lakota and allied victory that helped force U.S. abandonment of the Bozeman Trail forts.
- [[GreatSiouxReservation]] - 1868 treaty arrangement accepted by many Lakota but rejected by important non-reservation leaders.
- [[CoerciveReservationAssimilation]] - later system whose dependency and loss of autonomy Sitting Bull anticipated in refusing agency supplies.
- [[GeorgeArmstrongCuster]] - U.S. commander reintroduced through the 1873 Yellowstone survey expedition.

## Contradictions

- U.S. accounts called the Fetterman defeat a massacre, while Lakota perspectives could understand it as victory against an invading military force.
- Federal commissioners criticized hypocrisy toward Native resistance while continuing to accept Manifest Destiny and the displacement of Native societies.
- Red Cloud's accommodation and Sitting Bull's refusal were competing responses to coercive conditions, not a simple opposition between courage and capitulation.
- Sitting Bull's reported speeches, duel, visions, animal communications, naming story, political title, and personality come through translation and retrospective popular-history narration and remain source-scoped.
