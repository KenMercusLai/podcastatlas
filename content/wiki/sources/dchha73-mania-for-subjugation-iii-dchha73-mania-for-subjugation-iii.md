# Show 73 - Mania for Subjugation III

## Summary

This [[HardcoreHistory]] episode follows [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]] from the winter after Thebes through the Hellespont crossing, the Homeric ceremonies at Troy, and the [[BattleOfGranicus]]. Its central distinction is between structural pressure and personal escalation: [[PhilipIIMacedon|Philip II]] left a professional army and invasion plan that debt-strained Macedon could not readily sustain at home, while Alexander's pursuit of heroic fame helps explain why conquest continued after its immediate fiscal and strategic goals were met.

The episode also rejects a Greek propaganda picture of the [[AchaemenidEmpire]] as a decadent state awaiting collapse. Satrapal delegation, local accommodation, taxation, military diversity, and durable administration coexist with succession violence and coordination problems. Granicus then turns a precarious expedition into a self-financing campaign, but only after Alexander's visible front-line leadership nearly makes his death the campaign's single point of failure and his victory ends in the mass killing and enslavement of Greek mercenaries.

## Key Claims

- Philip's army, officer corps, Persian bridgehead, debts, and invasion plan made an attack on Persian territory plausible under almost any Macedonian successor.
- Alexander's distinctive contribution was not simply beginning the war but joining tactical audacity, personal exposure, divine or heroic self-conception, and an unusually weak stopping rule.
- The army's continuing pay, supply, fodder, transport, and equipment costs made conquest a fiscal necessity before captured treasuries made it self-financing.
- Achaemenid strength rested on a durable multiethnic imperial system using satraps, local elites, tribute, troops, labor, cavalry, and mercenaries rather than on an undifferentiated mass army.
- Court assassinations and a recent royal succession made [[DariusIII|Darius III's]] position vulnerable, but vulnerability is not evidence of terminal civilizational decay.
- [[MemnonOfRhodes|Memnon of Rhodes]] reportedly proposed scorched earth and a naval counteroffensive, yet regional commanders bore the immediate costs and hindsight cannot prove the strategy would have succeeded.
- Conflicting Granicus accounts leave the timing and exact crossing sequence unresolved, while Alexander's conspicuous front-line role inspired troops and exposed the expedition to catastrophic leader loss.
- The killing and enslavement of Greek mercenaries exposes the coercion beneath Alexander's Panhellenic liberation and revenge narrative.
