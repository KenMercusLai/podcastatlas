# Puzzle Rush

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory|Revisionist History]] episode has [[MalcolmGladwell]] and Camille Baptista take the [[LawSchoolAdmissionTest|LSAT]] to test why rapid answers count as evidence of legal aptitude. Gladwell's experience distinguishes inability to reason from inability to finish under an imposed deadline, while [[WilliamHenderson]]'s episode-reported research suggests that the LSAT predicts tightly timed law-school exams more strongly than take-home exams and papers.

The comparison with [[HikaruNakamura]] across classical, blitz, bullet, and Puzzle Rush chess makes the broader [[ExamStandardizationValidityTradeoff]] visible: changing only the time control can reorder competitors by changing the balance among calculation, intuition, and pattern recognition. Assessment formats therefore do not merely reveal a fixed ranking; they help define which capabilities become legible as merit.

## Key Claims

- The LSAT's fixed 35-minute sections combine reasoning accuracy with rapid reading, answer elimination, and decision-making under discomfort.
- Test-specific strategy can improve performance by prioritizing argument structure and eliminating weak answers before exhaustive analysis.
- Gladwell's reported panic during practice and logic games illustrates that timed failure is not equivalent to inability under less constrained conditions.
- [[WilliamHenderson]] reports that LSAT scores predicted timed in-class law-school exams better than take-home exams and papers, narrowing the meaning of the test's predictive validity.
- Classical, blitz, bullet, and Puzzle Rush chess reward different mixtures of calculation, intuition, and rapid pattern recognition even though they draw on the same underlying domain.
- [[HikaruNakamura]]'s relative strength at faster formats illustrates how time controls can change ordinal rankings rather than simply measure the same skill with more or less convenience.
- Changing an assessment method can reveal strong performance from students previously classified as average, but one episode does not establish the best format for every legal task.
