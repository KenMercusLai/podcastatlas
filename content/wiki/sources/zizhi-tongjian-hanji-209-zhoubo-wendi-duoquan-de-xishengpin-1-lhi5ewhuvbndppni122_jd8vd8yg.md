# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》209｜周勃，文帝夺权的牺牲品（1）

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode places [[ZhouBo|周勃]]'s first resignation as right chancellor inside [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]]'s early transfer of military, ministerial, and information control toward trusted former Dai officials. It argues that Zhou Bo's inability to answer public questions about annual judicial decisions and state revenue was not merely an administrative embarrassment but part of a ruler's attempt to make an accession-making veteran recognize that his practical authority had narrowed.

[[ChenPing|陈平]] answers the same questions by directing the emperor to the responsible specialist officials and defining the chancellor's work as assisting the ruler, ordering appointments, coordinating the administration, pacifying the realm, and keeping officials effective. The episode treats this both as an argument for organizational delegation and as political self-preservation: Chen Ping shows why experienced central coordination remains useful even after Liu Heng's own appointees control major offices. Zhou Bo, warned that long possession of prestige and reward could become dangerous, then resigns and leaves Chen Ping as sole chancellor.

## Key Claims

- Liu Heng reportedly moves military command toward trusted personnel by appointing [[SongChangWesternHan|宋昌]] to command the northern and southern armies and [[GuanYing|灌婴]] as grand commandant after moving Zhou Bo into the right chancellorship.
- The episode says six of the nine senior ministerial offices were held by Liu Heng's former Dai associates, giving the ruler direct channels below the chancellors.
- When Liu Heng publicly asks about annual criminal judgments and state revenue, Zhou Bo cannot answer and becomes visibly distressed.
- Chen Ping answers that the廷尉 and治粟内史 own the detailed data, while the chancellor is responsible for high-level assistance, appointments, coordination, external pacification, public order, and official performance.
- The host reads the public questioning as a political signal that the chancellors' information and operating power had been reduced, not simply as a neutral test of administrative knowledge.
- The source interprets Chen Ping's answer as both sound delegation and a defense of why an experienced chancellor still matters when specialist offices are controlled by the ruler's own coalition.
- After being warned that his anti-Lü and accession merit, high prestige, and long enjoyment of reward could bring disaster, Zhou Bo requests dismissal; Liu Heng accepts, and Chen Ping becomes sole chancellor.

## Key Quotes

The supplied episode document is a structured summary rather than a verbatim transcript, so no sentence is promoted as an exact quotation. Its central attributed distinction is between specialist officials who know case and revenue totals and a chancellor who coordinates the whole administration.

## Connections

- [[ZhouBo|周勃]], [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]], and [[ChenPing|陈平]] - accession-maker, consolidating ruler, and politically adaptive co-chancellor in the episode's power-transfer reading.
- [[GuanYing|灌婴]] and [[SongChangWesternHan|宋昌]] - military appointments through which the episode says Liu Heng moves armed force toward trusted hands.
- [[NewRulerCoalitionConstraint|新君拥立联盟约束]] - the episode shows the ruler reducing dependence on the veterans who enabled accession by rebuilding personnel and information channels.
- [[SecondInCommandRoleFit|二把手角色适配]] - Chen Ping's answer and later sole chancellorship qualify any fixed claim that a politically adaptive deputy cannot hold coordinating leadership.
- [[PowerExitTrap|权力退场困境]] and [[PostRestorationMeritThreat|复国功臣威胁化]] - Zhou Bo resigns because formal reward and restoration prestige can become liabilities once the new ruler consolidates.

## Contradictions

- The public-questioning-as-humiliation theory is the host's political interpretation. The supplied material establishes the questions and different answers but does not directly prove Liu Heng's private purpose.
- The claim that six of the nine senior ministers were former Dai associates, the precise office sequence, the exact wording of the exchange, and the date of Zhou Bo's resignation require comparison with transmitted histories.
- Zhou Bo's resignation may reflect prudent self-preservation, administrative embarrassment, outside warning, imperial pressure, or several of these together; the source does not isolate one motive conclusively.
- Chen Ping's later sole chancellorship complicates the earlier source-scoped formula that he was intelligent but could not be independently entrusted. It does not by itself establish that he was suited to every form of sole military or political command.
