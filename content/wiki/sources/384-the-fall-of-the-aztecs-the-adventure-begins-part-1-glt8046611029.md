# 384. The Fall of the Aztecs: The Adventure Begins (Part 1)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode opens its conquest series by treating the meeting of [[HernanCortes|Hernan Cortes]] and [[Moctezuma]] as both a dramatic encounter and a contested source problem. It then reconstructs Cortes's formation in Reconquista-era Spain, his colonial apprenticeship in Hispaniola and Cuba, and the westward expeditions through which rumors, Maya cities, trade, tribute politics, and gold made the Mexican mainland legible to Spanish armed entrepreneurs.

The episode places Cortes inside a [[SpanishCaribbeanExpansion|Spanish Caribbean expansion chain]] already marked by Indigenous dispossession, forced labor, epidemic collapse, African slavery, Christian militancy, and competition for royal favor. [[DiegoVelazquezDeCuellar|Diego Velazquez de Cuellar]]'s decision to give the next command to an ambitious subordinate he hoped to control becomes the immediate bridge to Part 2.

## Key Claims

- Cortes's ambitions were shaped by Hidalgo status without wealth, legal study, frontier violence, Christian militancy, and chivalric romance rather than by greed alone.
- Hispaniola and Cuba exposed him to colonial government, forced labor, Indigenous demographic catastrophe, African slavery, and organized violence before the Mexico expedition.
- Spanish expansion proceeded through competitive, privately organized ventures that joined conversion, status, gold, royal revenue, and personal advancement.
- The 1517 Hernandez de Cordoba and 1518 Juan de Grijalva expeditions revealed populous, urbanized societies on the Yucatan and Gulf coasts while exposing the Spaniards to dangerous military resistance.
- Totonac reports of Mexica tribute demands revealed regional political divisions that later Spanish expeditions could exploit.
- Gold gifts and reports of inland wealth transformed coastal reconnaissance into an urgent political and commercial opportunity.
- Traditional claims that the Mexica regarded Spaniards as gods or that Moctezuma simply surrendered require skepticism because Spanish testimony is translated, retrospective, politically interested, and sometimes self-deceiving.

## Key Quotes

> "armed entrepreneurs" - Matthew Restall's phrase for private adventurers seeking territory, wealth, status, and royal recognition from Caribbean bases.

> "a story of adventure, but also a tragedy and a story of horror" - the episode's warning against allowing narrative momentum to sanitize conquest.

## Connections

- [[HernanCortes]] - ambitious colonial notary and Cuban magistrate selected to command the next expedition west.
- [[DiegoVelazquezDeCuellar]] - Cuban governor whose sponsorship, rivalry, and need for a capable but controllable commander placed Cortes in charge.
- [[Hatuey]] - Taino resistance leader whose remembered execution stories indict Spanish greed and forced conversion.
- [[MexicaEmpire]] - inland tributary power first described to Spaniards through Totonac reports and diplomatic gifts.
- [[Moctezuma]] - ruler whose later meeting with Cortes frames the series and its source-critical problem.
- [[SpanishCaribbeanExpansion]] - island-to-mainland chain through which violence, labor extraction, livestock, slavery, shipping, rumor, and private enterprise enabled the Mexico expedition.
- [[SpanishConquestOfMexico]] - longer process whose prehistory began in Caribbean colonization and competitive coastal reconnaissance.
- [[ColonialSourceMediation]] - method required for translated encounters, remembered speeches, conquest justifications, and politically interested Spanish testimony.
- [[HistoricalCatastropheNarrativeEthics]] - discipline for retaining the story's contingency and drama without turning colonial suffering into adventure scenery.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with [[385-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-woman-who-changed-the-world-part-2-glt5124604457|Part 2]] was found; this episode supplies Cortes's formation, Caribbean colonial apprenticeship, and the reconnaissance sequence before his commission and break with Velazquez.
- The episode rejects the familiar gods-and-voluntary-surrender story as a secure baseline, aligning with later episodes' emphasis on translation, legal self-interest, and retrospective Spanish narrative construction.
- Hatuey's gold-basket warning and refusal of baptism, the origin of the name Yucatan, sacrifice reports, exact speeches, Cortes's private motives, and the reasons Velazquez selected him remain source-scoped or uncertain.
- Acknowledging mixed motives, fear, Christian belief, or Cortes's capability does not reduce responsibility for forced labor, killing, dispossession, enslavement, and colonial extraction.
