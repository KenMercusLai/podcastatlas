# 204. Gone with the Wind

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] conversation with [[SarahChurchwell|Sarah Churchwell]] uses [[GoneWithTheWind|*Gone with the Wind*]] to examine how romance, survival drama, spectacle, and the appeal of Scarlett O'Hara helped make a distorted account of slavery, the Confederacy, and its defeat emotionally durable. The episode presents the work as both a major Hollywood achievement and Confederate propaganda: its useful historical evidence lies less in an accurate Old South than in the white Southern victimhood, plantation nostalgia, and racial hierarchy it made persuasive.

The discussion connects [[MargaretMitchell|Margaret Mitchell]]'s family memory and the film's segregated Atlanta premiere to [[LostCauseMyth|Lost Cause mythology]], the violent defeat of Reconstruction, Jim Crow, convict labor, and plantation tourism. [[HattieMcDaniel|Hattie McDaniel]]'s exclusion from the premiere and qualified access to the Academy Awards expose the racial order surrounding the film's success, while Churchwell argues that morally compromised classics should be confronted and explained rather than censored or stripped of historical context.

## Key Claims

- *Gone with the Wind* joined mass entertainment, a powerful female survival story, romance, and technical spectacle to a white-supremacist account of the Civil War's aftermath, making the mythology more persuasive than a didactic tract would have been.
- Scarlett's recovery of Tara represents more than homecoming: the episode reads it as restoration of wealth, command over Black labor, and the racial power lost with Confederate defeat.
- The postwar United States could abolish slavery while preserving white supremacy; national reunion therefore accommodated segregation, disenfranchisement, racial violence, and Northern as well as Southern acceptance of the film's worldview.
- [[ReconstructionRollback|Reconstruction's rollback]] joined insufficient long-term federal commitment to organized white violence, compromised local authority, troop withdrawal after the disputed 1876 election, and the later legal consolidation of Jim Crow.
- Convict labor, felon disenfranchisement, plantation prisons, and nostalgic plantation tourism show how slavery's material and symbolic structures survived emancipation in altered forms.
- McDaniel's Oscar milestone coexisted with segregation, demonstrating that individual recognition did not dissolve the institutions governing Black participation in American cultural life.
- [[ProblematicClassicContextualization|Contextualization rather than erasure]] keeps the work available as both cinema and evidence of a mythology that affected politics, while refusing to mistake emotional power for historical truth.
