# 385. The Fall of the Aztecs: The Woman Who Changed The World (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode follows [[HernanCortes|Hernan Cortes]] from his unauthorized departure from Cuba to the founding of Vera Cruz and the decision to march inland. It argues that Christian conviction, gold, status, adventure, military technology, shipping, and factional politics all mattered, but centers the translation chain formed by [[GeronimoDeAguilar|Geronimo de Aguilar]] and [[Malinche]], whose language and political knowledge turned a coastal expedition into an intervention in Mesoamerican rivalries.

The episode also shows conquest being prepared through legal form. The [[Requerimiento]] demanded conversion and submission, while the [[VeraCruzLegalCoup|Vera Cruz legal coup]] let Cortes claim authority from a newly founded town rather than from [[DiegoVelazquezDeCuellar|Diego Velazquez de Cuellar]]. These maneuvers did not make coercion consensual, but they reveal that the expedition sought legitimacy as well as military advantage.

## Key Claims

- Velazquez authorized exploration, evangelization, information gathering, and a search for gold, but did not authorize Cortes to conquer Mexico.
- The expedition was a factional company of armed entrepreneurs rather than a unified professional army, forcing Cortes to manage rivals as well as external opponents.
- Steel swords, horses, cannon, and ships mattered differently; gunpowder alone does not explain later victory, and shipping enabled replenishment from across the Atlantic.
- Aguilar supplied Maya-language interpretation, while Malinche connected Nahuatl to Maya and later learned Spanish, giving Cortes access to language, political divisions, customs, and diplomacy.
- Malinche was enslaved and constrained but not politically inert; the episode treats her possible hostility to Mexica power and influence over Spanish interpretation as plausible rather than recoverable fact.
- The Requerimiento joined Christian universal claims and royal authority to a coercive demand for submission that even contemporary critic Las Casas condemned.
- Cortes founded Vera Cruz, accepted office from its council, sent treasure directly to Spain, and beached rather than burned his ships to bypass Velazquez and make retreat difficult.

## Key Quotes

> "armed entrepreneurs" - the episode's description of conquistadors as a profit-seeking, factional company rather than a modern standing army.

> "sickness of the heart" - the possibly mythologized phrase linking Spanish desire for gold to an incurable appetite.

## Connections

- [[HernanCortes]] - expedition leader whose motives, faction management, legal improvisation, and dependence on interpreters shape the episode.
- [[Malinche]] - enslaved Nahua interpreter whose linguistic and political knowledge made her indispensable.
- [[GeronimoDeAguilar]] - shipwreck survivor who supplied the Maya-Spanish link in the first interpretation chain.
- [[DiegoVelazquezDeCuellar]] - Cuban governor whose limited commission Cortes evaded and then displaced.
- [[MexicaEmpire]] - regional power first approached through Totonac intermediaries and imperial emissaries.
- [[Requerimiento]] - coercive declaration through which conquest sought a Christian and legal form.
- [[VeraCruzLegalCoup]] - municipal maneuver that converted defiance of Velazquez into a claim of direct royal service.
- [[ColonialLegalAdaptation]] - wider pattern in which law legitimized domination but later also supplied tools for Indigenous claims.
- [[SpanishConquestOfMexico]] - conquest process whose early phase joined translation, shipping, local politics, violence, faith, wealth, and legal improvisation.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with [[386-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-city-of-gold-part-3-glt2951201852|Part 3]] was found; this episode supplies the interpreters, coast diplomacy, legal break, and material commitment immediately before the inland march.
- The episode rejects the simple claim that Mexica observers regarded the Spaniards as gods and treats it as a later translation or narrative problem.
- Cortes's famous gold remark, Aguilar's captivity account, Malinche's childhood and private motives, Indigenous acceptance of conversion, and remembered dialogue remain source-scoped or uncertain.
- The Requerimiento's implicit recognition of Indigenous land claims did not make its demand meaningful consent, while later successful petitions do not establish equal access to imperial law.
