# Hitler’s Olympics, Part 2: Pangloss, Polonius, Prufrock

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode profiles [[CharlesHitchcockSherrill]], a Yale sprinter, lawyer, diplomat, memoirist, and [[InternationalOlympicCommittee|IOC]] member whose social confidence and access to power helped him shape the American response to the 1936 Berlin Olympics. Through the literary types Polonius, Pangloss, Prufrock's attendant lord, and [[DorothyThompson]]'s adaptable “Mr. B,” the episode argues that privilege, conventional optimism, and attraction to successful movements can turn elite diplomacy into accommodation.

At the IOC's 1933 Vienna meeting, Sherrill secured a written German assurance that Jewish athletes would not be formally excluded from Olympic selection and presented it as a victory for equality. The episode treats that narrow guarantee as the beginning of the failure: it preserved the Berlin Games while separating team eligibility from [[NaziGermany|Nazi Germany's]] visible persecution of Jewish people, and Sherrill's unedited scrapbooks would later document his admiration for the regime.

## Key Claims

- Sherrill's elite upbringing, Yale athletic success, Wall Street career, diplomacy, social access, memoirs, and scrapbooks reinforced exceptional confidence in his own judgment and historical importance.
- His disputed claim to have invented the crouching start illustrates a wider pattern of self-mythology that public institutions and press coverage repeated rather than tested.
- The IOC operated as an aristocratic international club whose authority, social exclusivity, and internal diplomacy made Sherrill a natural institutional fit.
- By 1933, Nazi policy had already excluded and attacked Jewish people in professional and sporting life, making Berlin's proposed Olympic role incompatible with professed equal competition.
- At Vienna, Sherrill and the IOC separated domestic persecution from Olympic team selection and pursued a written German promise that Jewish athletes would not be barred as Jews.
- Press praise converted the guarantee into a public moral victory even though the Nazi state still controlled training, selection, and the wider conditions of Jewish life.
- Sherrill's 44 scrapbooks are presented as unusually revealing because his 1936 death prevented retrospective editing after the regime's crimes became fully known.
