# 10 被阉割的爱人 & 牛郎织女：情人节特辑

## Summary

This [[WeirdHistoryPodcast|怪东西 Weird History]] episode compares the layered formation of [[ValentinesDayHistoricalFormation|Valentine's Day]] and [[QixiFestival|七夕]]. It argues that neither holiday has one timeless romantic origin: [[SaintValentine]]'s early martyr tradition lacks a love theme, while Qixi joins astronomy, seasonal textile work, [[QiqiaoRitualPractice|乞巧]], literature, popular custom, and the changing [[CowherdAndWeaverGirl|牛郎织女]] story.

The episode pairs those festival histories with [[PeterAbelard]] and [[Heloise|Heloise / Héloïse]], whose relationship raises questions about learning, intention, marriage, vocation, violence, and women's intellectual agency. Across both halves, its strongest synthesis is that traditions and love stories acquire new religious, literary, commercial, social, and ethical layers while older meanings remain recoverable but contested.

## Key Claims

- Early Valentine martyr stories concern faith, execution, and miracle rather than romance; secret weddings, heart-shaped parchment, the almond tree, and the “your Valentine” letter appear as later legendary additions.
- Chaucer's bird-pairing poetry is presented as an early documented bridge between the February feast and romantic choice, while a direct line from Lupercalia remains only a theory.
- Valentine's Day spread widely in China during the 1990s through commerce, media, and youth culture, alongside criticism of foreign festivals and consumerism.
- Qixi was first organized around women's textile skill, autumn work, star observation, and 乞巧, although poetry, marriage hopes, and public practice gradually made romance one legitimate later meaning.
- Gendered Qixi petitions reflect constrained social roles: women sought textile skill while men could seek learning, office, or wealth, so a women-centered festival did not necessarily imply autonomy.
- Abelard and Heloise joined intellectual partnership and mutual attachment to unequal teacher-student circumstances, secret marriage, retaliatory castration, monastic life, correspondence, and later memorialization.
- The Cowherd-Weaver tradition developed from early celestial names and ominous marriage associations through personification, annual reunion, magpie bridge, mortal-cowherd, Dong Yong, and [[SwanMaidenMotif|stolen-garment]] layers.
- Qixi's movement into Japan, Korea, and Vietnam and its continuing ethical reinterpretation show that tradition survives through localization rather than exact preservation.
