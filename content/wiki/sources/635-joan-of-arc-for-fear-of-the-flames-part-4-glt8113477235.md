# 635. Joan of Arc: For Fear of the Flames (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[JoanOfArc|Joan of Arc]] from English custody in Rouen through interrogation, abjuration, relapse, execution on 30 May 1431, and posthumous rehabilitation in 1456. It presents the proceeding as an [[EcclesiasticalTrialPoliticalLegitimacy|ecclesiastical trial serving political legitimacy]]: [[PierreCauchon|Pierre Cauchon]] observed substantial inquisitorial form, but the Lancastrian and Burgundian setting made a neutral outcome impossible because Joan's claimed revelations supported [[CharlesVIIOfFrance|Charles VII]] against [[HenryVIOfEngland|Henry VI]].

Joan's voices, refusal to submit private revelation unconditionally to the institutional church, and male clothing became the main routes to a heresy conviction. Her last-minute recantation prevented immediate execution, but her return to male dress and renewed affirmation of the voices made her a relapsed heretic; later French recovery allowed Charles VII to reopen the case and turn her condemnation from a political liability into evidence of a corrupt judgment.

## Key Claims

- English authorities needed Joan's condemnation to weaken the sacred legitimacy that her role at Orléans and Reims had given Charles VII, while Henry VI's weak French position increased the value of that symbolic victory.
- Joan was held by English guards, but heresy charges were placed before a church tribunal led by Pierre Cauchon, with extensive preparation and participation by French clerics aligned with the Anglo-Burgundian cause.
- Procedural seriousness and political bias coexisted: the episode rejects both a neutral legal-process reading and the idea that the proceeding was only a crude theatrical sham.
- Repeated questioning focused on Joan's voices, the sign allegedly given to Charles VII, obedience to the church, and male clothing because those issues could convert private religious conviction into legal vulnerability.
- Joan's public abjuration changed the sentence to perpetual imprisonment, but her resumption of male dress and renewed claim to hear St Catherine and St Margaret supported treatment as a relapsed heretic.
- Joan's fear, requests for a crucifix, and invocation of Jesus at the stake made her death morally powerful without requiring a story of fearless martyrdom.
- French military recovery, the Burgundian reconciliation of 1435, and Charles VII's capture of Rouen changed the political conditions under which the 1456 retrial annulled the original conviction.

## Key Quotes

> “fatal reply” - the episode's description of the clerk's annotation after Joan reaffirmed that she had heard her voices following the abjuration.

> “corrupt, deceitful, slanderous, fraudulent, and malicious” - the episode's summary of the 1456 judges' condemnation of the original case.

## Contradictions

- The episode complicates, rather than simply contradicts, a show-trial interpretation: the political need for conviction was strong, but Cauchon's preparation and use of inquisitorial procedure were more substantial than a crude predetermined performance.
- Joan's motives for abjuring, resuming male clothing, and reaffirming her voices remain disputed; coercion, prison safety, disappointment about release, renewed conviction, and preference for death over lifelong confinement may overlap.
- The saints' identities, the golden-crown sign, witness reactions, the unburned-heart story, and private motives attributed to Joan, Cauchon, Bedford, Warwick, Beaufort, or Charles remain source-scoped rather than independently established here.
