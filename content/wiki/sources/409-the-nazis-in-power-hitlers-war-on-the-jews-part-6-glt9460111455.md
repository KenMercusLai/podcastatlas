# 409. The Nazis in Power: Hitler's War on the Jews (Part 6)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode traces how [[NaziGermany|Nazi Germany]] moved from [[AdolfHitler]]'s longstanding eliminationist antisemitism into violence, professional exclusion, racial classification, and the [[NurembergLaws|Nuremberg Laws]] between 1933 and 1936. Its central mechanism is [[WorkingTowardsTheFuhrer|working towards the Führer]]: local activists and institutions tested increasingly radical measures while Hitler supplied ideological direction and sometimes converted disorder into national law.

The episode also develops [[IncrementalPersecutionFalseStabilization|incremental persecution and false stabilization]]. Failed boycotts, temporary restraints, ambiguous definitions, and the 1936 Olympic pause repeatedly allowed victims and bystanders to hope that the worst had passed even as legal, bureaucratic, educational, and social exclusion deepened.

## Key Claims

- Hitler's exterminatory rhetoric and presentation of Jews as a mortal racial enemy preceded Nazi rule, although immediate mass murder was not the regime's operational policy in 1933.
- The April 1933 national boycott faltered, but local intimidation, professional restrictions, school quotas, public signage, and communal exclusion continued to normalize persecution.
- [[WorkingTowardsTheFuhrer|Working towards the Führer]] joined broad ideological direction to decentralized initiative, allowing local Nazis to radicalize policy without a detailed order for every action.
- The regime's racial claims collided with the practical difficulty of defining Jewish identity, so ancestry records, religious affiliation, local knowledge, officials, judges, doctors, and genealogical work became instruments of classification.
- The [[NurembergLaws|Nuremberg Laws]] replaced some uncontrolled street disorder with systematic legal exclusion from citizenship, marriage, sexuality, and domestic employment; legal form intensified persecution rather than moderating it.
- [[VictorKlemperer]] illustrates why many German Jews stayed: German identity, family roots, military service, assets, immigration barriers, and deceptive pauses could outweigh the perceived urgency to flee.
- By 1936, professional expulsion, denunciation, school segregation, and ordinary social withdrawal had isolated Jewish Germans even while Olympic image management temporarily concealed open antisemitism.

## Key Quotes

> “working towards the Führer” - Ian Kershaw's formulation, used by the episode to explain competitive local radicalization under broad ideological direction.

> “taking Hitler seriously, but not literally” - the episode's formulation for a tragic misreading encouraged by gradual escalation and temporary pauses.

## Connections

- [[AdolfHitler]], [[JosephGoebbels]], and [[HeinrichHimmler]] - ideological direction, inflammatory mobilization, and growing SS-police enforcement.
- [[NurembergLaws]] - legal consolidation of racial citizenship and intimate-life exclusion in 1935.
- [[VictorKlemperer]] - case showing German belonging, war service, loss, and the difficulty of recognizing when flight had become urgent.
- [[AdolfEichmann]] - early SS involvement in coerced Jewish emigration efforts linked to Palestine.
- [[WorkingTowardsTheFuhrer]] - interaction between leader direction, local initiative, and competitive radicalization.
- [[IncrementalPersecutionFalseStabilization]] - fits-and-starts pattern that deepened exclusion while producing deceptive pauses.
- [[DistributedComplicityUnderAuthoritarianism]] and [[RationalizedStateViolence]] - participation and administrative mechanisms extended by the episode.
- [[TheHolocaust]] - later genocide whose ideological, legal, and institutional preconditions were being built.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content was found. The episode backfills the 1933–36 sequence before the existing [[410-the-nazis-in-power-the-night-of-broken-glass-part-7-glt5771265943|Part 7]] account of 1937–39 escalation.
- Early Nazi “removal” policy could mean expulsion, resettlement, or forced emigration; the episode does not establish that the later extermination system was already an operational blueprint in 1933.
- Nazi biological-race claims were internally incoherent and administratively unstable; religious records, ancestry rules, prejudice, and local knowledge substituted for the objective racial science the regime claimed to possess.
- Public attitudes, private motives, the degree of centralized control over particular local actions, and some numerical emigration estimates remain source-scoped.
