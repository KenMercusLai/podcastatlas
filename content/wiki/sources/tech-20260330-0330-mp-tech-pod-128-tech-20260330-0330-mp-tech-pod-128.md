## Summary
This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode has [[StephanieHughes]] interview [[NolaAgha]] of the [[UniversityOfSanFrancisco]] about [[MajorLeagueBaseball]]'s [[AutomatedBallStrikeChallengeSystem|Automated Ball Strike Challenge System]], or ABS. The episode explains how camera tracking, player challenges, and personalized strike-zone calibration are being added to the traditionally human task of calling balls and strikes.

The core synthesis is that [[SportsOfficiatingAutomation]] is being adopted as a legitimacy tool rather than as a simple replacement for umpires. ABS can make close calls more standardized and reviewable, but the source keeps [[BaseballStrikeZoneAutomation]] bounded by imperfect tracking, rulebook translation choices, fan culture, player and umpire feedback, and the rule that the human umpire's call stands when the system fails to record a pitch correctly.

## Key Claims
- [[MajorLeagueBaseball|MLB]] has introduced ABS as a challenge system rather than a fully automated strike-zone replacement.
- A batter, catcher, or pitcher can challenge a human umpire's ball-strike call by tapping his hat.
- The system uses cameras to decide whether a pitch passed through the batter's strike zone.
- [[NolaAgha]] says the plate is 17 inches wide and that [[Hawkeye]] has worked with MLB on ball tracking after years of system testing.
- The episode says the top of the ABS zone is set at 53.5% of a player's height and the bottom at 27%.
- The rulebook strike zone is three-dimensional, while the ABS zone described in the episode is two-dimensional.
- MLB testing found the middle of the plate to be the most accurate two-dimensional location for judging balls and strikes.
- Agha argues that fans already expect tracking in baseball, golf, tennis, football, and daily life, making digital review feel less exotic.
- The source frames accuracy and fairness as more important than preserving every part of baseball's older human-officiating ambiguity.
- Agha says human umpires should not expect immediate replacement because tracking technology is imperfect and missed or misrecorded pitches leave the umpire's call standing.
- The episode says spring-training exposure produced fan interest in more automation, including a reported 70% preference for fully automated ABS among some surveyed fans.
- MLB is expected to consider player feedback, umpire feedback, and detailed umpire-accuracy data when deciding how ABS evolves.
