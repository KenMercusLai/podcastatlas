# 25 东西方的卖官鬻爵

## Summary

This [[WeirdHistoryPodcast|怪东西 Weird History]] episode compares the monetization of rank, office, military commission, noble status, and church position across Qin-Han China, Ming-Qing donation systems, ancien-régime France, the Habsburg lands, Britain, and the Christian church. Its central distinction is between purchasable honor or status and governing office: both can raise money or absorb new wealth elites, but direct control over public authority creates the strongest competence, extraction, and legitimacy risks.

The episode extends [[RankSaleDisasterFinance|rank-sale disaster finance]] beyond a single emergency and qualifies [[OfficeSaleLegitimacyDamage|office-sale legitimacy damage]]. Repeated war, famine, disaster, and weak revenue systems can turn exceptional sales into routine income, while buyers may seek legal privilege, social standing, or access rather than immediate governing power. The mechanism can widen mobility and recruit capable outsiders, but those benefits do not make payment a reliable selection test or remove the risk of title inflation, patronage, and cost recovery from the governed.

## Key Claims

- Qin and Han rank systems made low status grades available beyond hereditary aristocracy; grain-for-rank policies converted honor and privilege into emergency resources without always transferring governing office.
- Repeated fiscal use could widen the menu from low rank and nominal posts to high office, as illustrated by [[HanWudi|Han Wudi]]'s war finance, [[EmperorLingOfHan|Emperor Ling]]'s priced senior appointments, and [[CaoSong|Cao Song]]'s reported purchase of a Three Excellencies post.
- Ming-Qing donation systems formalized prices for titles, student status, honors, and appointment eligibility; a purchased designation often did not include an active post, leaving buyers to wait or seek placement through relationships.
- States sold status and office not only for revenue but also to absorb merchants and surplus degree-holders into the established order, while buyers sought dress, ceremony, legal privilege, family honor, and social mobility.
- Individual merit must be separated from the selection mechanism: the episode names Sheng Xuanhuai, Zhang Yinhuan, Li Wei, Deng Shichang, Yang Shenxiu, and Xu Xilin as purchasers whose later records cannot be inferred from payment alone.
- French robe nobility and Habsburg military ennoblement show status purchase or fee payment admitting professional and bourgeois entrants into elite orders while binding new elites more closely to monarchy and state service.
- British Army commission purchase combined a financial stake, regimental social screening, private premiums, and non-purchase wartime vacancies; technical corps and the Royal Navy followed different personnel systems.
- Simony shows the same market logic reaching religious office, where payment raised a distinct question about whether sacramental acts and appointments obtained through corruption remained valid.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The source broadens existing emergency-finance and late-Han corruption pages by distinguishing rank, honor, eligibility, sinecure, and active governing office rather than treating every purchase as the same institution.
- The episode's strongest tension is internal rather than factual: payment can widen entry and include capable people, but it remains a poor independent test of fitness and can still damage public trust or encourage extraction.
- Prices, revenue shares, post counts, purchaser numbers, dates, legal privileges, appointment rules, military vacancy practices, the causes of British abolition, and all named career outcomes remain episode-attributed pending specialist verification.
- Cross-regional similarity establishes a recurring fiscal and status mechanism, not institutional equivalence among Qin-Han ranks, Qing donation, French robe nobility, Habsburg ennoblement, British commissions, and Christian ecclesiastical office.
