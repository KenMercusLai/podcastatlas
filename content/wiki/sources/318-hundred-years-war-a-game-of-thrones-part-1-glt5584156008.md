# 318: Hundred Years' War: A Game of Thrones (Part 1)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode opens a four-part [[HundredYearsWar|Hundred Years' War]] sequence by explaining why conflict between [[England]] and [[France]] became likely before formal war began in 1337. It contrasts [[ChivalricRomanceWarMemory|chivalric romance memory]], represented by [[ArthurConanDoyle|Arthur Conan Doyle]]'s *Sir Nigel*, with catastrophe-focused war memory, represented by Barbara Tuchman's *A Distant Mirror*.

The episode argues that [[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]]'s rejected claim to the French throne was only one layer of the crisis. Deeper pressures included France's wealth and prestige, England's remaining continental interests, the unstable legal status of [[Gascony]] inside [[Aquitaine]], the [[FrancoScottishAlliance|Franco-Scottish alliance]], and the succession rupture from the [[CapetianDynasty|Capetians]] to [[PhilipVIOfFrance|Philip VI]] and the [[ValoisDynasty|Valois]] line.

## Key Claims

- The [[HundredYearsWar]] is framed as a 116-year conflict, from 1337 to 1453, rather than a literal century.
- The hosts set up two interpretive lenses: adventure, knights, and patriotic longbow memory on one side, and mercenary violence, pillage, plague-era devastation, and social breakdown on the other.
- Early-14th-century [[France]] is presented as the dominant western European monarchy: richer, more populous, administratively stronger, and culturally prestigious compared with [[England]].
- The [[CapetianDynasty]] had produced an unusually stable father-to-son succession before Philip IV's sons died without surviving sons, making the succession crisis politically explosive.
- [[England]] is presented as weaker than France but more nationally consolidated after losing most Angevin holdings, while still retaining continental leverage through [[Gascony]], wool ties to Flanders, and links with Brittany.
- [[FeudalHomageSovereigntyConflict]] is central: the English king ruled in England but owed homage to the French king for Gascony, leaving Gascon appeals and French forfeiture threats as recurring structural irritants.
- [[EdwardIIOfEngland|Edward II]]'s weakness, the 1324 French invasion of Aquitaine, [[IsabellaOfFrance|Isabella]] and [[RogerMortimer|Roger Mortimer]]'s takeover, and the 1328 Scottish treaty left young Edward III with inherited humiliation to reverse.
- The 1328 French succession produces [[CapetianSuccessionCrisis]] because Edward III's claim through Isabella is rejected in favor of Philip of Valois before female-line exclusion has become fully settled law in the episode's account.
- [[Scotland]] turns Anglo-French rivalry into a two-front problem: Edward III's victory at [[HallidonHill|Hallidon Hill]] sends David II to France and forces Philip VI to treat Scottish rights as part of Anglo-French diplomacy.
- Philip VI's May 1337 confiscation of Aquitaine functions in the episode as the effective declaration of war, after which Edward III secures parliamentary backing and issues a manifesto tying royal claims to English interests.

## Key Quotes

> "little" - the episode's shorthand for how Italian observers compared England's king with the king of France.

> "shameful peace" - English label for the 1328 Scottish settlement made under Mortimer.

> "declaration of war" - the hosts' interpretation of Philip VI's confiscation of Aquitaine.
