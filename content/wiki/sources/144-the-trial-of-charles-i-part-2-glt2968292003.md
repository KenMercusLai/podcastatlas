# 144. The Trial of Charles I Part 2

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode with [[TedVallance|Ted Vallance]] follows [[CharlesIOfEngland|Charles I]] from the specially created High Court of Justice through sentence, execution, and political afterlife. It presents the proceeding as an improvised [[PoliticalTrialSovereigntyPerformance|sovereignty performance]]: because ordinary English law supplied no straightforward mechanism for trying a king, the prosecution joined laws-of-war reasoning, command responsibility, witness testimony, crowd management, and visible reversals of hierarchy to claim that even an anointed monarch could answer to the people.

The execution did not secure one public meaning. Charles's courtroom and scaffold composure, the publication of *Eikon Basilike*, European horror, blood relics, republican celebration, radical hope, and Restoration punishment made the regicide both a founding rupture and a [[PoliticalExecutionMemoryContest|memory contest]] that strengthened royalist martyrdom while preserving a later republican legacy.

## Key Claims

- The High Court was extraordinary in both legal basis and structure: [[JohnBradshaw]] presided over commissioners who acted as judge and jury, while the case relied more on laws of war, command responsibility, and Charles's command of royalist armies than on an ordinary common-law route.
- Charles's covered head, refusal to plead, claim to defend ancient liberties, fallen cane finial, and challenge to the court turned jurisdiction into public theatre; low-status witnesses and commissioners who withheld deference visibly reversed political hierarchy.
- Witness hearings and the staggered collection of death-warrant signatures preserve uncertainty within an outcome strongly pressured by army petitioners and rank-and-file demands for judgment of a tyrant.
- The Whitehall scaffold site, masked executioners, controlled access, shorthand recording, and security fears show a regime trying to stage lawful authority while fearing intervention by the public it claimed to represent.
- Charles's composure, final religious language, blood relics, and *Eikon Basilike* enabled an immediate royalist martyr narrative that Parliamentarian counter-publication could not match in popularity.
- Republican commemoration treated 1649 as restored freedom, while [[GerardWinstanley]] and other radicals read regicide as an opening against kingly power; the Restoration later punished surviving regicides and forced others into exile.
- The execution outlived the Commonwealth as both royalist national memory and a republican precedent available to later revolutionary traditions.
