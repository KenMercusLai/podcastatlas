# 112. Medieval Science

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] conversation with historian [[SebFalk|Seb Falk]] rejects the familiar picture of a scientifically empty European Middle Ages between classical learning and the Renaissance. Because modern professional science did not yet exist, the episode instead follows [[MedievalScientificPractice|systematic knowledge-making]] through astronomy, mathematics, textual commentary, instruments, calculation, observation, theology, and natural philosophy.

The life of [[JohnOfWestwick|John of Westwick]], together with equatoria, astrolabes, monastic clocks, translated Aristotle, Abbasid scholarship, and [[NicolausCopernicus|Copernicus]], supports [[MedievalScienceContinuity|a continuity argument]] rather than a story of sudden rebirth. The episode also stresses [[PremodernScienceReligionEntanglement|the entanglement of religious and natural inquiry]] and the importance of Greek, Syriac, Arabic, Persian, Central Asian, and Latin transmission networks.

## Key Claims

- “Medieval” and “science” are later categories that can conceal both Renaissance polemic and real differences between premodern knowledge-making and modern professional science.
- The decline of learning in parts of post-Roman western Europe is explained mainly through economic and communications breakdown rather than a blanket Christian rejection of knowledge.
- Medieval astronomy joined calculation, observation, astrology, medicine, timekeeping, and instruments such as astrolabes and equatoria.
- [[JohnOfWestwick|John of Westwick]] is valuable as a non-heroic case of copying, glossing, translating, adapting, traveling, and constructing computational instruments.
- Monastic schedules encouraged timekeeping, but complex astronomical clocks also modeled a divinely ordered cosmos and exceeded immediate practical need.
- [[AbbasidTranslationMovement|Islamicate scholarly networks]] transformed and transmitted Greek, Indian, Persian, Central Asian, Syriac, and Arabic learning; European science developed within this cross-cultural circulation.
- [[NicolausCopernicus|Copernicus]] retained ancient and medieval commitments, drew on earlier mathematical resources, and initiated debate rather than instantly replacing an obsolete system.
- The modern ideal of designed, repeatable experiment emerged gradually from older practices of experience, calculation, argument, observation, and model criticism.

## Connections

- [[SebFalk]] - historian using ordinary practice, instruments, and transmission to challenge the scientific-dark-age story.
- [[JohnOfWestwick]] - monk and instrument maker who anchors the episode's account of medieval knowledge work.
- [[MedievalScienceContinuity]] - historiographical framework replacing disappearance-and-rebirth with uneven preservation, adaptation, and change.
- [[MedievalScientificPractice]] - joined practice of texts, calculation, observation, instruments, and skilled construction.
- [[PremodernScienceReligionEntanglement]] - explains why theology and natural inquiry cannot be projected into a modern science-versus-religion binary.
- [[AbbasidTranslationMovement]] - cross-confessional and multilingual network central to the movement of learned traditions.
- [[Aristotle]] and [[NicolausCopernicus]] - major figures whose reception illustrates criticism and continuity rather than passive authority or abrupt rupture.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content was found. The episode extends the wiki's institutional and transmission-centered history of science.
- Its rejection of a “dark age” does not claim that medieval practice was identical to modern science or that learning was uninterrupted everywhere; professional specialization, laboratories, designed repeatability, and a strong norm of novelty developed later.
- The exact technology behind early clock references, John of Westwick's biography and equatorium attribution, the motives behind individual religious positions, and the transmission route from al-Tusi's geometry to Copernicus remain uncertain or source-scoped.
- “The church,” “Islamic science,” and “the scientific revolution” each cover internally diverse institutions, regions, periods, and practices that the episode necessarily compresses.
