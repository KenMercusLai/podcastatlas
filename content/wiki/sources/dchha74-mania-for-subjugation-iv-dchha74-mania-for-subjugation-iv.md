# Show 74 - Mania for Subjugation IV

## Summary

This [[HardcoreHistory]] episode follows [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]] from the aftermath of Granicus through western Anatolia, the sieges of [[Miletus]] and [[Halicarnassus]], and the [[BattleOfIssus]]. Its central synthesis is that speed, shared danger, personal attention, and repeated victory gradually turn [[PhilipIIMacedon|Philip II's]] inherited army into Alexander's own instrument, while terrain, supply, formation cohesion, and panic explain victory more reliably than heroic reputation alone.

The episode also treats conquest as politically and morally cumulative. Alexander's liberation claim meets local factionalism and resistance; celebrated battles sit within sieges, executions, collective punishment, and post-rout killing; and Issus gives him wealth, prestige, and custody of [[DariusIII|Darius III's]] family while raising the question of whether success and royal power are expanding his aims beyond any attainable stopping point.

## Key Claims

- Alexander builds a personal bond with an inherited army through shared campaigning, visible risk, attention to soldiers, relentless movement, and repeated success.
- Miletus shows him refusing battle in Persia's strongest medium, using harbor control and land operations to neutralize superior naval power before controversially disbanding most of his fleet.
- Halicarnassus shows siege warfare suppressing Macedonian cavalry and phalanx advantages; the costly result leaves [[MemnonOfRhodes|Memnon of Rhodes]] and Persian naval power active.
- Darius's royal army depends on imperial roads, depots, supply organization, ceremony, and political display as well as battlefield strength; ancient force totals remain unreliable.
- At Issus, constricted ground reduces Persian numerical and cavalry advantages, while disciplined Macedonian deployment preserves alignment during the approach.
- Alexander's right-wing breakthrough coexists with a near-disaster in the center, where Greek mercenaries exploit gaps in the river-crossing phalanx before a flank response stabilizes it.
- Darius's departure triggers a morale cascade and bottlenecked rout in which most killing occurs after organized resistance has collapsed.
- Arrian, Diodorus, Curtius, Plutarch, and modern historians preserve incompatible, culturally biased, and often dramatic accounts, so numbers, speeches, motives, and exact tactical sequences require explicit qualification.
