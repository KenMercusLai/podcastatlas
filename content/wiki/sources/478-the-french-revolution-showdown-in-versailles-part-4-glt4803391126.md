# 478. The French Revolution: Showdown in Versailles (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode traces the [[FrenchRevolution|French Revolution]] from the 1788 harvest disaster and fiscal crisis through the [[EstatesGeneral1789|Estates-General]], the creation of the [[NationalAssembly1789|National Assembly]], and the [[TennisCourtOath|Tennis Court Oath]]. It presents June 1789 as a sovereignty break produced by converging environmental, subsistence, financial, institutional, and political pressures rather than by a single cause or an entirely inactive monarchy.

The episode also emphasizes expectation and spectacle. The invitation to submit grievances, pamphlet politics, court ceremony, public galleries, rumor, and troop movements made constitutional procedure visible to a mass audience, while [[LouisXVI|Louis XVI]] repeatedly offered partial reform without resolving whether the estates or the nation held sovereign authority. His final concession to a united Assembly did not restore control, and [[JacquesNecker|Jacques Necker]]'s dismissal on 11 July converted the unresolved settlement into the immediate prehistory of the Bastille.

## Key Claims

- The July 1788 hailstorm, drought, failed harvest, severe winter, high bread prices, rural debt, migration, and urban unemployment intensified a monarchy already trapped by fiscal and political crisis.
- Royal, ecclesiastical, lottery-funded, and employer relief complicate a simple picture of elite indifference, but relief could not match the scale of scarcity or raised expectations.
- Necker returned as a trusted financial outsider who could borrow time until the Estates-General met, yet his compromise doubled Third Estate representation without settling whether voting would occur by head or by order.
- Roughly 25,000 grievance lists expanded participation and expectation while exposing demands that could not all be satisfied together.
- [[EmmanuelJosephSieyes|Emmanuel-Joseph Sieyes]] recast the Third Estate as the complete nation, turning a dispute over procedure into a claim about sovereignty and privilege.
- The [[ReveillonRiot|Réveillon riot]] shows rumor acting upon real hardship: a false wage claim helped trigger destruction and lethal repression despite Jean-Baptiste Réveillon's paternalistic employment record.
- Court dress, processions, entrances, seating, and public galleries made hierarchy and constitutional conflict into political theatre before a national audience.
- On 17 June the Third Estate declared itself the [[NationalAssembly1789|National Assembly]], claimed authority over taxation, and displaced representation by estate with representation of the nation.
- The [[TennisCourtOath|Tennis Court Oath]] transformed exclusion from a meeting hall into a collective promise not to disperse before France had a constitution.
- Louis offered substantial reforms on 23 June but rejected the Assembly's sovereign claim; his refusal to use force after [[HonoreGabrielRiquetiDeMirabeau|Mirabeau]]'s defiance and his order uniting the estates on 27 June marked royal retreat without a stable settlement.
- Necker's dismissal amid troop concentrations made coercive counterrevolution seem imminent and connects this constitutional showdown directly to the Paris mobilization of 14 July.

## Key Quotes

> “Everything” - Sieyes's answer to what the Third Estate was, as presented by the episode.

> “We will not leave except at the point of bayonets” - Mirabeau's defiance after the royal session, as paraphrased by the episode.
