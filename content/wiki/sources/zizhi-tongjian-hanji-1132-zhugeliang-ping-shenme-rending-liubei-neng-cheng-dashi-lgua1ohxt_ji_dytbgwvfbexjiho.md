# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》1132丨诸葛亮凭什么认定刘备能成大事？

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode retrospectively joins [[ZhugeLiang|诸葛亮]]'s [[LongzhongPlan|隆中对]] to [[LiuBei|刘备]]'s later acquisition of territory in [[Jingzhou|荆州]] and [[YizhouLateHan|益州]]. Its answer to why Zhuge Liang chose an itinerant and materially weak patron is that Liu Bei's claimed descent from the Han royal house supplied [[ClaimantLineagePoliticalCapital|宗室谱系政治资本]]: a recognizable legitimacy story around which advisers, soldiers, local supporters, and a restoration program could organize.

The episode then compresses Liu Bei's entry into Yizhou, rupture with [[LiuZhang|刘璋]], military advance, and eventual founding of Shu-Han into a long-horizon execution story. It also complicates the benevolent-ruler image by arguing that reputation constrained how Liu Bei justified expansion without eliminating strategic deception, insider defection, or force.

## Key Claims

- The episode presents the Longzhong Plan as a staged program: establish a base through Jingzhou and Yizhou, cooperate against [[CaoCao|曹操]], and preserve Han restoration as the coalition's legitimating objective.
- Liu Bei's claimed descent from [[LiuShengZhongshanKingWesternHan|中山王刘胜]] is treated as politically useful even though the genealogical distance is large and the episode does not independently verify the full lineage.
- In the host's interpretation, Han-clan identity differentiates Liu Bei from Cao Cao's control of the emperor and Sun Quan's regional family power, giving Zhuge Liang a claimant around whom a restoration project can be narrated.
- The episode reads that identity as recruitment and coalition capital rather than a guarantee of competence: Liu Bei still needs advisers, soldiers, territory, resources, and years of execution.
- After Red Cliffs, Liu Bei's southern Jingzhou gains and later territorial settlement supply the intermediate base from which the Yizhou branch becomes possible.
- Liu Zhang's invitation gives Liu Bei entry, delegated capacity, and local access; the episode then presents requests for troops and provisions, Liu Zhang's partial refusal, and Liu Bei's public accusation as the immediate pretext for open war.
- The rejection of 郑度's scorched-earth proposal, followed by defections from Liu Zhang's side, is used to explain why the defense collapses without reducing the outcome to one battle.
- Liu Bei's 221 imperial claim is presented as the culmination of a fourteen-year arc from the Longzhong meeting, with the Han name preserving continuity rather than openly founding an unrelated dynasty.
- The closing “late success” and workplace lessons are modern motivational analogies, not independent evidence for historical motive or causation.

## Key Quotes

> “兴复汉室。” - the political objective through which the episode connects Liu Bei's lineage claim to the Longzhong program.

> “先取荆州，再取益州。” - the host's compressed territorial sequence for the long-horizon plan.

- [[LiuShengZhongshanKingWesternHan|中山王刘胜]] - claimed ancestor used to ground Liu Bei's Han-clan identity, without reopening Liu Sheng's own profile.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The source mostly compresses and interprets events already developed in Hanji 1082-1109 and 1124-1126.
- Genealogy caution: the episode treats Liu Bei as a descendant of Liu Sheng but also acknowledges a gap of more than ten generations; the exact chain and the political recognition attached to it require direct historical verification.
- Chronology and jurisdiction caution: the Jingzhou acquisition, “borrowing” of Nan Commandery, 215 boundary settlement, Yizhou campaign sequence, force transfers, offices, dates, and the fourteen-year symmetry are popular-history compression rather than a complete reconstruction.
- Motive caution: Zhuge Liang's reason for choosing Liu Bei, Liu Bei's original intent on entering Yizhou, the manufacture of a war pretext, and Liu Zhang's or defectors' inner calculations remain interpretations rather than demonstrated private motives.
- Ethical caution: the episode's contrast between benevolent reputation and strategic conduct usefully complicates a simple hero image, but it does not prove that every act of restraint was cynical image management or that every harsh choice was strategically necessary.
