# 407. The Nazis in Power: The Conquest of Austria (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode reconstructs the March 1938 [[Anschluss|Anschluss]], following [[AdolfHitler]] from expansion planning and the removal of senior army restraints through the intimidation of [[KurtVonSchuschnigg]], diplomatic isolation, invasion, and the complete absorption and plunder of [[Austria]] by [[NaziGermany|Nazi Germany]]. It uses [[AustrianFirstVictimNarrative|Austria's “first victim” narrative]] to hold together two truths: Austria lost its state, resources, and political independence under coercion, while many Austrian Nazis and ordinary citizens welcomed Hitler or participated in the persecution of Jews.

The immediate aftermath connects foreign aggression to domestic radicalization. Mass detention, street humiliation, seizure of Jewish businesses, flight barriers, and [[AdolfEichmann]]'s centralized forced-emigration apparatus made Austria a proving ground for methods later extended across the Reich and occupied Europe.

## Key Claims

- Hitler's November 1937 expansion planning identified Austria and Czechoslovakia as early targets, while the Blomberg-Fritsch removals weakened senior military resistance to risky action.
- Austria's small post-Habsburg state contained competing socialist, Catholic-authoritarian, pan-German, and Nazi currents; the Allies had blocked union with Germany after 1918.
- [[BenitoMussolini]]'s move from defending Austrian independence in 1934 to accepting German expansion after the Abyssinian crisis removed a major external constraint.
- Hitler used the Berghof meeting, cabinet demands, Austrian Nazi pressure, and invasion threats to break Schuschnigg's position; Britain offered no guarantee of protection.
- German troops met substantial public enthusiasm on 12 March, but organized attendance, intimidation, opportunism, fear, and genuine Nazi or pan-German commitment cannot be reduced to one motive.
- Annexation erased Austrian statehood, subordinated its institutions, and transferred its gold, foreign currency, banks, transport, military capacity, art, and raw materials to Germany.
- Austrian Nazis, neighbors, police, and crowds participated in anti-Jewish violence and humiliation, while the regime seized businesses, detained opponents, and trapped or dispossessed people trying to flee.
- Eichmann's Vienna emigration office turned forced departure into centralized administrative persecution and anticipated later Nazi systems without making the later extermination program a fixed March 1938 blueprint.

## Key Quotes

> “first victim” - the postwar Austrian claim that the episode treats as partly true but radically incomplete.

> “free, German, independent and social, Christian and united Austria” - the loaded independence formula attributed to Schuschnigg's proposed referendum.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content was found. The episode fills the March 1938 step between the 1933–36 escalation in Part 6 and the Anschluss references in Part 7.
- Austria was coerced, occupied, plundered, and erased as a state, but those facts do not establish that Austrian society was uniformly resistant or merely passive.
- Cheering crowds do not by themselves measure every Austrian's settled beliefs; enthusiasm, organization, fear, novelty, opportunism, pan-German feeling, and Nazi commitment could overlap.
- Crowd sizes, private motives, exact influence of particular generals, legal drafting details, and some detention, population, and emigration figures remain source-scoped.
