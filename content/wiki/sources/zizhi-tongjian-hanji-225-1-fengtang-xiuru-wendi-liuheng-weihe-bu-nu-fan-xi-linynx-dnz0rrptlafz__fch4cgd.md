# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》225-1｜冯唐羞辱文帝，刘恒为何不怒反喜？

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode places [[FengTangWesternHan|冯唐]]'s famous rebuke inside a 166 BCE [[Xiongnu|匈奴]] invasion and an inconclusive Han response. When [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] wishes for generals like [[LianPo|廉颇]] and [[LiMu|李牧]], Feng Tang says that even if they were alive the emperor could not use them; Liu Heng first leaves in anger, then privately asks for the reasoning instead of treating wounded dignity as the end of the exchange.

Feng Tang's answer joins command discretion to credible incentives. He contrasts Li Mu's authority over frontier revenue and troop rewards with [[WeiShangWesternHan|魏尚]]'s imprisonment for overreporting six enemy heads, arguing that the Han court rewards too lightly and punishes too heavily. Liu Heng admits error, sends Feng Tang with a tally to pardon Wei Shang, and appoints Feng Tang as chariot-and-cavalry commandant. The closing notices add Feng Tang's later old-age reputation and Liu Heng's order that sacrificial prayers no longer reserve blessing for the emperor alone.

## Key Claims

- A large Xiongnu incursion reaches the Beidi-Ganquan approach, kills the Beidi commandant, and prompts layered Han deployments and a proposed imperial expedition, but the returning Han army does not inflict a decisive loss.
- Feng Tang converts Liu Heng's wish for famous generals into a criticism of the ruler's own command system: capable commanders are ineffective if the center withholds real authority or makes field responsibility legally dangerous.
- Li Mu's frontier success is attributed to control over local revenue, troop provisioning, rewards, training, and operational decisions; Zhao's later killing of Li Mu becomes the failure contrast.
- Wei Shang reportedly uses tax revenue and part of his salary to feed and reward Yunzhong troops, making that frontier harder for Xiongnu forces to approach.
- The court nevertheless imprisons Wei Shang for overreporting six enemy heads, which Feng Tang uses to condemn a system of light reward and heavy punishment.
- Liu Heng's strongest contribution is not never becoming angry, but reopening the conversation, asking for reasons, admitting error, pardoning Wei Shang, and appointing Feng Tang.
- The closing sacrificial edict extends Liu Heng's self-restraining posture by rejecting prayers that assign divine blessing only to the emperor and exclude the people.

## Key Quotes

> “虽有廉颇、李牧，不能用也。” - Feng Tang's reported challenge to Liu Heng's claim that famous generals would solve the frontier problem.

> “赏太轻，罚太重。” - the episode's condensed diagnosis of the court's treatment of frontier merit and error.

## Connections

- [[FengTangWesternHan|冯唐]] - elderly palace officer whose historical analogy and Wei Shang case turn an insult into a concrete personnel correction.
- [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] - ruler who moves from anger to inquiry, admission of error, pardon, and appointment.
- [[WeiShangWesternHan|魏尚]] - Yunzhong governor whose troop support and punishment supply the real-policy test behind the exchange.
- [[LiMu|李牧]] and [[LianPo|廉颇]] - exemplary Zhao commanders used to shift discussion from finding talent to making talent usable.
- [[Xiongnu|匈奴]] - frontier threat that exposes the gap between emergency deployment and durable command capacity.
- [[ReceptiveRemonstranceGovernance|纳谏转政策式治理]] - the episode is a strong case because uncomfortable speech produces a pardon and appointment rather than mere tolerance.
- [[CommandAuthorityBottleneck|将权掣肘]] - Feng Tang identifies the mismatch between field responsibility and control over resources, rewards, and decisions.
- [[MeritBasedRewardPunishment|因功赏罚]] - Wei Shang's case shows how disproportionate punishment can erase the incentive value of military merit.
- [[ImperialSelfCriticismEdict|帝王自责诏]] - the closing prayer order turns exclusive imperial blessing into a declared source of ruler shame.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode adds an earlier frontier-remonstrance layer to Liu Heng's existing profile and is compatible with the later Xiliu case in which he also respects bounded military authority.
- The title compresses the emotional sequence: Liu Heng does become angry and leave; the important turn is that he later resumes inquiry and converts the criticism into action.
- The transcript's opening timestamp labels are irregular, and names such as 卢卿 and 魏遫, the campaign sequence, and force and casualty totals require comparison with transmitted histories.
- Li Mu's tax authority, the scope of a commander's independent reward power, Wei Shang's provisioning practice, the six-head discrepancy, exact offices, Feng Tang's later appointments and age, quoted dialogue, and the sacrificial edict remain episode-attributed and source-scoped.
