## Summary
This [[TheSocialRadars]] follow-up has [[BlakeScholl]] explain how [[BoomSupersonic]] moved from the public collapse of its [[RollsRoyce|Rolls-Royce]] engine plan to internal engine development, [[BoomlessCruise]], and a proposed 42-megawatt data-center turbine. The episode adds the strongest chronology yet for [[CrisisForcedVerticalIntegration]], shows [[Makeboom]] supporting [[AircraftPropulsionCoDesign|aircraft–propulsion co-design]], and frames the power product as a revenue, manufacturing, and talent bridge to [[OvertureSupersonicAirliner|Overture]]. It then broadens the case into a source-scoped argument that flexible commercial manufacturing can create [[CommercialManufacturingSurgeCapacity|national surge capacity]] without keeping incumbents alive solely through continuous defense work.

## Key Claims
- Scholl calls outsourcing Boom's critical engine technology a major mistake: the Rolls-Royce relationship failed amid leadership paralysis, dependence on a discontinued next-generation platform, and an abrupt public separation after American Airlines announced a preorder.
- He says the breakup made Boom temporarily “unfundable,” but a Florida team of former Pratt & Whitney specialists and [[BrianChesky]]'s crisis advice helped the company commit to its own engine architecture.
- Engine ownership improved transonic optimization enough that Boom's simulations revealed an atmospheric-cutoff route to [[BoomlessCruise]] that the proposed supplier engine could not support.
- In March 2025, AI data-center power shortages led Boom to validate a ground derivative; removing the fan and adding turbine stages plus a generator yields a proposed 42-megawatt unit, with about 24 units per gigawatt.
- The power product is presented as a bridge rather than a mission change: revenue could fund Overture, shared manufacturing could advance the engine core, and the supersonic mission could continue attracting engineers.
- Scholl distinguishes hard tech from software by the capital required before a decisive proof point, arguing that short funding runways can make physical development less efficient by preventing long-horizon decisions.
- [[Makeboom]] lets Boom wrap an aircraft design around alternative engine configurations and simulate a mission; Scholl says this co-design recovered most of a roughly 1,000-mile range loss created by a more ambitious cabin concept.
- Boom wants the aircraft experience to include no middle seats, rapid boarding and deplaning, and integrated Starlink hardware rather than treating connectivity as a drag-producing retrofit.
- Scholl argues that asset-light financial management, quarterly targets, regulation, and prestige-program procurement weakened aerospace and energy innovation, but these broad causal claims are not independently tested in the supplied source.
- Boom's proposed defense role is to remain a capable commercial manufacturer whose digital engineering and reconfigurable production can pivot during emergencies rather than depend on permanent government work.

## Key Quotes
> "unfundable" - Scholl's description of Boom after Rolls-Royce publicly ended the engine relationship.

> "blade servers for energy" - his analogy for modular arrays of the proposed 42-megawatt turbine.

> "no more moonshots" - the Boeing management posture Scholl interprets as a retreat from clean-sheet products.
