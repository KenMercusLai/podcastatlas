# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》350-1｜职场生存，这个道理必须懂！

## Summary

This short [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode joins two Western Han power mechanisms. [[GongsunQingFangshi|公孙卿]] uses a purported bamboo text, a precisely timed cauldron omen, and [[Huangdi|黄帝]]'s dragon ascent to satisfy [[HanWudi|汉武帝]]'s hope for immortality; Wudi responds with appointment and a divine-search mission, making the exchange an early case of [[ImmortalityQuestPolitics|求仙政治]].

The second branch describes the Longxi governor's suicide after an unexpectedly large imperial party leaves the emperor's attendants unfed. The host reads the terse notice through [[PalaceProximityPower|宫廷近身权力]]: the governor may have feared not only Wudi's severity but also future accusations from trusted attendants and may have treated his own death as protection for his household. That motive is interpretive rather than established by the supplied summary, and the episode provides no evidence that the attendants actually threatened him.

## Key Claims

- The opening distinguishes principled people from self-interested “小人” through the host's moral language of integrity under wealth, poverty, and coercion; this is framing rather than a verified classification of the historical actors.
- In 113 BCE, Wudi travels to Yong for sacrifice and considers advice to establish and personally worship at a Taiyi temple alongside the Five Emperors.
- Gongsun Qing reaches Wudi through a favored intermediary with a purported bamboo text claiming that Wudi's cauldron appeared at the same winter-solstice moment as Huangdi's.
- Gongsun Qing says Huangdi sacrificed, cast a cauldron, and later ascended on a dragon with more than seventy court and harem companions; Wudi welcomes the prospect even at the cost of leaving wife and children.
- Wudi appoints Gongsun Qing a court gentleman and sends him east to await a divine being at Taishi Mountain, converting an unverifiable sacred narrative into office and state action.
- In 112 BCE, Wudi crosses Longdi after sacrificing at Yong; the Longxi governor kills himself after the emperor's attendants lack provisions during the sudden visit.
- The host interprets the suicide as fear of the attendants' later influence and possible collective punishment, but the source does not establish the governor's reasoning, a threat, or the fate of his family.

## Key Quotes

> “富贵不能淫，贫贱不能移，威武不能屈。” - the episode's standard for integrity in its opening moral frame.

> “天子从官不得食，郡守自杀。” - the terse incident from which the host develops the proximity-power interpretation.

## Connections

- [[GongsunQingFangshi|公孙卿]], [[HanWudi|汉武帝]], and [[ImmortalityQuestPolitics|求仙政治]] - sacred genealogy and calendrical coincidence become appointment and a divine-search mission.
- [[Huangdi|黄帝]], [[AuspiciousOmenPolitics|祥瑞政治]], and [[Taiyi|太一／太乙]] - cauldron timing, ascent narrative, and imperial sacrifice make mythic precedent actionable at court.
- [[PalaceProximityPower|宫廷近身权力]] and [[MessageRelayPoliticalPower|传话式信息权力]] - trusted attendants may shape how a distant local official is later represented to the ruler.
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-350-2-weishenme-buneng-re-dongshizhang-mishu-lmxrw9cg4lsqzzbx_628vwqtwfgu|Hanji 350-2]] - direct continuation that generalizes the attendants' leverage through trust, proximity, delegated authority, and repeated reports.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. Hanji 350-1 supplies the concrete Longxi incident that Hanji 350-2 turns into a general account of trusted-attendant power.
- The cauldron's date, the bamboo text's provenance, Huangdi's ascent, the dragon story, and divine beings are reported claims rather than verified supernatural events.
- The exact temple proposal, the role of Wudi's favored intermediary, Gongsun Qing's wording and office, the Taishi Mountain mission, route details, and party size remain episode-attributed pending primary-source comparison.
- The governor's fear of later retaliation, choice of death to protect his family, and the host's modern workplace analogy are interpretations; the supplied source does not establish his motive or show that attendants threatened, accused, or punished him.
