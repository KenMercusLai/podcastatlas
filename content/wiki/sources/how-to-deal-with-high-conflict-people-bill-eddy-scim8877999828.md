# How to Deal With High Conflict People | Bill Eddy

## Summary
This [[HubermanLab]] interview has [[AndrewHuberman]] speak with lawyer, mediator, and therapist [[BillEddy]] about recognizing and responding to recurring high-conflict behavior without casually diagnosing or labeling people. Eddy distinguishes an observable pattern of blame, unmanaged emotion, all-or-nothing thinking, and unresolved escalation from formal personality-disorder diagnosis, then organizes practical response through [[HighConflictBehaviorPattern]] and [[HighConflictCommunicationToolkit]]. The central synthesis is that de-escalation requires adapting one's own contact, communication, limits, and exit planning while preserving safety exceptions for coercion or serious harm.

## Key Claims
- [[HighConflictBehaviorPattern]] is defined behaviorally: repeated blame, poorly regulated emotion, all-or-nothing thinking, and extreme actions keep conflict escalating rather than resolving.
- Eddy's WEB recognition method asks observers to notice Words, their own Emotions, and the other person's Behavior without converting those signals into an amateur diagnosis.
- A charming, credible, or professionally successful presentation does not rule out harmful close-relationship behavior; Eddy therefore recommends observing long-term relationships and allowing time before irreversible commitments.
- [[EmotionalContagion]] can recruit uninformed “negative advocates,” professionals, media audiences, or political groups into another person's fear and anger before they understand the full situation.
- The episode distinguishes high-conflict behavior from personality disorder: the categories may overlap, but neither one can be inferred reliably from a disliked act, a large personality, or public shorthand such as “narcissist.”
- [[HighConflictCommunicationToolkit]] combines connection through empathy, attention, and respect; analysis through choices and proposals; BIFF responses that are brief, informative, friendly, and firm; and limits paired with consequences.
- Eddy's “four forget-about-its” advise against trying to force insight, relitigating the past, dwelling on emotion, or applying labels when those moves predictably increase defensiveness and blame.
- Disengagement should avoid reciprocal blame and repeated emotional reopening, but situations involving physical danger, emotional abuse, stalking, or serious workplace harm may require rapid exit and professional safety planning.
- Parents can teach flexible thinking, emotion management, moderated behavior, and self-checking; the source also argues that online groups can reinforce adult bullying that face-to-face peers might otherwise correct.

## Key Quotes
> "Words, Emotions, and Behavior" - the three observation channels in Eddy's WEB method.

> "brief, informative, friendly, and firm" - the BIFF structure for containing a response.

> "Connecting, Analyzing, Responding, and Setting limits" - the four parts of the CARS method.

## Contradictions
- No settled contradiction found.
- The source qualifies symmetric conflict-repair models: curiosity and mutual repair may help ordinary rupture, but a persistent blame-and-escalation pattern can require structured limits, reduced contact, or exit rather than deeper emotional processing with the other person.
- The prevalence figures, diagnostic overlap estimates, institutional generalizations, family-size theory, social-media and school-shooter discussion, and Steve Jobs example are source-scoped claims rather than current systematic evidence about a validated “high-conflict personality” diagnosis.
- The episode is public education, not a basis for diagnosing a partner, colleague, relative, public figure, or oneself; legal, clinical, and safety decisions require situation-specific professional support.
