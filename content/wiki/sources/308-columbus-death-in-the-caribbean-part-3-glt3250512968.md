# 308: Columbus: Death in the Caribbean (Part 3)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode follows [[ChristopherColumbus|Christopher Columbus]] from his 1493 return through the [[ColumbusSecondVoyageColonization|second voyage]], the destruction of La Navidad, the founding and failure of [[LaIsabela]], escalating conflict on [[Hispaniola]], and his return to Spain seeking a third voyage. A fleet of 17 ships and roughly 1,500 mostly male settlers turns the venture from exploratory navigation into [[SpanishCaribbeanExpansion|state-backed colonization]], but weak administration, scarce gold, hunger, disease, enslavement, sexual violence, tribute demands, and resistance rapidly destabilize it.

The episode's central contradiction is [[ColonialConversionEnslavementContradiction]]: Ferdinand and [[IsabellaIOfCastile|Isabella I]] frame the [[TainoPeople|Taíno]] as subjects and potential Christians to be treated well, while Columbus and other colonists kidnap, exploit, and ship them to Spain. It also treats [[CannibalismColonialJustification|cannibalism claims]] as evidence-limited reports that could become instruments of coercion, and it presents Columbus's forced declaration that Cuba was Asian mainland as an attempt to suppress evidence threatening his commission and self-image.

## Key Claims

- Papal backing, Portuguese competition, royal sponsorship, settlement instructions, and the much larger fleet changed the second voyage from speculative exploration into a colonial project.
- Spain transferred settlement, conversion, and frontier assumptions shaped by the Canaries and Reconquista into the Caribbean, while religious concern and coercive practice remained in unresolved tension.
- The destruction of La Navidad and the poor siting, shortages, illness, and factional conflict at La Isabela exposed Columbus's weakness as a colonial administrator despite his maritime skill.
- Spaniards began kidnapping Indigenous people immediately; the source presents Miguel Cuneo's account of a captive Taíno woman as rape and as evidence of a wider pattern of sexual violence.
- Columbus turned toward captive export, mass seizure, tribute, gold extraction, cotton, and compelled labor when the colony failed to produce its promised return.
- Reports of human remains and cannibalism on Guadeloupe remain uncertain, while the category could help distinguish supposedly enslaveable enemies from peaceful potential converts.
- Taíno catastrophe emerged from interacting violence, captivity, coerced labor, ecological disruption, hunger, and later disease rather than one securely quantified mechanism or a settled claim about exterminatory intent.
- Isabella's objection to enslaving royal subjects, returning clerical criticism, the breaking of Columbus's monopoly, and Juan Aguado's investigation show that colonial conduct was politically and morally disputed at the time.
- Columbus's coerced Cuba declaration and providential biblical self-presentation show commercial, contractual, religious, and psychological pressures reinforcing geographical denial.

## Key Quotes

> "very well and lovingly" - the royal instruction whose protective and conversionary language contrasts with colonial conduct.

> "the beginning of the Asian continent" - the claim Columbus compelled his crew to affirm about Cuba under severe threatened penalties.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with [[309-columbus-villain-or-hero-part-4-glt6751575003|Part 4]] was found; this episode supplies the second-voyage settlement, tribute, enslavement, investigation, and geographical-denial prehistory for the later removal of Columbus as governor.
- The hosts say Spanish participants believed at least some cannibalism reports, but the surviving evidence does not establish their scope or accuracy and the category carried coercive political consequences.
- The source distinguishes catastrophic Taíno destruction from a narrow claim of genocidal intent, but that distinction does not reduce the documented violence, enslavement, sexual abuse, labor demands, hunger, or ecological harm.
- Las Casas's claim that Columbus destroyed two-thirds of Hispaniola's population is presented as a likely exaggeration; exact population baselines, mortality totals, and the relative shares of violence, labor, hunger, introduced animals, and disease remain unresolved.
- Syphilis origin and prevalence, reported dialogue, motives, precise settler totals, the truth of griffin and Prester John stories, and Columbus's mental state remain source-scoped popular-history claims.
