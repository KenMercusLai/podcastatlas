# 643. Rome's Greatest Enemy: Carthage Destroyed (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode traces how the [[RomanRepublic]] moved from defeating [[HannibalBarca]] to destroying [[Carthage]] in 146 BC. It argues that Carthage's military weakness after the Second Punic War did not erase Roman fear: [[RomanEnemyMemory]] joined [[Massinissa]]'s territorial pressure, [[CatoTheElder]]'s campaign, greed, and Carthage's unauthorized war to produce the [[ThirdPunicWar]].

The war itself becomes a study in coerced disarmament and annihilation. Rome demanded Carthage's weapons before revealing that the population must abandon the city; Carthaginian resistance then prolonged the siege until [[ScipioAemilianus]] cut the harbor, stormed the city, enslaved the survivors, and carried out the Senate's destruction order.

## Key Claims

- The post-201 BC settlement left Carthage without an independent foreign policy or meaningful naval power, while Massinissa repeatedly encroached on its territory under Roman protection.
- Memory of Hannibal's devastation remained politically active even after Carthage ceased to pose the same immediate military threat.
- Cato treated renewed Carthaginian prosperity and proximity as reasons for destruction, while other Romans argued that preserving a rival could restrain domestic decadence and conflict.
- Carthage's unauthorized war against Massinissa breached its treaty with Rome and supplied the legal-religious pretext Roman leaders wanted.
- Rome disclosed its demand to evacuate and level Carthage only after obtaining the city's weapons, turning attempted submission into desperate resistance.
- Scipio Aemilianus ended the siege through blockade and harbor assault; six days of urban fighting preceded mass surrender, enslavement, demolition, and a ban on resettlement.
- The near-contemporary destruction of Corinth made 146 BC a demonstration that Rome would tolerate neither a rival nor serious disobedience in the Mediterranean.

## Key Quotes

> "Carthage must be destroyed" - the demand associated with Cato's recurring Senate campaign.

> "the destruction of Italy" - the episode's description of how Romans remembered Hannibal's campaign.
