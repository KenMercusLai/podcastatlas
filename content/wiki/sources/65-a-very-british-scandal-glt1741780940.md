# 65. A Very British Scandal

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode reconstructs the [[JeremyThorpe|Jeremy Thorpe]] scandal from Thorpe's Liberal ascent and relationship with [[NormanScott|Norman Scott]] through alleged murder discussions, [[AndrewNewton|Andrew Newton]]'s failed attack, the 1979 trial, and Thorpe's acquittal. It treats the story as both farce and tragedy: extraordinary schemes and political jokes coexist with coercive secrecy, the killing of Scott's dog Rinka, and a credible attempt on Scott's life.

The episode places the scandal inside the [[LiberalPartyUK|Liberal Party]]'s revival, the persistence of anti-gay stigma after decriminalization, tabloid competition, anti-apartheid politics, and the exhaustion of Britain's post-war political order. Its strongest historical judgment is qualified rather than legal: the hosts regard the off-book money trail as evidence that the plot probably moved beyond talk, while acknowledging the weaknesses that made acquittal understandable.

## Key Claims

- Thorpe's flamboyance, wit, anti-apartheid position, and strategic choice of the diminished Liberal Party helped him rise rapidly to party leadership.
- Criminalization and enduring social stigma made exposure of a same-sex relationship potentially career-ending, turning private letters and Scott's public claims into political leverage.
- Thorpe's growing importance, especially during the February 1974 coalition negotiations, raised the perceived cost of Scott's continued demands.
- Alleged discussions with David Holmes and Peter Bessell moved through a chain of intermediaries until Newton was recruited, creating deniability but also disorder.
- Newton killed Rinka and tried to shoot Scott, but the gun jammed; the attack made Scott's previously dismissed allegations harder to ignore.
- Rumours, newspaper payments, Private Eye ridicule, anti-apartheid intrigue, and Harold Wilson's suspicions placed the case inside a wider culture of tabloid appetite and political paranoia.
- Thorpe's acquittal reflected hearsay problems, compromised witnesses, and a favorable judicial summing-up, but the episode treats Sir Jack Hayward's off-book money as strong evidence of probable involvement.
- The scandal became a symbol of establishment seediness and changing public discussion of homosexuality rather than a direct cause of Thatcherism.

## Contradictions

- The episode preserves a distinction between legal acquittal and historical judgment. It does not overturn the 1979 verdict, but the hosts' view that Thorpe probably sponsored an attempt to kill Scott conflicts with Thorpe's claim that violent discussions were jokes and with any reading of acquittal as proof that no plot existed.
- The episode rejects the idea that South African intelligence invented the scandal, while accepting that Thorpe's anti-apartheid campaigning gave South African actors reason to dislike him. The intelligence dimension remains contextual rather than a demonstrated causal explanation.
