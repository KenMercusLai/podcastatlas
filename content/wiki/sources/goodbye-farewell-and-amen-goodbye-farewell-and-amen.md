# Goodbye, Farewell and Amen

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode closes [[MalcolmGladwell]]'s series about [[CurtisLeMay]] and [[NapalmWarfare|napalm]] by following incendiary bombing from Japan through Korea and Vietnam. It takes seriously the argument that bombing Japan accelerated surrender and may have prevented invasion, partition, and starvation, but sets that counterfactual beside civilian death, the destruction of North Korea, and the failure of LeMay's preferred air-power solution in Vietnam.

The episode also examines how accountability recedes. Harvard does not publicly commemorate napalm's invention, its scientific creators moved into respected civilian work, and [[MASHTelevisionSeries|M*A*S*H]] turns an unexplained incendiary strike into an American experience of victimhood. The closing visit to Tokyo's air-raid museum restores individual testimony beneath strategic casualty calculations.

## Key Claims

- LeMay's postwar promotion and honors show how victory can convert conduct he acknowledged might have been prosecuted after defeat into public prestige.
- The military defense of bombing Japan rests on uncertain counterfactuals about invasion casualties, Soviet and American occupation, national partition, and winter starvation.
- [[LouisFieser]] and other napalm researchers later pursued fire prevention, medicine, and chemistry without a public institutional reckoning that linked those achievements to the weapon.
- Napalm's use expanded from a weapon rationalized against Japanese urban structures into an openly anti-personnel instrument used extensively in Korea and advocated again in Vietnam.
- LeMay's doctrine that overwhelming violence at the beginning could shorten war makes total casualties the principal moral measure while depending on uncertain predictions and excluding political alternatives.
- Vietnam exposed [[MilitaryDoctrineEraFit|the danger of treating one historically successful method as a universal solution]] when the opponent, political objective, and battlefield system had changed.
- Harvard's silence, American forgetting of the Korean air campaign, and *M*A*S*H*'s ambiguous fire show [[InstitutionalForgettingByOmission|how omission can redistribute responsibility without formally denying an event]].

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode deepens the existing [[TheBomberMafiaBook|Bomber Mafia]] branch by giving more weight to Korea, Vietnam, institutional memory, and civilian testimony.
- The claim that bombing Japan prevented invasion, partition, and starvation is presented as a consequentialist defense, not a verified counterfactual. The episode itself refuses to let that defense settle the moral judgment.
- The episode's claim that the incendiary attack in the *M*A*S*H* finale could only have come from the U.S. Air Force conflicts with Alan Alda's view that the fictional source did not matter to the show's anti-war purpose; this is a disagreement about historical responsibility versus universal message, not about whether the scene exists.
