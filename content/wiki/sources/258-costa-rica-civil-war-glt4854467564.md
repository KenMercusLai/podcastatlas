# 258. Costa Rica: Civil War

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode explains [[CostaRica|Costa Rica]]'s democratic exceptionalism through its relatively weak colonial hierarchy, coffee-led nineteenth-century development, and especially the [[CostaRicanCivilWar1948|1948 civil war]]. After a disputed election and congressional annulment, [[JoseFigueresFerrer|Jose Figueres Ferrer]] led an armed victory over the order associated with [[RafaelAngelCalderon|Rafael Angel Calderon]], then used an eighteen-month junta to widen political rights, preserve social reform, write a new constitution, and transfer power.

Its central argument is that institutions were shaped by contingent postwar choices rather than national destiny. [[ArmyAbolitionDemocraticSafeguard|Abolishing the army]] reduced a potential coup-making center and made civilian education the symbolic beneficiary, while [[PostVictoryPowerSurrender|relinquishing power after military victory]] kept Figueres from converting revolutionary legitimacy into dictatorship.

## Key Claims

- Costa Rica's sparse colonial settlement, limited labor supply, and weaker estate hierarchy are presented as background conditions that reduced some inherited social conflicts without predetermining democracy.
- Coffee exports, British commercial links, banking, railways, the abolition of the death penalty, religious liberty, church-state separation, and divided powers gave the nineteenth-century republic an unusually progressive institutional profile in the episode's account.
- Calderon's welfare populism broadened social policy but, under successor Teodoro Picado, became entangled with political intimidation, Communist-linked militias, and mounting opposition from commercial and professional groups.
- Otilio Ulate reportedly defeated Calderon in the February 1948 election; a ballot fire, congressional annulment, killing, arrests, and escapes moved the dispute from electoral contest into civil war.
- Figueres's National Liberation Army joined anti-communists, coffee interests, and democratic leftists; US anti-communist pressure helped end the 44-day conflict before destructive fighting in San Jose.
- During eighteen months in power, Figueres expanded suffrage, retained welfare laws, nationalized banks, widened education and citizenship, drafted a constitution, and abolished the army before handing power over.
- Army abolition joined practical distrust of the force to a constitutional and symbolic bet that civilian institutions could reduce future coup and civil-war risk.
