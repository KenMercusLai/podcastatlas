# McDonald’s Broke My Heart

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode has [[MalcolmGladwell]] reconstruct the 1990 decision by [[McDonalds|McDonald’s]] to stop frying potatoes in beef tallow after pressure from heart-health campaigner [[PhilSokoloff]]. The change becomes a case in [[IngredientReformulationStrategy]]: the company responded to saturated-fat criticism with vegetable-oil formulations that changed repeatedly, initially included substantial trans fat, and in Gladwell’s judgment lost the flavor and texture [[RayKroc]] had treated as central to the product.

A blind tasting at Mattson favored fries cooked twice in beef tallow, including among younger tasters who had never eaten the original McDonald’s version. That result supports a sensory claim, not a health verdict. The episode’s wider argument is that food reform should keep pleasure, craft, serving size, replacement composition, and clinical evidence visible rather than treating one nutrient target as a complete measure of value.

## Key Claims

- After a heart attack at 43, [[PhilSokoloff]] used his personal fortune to fund cholesterol testing, low-fat milk advocacy, advertisements, and campaigns against saturated fat.
- Sokoloff targeted [[McDonalds|McDonald’s]] for frying potatoes in beef tallow; after initially resisting, the company announced on July 23, 1990 that it would switch oils, and [[Wendys|Wendy’s]] and [[BurgerKing]] later announced similar changes.
- [[RayKroc]] regarded the original fries as central to McDonald’s appeal. The episode describes a process using Russet Burbank potatoes and a dedicated beef-tallow mixture called Formula 47.
- The source says McDonald’s first replacement blend contained substantial trans fat and that the company reformulated again in 2002 and 2008, illustrating that “vegetable oil” did not identify one stable recipe or risk profile.
- The episode reports that repeatedly heated vegetable oils can degrade and form aldehydes, but the supplied summary does not provide comparative exposure measurements or clinical outcome evidence.
- Mattson prepared vegetable-oil, mixed-oil, and all-tallow fries for a blinded tasting; the all-tallow batch was preferred for crispness, interior texture, and flavor.
- Gladwell ultimately assigns more responsibility to McDonald’s than to Sokoloff: Sokoloff pursued a public-health goal, while the company abandoned a founder-defining product without preserving its sensory identity or making moderation the alternative.

## Contradictions

- The episode materially qualifies the reassuring side of [[SeedOilEvidenceBoundary]] by describing an initial trans-fat-containing replacement and by raising high-heat degradation concerns. It does not overturn the current contested synthesis because its blind tasting measures preference, not health, and the supplied summary does not compare clinical outcomes.
- The source’s broad defense of beef tallow sits in tension with sources that report more favorable blood-lipid or cardiovascular-risk markers when unsaturated oils replace butter, lard, or tallow. The wiki keeps the disagreement comparator-, formulation-, endpoint-, and source-specific.
- The episode treats the 1990 switch as a loss of product quality, whereas the existing [[McDonalds|McDonald’s]] profile primarily covers current demand, localization, and operational strategy. This is a historical extension rather than a contradiction.
