# Bubbles with Isaac Adamson | Development Hell

## Summary

In this [[RevisionistHistory]] installment of *Development Hell*, [[MalcolmGladwell]] interviews [[IsaacAdamson]] about [[BubblesFilm|Bubbles]], his acclaimed but unproduced stop-motion screenplay about [[MichaelJackson]] narrated by [[BubblesChimpanzee|Bubbles the chimpanzee]]. Adamson gives Bubbles the dignity and language of an exiled Shakespearean heir whose literal belief in the “King of Pop” converts celebrity history into comedy, tragedy, and [[MoralBlindnessUnreliableNarration|morally consequential unreliable narration]].

The project reached substantial pre-production before *Leaving Neverland* changed the cultural and commercial conditions around Jackson. Revisions made Bubbles more explicit about alleged abuse and more final in rejecting Jackson, but weakened some of the original narrator's tragic blindness; Taika Waititi withdrew, Netflix ended production, and the rights reverted to Adamson. The episode argues that the controversy which made the film harder to market also made its examination of loyalty, denial, and public complicity more meaningful.

## Key Claims

- Adamson made Bubbles the protagonist rather than a comic observer, imagining him as Jackson's loyal heir who interprets human relationships through chimpanzee hierarchy, succession, and rank.
- Pseudo-Shakespearean narration gives Bubbles dignity and makes the contrast between his solemn self-understanding and physical situation both comic and painful.
- Bubbles's mistaken interpretations of celebrity, cosmetic surgery, family power, and children preserve an innocence unavailable to a fully informed human narrator.
- The screenplay's darker second act turns children into rivals for Jackson's attention and links Bubbles's abandonment to accounts from children who felt favored and later displaced.
- After *Leaving Neverland*, later drafts reduced Bubbles's ambiguity and moved him from loyal blindness toward explicit moral disillusionment.
- Netflix had acquired the project, legal review and pre-production were advanced, and more than forty animators were reportedly working before Waititi withdrew and production was terminated.
- Gladwell and Adamson argue that cultural danger increased the story's artistic stakes while making directors, marketers, financiers, and studios less willing to assume its reputational risk.
