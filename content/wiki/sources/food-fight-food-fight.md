# Food Fight

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode contrasts [[BowdoinCollege|Bowdoin College's]] celebrated dining with [[VassarCollege|Vassar College's]] less luxurious food to make a higher-education finance problem visible. Under [[CatherineHill]], Vassar increased financial aid and Pell Grant enrollment, but the resulting loss of tuition revenue made the college more dependent on its endowment and on affluent students who could pay the full price.

The episode develops [[CollegeAccessAmenityCompetition]]: spending less on amenities can release money for access while weakening a college's ability to attract the full-paying students who sustain that access. [[FullPayStudentCrossSubsidy]] and [[UniversityEndowmentPublicBenefit]] separate the financing mechanism from the moral claim that tax-advantaged institutional wealth should serve a public purpose rather than accumulate without reference to whom a college educates.

## Key Claims

- The episode reports that Vassar's financial-aid budget rose from about $25 million in 2006 to roughly $60 million, while its Pell Grant share increased from the low teens to about 23 percent.
- It reports a 23 percent Pell Grant share at Vassar and 13 percent at Bowdoin, and uses a New York Times access index to rank Vassar substantially higher.
- About 1,000 of Vassar's roughly 2,450 undergraduates reportedly paid full or nearly full tuition and generated about $60 million annually, making even a modest loss of those students financially consequential.
- Vassar protects teaching quality and enrollment capacity by limiting food, housing, speaker, and facility spending rather than treating those amenities as its principal competitive focus.
- Colleges that spend less on aid can use amenities to attract affluent applicants, creating a feedback loop in which an access-oriented college must compete for the full-paying students whose tuition supports its aid policy.
- [[RobertWalton]] argues that tax exemption and large endowments create an obligation to deliver public benefit rather than primarily educate wealthy students.
- Gladwell asks students, parents, and donors to judge colleges by educational opportunity and student composition rather than campus luxury alone.
