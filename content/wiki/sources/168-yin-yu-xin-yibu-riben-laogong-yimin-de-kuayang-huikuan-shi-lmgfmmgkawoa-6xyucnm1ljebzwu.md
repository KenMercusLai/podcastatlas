# 168. 银与信：一部日本劳工移民的跨洋汇款史

## Summary

This [[QizhulouYanBinke|起朱楼宴宾客]] episode with [[DavidWeng|大卫翁]] and [[Su|素]] uses late-19th- and early-20th-century [[JapaneseLaborMigrationRemittances|Japanese labor-migration remittances]] to connect [[Japan]], [[Hawaii]], the [[UnitedStates]], family obligation, state capacity, banking infrastructure, and racialized immigration politics. Its central contribution is the contrast between Japan's increasingly [[StateMediatedRemittanceSystem|state-mediated remittance system]], anchored by consulates and [[YokohamaSpecieBank|Yokohama Specie Bank]], and Chinese [[QiaopiRemittanceNetworks]], which relied more heavily on private couriers, associations, clan networks, and hometown trust. The episode also adds a research-method layer: archives make some people visible because states, banks, and museums chose to preserve particular records, so remittance history must be read through [[ArchivePreservationBias]] rather than treated as neutral data.

## Key Claims

- [[Su|素]] frames the topic as a financial-history entry point into migration history: remittances show how overseas work connected laborers, households, banks, consulates, and national image.
- The episode argues that North American Asian migration history often emphasizes identity, exclusion, law, and discrimination, while the economic mechanics of earning abroad and sending money home deserve equal attention.
- Japanese families left behind in Japan used village officials, the foreign ministry, and overseas consulates to find relatives and press for remittances, making [[MigrantFamilyRemittanceObligation]] visible in official paperwork.
- The archived requests moved from handwritten petitions toward standardized forms that recorded names, village origins, family conditions, last contact, remittance amounts, and proof of payment.
- The first 1868 group of Japanese contract laborers to [[Hawaii]] suffered poor treatment, which pushed the Meiji state toward later control over labor export, savings, and overseas reputation.
- From 1885 onward, official contract migration to Hawaiian sugar plantations created a large Japanese labor presence; by around 1900, Japanese workers numbered in the tens of thousands in Hawaii.
- Government-required savings and return-fare reserves, initially handled through employers and the Hawaiian treasury, eventually became too complex for ministries alone and helped bring [[YokohamaSpecieBank|Yokohama Specie Bank]] into the system.
- [[YokohamaSpecieBank|Yokohama Specie Bank]] functioned less like a purely private bank than a semi-official financial arm of Japanese modernization, foreign exchange, and overseas migrant governance.
- Official migrant handbooks tried to shape the "good" overseas Japanese worker as thrifty, dutiful, remittance-sending, and careful not to damage national reputation.
- Once official contract migration declined, private migration companies, family migration, picture brides, and higher overseas wages kept Japanese migration and remittance circuits active.
- Japanese workers used multiple transfer channels, including [[YokohamaSpecieBank|Yokohama Specie Bank]], postal money orders, and private firms, while Japanese-language guides explained how to navigate English-language remittance forms.
- [[DillinghamCommission|Dillingham Commission]] material and anti-Japanese politics show [[RacializedRemittancePolitics]]: similar remittance behavior could be interpreted differently depending on whether migrants were Italian, Japanese, Chinese, or otherwise racialized.
- Japanese farmers' movement from Hawaii to California made remittances part of a wider labor and land story, including agricultural success, anti-Japanese agitation, and later exclusion laws.
- Compared with Japanese remittances, Chinese [[QiaopiRemittanceNetworks]] and silver-letter systems were more decentralized, relying on water couriers, associations, family networks, batch offices, and later banks.
- The episode treats the Japanese-Chinese contrast as institutional rather than moral: the difference is not whether migrants sent money home, but whether remittance became legible through a centralized state-bank archive or through private and hometown trust networks.
- [[ArchivePreservationBias]] matters because Japanese foreign-ministry files, bank records, qiaopi collections, museums, and state archives each preserve different people and different kinds of action.
- The ending links the past to current [[JapaneseLaborShortageImmigrationTension]]: the country that once sent workers abroad now manages incoming workers and their outward remittances.
