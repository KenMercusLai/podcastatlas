# 249. Treason in Modern Britain (Part 2)

## Summary

Recorded at [[NationalArchivesUK|The National Archives]] exhibition *Treason: People, Power and Plot*, this [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode with [[NeilJohnston]] follows British treason law from the trial of [[CharlesIOfEngland|Charles I]] through the Restoration, the Popish Plot, American independence, Irish republicanism, and [[WilliamJoyce|William Joyce]]. It presents treason not as a fixed offence but as a contested language through which monarchs, parliaments, republics, and rebels claim authority to define allegiance.

The central synthesis is [[TreasonAsSovereigntyContest|treason as a sovereignty contest]]. Charles's judges reversed the medieval logic by accusing the king of levying war against the people; American revolutionaries inherited British treason law and redirected allegiance toward a republic; and Irish rebels understood themselves as independence fighters while British authorities prosecuted or punished them as traitors. Legal procedure matters, but prosecution, spectacle, public reaction, and victory determine which sovereignty claim becomes enforceable.

## Key Claims

- The trial of Charles I transformed treason from an offence against the monarch into an accusation that the monarch had waged war against the people, while Charles denied the court's authority and refused to plead.
- Restoration policy combined pardon and political forgetting with selective punishment of the men responsible for Charles I's death; bloody executions and posthumous punishment also exposed the political risk of exemplary spectacle.
- The Popish Plot shows how pre-existing anti-Catholic fear, coincidental apparent confirmation, elite sponsorship, and courtroom process can turn false conspiracy claims into executions and party formation.
- From the British perspective the Declaration of Independence was treason, but the new United States preserved an English legal inheritance while redirecting allegiance from monarch to republic and treating loyalists as possible traitors.
- The [[UnitedIrishmen]] moved from parliamentary reform and Catholic representation toward clandestine republicanism and French military support when legal prosecution and political suppression narrowed their options.
- Thomas Hardy's acquittal showed that radical reform and sedition could no longer be converted automatically into provable treason by alleging an indirect threat to the king.
- The 1916 rebels treated the [[EasterRising]] as a war for independence, whereas martial law, secret courts-martial, and execution expressed the British state's treason-and-rebellion frame.
- Joyce's British passport allowed the court to treat him as owing allegiance to the Crown, making his broadcasts from Nazi Germany the basis for Britain's last execution for treason.

## Key Quotes

> "political theatre" - the episode's recurring description of treason proceedings whose political conclusion could be largely determined in advance.

> "Governments have repeatedly updated the terms on which treason law is useful to them" - the episode's closing account of legal adaptability.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode deepens the existing claim that Charles I's execution created a founding rupture without supplying a durable republican settlement.
- The source distinguishes political or seditious opposition from legally provable treason, especially in Thomas Hardy's acquittal, and therefore does not treat all radical reform or republican belief as equivalent to armed betrayal.
- Trial chronology, exact legal formulations, crowd reactions, disputed records, the evidentiary details of individual prosecutions, and judgments about whether treason law can be modernized remain source-scoped.
