# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》1127丨曹操篡位三件套，指的是什么？

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode links [[CaoCao|曹操]]'s post-[[WeinanBattleLateHan|渭南之战]] position to three court privileges: he need not be announced by personal name, hurry into audience, or remove sword and shoes before entering the hall. The source reads these exemptions as a public reduction of [[ImperialRitualDistance|君臣礼制距离]], placing Cao Cao visibly above ordinary ministers without treating them alone as a completed dynastic transfer.

The second branch follows [[CaoPi|曹丕]]'s handling of a Hejian rebellion. [[ChangLinLateHan|常林]] dissuades him from personally chasing a low-value victory, while debate over more than one thousand surrenderers separates a wartime order from a settled post-capture decision. [[GuoYuanInvestigatorLateHan|国渊]] then reports the domestic revolt without customary inflation, because exaggerating victory over one's own population would advertise internal disorder rather than external power.

## Key Claims

- In 212 CE, after the Guanzhong campaign, Cao Cao returns to Ye and receives three exceptional privileges from [[EmperorXianOfHan|汉献帝]].
- “赞拜不名” removes the use of Cao Cao's personal name in formal announcement; “入朝不趋” removes the hurried, deferential gait expected on entering court; “剑履上殿” permits him to retain sword and shoes in the central palace space.
- The source calls these privileges a “usurpation three-piece set,” but their demonstrated effect is status elevation and the public weakening of ordinary ministerial posture, not dynastic replacement by themselves.
- During Cao Cao's western campaign, Tian Yin and Su Bo raise a rebellion in Hejian; Chang Lin argues that Cao Pi's person is too important to risk for a rebellion that a subordinate force can suppress.
- After Jia Xin defeats the rebels, advisers argue that Cao Cao's earlier order to kill those who surrender only after encirclement belongs to a harsher wartime context and should not automatically govern secured captives in a stabilizing realm.
- Cao Pi asks Cao Cao rather than treating general military discretion as authority to execute controlled prisoners; Cao Cao pardons the surrenderers.
- Guo Yuan reports the victory without multiplying the killed and captured, distinguishing external deterrent signaling from the shame of boasting about domestic rebellion.
- Guo Yuan's earlier agricultural administration and later clue from the anonymous letter's use of Zhang Heng's *Er Jing Fu* set up the investigation completed in Hanji 1128.

## Key Quotes

> “赞拜不名、入朝不趋、剑履上殿。” - the three court privileges discussed as markers of Cao Cao's exceptional rank.

> “境内之民反，惭愧之事，不应以为荣。” - the episode's rendering of Guo Yuan's reason for not inflating the victory report.

- [[JiuXiRitualPromotion|九锡礼遇]] - the next ceremonial elevation in Hanji 1128, extending rather than duplicating the three privileges here.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode supplies the immediate prequel to Hanji 1128's completed anonymous-letter investigation and Nine Bestowments branch.
- Ritual qualification: the three privileges materially elevate status, but the label “usurpation three-piece set,” the claim that they psychologically prepare all under heaven for replacement, and Cao Cao's exact intention are the host's interpretation.
- Command qualification: the episode's argument depends on distinguishing an earlier emergency order from a later secured-captive decision; it does not establish a general prohibition on battlefield discretion.
- Source-scope caution: the exact rebel names, force and captive totals, wording of discussion, Cao Cao's praise, Guo Yuan's harvest record, appointment chronology, and anonymous-letter procedure require direct textual corroboration.
