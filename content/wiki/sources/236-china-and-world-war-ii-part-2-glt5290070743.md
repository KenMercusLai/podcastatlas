# 236. China and World War II (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode with [[RanaMitter]] follows China's war against Japan from the 1937 [[NanjingMassacre]] through Pearl Harbor, the strained American alliance, communist growth, Japan's 1945 defeat, and the postwar struggle over memory. Its central synthesis is [[ChinaWartimeResistanceAndPoliticalRealignment]]: continued resistance kept China in the war and denied Japan a stable client settlement, but mass violence, famine, coercion, displacement, and military exhaustion weakened the [[NationalistGovernment]] and changed the field on which the civil war resumed.

The episode preserves moral and causal ambiguity. [[ChiangKaiShek]] is presented as both the authorizer of the catastrophic [[YellowRiverFlood1938]] and the leader whose refusal to surrender remained strategically important; [[WangJingwei]]'s collaboration claimed to reduce suffering while operating under Japanese domination; and the communists combined disciplined growth with ideological coercion and revolutionary land violence. The later memory settlement is treated through [[ChineseWarMemoryReconciliation]], which widened public recognition beyond a Communist-only victory story without dissolving the political conflict over who represented China.

## Key Claims

- The [[NanjingMassacre]] was an extreme campaign of murder, rape, plunder, and terror after Japanese forces occupied Nanjing in December 1937; casualty totals remain uncertain, but the episode places the violence among the era's worst war crimes.
- In June 1938 Chiang's government breached Yellow River dikes without warning nearby civilians. The episode presents the resulting [[YellowRiverFlood1938]] as both a mass-casualty act against China's own population and a disputed attempt to delay Japan.
- Chiang repeatedly refused Japanese terms and continued resistance through near-defeat in 1938, 1940, and 1944, preventing Japan from securing the client-government settlement it preferred.
- [[WartimeCollaborationUnderOccupation]] cannot be reduced to elite treason alone: Wang's Japanese-backed Nanjing regime was coercively subordinate, yet local responses also reflected survival, property, order, and fear.
- Pearl Harbor brought American backing but produced a mutually resentful alliance; Joseph Stilwell pressed for offensives while Chiang sought to conserve forces depleted by years of war.
- The [[ChineseCommunistParty]] expanded its rural position while Nationalist resources and legitimacy deteriorated, but the episode rejects both an American “lost chance” with Mao and the claim that communist victory was inevitable in 1945.
- China's war remained central to later national memory, while the 2015 VJ Day commemoration briefly placed Nationalist and communist veterans inside a broader shared resistance story.

## Key Quotes

> “toxic alliance” — Mitter's description of wartime US-Nationalist cooperation amid deep mutual resentment.

> “lost chance” — the counterfactual the episode ultimately rejects for a durable wartime US-communist alignment.

- [[ChiangKaiShek]], [[NationalistGovernment]], and [[YellowRiverFlood1938]] - resistance leadership, coercive rule, and the episode's most severe military-necessity dilemma.
- [[WangJingwei]] and [[WartimeCollaborationUnderOccupation]] - client rule and survival under occupation.
- [[MaoZedong]] and [[ChineseCommunistParty]] - rural growth, ideological discipline, revolution, and the limits of the American “lost chance” thesis.

- [[ChinaWartimeResistanceAndPoliticalRealignment]] - synthesis joining continued resistance to Nationalist attrition and postwar civil-war contingency.
- [[ChineseWarMemoryReconciliation]] - later widening of public commemoration to acknowledge Nationalist as well as communist resistance.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with the existing wiki was found. The episode itself preserves disputes over the Yellow River flood's military effectiveness, local attitudes toward collaboration, the possibility of US-communist alignment, and when communist victory became likely.
- Casualty estimates for Nanjing, the Yellow River flood, and the Henan famine; refugee totals; reported private motives; and postwar counterfactuals remain attributed to the episode rather than adopted as independently verified figures.
