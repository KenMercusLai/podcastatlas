# 35. The Prime Ministers’ World Cup

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode reviews the first round of a listener-voted knockout tournament among British prime ministers, sending Churchill, Disraeli, Gladstone, Pitt the Younger, Attlee, Asquith, Peel, and Lloyd George into the quarterfinals. The hosts treat the result as deliberately playful rather than authoritative: audience composition, present ideology, name recognition, vivid personality, scandal, and cultural memory repeatedly outweigh any consistent measure of administrative achievement.

The first round therefore adds an especially clear case to [[HistoricalEntertainment]] and [[HistoricalReputationConstruction]]. Comparisons such as Disraeli against Salisbury and Peel against Walpole expose the advantage of a memorable story, while Blair, Thatcher, Palmerston, and Wilson show how later controversy or current political feeling can dominate retrospective judgment.

## Key Claims

- The tournament measures the preferences of a historically interested Twitter audience rather than representative national opinion or comparative governing merit.
- Disraeli's 79% victory over Salisbury contrasts theatrical personality and cultural memory with a longer but less vivid record of government.
- Gladstone's narrow victory over Tony Blair shows how Iraq, post-office wealth, and recent controversy can outweigh three election victories.
- Churchill's victory over Palmerston and Pitt the Younger's victory over Pitt the Elder combine name recognition with modern moral and ideological filters.
- Attlee's 81% victory over Lord Liverpool, the widest first-round margin, joins Attlee's living reputation to Liverpool's weak public recognition.
- Asquith's victory over Thatcher reflects the latter's exceptional divisiveness in the episode's Twitter setting, not a settled comparison of their records.
- Peel's victory over Walpole and Lloyd George's victory over Wilson show how schoolbook associations, personality, and compressed later reputation shape audience choice.

## Key Quotes

> “Prime Ministerial World Cup” - the tournament's sporting frame for comparing unlike political careers.

> “greatest British prime minister” - the deliberately underspecified question put to the audience.
