# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》242｜从丞相到免官，周亚夫犯了职场大忌！（1）

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode traces [[ZhouYafu|周亚夫]]'s removal from the chancellorship through three successive policy conflicts with [[HanJingdi|汉景帝]]. Zhou opposes the removal of crown prince Liu Rong, rejects enfeoffing Empress Wang's brother Wang Xin without public merit, and argues that granting marquis titles to six surrendered [[Xiongnu|匈奴]] leaders would reward disloyalty and weaken the court's ability to condemn treachery.

The episode presents Zhou's positions as principled but politically cumulative: the succession dispute alienates Jingdi, [[LiuWuLiangKingWesternHan|梁孝王刘武]] repeatedly attacks him before [[EmpressDowagerDouWesternHan|窦太后]], the Wang Xin objection frustrates the dowager's family demand, and the Xiongnu dispute ends with Jingdi rejecting his advice. Zhou then reports illness and stays away from court before being dismissed and replaced by Liu She. The durable synthesis is not that correctness itself is a workplace mistake, but that repeated direct opposition across sovereign, dowager, succession, kinship, and frontier policy can exhaust a minister's protection when principle is not joined to coalition, timing, or an acceptable alternative.

## Key Claims

- Zhou strongly opposes Jingdi's removal of crown prince Liu Rong; the emperor proceeds and thereafter dislikes and distances himself from the chancellor.
- Liu Wu's hostility from the Rebellion of the Seven States continues after the war through repeated criticism of Zhou before Dowager Dou.
- Dowager Dou asks Jingdi to enfeoff Wang Xin, the empress's brother; Zhou invokes the founding rule that a person without state merit should not receive a marquis title.
- Six important Xiongnu leaders surrender to Han, and Jingdi sees enfeoffment as a way to reward defection and attract further submission.
- Zhou argues that rewarding men who abandoned their own ruler would weaken the moral basis for punishing disloyal Han subjects; Jingdi rejects the analogy and enfeoffs all six.
- Zhou reacts by claiming illness and no longer attending court; Jingdi removes him as chancellor and appoints Liu She in his place.
- The episode continues into disasters and a later instruction to reopen doubtful criminal judgments, but does not establish a direct causal link between those measures and Zhou's dismissal.

## Key Quotes

> “非刘姓皇族不可封王，非有功不可封侯。” - the merit-and-rank principle attributed to Zhou in the Wang Xin dispute.

> “将来就难以责备臣僚不忠。” - the episode's summary of Zhou's objection to rewarding surrendered Xiongnu leaders.

## Connections

- [[ZhouYafu|周亚夫]] - chancellor whose repeated principled opposition across succession, kinship, and frontier policy narrows his political protection.
- [[HanJingdi|汉景帝]] - ruler who rejects Zhou's advice, chooses diplomatic incentive over the minister's loyalty analogy, and removes him from office.
- [[EmpressDowagerDouWesternHan|窦太后]] - senior family authority whose Wang Xin request places merit rules against dynastic kin preference.
- [[LiuWuLiangKingWesternHan|梁孝王刘武]] - earlier military-policy opponent who continues weakening Zhou through criticism before the dowager.
- [[RigidIdealismPoliticalRisk|刚直理想主义的政治风险]] - framework separating the merits of a principle from the leverage, timing, coalition, and alternatives needed to sustain it.
- [[Xiongnu|匈奴]] - frontier polity whose surrendered leaders create the conflict between inducement strategy and loyalty norms.

## Contradictions

- No settled factual contradiction is adopted. This source supplies the pre-dismissal disputes that Hanji 244 part 2 only summarizes retrospectively, and it leaves the later banquet, burial-goods prosecution, and prison death to that sequel.
- The episode treats the three disputes as a cumulative downfall chain, but the precise causal weight of each conflict, Liu Wu's lobbying, Dowager Dou's response, Zhou's illness, and the formal grounds for dismissal remain source-scoped.
- Wang Xin's identity, the founding compact's exact wording and legal force, the surrendered leaders' names and ranks, the diplomatic effect of their rewards, the date of Zhou's removal, and Liu She's exact prior title require comparison with transmitted records.
- Zhou's objection identifies a real loyalty-signaling problem, while Jingdi's policy identifies a real inducement problem; the supplied source does not demonstrate which approach produced better frontier outcomes.
- Disaster chronology, astronomical dates, and the later judicial instruction are episode-attributed and should not be used to infer divine judgment or a causal connection to Zhou's case.
