# 627. Jack The Ripper: From Hell (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode reconstructs the murder and uncertain life of [[MaryJaneKelly|Mary Jane Kelly]], the last of the canonical Whitechapel victims, while refusing to treat unverifiable biography or detailed nighttime sightings as settled fact. Rent arrears, [[JosephBarnett|Joseph Barnett]]'s unemployment, indoor privacy, weak witness evidence, police delay, public mourning, and Kelly's missing family make the crime both a personal catastrophe and a concentrated case of [[VictorianWomenPrecarity]].

The second half develops [[JackTheRipperMythFormation]]. [[WTStead|W. T. Stead]]'s campaigns, *Dr Jekyll and Mr Hyde*, medical suspicion, [[SherlockHolmes|Sherlock Holmes]], and [[RichardVonKrafftEbing|Richard von Krafft-Ebing]] supplied overlapping languages for hidden evil, failed policing, scientific detection, sexual pathology, and apparently motiveless serial murder. The episode argues that the Ripper's modernity lies partly in this new cultural legibility, not simply in chronological priority.

## Key Claims

- Kelly's life before London remains largely unverifiable: accounts of Irish and Welsh origins, marriage, education, family, elite prostitution, Paris, and even physical appearance conflict or depend on her own reported stories.
- Six weeks of rent arrears, Barnett's job loss, their separation, and Kelly's return to soliciting show how money, housing, intimate partnership, and public-night work combined to narrow her choices.
- George Hutchinson's highly detailed description of a wealthy man in an Astrakhan coat is treated cautiously because lighting, elapsed time, theatrical resemblance, and declining police confidence weaken it.
- Cries around 4 a.m. and medical estimates support a possible time of death but do not establish the killer's identity or validate Hutchinson's chronology.
- Kelly's indoor murder gave the offender more privacy and time than earlier attacks, while the later absence of a clear next victim helped turn abrupt cessation into an enduring mystery.
- Stead linked police failure to class and state power, but his reform journalism also had unintended effects: clearance worsened some overcrowding, and brothel closures could displace women into more dangerous street conditions.
- Newspapers, melodrama, *Jekyll and Hyde*, medical bags, doctor theories, and Holmesian detection did not merely report the case; they supplied recognizable character types and explanatory scripts for it.
- Krafft-Ebing's sexual-pathology vocabulary made cruelty and “lust murder” newly legible to later audiences, helping construct the Ripper as a modern serial killer without proving motive or diagnosis.

## Contradictions

- The source does not resolve contradictions in Kelly's biography, witness descriptions, hair color, accents, time of death, or the identity and significance of men reportedly seen with her.
- Kelly is described by police as a prostitute, but the episode's fuller account qualifies any attempt to make that label an exhaustive identity or simple explanation of her death.
- Medical knowledge, a doctor's bag, or a psychiatric category may explain public suspicion and later interpretation; none identifies the offender or establishes a clinical motive.
- Alice McKenzie and Frances Coles are discussed as possible later victims, but the episode says the balance of expert opinion rejects both, leaving Kelly as the likely final canonical victim rather than proving the murders ended with her.
