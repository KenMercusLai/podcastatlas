# 53. Game of Thrones

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode treats [[GameOfThrones|Game of Thrones]] and [[ASongOfIceAndFire|A Song of Ice and Fire]] as major forces in the modern imagination of the Middle Ages rather than accurate reconstructions of one medieval society. [[TomHolland]] and [[DominicSandbrook]] trace the setting through the [[WarsOfTheRoses]], Shakespeare, [[JRRTolkien|J.R.R. Tolkien]], Rome, northern myth, and late-twentieth-century war while arguing that its sexual violence, political nihilism, weak religious belief, and mass armies often reflect classical, Enlightenment, or modern assumptions. Its strongest contribution is [[FantasyMedievalRealism]]: visible brutality can feel historically authentic while omitting the institutions, moral expectations, belief, and limits that also structured medieval life.

## Key Claims

- The series has strongly shaped twenty-first-century popular ideas of the past even though Westeros combines different centuries, regions, genres, and political anxieties rather than depicting one historical period.
- [[HistoricalFantasySourceLayering]] explains the setting better than prototype hunting: King's Landing mixes London, Constantinople, Tudor politics, and Roman court imagery, while the North, Iron Islands, Wall, and East draw on other historical and mythic repertoires.
- Dragons bridge medieval supernatural imagination and modern strategic fear; the hosts compare them with air power and weapons of mass destruction, while the White Walkers combine ancient undead traditions with climate-catastrophe anxiety.
- The books' 1990s formation matters. Civil-war suffering, refugees, rape, and destroyed villages are read partly through Yugoslavia, Rwanda, post-Cold-War confidence, and the return of history after claims that it had ended.
- The episode rejects bleakness as a sufficient test of medieval accuracy. It argues that warfare was constrained by institutions and expectations, rape was morally and legally legible as an offense, hospitality and sacrament made the Red Wedding exceptional, and religious belief cannot be reduced to fraud or fanaticism.
- [[JRRTolkien|Tolkien]] may appear less institutionally medieval, yet his Catholic and northern moral structure is presented as closer to medieval ethical imagination than Martin's more visibly medieval but often modern-cynical world.
- Daenerys Targaryen is read as a modern interventionist and white-saviour figure whose liberation project turns into domination; comparisons with Galla Placidia, Robespierre, Cortes, and Tony Blair are analogies rather than one-to-one identities.
- The series' historical influence is material as well as imaginative: filming-driven tourism intensified Dubrovnik's transformation into a crowded theme-park-like destination.

## Key Quotes

> "nothing in the 21st century has done more ... to shape the broad landscape of how people think about the past" - Tom Holland's assessment of the series' cultural reach.

> "massively, massively inaccurate" - Holland's rejection of the claim that the series depicts the Middle Ages more accurately than Tolkien.

> "we associate realism with grimness, with cynicism" - Dominic Sandbrook's explanation for why modern audiences can mistake bleakness for authenticity.

## Connections

- [[GameOfThrones]] and [[ASongOfIceAndFire]] - screen and literary works whose historical influence the episode evaluates.
- [[GeorgeRRMartin]] and [[JRRTolkien]] - contrasting models of fantasy, historical texture, and moral imagination.
- [[HistoricalFantasySourceLayering]] - framework for the setting's compressed historical and mythic materials.
- [[FantasyMedievalRealism]] - distinction between surface brutality and socially grounded historical plausibility.
- [[WarsOfTheRoses]] - major dynastic analogy qualified by the setting's many other source layers.
- [[DragonAsDangerousPower]] - framework for dragons as concentrated military force and catastrophic risk.
- [[ChivalricSacralization]] - adjacent account of Christianity's effort both to restrain and authorize warrior violence.
- [[InterpretationAndOverinterpretation]] - guardrail against treating Daenerys, Cersei, Valyria, or any other element as one historical figure or event in disguise.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode complements the existing source-layering account by separating cultural influence from historical accuracy: a world can be richly historical in texture while projecting modern assumptions onto medieval violence, religion, state capacity, sexuality, and trust.
- The claims that medieval warfare was comparatively restrained and that the series is less medieval than Tolkien are broad popular-history judgments, not comprehensive comparisons across the whole medieval period.
- Character analogies, climate and WMD readings, tourism effects, financial speculation, army scale, dialogue, and claims about authorial intent remain interpretive or source-scoped.
