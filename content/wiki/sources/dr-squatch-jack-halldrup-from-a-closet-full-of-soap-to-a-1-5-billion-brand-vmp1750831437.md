## Summary
This [[HowIBuiltThis]] episode features [[GuyRaz]] interviewing [[JackHalldrup]] about building [[DrSquatch]] from a $1,500 side project into a men's personal-care brand acquired by [[Unilever]] in 2025 for a reported $1.5 billion. The account connects a psoriasis-driven product discovery, cold-process soap, e-commerce, a forced rebrand, early Facebook advertising, co-founder separation, a cash-flow crisis, outside capital, retail expansion, and founder role transition. Its main synthesis is that the 2018 breakout advertisement became transformative only because product margins, repeat demand, fulfillment, retention, and reinvestment were already sufficiently aligned to support a [[PerformanceCreativeScaleLoop]].

## Key Claims
- Halldrup's psoriasis and dissatisfaction with mass-market body wash led him to cold-process soap, while his friends' limited exposure to farmers markets suggested an e-commerce distribution gap rather than a need to invent a new product.
- He launched Sasquatch Soap within 90 days with roughly $1,500, four or five products, and 400 to 500 bars stored and shipped from his bedroom closet.
- The source reports about $12,000 in first-partial-year sales and roughly $80,000 in 2013, with family-assisted fulfillment and later use of a professional fulfillment center.
- [[AJFountain]] moved from commission-based door-to-door selling into investment and co-ownership, while subscription boxes including Bespoke Post and Birchbox supplied early sampling and conversion.
- A General Assembly course helped Halldrup see that Facebook advertising could create demand for an unfamiliar men's product more effectively than search advertising that depended on existing intent.
- A prior trademark claim forced Sasquatch Soap to become Dr. Squatch; the new name strengthened the character, humor, and elevated identity rather than merely preserving continuity.
- The source reports revenue of roughly $750,000 in 2015 and $1.8 million in 2016 as paid advertising, subscriptions, merchandising, email, and retention became an integrated e-commerce system.
- Halldrup and Fountain separated because they wanted different companies: Halldrup sought a much larger brand, while Fountain preferred a business compatible with an outdoor-centered lifestyle. The buyout spread cash, monthly, and performance-linked payments over several years.
- Hiring, office, and agency costs then outpaced cash generation; declining marketing performance led Halldrup to cut expenses and dismiss two employees in 2018.
- Raindrop produced a roughly three-minute humorous advertisement for about $15,000 after a Harmon Brothers proposal was unaffordable. The campaign reportedly acquired some customers for $3 to $4 and established the brand's repeatable voice.
- Dr. Squatch reportedly generated a little over $5 million in 2018 revenue, with Halldrup attributing 30% to 50% to the video, then raised $1.3 million from Guild Capital in 2019 to hire and increase advertising.
- The source reports revenue rising to roughly $20 million to $23 million in 2019 and $113 million in 2020, with product expansion increasing order and customer value while the pandemic added a substantial tailwind.
- A private-equity group bought a majority stake in 2021. Halldrup stepped down as CEO in 2023, when the company had roughly 300 employees, because his strengths, motivation, and desired family life no longer matched the large-company operating role.
- Unilever acquired Dr. Squatch in June 2025 for a reported $1.5 billion. By then, the company had built cold-process manufacturing capacity of about five million bars per month and generated most sales through retail rather than e-commerce.
- Halldrup attributes the outcome about equally to execution and luck: starting, surviving, and operating well created exposure to favorable e-commerce timing, Facebook advertising, recurring demand, category readiness, and useful relationships.

## Key Quotes
> "roughly half execution and half luck" - Halldrup's estimate of how skill, survival, timing, category conditions, and serendipity combined.

> "soap for men" - the simple positioning used in an early Facebook advertisement.
