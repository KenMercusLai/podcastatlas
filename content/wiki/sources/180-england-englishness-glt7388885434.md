# 180. England & Englishness

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] conversation with [[JasonCowley]] uses [[GeorgeOrwell]], devolution, empire, immigration, landscape, football, [[Brexit]], and electoral realignment to examine why [[EnglishNationalIdentity]] is both durable and difficult to define. Its central formulation is “changing changelessness”: England changes demographically, culturally, and politically while inherited places, symbols, habits, and stories continue to make it recognizable.

The episode argues that Englishness need not be confined to nostalgia or reaction. The [[EnglandNationalFootballTeam]], especially under [[GarethSouthgate]], becomes its strongest example of a more inclusive identity in which the St George's flag, black English belonging, monarchy, military memory, and progressive causes can coexist.

## Key Claims

- England is an old nation without a separate modern nation-state or discrete devolved political institutions, so Englishness is often submerged within or confused with Britishness.
- [[GeorgeOrwell]]'s wartime writing presents English identity through ordinary life and paradox; invasion danger temporarily made national unity more legible.
- Devolution, Scottish nationalism, immigration, Brexit, and possible United Kingdom fragmentation have made English identity more politically visible since 1997.
- Nostalgia and loss recur in English self-description, but the episode treats anxiety about decline as a historical continuity rather than proof that the identity cannot change.
- Empire, slavery, colonial violence, racism, hooliganism, and class conflict qualify gentle or pastoral national self-images.
- Countryside and landscape function as imagined inheritance even though England is urbanized, suburbanized, environmentally depleted, and under housing pressure.
- Football supplies a bottom-up national arena: the St George's flag displaced older Union-flag dominance, while a diverse team widened who could publicly claim Englishness.
- London embodies a productive contradiction as English and British capital, global city, center of inequality, and symbol of cosmopolitan distance from “deep England.”
- Brexit is framed as democratic turbulence and a “tug from below,” but the episode does not resolve its policy consequences or the future of the Union.
