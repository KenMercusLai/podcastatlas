# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》224｜缇萦救父，14岁少女如何感动汉文帝？

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode places [[ChunyuTiying|淳于缇萦]]'s rescue of [[ChunyuYi|淳于意]] inside [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]]'s 167 BCE penal reform. Tiying reportedly follows her condemned father to Chang'an and petitions that death cannot be reversed and a mutilated body cannot be restored, so permanent punishment leaves no usable path to repentance; she offers herself as a government slave in exchange for his opportunity to reform.

The episode says Liu Heng pardons Chunyu Yi and converts the individual appeal into a decree abolishing mutilating punishments. Yet the replacements include penal labor, three hundred or five hundred strokes, and execution for specified offenses. The durable synthesis is therefore [[MutilatingPunishmentAbolition|肉刑废除与可改过性]] with an implementation qualification: ending tattooing, nose-cutting, and foot amputation did not end bodily coercion, and severe flogging created the lethal substitution problem later addressed through [[CorporalPunishmentHarmReduction|肉刑执行减害]].

## Key Claims

- The episode situates the reform within a wider Liu Heng program of imperial responsibility, agriculture, lighter burdens, and rejection of secret ritual transfer of the ruler's fault onto subordinates.
- Chunyu Yi is sentenced to be taken from Qi to Chang'an for a mutilating punishment; his complaint that five daughters are useless in an emergency prompts his youngest daughter to act.
- Tiying follows her father to Chang'an and argues that execution and bodily mutilation are irreversible, preventing a punished person from repairing the wrong or serving again.
- She offers to enter government servitude to redeem her father's offense, joining filial rescue to a general argument about punishment and reformability.
- Liu Heng reportedly pardons Chunyu Yi and issues a decree condemning punishments that permanently destroy skin and limbs before education has made reform possible.
- The replacement schedule substitutes labor for some sentences, three hundred strokes for nose-cutting, five hundred for left-foot amputation, and execution for a specified group of more serious offenses.
- The reform removes named mutilations but does not abolish all corporal or capital punishment; later reductions in flogging counts show that formal substitution could remain lethally severe.

## Key Quotes

The supplied episode document is a structured summary rather than a verbatim transcript, so no sentence is promoted as an exact quotation. Its central attributed argument is that the dead cannot return to life and severed limbs cannot be restored, leaving the punished without a path to reform.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode supplies the missing Wen-era abolition stage before the Jing-era reductions already recorded in the wiki.
- Terminology boundary: the summary says Liu Heng abolished “all 肉刑,” but its own replacement schedule retains flogging and execution. The defensible claim is abolition of specified mutilating punishments, not the end of state-inflicted bodily punishment.
- Humanitarian boundary: replacing nose-cutting or amputation with three hundred or five hundred strokes changes the legal form but does not establish lower mortality or suffering; later reforms are evidence that the first replacement schedule remained dangerously severe.
- Source-scope caution: Tiying's age, Chunyu Yi's offense and reputation, the journey, dialogue, petition wording, pardon, decree, dates, official roles, exact replacement schedule, release rules, social effects, and tax policy require comparison with transmitted legal and historical texts.
- Causal boundary: the source presents Tiying as the trigger for reform, but it does not establish that one petition alone caused the entire institutional change or measure implementation and outcomes.
