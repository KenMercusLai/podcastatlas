## Summary
This [[TomorrowToday]] episode has [[TaraChklovski]] explain how [[Technovation]] uses a time-bounded accelerator, mentors, local problem selection, user research, prototyping, and pitching to help young people—historically girls—develop entrepreneurial agency. Its strongest synthesis is [[ProjectBasedEntrepreneurshipEducation]]: the durable outcome is a learner's changed sense of capability rather than the survival or competition rank of one app. Chklovski also develops [[ParticipatoryAISafety]], arguing that people who see AI risks should understand the tools and help build alternatives while retaining explicit concern about alignment, access, and harm.

## Key Claims
- Technovation treats uncertain, real-world building as developmental practice: students identify a problem, interview users, test an intervention, iterate, and present what they learned.
- Projects concerning mine safety in Kazakhstan, family memory in India, and menstrual-product access for deaf girls in Nigeria illustrate how lived experience can surface locally important problems that distant product teams may overlook.
- Chklovski says the program's central product is the participant rather than the app and reports long-run career, income, entrepreneurship, and leadership effects, but the supplied summary does not provide the underlying longitudinal methods or datasets.
- She argues that unequal time to experiment with frontier tools may cause women to adopt AI later, so participation must include access, mentorship, confidence, and safeguards against girls being displaced when programs broaden to boys.
- Falling entry-level hiring and AI task substitution increase the urgency of giving young people practice in independent learning, problem solving, uncertainty, and building real things.
- Her AI-safety position combines caution with agency: critics should learn capabilities and risks, participate in development, and build better alternatives rather than assuming either abstention or adoption is sufficient.
- Schools may change slowly because childcare and civic-socialization functions create inertia; Chklovski expects nonprofits between industry and government to carry part of the experimentation burden.
