This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode fills the memorial-and-reply step between [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-437-genzhe-xihan-laojiang-zhaochongguo-xuexi-qiye-guanli-zhihui-lkll2dzmkhizux3tgkz5ozakt5dr|Hanji 437]] and [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-439-gongbi-weiqing-huoqubing-zhichang-guanlizhe-bangyang-zhaochongguo-lirqsoujwewu6efd-shb7mypqvu3|Hanji 439]]. [[ZhaoChongguoWesternHan|赵充国]] answers [[EmperorXuanOfHan|汉宣帝]]'s demand for a timeline by arguing that the [[XianlingQiang|先零羌]] are already losing land, pasture, cohesion, and confidence, so the right move is to withdraw cavalry, keep infantry for 河湟屯田, and let pressure, settlement, and surrender finish the war.

The durable synthesis is that Zhao's memorial is both frontier strategy and upward communication. He does not simply reject the ruler's urgency; he restates the ruler's goal, gives concrete enemy-condition evidence, enumerates twelve advantages of屯田, and then answers Xuan's security objections about raids, road guards, and civilian danger.

## Key Claims

- Zhao frames imperial warfare as victory with minimal damage, moving the discussion away from visible killing toward谋略, cost control, and reliable end conditions.
- He argues that the Qiang side is already under pressure because it has lost fertile land and pasture, faces survival stress, and is beginning to fragment through surrender and persuasion.
- His plan keeps infantry for屯田 while withdrawing expensive cavalry, making food production, guard duty, roads, bridges, and local pressure part of the military answer.
- The twelve advantages include military readiness, land denial against the Qiang, protection of farming, cavalry-cost savings, river transport of grain, relay-station repair, avoidance of risky pursuit, reduced casualties, preserved court prestige, prevention of wider Qiang panic, bridge construction, and lower fiscal/labor burden.
- Zhao's reasoning treats a non-attack policy as active containment: hostile groups remain exposed to cold, illness, poverty, and desertion while Han improves logistics and settlement capacity.
- Emperor Xuan remains concerned that withdrawing cavalry could let Qiang forces attack屯田 soldiers, road guards, and civilians, so he orders Zhao to think further and reply again.
- The host turns the exchange into a management lesson: principled disagreement with a high-power superior should first show seriousness and respect, then use concrete data, current problems, and a workable alternative plan.
- The episode's opening age proverb about seventy-three and eighty-four introduces Zhao as a counterexample to fatalistic age talk: he is old, but still capable of active frontier command.
