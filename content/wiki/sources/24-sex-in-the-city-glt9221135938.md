# 24. Sex in the City

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] conversation with [[HallieRubenhold]] examines sex, prostitution, desire, reputation, and class in Georgian and Victorian Britain. It rejects a simple transition from uninhibited Georgians to repressed Victorians, separating middle-class public ideals from less documented behavior and placing sexual opportunity, exploitation, and respectability inside changing social and urban structures.

The episode also foregrounds method. [[SexualHistoryEvidenceInterpretation]] treats diaries, letters, memoirs, pornography, scandal reports, and legal records as intimate but performed evidence; [[SexWorkEconomicSpectrum]] distinguishes coercion, poverty, family strategy, occupational risk, celebrity, and limited agency; and [[ClassStratifiedSexualRespectability]] explains why male privilege and female vulnerability were distributed differently across class.

## Key Claims

- Historical accounts of sex must distinguish lived experience from male-authored fantasy, commercial titillation, confession, reputation management, and legal spectacle.
- Prostitution was integrated into London's geography and economy rather than confined to one social world, moving with urban development and serving clients across class levels.
- Women entered sex work through varied combinations of poverty, coercion, family economics, upbringing, strategic choice, and limited alternatives; neither pure victimhood nor uncomplicated empowerment describes the whole field.
- Pregnancy, abortion before quickening, and unreliable contraception formed part of sex work's occupational risk, although the episode's remedies and legal-cultural claims require specialist corroboration.
- Georgian and Victorian sexual behavior cannot be reduced to a linear shift from freedom to repression because ideals, records, and lived practices varied by class, gender, and setting.
- Middle-class respectability demanded sexual knowledge from men and managed ignorance from women, while elite men's freedom could depend on the sexual availability and disposability assigned to poorer women.
- Courtesan celebrity could create fashion, bargaining leverage, and status for male patrons without removing economic precarity or the possibility of poverty.
