# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》242｜从丞相到免官，周亚夫犯了职场大忌！（2）

## Summary

Despite its [[ZhouYafu|周亚夫]] title, this [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode is about [[LiuWuLiangKingWesternHan|梁孝王刘武]]'s final court visit, death, and political afterlife. [[HanJingdi|汉景帝]] refuses Liu Wu a longer stay in Chang'an after their trust has deteriorated, while the episode links that distrust to the earlier succession dispute, Yuan Ang's assassination, Liang's wealth, weapons, and emperor-like display.

After Liu Wu dies, [[EmpressDowagerDouWesternHan|窦太后]] blames Jingdi and refuses food. Jingdi, after consulting [[LiuPiaoWesternHan|馆陶长公主刘嫖]], gives each of Liu Wu's five sons a kingship and each daughter a county estate. The episode reads this as a dual-purpose settlement: lavish family consolation calms the dowager while division of Liang prevents one heir from inheriting its full territorial and military concentration. The closing discussion moves from Liu Wu's reported wealth and rock-cut tomb to [[CaoCao|曹操]] and later grave robbery, treating even extraordinary power and property as vulnerable after death.

## Key Claims

- Jingdi refuses Liu Wu's request to remain longer in Chang'an, and the episode reads that refusal as evidence that sovereign suspicion has overtaken sibling intimacy.
- The episode connects Liu Wu's decline to the abandoned succession project, the assassination of Yuan Ang, and Liang's exceptional wealth, weapons, and imperial-style ceremony.
- After Liu Wu's death, Dowager Dou accuses Jingdi of causing it; Jingdi responds under family and political pressure rather than treating the death as a private matter.
- Jingdi divides Liang among Liu Wu's five sons and grants county estates to five daughters, satisfying the dowager while dispersing concentrated princely power.
- One beneficiary is [[LiuPengliJidongKingWesternHan|刘彭离]], who becomes king of Jidong before his later deposition and exile.
- The episode reports vast Liang treasure, armaments, palaces, and a large rock-cut tomb, but its ancient quantities and modern gold conversion are not independently verified here.
- The tomb's reported plunder by Cao Cao and later robbers becomes a moral ending about the impermanence of wealth rather than evidence for the episode title's promised Zhou Yafu story.

## Key Quotes

> “景帝果杀吾子。” - the accusation attributed to Dowager Dou after Liu Wu's death.

> “斩山作椁，穿石为藏。” - the episode's description of the rock-cut tomb attributed to Liu Wu.

## Connections

- [[LiuWuLiangKingWesternHan|梁孝王刘武]] - central prince whose damaged trust, death, wealth, and divided inheritance structure the episode.
- [[HanJingdi|汉景帝]] - emperor who limits Liu Wu's capital stay and converts a family crisis into territorial fragmentation.
- [[EmpressDowagerDouWesternHan|窦太后]] and [[LiuPiaoWesternHan|馆陶长公主刘嫖]] - senior family pressure and intermediary consultation shaping the posthumous settlement.
- [[LiuPengliJidongKingWesternHan|济东王刘彭离]] - one of five sons who receives a kingship from the division of Liang.
- [[KinshipProtocolBoundary|亲情名分边界]] - affection is preserved through honors while proximity and concentrated power are reduced.
- [[TuiEnLing|推恩令]] - later systematic analogue for using grants to royal sons to fragment large kingdoms.
- [[CaoCao|曹操]] - later warlord whom the episode associates with plundering Liu Wu's tomb for military finance.

## Contradictions

- Title-content mismatch: the supplied title promises the second half of a Zhou Yafu workplace lesson, but the body supplies no substantive Zhou Yafu evidence. This note does not update Zhou's page from the title alone.
- No settled factual contradiction is adopted. The episode extends the existing Liu Wu arc from post-rebellion protocol excess into succession grievance, distrust, death, and territorial partition.
- The division resembles the later 推恩令 in effect but is not treated as that edict: this is a one-house posthumous settlement under Jingdi, whereas the later policy creates a broader recurring mechanism under Wudi.
- The claim that Liu Wu died of grief, Dowager Dou's and Jingdi's private motives, the assassination details, exact court-stay rules, dates, speeches, five titles, political intent, wealth and weapon totals, gold conversion, tomb measurements, Cao Cao's role, and the grave's later history require comparison with transmitted and archaeological evidence.
