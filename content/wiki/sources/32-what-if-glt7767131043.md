# 32. What if?

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode uses Nazi victory, Persian victory over Athens, Rome's survival, the Reformation, the First World War, the Falklands War, the conquest of Mexico, and Britain in May 1940 to distinguish disciplined [[HistoricalCounterfactualReasoning]] from unconstrained alternative-history fantasy. [[DominicSandbrook]] and [[TomHolland]] agree that what-if questions can expose choice, contingency, and hidden assumptions about inevitability, while disagreeing productively over how often individuals create genuine historical choke points rather than embodying deeper forces.

The resulting [[HistoricalStructureAgencyCausation|structure-agency]] synthesis is conditional rather than binary. Battles, dynastic succession, and rare political decisions may create narrow branching points, but plausible alternatives must retain the institutions, economic pressures, military capacity, culture, and technological conditions around them. The episode therefore treats counterfactuals as tests of causal explanation, not predictions of an observable alternate world.

## Key Claims

- Historical actors faced uncertain futures, so narrative and analytical history already contain implicit alternatives whenever they explain why one option was chosen over another.
- Useful counterfactuals make a limited change near a real decision point and preserve known constraints; confidence declines rapidly when one alteration is asked to generate decades of parallel history.
- Individuals can matter decisively at rare choke points, but removing one leader often leaves the social, economic, military, ideological, or technological pressures that made similar outcomes possible.
- Persian victory at Marathon, a surviving Catholic succession in England, Franz Ferdinand's survival, Hannibal taking Rome, and Cortes failing in Mexico produce different judgments because their surrounding replacement mechanisms and institutional resilience differ.
- Technology cannot be transplanted cleanly between eras: steam power, computers, or industrialization require enabling cultural, economic, political, and competitive conditions rather than invention alone.
- The episode treats May 1940 as an unusually consequential decision point because Churchill's authority and Britain's decision to continue fighting may have prevented negotiation from turning the country into a Nazi-dominated satellite.
- Alternative history often favors dystopia and entertainment, revealing the writer's politics and theory of causation as much as it reconstructs a plausible past.

## Key Quotes

> “pub game” - the episode's shorthand for the playful form of counterfactual history, contrasted with disciplined causal inquiry.

> “choke point” - Holland's description of the rare moments, especially May 1940, when one decision may sharply redirect later possibilities.

- [[BattleOfMarathon]], [[Herodotus]], and [[Plato]] - ancient case linking a local military outcome to contested civilizational consequences.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. Holland gives greater weight to epochal individuals and rare choke points, while Sandbrook more often expects structural pressures or replacement actors to preserve the broad trajectory; the episode retains this as a causal disagreement rather than forcing one universal rule.
- The claim that war before 1920 was likely even without Franz Ferdinand's assassination qualifies any reading of Sarajevo as a sufficient cause, but does not contradict the assassination's contingent execution or the later choices of the [[JulyCrisis]].
- Claims about Persian victory, German authoritarianism without Hitler, Catholic England, Roman survival, industrialization, electoral outcomes, conquest, likely peace terms, and all alternative timelines remain source-scoped and unobservable.
