# 26. Witches

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode with historian [[SusannahLipscomb]] separates recurrent belief in harmful magic from the specific early-modern system that joined maleficium to a diabolic pact, heresy, and prosecutable crime. Its central synthesis is [[EarlyModernWitchcraftProsecution]]: elite demonology and legal categories interacted with Reformation anxiety, famine, neighborhood grievance, gender hierarchy, reputation, and local fear to turn misfortune into accusation.

The episode also strengthens [[WitchcraftBeliefEvidenceThreshold]]. Witchcraft prosecutions declined not simply because belief disappeared, but because courts became less willing to accept confession, spectral claims, and weak testimony as sufficient proof. Modern child accusations, satanic panic, and online public shaming are presented as comparisons within a broader [[WitchHuntMechanism]], while differences in law, theology, and lethal punishment remain important.

## Key Claims

- Belief in harmful magic recurs across ancient, medieval, early-modern, and modern settings, but intense prosecution requires historically specific theological and legal machinery.
- The *Malleus Maleficarum* helped frame witchcraft as a diabolic pact and heresy, while elite acceptance later made it a prosecutable crime.
- Reformation-era fear intensified trials in Protestant and Catholic territories alike; confessional anxiety mattered more than a simple Protestant-versus-Catholic explanation.
- Accusations often grew from a "perfect storm" of harvest failure, high food prices, war, apocalyptic fear, neighbor conflict, reputation, and refusal guilt after denied charity.
- Most convicted witches were women, especially older women, but regional variation and cases involving men, children, priests, and judges resist a single target profile.
- Status and patronage helped distinguish legitimate or useful magic from criminal maleficium, illustrated by [[JohnDee]]'s protection under [[ElizabethI|Elizabeth I]].
- Courts could accept witchcraft as possible while acquitting particular defendants, and rising evidentiary demands helped end capital prosecution before popular belief disappeared.
- Modern witch-hunt analogies preserve patterns of scapegoating and social exclusion only if they also preserve differences in institutional setting and physical punishment.

## Key Quotes

> "perfect storm" - [[SusannahLipscomb]]'s description of the conditions from which witchcraft trials emerged.

> "refusal guilt syndrome" - the episode's label for accusations that follow a rejected request for help and a later misfortune.

## Contradictions

- The episode qualifies any exclusively Protestant explanation by identifying trials in Catholic territories as well as Protestant ones.
- No settled contradiction was adopted. The alleged ancient Greek case, prosecution and execution totals, regional target patterns, last-execution dates, modern country examples, causal weighting, and parts of the garbled witch-finding discussion remain source-scoped.
