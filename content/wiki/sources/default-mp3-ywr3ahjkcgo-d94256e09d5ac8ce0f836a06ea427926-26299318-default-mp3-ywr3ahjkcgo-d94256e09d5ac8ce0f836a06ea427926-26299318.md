# You Can Seize Everything (The U.S. is reviving pirate law)

## Summary

This [[PlanetMoney]] episode asks whether the [[UnitedStates|United States]] can use [[MaritimePrizeLaw|maritime prize law]] to process oil tankers captured in operations involving [[Iran]] and [[Venezuela]] more quickly than ordinary forfeiture litigation. The [[MajesticX|Majestic X]] case illustrates the incentive: the episode reports $42 million in custody, maintenance, and repair costs within roughly six weeks for a vessel valued below $3 million, while claimants and due-process requirements kept both ship and oil from rapid disposition.

The [[USDepartmentOfJustice|U.S. Department of Justice]], [[USNavy|U.S. Navy]], and Chief Judge [[RandyCrane]] have explored or drafted modern prize procedures, but the central legal threshold remains unresolved. Historical prize taking presupposed war, Congress has not formally declared war against Iran, and the first court may have to decide whether an armed but undeclared conflict is enough to activate [[PrizeLawWarThreshold|prize jurisdiction]].

## Key Claims

- Civil asset forfeiture permits owners, cargo buyers, victims, and other claimants to contest disposition, protecting private property but making rapid sale difficult.
- The source says the Majestic X accrued $42 million in custody, maintenance, and repair expenses during its first month and a half in government hands, despite a reported vessel value below $3 million.
- Historical prize courts determined whether ships and cargo captured during war could be condemned, returned, or auctioned; privateers operated under state commissions and could share in proceeds.
- [[NathanPerlRosenthal]] characterizes historical prize taking as comparatively bloodless because combat endangered crews and valuable property, while acknowledging that violence still occurred.
- Officials are exploring prize-court procedure for regular naval captures, not a return to private mercenaries or a civilian privateering fleet.
- [[RandyCrane]]'s Southern District of Texas team drafted 30 pages of modern rules incorporating agencies and evidence systems such as the FBI, NCIS, and AIS vessel tracking.
- The rules permit sale of perishable or hazardous property, explicitly including crude oil, before final judgment.
- Justice Department officials argued that armed conflict should suffice, but the drafting team declined language presuming that interpretation because the governing statute says “war.”
- The first modern case could test both maritime property procedure and the boundary between executive military action and Congress's war authority.

## Key Quotes

> “bonus navy” — the episode's shorthand for the historical use of privateers, which officials are not proposing to restore.

> “war” — the statutory term Judge Crane's team left for the first assigned judge to interpret.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction found.
- The source distinguishes modern prize-court planning from reviving privateering; historical analogy does not establish that civilian raiders will be authorized.
- The expected speed, validity, and safety of the new procedure remain untested because no modern case had applied the Texas rules at the time of recording.
- The seizure facts, ownership links, claimant rights, custody costs, vessel valuation, and legal positions are episode-reported rather than independently adjudicated here.
- Whether current hostilities constitute “war” for the statute is deliberately unresolved, so the page does not adopt the Justice Department's proposed interpretation.
