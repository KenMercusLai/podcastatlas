# 259: Iran: England - 'The Little Satan'

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] conversation with [[AliAnsari]] argues that modern Iranian suspicion of Britain grew partly from disappointed admiration. Nineteenth-century reformers treated British law, education, newspapers, industry, and parliamentary government as models, but British accommodation with Russia and imperial priorities repeatedly exposed a gap between those ideals and policy toward [[Iran]].

The episode follows that gap through the [[IranianConstitutionalRevolution]], the D'Arcy concession, the [[AngloIranianOilCompany]], wartime occupation, [[OilNationalization]] under [[MohammadMossadegh]], and the [[IranianCoup1953|1953 coup]]. It closes by showing that the resulting image of Britain as an “old fox” coexists with continued Iranian interest in English education, language, popular culture, and football.

## Key Claims

- Britain gained influence in nineteenth-century Iran through persuasion and institutional prestige as well as imperial power; Iranian visitors associated British strength with law, education, consultation, industry, newspapers, and liberty.
- Admiration raised expectations, so British unwillingness to support Iranian constitutionalists against Russian pressure was experienced as betrayal rather than routine great-power indifference.
- The episode presents the 1906 constitutional revolution as strongly influenced by British parliamentary models while keeping its Iranian agency central.
- Oil changed Iran in British strategy from a buffer protecting India into a strategic asset, especially after the Royal Navy's conversion from coal to oil.
- Opaque profit-based royalties, privileged naval pricing, taxation benefits, and the oil company's resistance to renegotiation made the concession a sovereignty and distribution conflict.
- Iran's 1951 nationalization under Mossadegh drew force from both postwar anti-concession politics and the contradiction of a British Labour government nationalizing at home while resisting Iranian control abroad.
- Ansari characterizes the 1953 coup as British-devised but dependent on Eisenhower-administration approval and operational use of the American embassy after Iran closed the British mission.
- Modern anti-British conspiracy narratives coexist with educational, linguistic, medical, and football ties, making hostility toward the British state distinct from attitudes toward British people and culture.

## Key Quotes

> “ideological intimacy” - Ansari's description of the attraction that made later policy disappointments unusually consequential.

> “old fox” - recurring Iranian image of Britain as politically cunning and manipulative.
