## Summary

This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode follows [[KipPolly]], technical director at [[ThirteenthFloorEntertainment|13th Floor Entertainment]], through the [[HollywoodHauntedHayride]]. Motion sensors, show controllers, DMX lighting, audio, video, animatronics, fans, carbon-dioxide blasts, and addressable LEDs are coordinated to support live performers and short-form storytelling while keeping the machinery unobtrusive to guests.

Polly's path from roller-coaster ambitions and manufacturing engineering through special-effects makeup, maintenance, prop entrepreneurship, and attraction engineering illustrates how creative design and practical implementation develop together. The episode's durable principle is [[AudienceInvisibleExperienceTechnology]]: technical complexity earns its place by improving timing, atmosphere, story, and embodied response rather than drawing attention away from the experience.

## Key Claims

- [[ThirteenthFloorEntertainment|13th Floor Entertainment]] operates roughly 18–20 U.S. attractions according to Polly, including temporary California productions that require seasonal setup, teardown, travel, and relocation.
- At [[HollywoodHauntedHayride]], audio and lighting establish scenes while motion sensors and small show controllers trigger animatronics and other effects at selected moments.
- Technology supplements rather than replaces live performers: an automated effect can prepare or intensify a scare, but the episode treats a person jumping out at a guest as uniquely effective.
- [[AudienceInvisibleExperienceTechnology]] coordinates lighting, audio, video, wind, carbon-dioxide blasts, DMX devices, and addressable LED candles while keeping the audience's attention on story, atmosphere, and surprise.
- Attraction pacing changes with format. A maze or hayride scene may have seconds or about a minute to communicate, while a seated ghost show can hold guests for several minutes and build effects more deliberately.
- Polly's work combines creative conception with implementation constraints, making artist and engineer overlapping roles rather than sequential handoffs.
- His career joined formal manufacturing-engineering education, self-taught makeup and scene skills, hands-on maintenance, a roughly $10,000 entrepreneurial investment, and later technical leadership.
- The work requires travel and long hours, but Polly values opening-day evidence that guests laugh, scream, and make shared memories.
