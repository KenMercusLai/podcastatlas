# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》519丨爱国者王章的死，缘于跟错了主子

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode fills the Yangshuo 1 / 24 BCE confrontation immediately before [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-520-daji-duishou-weishenme-yao-xian-faqi-yulunzhan-lh1ibhc9xsihtrh6mhzznqq9ioso|Hanji 520]]. [[WangZhangWesternHan|王章]] tells [[HanChengdi|汉成帝]] that the solar eclipse points to [[WangFengWesternHan|王凤]]'s monopolization of power rather than the emperor's closeness to the Dingtao king, and he recommends [[FengYewangWesternHan|冯野王]] as a replacement.

The durable synthesis is that correct diagnosis does not protect an adviser whose principal cannot sustain a decision. Chengdi initially agrees, but the plot leaks; Wang Feng offers to resign, [[WangZhengjun|王政君]] applies filial pressure, and Chengdi turns the legal machinery against Wang Zhang. Wang Zhang dies in prison and his wife and children are exiled, making weak authority a risk borne most heavily by the subordinate who trusts it.

## Key Claims

- Wang Zhang argues that Chengdi's closeness to the Dingtao king supports succession stability and that the eclipse should instead be read as a warning about a minister suppressing the ruler.
- He accuses Wang Feng of isolating Chengdi, engineering Wang Shang's removal, and using palace access for private advantage.
- Chengdi initially accepts the diagnosis, meets Wang Zhang privately, and asks him to recommend a replacement; Wang Zhang names Feng Yewang.
- Wang Yin overhears the secret meetings and warns Wang Feng, converting a private imperial plan into an outer-relative survival crisis.
- Wang Feng's resignation offer works as a pressure tactic because Wang Zhengjun responds through anger, refusal to eat, and reminders of family support for Chengdi's accession.
- Chengdi reverses course and has Wang Zhang impeached through allegations about recommending Feng Yewang and speaking improperly about the woman Wang Feng had introduced to the palace.
- Wang Zhang dies in prison and his wife and children are exiled; the episode reads his failure as reliance on a ruler who understood the danger but could not bear the cost of confronting it.
- The closing Chao Cuo analogy broadens the warning: a ruler's private confidence is not protection when political fear makes an adviser expendable.
