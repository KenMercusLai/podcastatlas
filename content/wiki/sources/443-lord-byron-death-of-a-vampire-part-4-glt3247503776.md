# 443. Lord Byron: Death of a Vampire (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[LordByron|Lord Byron / 拜伦]] from the 1816 Geneva circle through Venice, Ravenna, Pisa, and the [[GreekWarOfIndependence|Greek War of Independence]]. It connects [[JohnPolidori|John Polidori / 约翰·波利多里]]'s misattributed *The Vampyre*, Byron's European celebrity, *Don Juan*, Italian radical politics, and his death at Missolonghi to show how scandalous literary persona became both a Gothic type and a political resource.

The episode's central synthesis is that Byron did not leave celebrity behind in exile. [[ByronicCelebrityPolitics]] describes how he repeatedly converted scandal, writing, money, costume, and public attention into new roles, while [[AristocraticVampire]] tracks the literary transformation of his dangerous allure. His final Greek commitment carried theatrical self-fashioning, but also substantial financial risk, administrative work, illness, and death.

## Key Claims

- Polidori's 1819 *The Vampyre*, initially attributed to Byron, recast Balkan vampire tradition as a beautiful, destructive aristocrat modeled on Byron's public persona.
- The 1816 Geneva gathering joined [[LordByron]], [[JohnPolidori]], [[PercyShelley|Percy Shelley / 珀西·雪莱]], [[MaryShelley|Mary Shelley / 玛丽·雪莱]], and [[ClaireClairmont|Claire Clairmont / 克莱尔·克莱尔蒙特]] under the climatic pressure of the [[YearWithoutASummer|Year Without a Summer / 无夏之夏]], producing linked Frankenstein and vampire afterlives.
- European attention turned Byron's post-scandal exile into a mass spectacle; Venice then let him perform pleasure, decadence, and literary reinvention while writing *Childe Harold*, *Beppo*, and later *Don Juan*.
- [[TeresaGuiccioli|Teresa Guiccioli]] and Ravenna brought Byron closer to sustained attachment and Carbonari politics, while failed Italian revolt pushed her family and Byron onward to Pisa.
- The deaths of [[AllegraByron|Allegra Byron]] and Percy Shelley helped dissolve the social world around Byron and sharpened his isolation.
- Byron's Greek intervention combined image and material commitment: he raised money, purchased supplies, mediated among British and Greek actors, and accepted the unhealthy conditions at Missolonghi.
- Byron died on 19 April 1824 after fever, seizure, and bloodletting; the episode treats malaria as likely but does not claim a certain diagnosis.
- His posthumous reputation remained contested: Westminster Abbey denied him burial in Poets' Corner, while Hobhouse and Murray destroyed the memoir manuscript entrusted to Thomas Moore.
