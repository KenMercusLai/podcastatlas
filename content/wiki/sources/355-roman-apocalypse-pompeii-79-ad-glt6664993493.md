# 355: Roman Apocalypse: Pompeii 79 AD

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode reconstructs the [[EruptionOfVesuvius79|79 CE eruption of Vesuvius]] and the destruction of [[Pompeii]] and [[Herculaneum]] through the letters of [[PlinyTheYounger|Pliny the Younger]], archaeology, victim remains and possessions, seasonal clues, and volcanology. It presents a [[StagedVolcanicCatastrophe|staged catastrophe]] rather than one instantaneous event: wind first directed pumice toward Pompeii, a pyroclastic surge destroyed Herculaneum, and the later collapse of the eruption column overwhelmed Pompeii.

The episode also follows [[PlinyTheElder|Pliny the Elder]] from observation at Misenum into an attempted naval rescue and death at Stabiae. Its larger synthesis is that [[MultiEvidenceDisasterReconstruction|multi-evidence disaster reconstruction]] can recover decisions and sequences with unusual vividness while leaving the exact eruption date, literary shaping, precise timing, motives, and some individual scenes qualified.

## Key Claims

- The pre-eruption [[BayOfNaples|Bay of Naples]] was a wealthy, productive, densely connected Roman landscape of cities, villas, ports, naval infrastructure, agriculture, and volcanic building material.
- Earthquake damage from 62 CE, renewed tremors, and drying springs preceded the eruption, but inhabitants lacked a usable model connecting those signs to imminent volcanic catastrophe.
- Pliny the Younger's transmitted date of 24 August conflicts with autumnal archaeological clues, including fruit, wine production, heavy clothing, and a charcoal inscription dated 17 October.
- The eruption began after midday with a high column, darkness, falling pumice, blocked streets, collapsing roofs, and failed attempts to escape by land or sea.
- Herculaneum was killed and buried first by a fast pyroclastic surge; Pompeii retained a temporary escape window before later column collapse sent a fatal flow over the city.
- Pliny the Elder turned observation into an attempted fleet evacuation, reached Stabiae when Pompeii's harbor was inaccessible, and died there as ash and sulfur reached the coast.
- Written testimony, deposits, architecture, bodies, food, clothing, tools, religious objects, and later volcanology together support the sequence, but not every dramatic detail has equal evidentiary status.
