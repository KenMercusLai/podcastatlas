# 9. Causes of the First World War

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode treats the outbreak of the [[FirstWorldWar|First World War]] as a collision of the [[JulyCrisis|July Crisis]], alliance expectations, imperial interests, status anxiety, military timing, and contingent choice rather than the product of one master cause. [[DominicSandbrook]]'s closest unifying explanation is fear: leaders repeatedly saw war as the least bad option because delay, retreat, or neutrality seemed likely to leave their state weaker, poorer, humiliated, or strategically exposed.

The episode tests that synthesis through a British counterfactual. Britain was not mechanically bound to intervene, yet French naval expectations, fear of German control of the Channel coast, imperial security, the naval race, honour, and Belgian neutrality made staying out costly. The hosts use the possibility of British neutrality or alignment with Germany to expose assumptions rather than to claim that a quick German victory or a better twentieth century can be known.

## Key Claims

- Austria-Hungary's determination to punish [[Serbia]] after the Sarajevo assassination began the immediate interstate crisis, while Russia's decision to mobilise in support of Serbia widened it.
- Each great power could describe escalation as defensive: Austria-Hungary feared Serbian pressure, Russia feared humiliation and lost Balkan status, Germany feared Russia's future strength, France feared strategic isolation, and Britain feared German dominance of Europe and the Channel coast.
- Preventive-war reasoning made time itself threatening, especially where German leaders expected Russian industrial and railway development to worsen Germany's future position.
- Mobilisation plans and railway timetables accelerated escalation but did not eliminate political agency; leaders understood that preparation would be difficult to stop and chose it anyway.
- Britain's entry was contingent rather than automatic, but private naval coordination with France, imperial calculations, honour, cabinet politics, and Germany's violation of Belgian neutrality narrowed the practical alternative.
- The Ottoman decline and Balkan Wars intensified competition in southeastern Europe, while European empires globalized a conflict that began in the Balkans.
- The episode rejects both a monocausal Marxist account based on capitalism and profits and a simple story of foolish leaders stumbling unknowingly into a short war.
- Counterfactuals about British neutrality or Anglo-German alignment are useful causal tests, but claims that German victory would have prevented Nazism, communism, the Holocaust, or a second world war remain unobservable.

## Key Quotes

> "the least bad option" - the episode's recurring description of how leaders rationalized escalation.

> "the lights are going out all over Europe" - Edward Grey's remembered image, used to show that catastrophe was anticipated rather than wholly unforeseen.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode's emphasis on Russia's mobilisation as the key avoidable widening decision is an attributed interpretation, not a replacement for Austria-Hungary's declaration of war, Germany's blank cheque and operational choices, French alliance policy, or Britain's later intervention.
- The claim that Britain could have stayed out qualifies any automatic-alliance account, while the French naval understanding, Channel strategy, Belgian neutrality, honour, and domestic politics qualify any claim that neutrality would have been costless.
- The episode's discussion of Serbia as close to a terrorist state, Belgium's Congo rule, a possible quick German victory, imperial violence returning to Europe, public enthusiasm, and leaders' foreknowledge remains provocative, interpretive, or source-scoped.
