## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode backfills 61-63 CE / Yongping 4-6 immediately before [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-737-huanghou-shihao-zhidu-diyiren-liuxiu-laopo-bu-jiandan-lsdhr-gdx-yep3tzprjg9wtjbul2|Hanji 737]]. It centers [[LiuCangDongpingKing|东平王刘苍]]'s decision to return authority and leave the capital, then links that survival pattern to [[ZhengZhongFrontierEasternHan|郑众]]'s earlier refusal of private princely recruitment, [[LiangSongLinxiangMarquis|临湘侯梁松]]'s fall, [[KhotanKingGuangdeLateHan|于阗王广德]]'s Shache intervention, [[DouMuEasternHan|窦穆]]'s forged-dowager-order marriage coercion, and [[EmperorMingOfHan|汉明帝]]'s anti-flattery handling of a treasure-cauldron omen.

The durable synthesis is that early Yongping court order makes proximity to power useful but dangerous. Liu Cang survives because he voluntarily lowers his exposure while preserving formal loyalty; Zheng Zhong survives because he refuses a crown-prince and royal-prince client channel before it becomes incriminating; Liang Song and Dou Mu show how resentment, private networks, and forged authority can turn elite status into punishment; and Mingdi's treasure-cauldron edict turns auspicious-sign language into a warning against court flattery.

## Key Claims

- Liu Cang's safety depends on [[RoyalKinPowerWithdrawal|royal-kin power withdrawal]]: he recognizes that assisting government and holding high reputation near Mingdi creates suspicion risk, repeatedly requests return to his fief, and exits without publicly challenging the emperor.
- Mingdi's treatment of Liu Cang combines accepted remonstrance, territorial distance, retained honor, and large gifts, showing royal affection managed through public hierarchy rather than pure private brotherhood.
- Zheng Zhong's refusal to become a private client of the crown prince and [[LiuJingGuanglingKingEasternHan|Liu Jing]] anticipates his later frontier profile: he treats legal and institutional taboo as safer than attractive access to power.
- Liang Song's execution and the implication of his guests show why private retainer networks around royal or princely actors can become incriminating after the patron falls.
- Khotan king Guangde's killing of the Shache king, submission under Xiongnu pressure, and later replacement of Shache's ruler show Western Regions kingship as control over dependent rulers rather than title alone.
- Dou Mu's false use of [[YinLihua|Yin Taihou]]'s authority to force a marriage alliance makes forged order a family-power tool before his later execution with his sons.
- Mingdi's treasure-cauldron edict accepts the possibility of auspicious meaning while warning officials not to submit exaggerated praise or sanctifying flattery.
