# Divide and Conquer

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode has [[MalcolmGladwell]] follow [[MichaelStokesPaulsen]] and [[VasanKesavan]]'s reading of Article IV, Section 3 of the [[UnitedStatesConstitution]] from punctuation and grammatical structure to [[WestVirginia|West Virginia]]'s statehood and [[Texas]]'s possible subdivision. Their argument is that the consent clause applies to both creating a state inside another state and combining states, and that Congress supplied advance consent in the 1845 Texas admission resolution.

The political thought experiment then moves from constitutional authority to representation. Dividing Texas into as many as five states could produce ten senators, but [[MichaelLiTexasDemographer|Michael Li]]'s regional map suggests that demographic change makes the partisan result less predictably Republican than the proposal's early advocates assumed.

## Key Claims

- [[MichaelStokesPaulsen]] and [[VasanKesavan]] argue that the final consent language in Article IV, Section 3 governs both subdivision and combination, so the clause permits state subdivision with the required legislative and congressional consent.
- [[MaryNorris]] independently reads the “but–nor” construction as linking the provisions across the disputed semicolon, illustrating [[ConstitutionalGrammarAndPunctuation|how grammar and punctuation can shape constitutional interpretation]].
- Paulsen and Kesavan use drafts, constitutional history, and founding-era records to conclude that [[WestVirginia|West Virginia]] was constitutionally created during the Civil War.
- The 1845 congressional resolution admitting [[Texas]] allowed up to four additional states to be formed from its territory with Texas's consent.
- The episode presents that congressional permission as still operative, while Paulsen says Congress could revoke it before Texas completed subdivision.
- Replacing one Texas with five states would increase its territory's Senate representation from two seats to ten because each new state would receive equal representation.
- [[MichaelLiTexasDemographer|Michael Li]] maps five regions and argues that later demographic change could yield two safely Republican seats, four safely Democratic seats, and four competitive seats rather than a simple Republican windfall.
- The 2003 Texas quorum break led by [[JimDunnam]] supplies the episode's practical background: political actors were already using extraordinary tactics in a struggle over electoral boundaries and representation.
