# General Chapman’s Last Stand

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode has [[MalcolmGladwell]] use Robert Frost’s “Mending Wall” and the career of [[LeonardChapman]] to question whether a more effective United States–Mexico border produced its intended result. Chapman brought discipline, modernization, publicity, and political salience to the Immigration and Naturalization Service, but the episode argues that the enforcement system he helped energize misunderstood a historically circular migration pattern.

Research associated with [[DouglasMassey]], [[JorgeDurand]], and the [[MexicanMigrationProject]] supplies the episode’s counterintuitive mechanism. As crossing became more expensive and dangerous without eliminating the economic incentive to migrate, workers had stronger reasons to remain in the [[UnitedStates]], bring family members north, and replace repeated return trips with permanent settlement. [[BorderEnforcementSettlementBackfire]] captures this claim while preserving its evidentiary boundary: the supplied document summarizes one episode and one research program rather than independently adjudicating every cause of undocumented population growth.

## Key Claims

- Mexican labor migration is presented as historically [[CircularMigration|circular migration]] in which many workers repeatedly entered the United States, earned money, and returned to families and communities in [[Mexico]].
- Entry counts alone can misdescribe a circulating population; net migration requires accounting for departures as well as arrivals.
- Cheap, repeatable crossing reduced the incentive to remain permanently in the United States, while costly and dangerous crossing increased it.
- Chapman modernized the INS, visited its field offices, built public-affairs capacity, testified and spoke widely, and made unauthorized immigration more politically salient.
- Later enforcement concentrated routes away from El Paso and San Diego and toward more dangerous terrain without removing the wage incentive that motivated travel.
- The episode says longer stays encouraged family reunification and settlement, turning an entry-control policy into [[BorderEnforcementSettlementBackfire|a mechanism that could keep migrants in]].
- Administrative competence can worsen outcomes when an institution optimizes a mistaken model of the system it governs.
- Chapman’s defense of racial equality in the Marine Corps complicates a simple account of his immigration work as personal racial hostility without resolving the consequences of the policy direction he advanced.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content was found. The episode adds a behavioral consequence to [[WallBorderSystems]] rather than disputing its purpose-specific account of border effectiveness.
- The claim that holding 1986 enforcement resources constant would have produced an undocumented Mexican population about one-third smaller is a counterfactual estimate attributed to a 2016 study, not an observed comparison.
- The stated fall in first-trip return probability, the claim that departures offset 85 percent of undocumented entries from 1965 to 1985, poll figures, staffing and budget changes, and migration totals remain source-scoped pending the underlying datasets and papers.
- The link between Chapman’s Vietnam experience and his decision to lead the INS is presented as Gladwell’s interpretation rather than a confirmed motive.
- Rising crossing costs are a plausible mechanism, but the episode summary does not isolate them from economic, demographic, legal, labor-market, family, and political changes that could also affect settlement.
