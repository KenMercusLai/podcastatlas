# 656. The Ku Klux Klan: Birth of a Nation (Part 3)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode explains how the [[KuKluxKlan|second Ku Klux Klan]] turned [[LostCauseMyth|Lost Cause mythology]], [[BirthOfANation|film imagery]], and American lodge culture into a modern mass-membership movement. [[ThomasDixonJr]] and [[DWGriffith]] supplied a heroic fictional Klan and symbols that [[WilliamJosephSimmons]] adopted in 1915; [[EdwardYoungClarke]] and [[ElizabethTyler]] then used commissioned recruiters, advertising, Protestant networks, and localized grievance to produce rapid national growth.

The movement sold “100% Americanism” by defining African Americans, Catholics, Jews, immigrants, bootleggers, and alleged moral offenders as threats to white Protestant order. Public-school politics, Prohibition enforcement, clergy, civic charity, titles, costumes, and sociability gave it respectability, while Mer Rouge, Goose Creek, Dallas, and other cases show kidnapping, torture, flogging, branding, murder, and official complicity behind that public face.

## Key Claims

- The second Klan was a distinct 1915 organization that borrowed the first Klan's name and white-supremacist memory while becoming a public, commercialized, mass fraternal movement strongest well beyond the former Confederacy.
- Dixon's fiction and Griffith's [[BirthOfANation|*The Birth of a Nation*]] did more than celebrate a false past: Simmons converted their invented cross-burning and costume imagery into real organizational ritual through [[MediaMythToMovementRitual]].
- Simmons adapted lodge hierarchy, ceremony, titles, secrecy, and belonging into a Klan structure, while Clarke and Tyler built [[CommercializedExtremistRecruitment]] through territorial sales networks, Klegels, commissions, and compulsory regalia.
- “100% Americanism” bundled white supremacy with anti-Catholicism, antisemitism, nativism, public-school politics, Prohibition, sexual regulation, and fear of social change.
- Ministers, Masonic contacts, businessmen, professionals, and local officials helped make Klan membership appear compatible with white Protestant middle-class respectability.
- Political breakthroughs in Oregon and Texas show how recruitment networks could become endorsements, officeholding, school policy, and coercive local power.
- Civic charity and proclaimed law enforcement did not displace terror: the episode places public benevolence beside abduction, whipping, torture, murder, and protection by sympathetic authorities.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content is adopted. This episode supplies the founding, recruitment, and early political prehistory for [[657-the-ku-klux-klan-american-fascists-part-4-glt4147819855|Part 4]]'s account of peak power and collapse.
- The source's broad membership range, Texas total, recruitment split, congressional influence claims, and causal judgments about film, Prohibition, and publicity remain episode-attributed rather than independently verified here.
- The statement that the second Klan was not involved in the Tulsa massacre and the relative priority of anti-Black, anti-Catholic, antisemitic, and nativist targets require geographic and organizational qualification; lack of formal involvement would not separate the movement from the wider white-supremacist order.
- The source uses “Edward Young Clark”; the canonical page uses the established spelling [[EdwardYoungClarke|Edward Young Clarke]].
