# Blame Game

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory|Revisionist History]] episode re-examines the [[Toyota]] sudden-acceleration scandal through the fatal crash involving [[MarkSaylor|Mark Saylor]], vehicle tests, official event-recorder evidence, and [[DickSchmidt|Dick Schmidt]]'s human-performance research. It argues that functioning brakes could overpower the tested vehicles' engines and that many reported incidents are better explained by [[PedalMisapplicationUnderStress|pedal misapplication under stress]] than by an autonomous electronic-throttle failure.

Its broader claim is about [[InstitutionalTechnologyBlame|institutional technology blame]]. A vivid 911 recording, political pressure, litigation, consumer advocacy, and a staged media demonstration made mechanical or software explanations easier to accept than ordinary [[HumanErrorWithoutNegligence|human error without negligence]], even though the supplied summary also leaves the episode's software, corporate-conduct, and causal claims source-scoped.

## Key Claims

- A displaced oversized floor mat could trap a Lexus accelerator, but that explanation did not cover every reported sudden-acceleration case.
- In the episode's track tests, a full-throttle Toyota Camry stopped under braking at speeds up to 100 miles per hour; a more powerful Roush Mustang also stopped, although over a much longer distance.
- The episode infers that a vehicle with functioning brakes should remain stoppable despite an open throttle, making non-application or misapplication of the brake central to unexplained cases.
- [[DickSchmidt]]'s research links sudden-acceleration reports across manufacturers to unfamiliar vehicles, altered pedal geometry, older or shorter drivers, parking contexts, and intended movements that differ from executed movements.
- The episode reports that a 2011 NHTSA investigation with NASA involvement found the overwhelming number of examined events consistent with pedal error and that many event recorders showed no brake application.
- A floor mat may contribute twice: first by trapping the throttle and then by changing the expected geometry of the footwell, increasing the risk of [[PedalMisapplicationUnderStress|pedal error]] in an unfamiliar car.
- [[MarkSaylor]] is presented as a competent, non-reckless driver whose fatal mistake, if the episode's inference is correct, illustrates fallibility rather than negligence.
- The episode argues that media, political, and consumer institutions amplified an electronic-defect theory before adequately confronting evidence for human error.
