## Summary
This [[PlanetMoney]] episode uses the thefts of 40,000 pounds of turkey and 10,800 bottles of [[NobleOak]] bourbon to show how [[StrategicCargoTheft]] has shifted from breaking into trucks toward phishing, carrier impersonation, long-con trucking companies, and compromised freight-booking identities. The cases connect [[FreightIdentityTakeover]] to a fast, fragmented logistics market in which real drivers can unknowingly collect loads for remote criminal organizers.

The episode also develops a distributional account. [[CargoSecurityVerificationTradeoff]] makes stronger identity checks desirable but potentially costly to freight speed and carrier supply, while [[CargoTheftCostPassThrough]] moves losses through replacement production, insurance premiums, freight rates, product prices, and reduced coverage availability. Trackers, RFID, escorts, regulation, and federal investigation each help only conditionally.

## Key Claims
- The turkey load was worth about $102,000; the [[A21WineAndSpirits]] bourbon load contained 1,800 cases on 18 pallets and was worth about $500,000.
- The episode reports $725 million in officially recorded U.S. cargo theft in the prior year, but cites industry estimates of $10 billion to $20 billion and a 47% increase in food-and-beverage theft.
- Stolen food and beverages are attractive because they are difficult to trace and can be resold through local stores, restaurants, parking lots, online marketplaces, or overseas channels.
- In the bourbon case, criminals allegedly took over a legitimate dispatcher's phone number, website, email, and communications after a malicious contract link, then sent a real truck to collect the load.
- The turkey carrier reportedly completed legitimate deliveries for about a year before stealing cargo, illustrating how reputation can be deliberately cultivated before fraud.
- [[WayneJacobs]] describes international syndicates directing schemes through willing, coerced, or unwitting domestic participants, with overseas organizers difficult to arrest.
- Stronger carrier verification and permanent truck identifiers could reduce fraud, but added friction could create backlogs in a system dominated by small carriers and moving roughly $13 billion of goods per day.
- Trackers can be found and removed, item-level RFID can be uneconomic for low-margin goods, and even armed escorts can be defeated.
- Theft losses can raise insurance premiums and freight rates, encourage underreporting, reduce cargo-theft coverage, and ultimately increase consumer prices or deter small-business entry.
