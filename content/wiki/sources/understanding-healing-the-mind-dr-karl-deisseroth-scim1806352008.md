## Summary
This [[HubermanLab]] episode has [[AndrewHuberman]] interview psychiatrist and neuroscientist [[KarlDeisseroth]] about psychiatry's dependence on language and behavior, the search for quantitative biomarkers, and the move from serendipitous treatment toward causal circuit understanding. Deisseroth explains how [[Optogenetics]] and [[CLARITY]] can test function and map structure, while emphasizing that their main psychiatric value is target discovery rather than immediate direct treatment. The conversation extends that cautious framework to vagus and deep-brain stimulation, dissociation, psychedelics, MDMA, clinical observation, and [[ProjectionsBook|Projections]].

## Key Claims
- Psychiatry often lacks decisive individual-level scans or blood tests, so diagnosis still depends on language, behavior, baseline change, symptom course, and functional impairment.
- [[Optogenetics]] uses light-sensitive microbial opsins to activate or inhibit selected neurons, enabling causal tests of which cells, projections, and activity patterns produce behavior.
- The near-term clinical value of optogenetics may be indirect: causal circuit discovery can improve conventional drugs or other treatments without requiring light delivery in the brain.
- [[CLARITY]] preserves molecular and cellular structure in a hydrogel scaffold while removing light-blocking lipids, allowing intact tissue to become optically transparent for mapping.
- Broad electrical stimulation can help selected patients but also recruits off-target tissue; precision requires target knowledge as well as better devices.
- Cross-species dissociation work illustrates a causal-inference ladder from a drug-associated activity pattern to a homologous human region and optogenetic recreation of a dissociative-like state in animals.
- Psychedelics and MDMA may make alternative models, connection, or learning more available, but mechanisms, lasting risks, eligibility, and clinical delivery remain unresolved.
