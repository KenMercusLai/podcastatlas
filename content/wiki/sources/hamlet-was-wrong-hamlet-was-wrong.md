# Hamlet Was Wrong

## Summary

In this [[RevisionistHistory]] episode, [[MalcolmGladwell]] uses his impulsive hiring of assistants and an accountant to develop [[HiringNihilism]]: people differ in ability, but conventional résumés, interviews, and promotion rules may predict performance far less reliably than their ritual authority suggests. His cases are provocations rather than comparative evidence that casual selection is generally superior.

The organizational evidence comes from [[AlanBenson]]'s study of roughly 100,000 salespeople at 400 firms. Star sellers were promoted more often even though their prior sales success did not predict management skill; teams led by collaborative sellers reportedly performed better, yet passing over top performers reduced those sellers' output. [[AlbertOHirschman]] supplies the philosophical conclusion: accepting uncertainty can release action and support humility when a decision fails, rather than turning doubt into paralysis or a job mismatch into a verdict on a person's worth.

## Key Claims

- Gladwell hired assistants after brief, informal conversations that largely ignored education, credentials, and relevant experience, and several of those hires developed into durable working relationships.
- [[HiringNihilism]] differs from agnosticism: it accepts that people vary in ability while doubting that prevailing selection rituals can reliably identify the best candidate in advance.
- The [[PeterPrinciple]] describes promotion based on success in one role until a person reaches a different role whose demands exceed or bypass the skills that produced the promotion.
- [[AlanBenson]]'s sales data reportedly showed that star sellers were more likely to become managers even though their teams subsequently performed worse than teams led by collaborative sellers.
- The promotion problem is a trade-off: promoting stars can remove strong individual contributors and create weak managers, while not promoting them can reduce motivation and sales performance.
- Gladwell's use of [[AlbertOHirschman]] reframes uncertainty as permission to act, revise, and improvise rather than a reason to wait for impossible predictive certainty.
- A failed placement need not define the worker's general worth; it can indicate a mismatch between a person and a particular role.
