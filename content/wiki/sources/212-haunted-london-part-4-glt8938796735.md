# 212. Haunted London (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode walks from [[NewgatePrison|Newgate]] and the Old Bailey through [[Smithfield]], [[StBartholomewTheGreat|St Bartholomew the Great]], the buried [[RiverFleet|River Fleet]], and Saffron Hill. Its central argument is that [[London]]'s past often survives not as an intact monumental landscape but through reused buildings, street names, plaques, buried infrastructure, literature, ritual, and stories that make ordinary-looking places historically legible.

The route layers imprisonment, execution, religious foundation and dissolution, markets, fire, medicine, revolt, martyrdom, waste, haunting, and fiction within a compact district. It therefore extends [[UrbanArchitecturalMemory]] beyond preservation alone: interpretation can recover historical density even where demolition, rebuilding, underground rivers, or mundane modern streets have reduced the visible evidence.

## Key Claims

- Newgate joined a Roman gate, a long-lived prison, the Old Bailey, nearby public executions, St Sepulchre's execution bell, and later Victorian reform infrastructure within one legal and commemorative landscape.
- [[Smithfield]] accumulated overlapping roles as livestock market, fairground, tournament field, execution site, revolt setting, religious precinct, and later memorial landscape.
- [[StBartholomewTheGreat|St Bartholomew the Great]] and its hospital show institutional continuity through medieval foundation, Reformation disruption, changing secular uses, later restoration, medicine, and popular culture.
- The buried [[RiverFleet]] explains topography, street naming, sanitation, and the placement of a meat market whose waste could be swept downhill.
- Plaques and memorials for William Wallace, the Peasants' Revolt, and Protestant martyrs preserve later political interpretations as well as the events commemorated.
- [[CharlesDickens]]'s use of Little Britain and Saffron Hill shows fiction operating as a map of historical urban imagination, especially where the modern street itself supplies little obvious evidence.
- Ghost stories and antiquarian anecdotes are useful evidence for later memory and fascination, but not automatically reliable evidence for the events they claim to describe.
