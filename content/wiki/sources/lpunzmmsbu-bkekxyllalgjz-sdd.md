# 376-我们如何从网民变成了用户？卡夫卡《审判》

## Summary

This [[DushuBuchenglin|独树不成林]] episode reads [[TheTrialKafka|Kafka's *The Trial* / 卡夫卡《审判》]] as a study of procedure rather than only an unjust court. It compares Josef K.'s inability to understand, alter, or exit the court process with platform agreements, moderation, recommendation, and appeals, arguing that [[NetizenToPlatformUserTransition]] replaces participation in rule-making with operation inside predefined choices. Its strongest synthesis joins [[OpaquePlatformProcedure]] to [[AlgorithmicCreatorSelfDiscipline]]: when outcomes arrive without accountable reasons, people learn to optimize language for an imagined system, and procedural skill can displace the prior question of whether the rules are legitimate.

## Key Claims

- “Netizen” once implied membership in an online community where participants could help set rules, choose moderators, or leave to build another forum; “user” more often names a role inside a platform whose rules and options are already fixed.
- Platform appeals can redirect attention from whether a decision or rule is justified toward which operation, wording, or compliance signal might obtain a better result.
- The episode interprets the German title of [[TheTrialKafka|*The Trial*]] as carrying a broader process or procedure sense and treats Josef K.'s central predicament as entry into a system he cannot understand, change, or leave.
- Diffuse authority makes responsibility hard to locate: guards, lawyers, officials, and customer-service agents may each execute a procedure while claiming not to control it.
- Opaque recommendation and moderation outcomes encourage people to infer preferred timing, topics, titles, and sensitive wording from individual results, turning uncertain guesses into practical expertise.
- Language then shifts from communication, persuasion, and public argument toward an instrument for pleasing an imagined evaluator and managing procedural consequences.
- Josef K.'s movement from publicly questioning the court to wondering how he should cooperate with his own execution represents the internalization of procedure, culminating in shame without an explained offense or judgment.
- The contemporary analogy distinguishes fair rule execution from fair rule-making: users may demand consistent treatment while losing the expectation that they can participate in governing the system itself.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode extends existing creator-side accounts of opaque platform feedback from content tactics to language, cognition, legitimacy, and political membership.
- The platform analogy is interpretive rather than an equivalence: platforms differ in ownership, appeal, moderation, portability, community participation, and exit costs, while Josef K.'s court ends in execution.
- Claims about early-internet self-government, the semantic history of “netizen” and “user,” the German title, and the novel's meaning remain source-scoped pending historical, linguistic, and textual comparison.
- The source diagnoses procedural adaptation but does not supply a tested reform program or comparative evidence about which governance arrangements restore meaningful participation.
