# 321: Hundred Years' War: A Storm of Swords (Part 4)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows the first phase of the [[HundredYearsWar|Hundred Years' War]] from England's apparent supremacy after Poitiers to the collapse of most English gains under [[CharlesVOfFrance|Charles V of France]]. Captive [[JohnIIOfFrance|John II]], the [[Jacquerie]], Parisian disorder, [[CharlesTheBad|Charles the Bad]], and roaming [[FreeCompaniesHundredYearsWar|free companies]] show why an overwhelming battlefield advantage did not produce a governable settlement.

[[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]] failed to convert victory into conquest and accepted the [[TreatyOfBretigny|Treaty of Brétigny]], but [[EdwardTheBlackPrince|the Black Prince's]] taxation and Castilian debts weakened English rule in [[Aquitaine]]. Charles V restored revenue and local loyalty, while [[BertrandDuGuesclin|Bertrand du Guesclin]] avoided England's preferred open battles and pursued [[AttritionalReconquest|attritional reconquest]], leaving both kingdoms exhausted and politically unstable.

## Key Claims

- England's possession of John II and David II, together with loot from France, made victory after Poitiers appear decisive without creating durable control.
- The proposed Windsor settlement depended on a French state able to raise the king's ransom and enforce territorial concessions precisely when royal, urban, noble, peasant, and military order was breaking down.
- Edward III's 1359-1360 campaign failed to take Reims or Paris, showing that raids and battlefield superiority could not by themselves conquer France.
- Brétigny gave Edward a greatly enlarged sovereign Aquitaine in exchange for renouncing his French crown claim, but implementation and allegiance remained unstable.
- The Black Prince's expensive court, taxation, and Castilian intervention intensified Aquitanian resistance and supplied Charles V with a legal-political opening.
- Charles V rebuilt French revenue and loyalty, while du Guesclin's avoidance of open battle shifted the war toward attrition and the recovery of territory.
- English reconquest attempts deteriorated into costly raids, including John of Gaunt's 1373 chevauchée, while Edward III and the Black Prince ended their lives amid illness, decline, and lost prestige.
- The first phase closes with most English gains lost except Calais and with young kings inheriting war damage, taxation, and unresolved dynastic conflict.

## Key Quotes

> "lose in one day what it had gained in twenty years" - the Duke of Lancaster's warning in favor of negotiation.

> "war of attrition" - the episode's description of du Guesclin's alternative to open battle.
