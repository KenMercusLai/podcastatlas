# A Polite Word for Liar

## Summary
This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode uses [[LarryAdler]] and [[JohnWeitz]]'s incompatible accounts of a postwar Munich raid to ask whether a false or changing memory reliably reveals dishonesty or character. Adler's admitted story improvement, glider error, anti-McCarthy sacrifice, and late-life candor extend [[ReconstructiveMemory]] and [[AutobiographicalMemoryHistoricalEvidence]], while Weitz's secrecy and later third version show how loyalty, trauma, and friendship can shape what a witness is willing to narrate. The resulting [[MemoryErrorCharacterInference]] principle separates factual correction from unsupported moral diagnosis.

## Key Claims
- Adler describes repeated embellishment as capable of displacing a less exciting original until the improved story feels true.
- Adler and Weitz gave sharply different accounts of a Munich operation: Weitz initially remembered armed resistance, deaths, and arrests, while Adler remembered no shooting and only two older women in the house.
- A later published account and Weitz's 1990s version preserve arrests or Nazi participants while moving toward Adler's memory of no resistance and distressed older women.
- Adler's mistaken glider memory shows wish-shaped reconstruction, but his career-ending refusal to cooperate with anti-communist naming demands complicates a global judgment of vanity or dishonesty.
- Weitz's wartime trauma, operational secrecy, and reluctance to narrate Germany contrast with Adler's expansive storytelling without making either style a transparent measure of truthfulness.
- Memory discrepancies can justify corroboration and claim-level correction without independently proving motive, moral character, or deliberate deception.
- The episode cannot establish exactly what happened during the Munich operation, so its conclusion is interpretive rather than forensic.

## Contradictions
- The Munich accounts conflict on resistance, deaths, arrests, occupants, and timing; the supplied structured summary does not adjudicate the operation's exact sequence.
- The episode qualifies [[OralHistoryMemoryAnalysis]]: self-image can shape memory, but an error does not reliably disclose a stable trait or single motive.
- Adler's anti-McCarthy conduct and Rushdie reading complicate, but do not logically disprove, vanity or self-dramatization in particular stories.
- Biographical details, remembered dialogue, the Munich accounts, family interpretations, and Gladwell's motive analysis remain episode-scoped because the supplied file is a structured summary rather than a transcript, operational archive, diary, or complete biography.
