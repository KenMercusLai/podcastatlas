# 12 Days: Reconquest of Spain and the Old Queen of Hawaii

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] anniversary episode uses 2 January to pair Granada's surrender in 1492 with the birth of [[QueenEmmaOfHawaii|Queen Emma of Hawaii]] in 1836. The first half treats the end of Muslim rule in Iberia as both the culmination of [[IberianReconquista|Christian conquest]] and the beginning of a confessional rupture involving forced conversion, Jewish expulsion, Atlantic expansion, and the contested sincerity of religious motives.

The second half places Emma and [[KamehamehaIV]] inside a [[HawaiianMonarchyForeignPressure|Hawaiian monarchy negotiating British and American pressure]]. Emma's English-influenced upbringing, Anglican commitment, hospital and educational work, European royal connections, and unsuccessful 1874 candidacy make her a lens on Indigenous royal agency before American commercial power helped overthrow the monarchy.

## Key Claims

- The source retains “Reconquista” as the Christian actors' own recovery narrative while warning that the term can cast centuries of Muslim life in Iberia as a temporary intrusion.
- Granada's isolation enabled [[FerdinandIIOfAragon|Ferdinand II]] and [[IsabellaIOfCastile|Isabella I]] to receive the city's surrender from Muhammad XII, commonly called Boabdil, on 2 January 1492.
- The capitulation's initial protections did not prevent later forced conversion, confiscation, and expulsion; the same year also brought [[ChristopherColumbus|Columbus's]] voyage and the expulsion of Spain's Jews.
- The hosts argue that apocalyptic Christianity and conversion were genuine historical motives for Ferdinand, Isabella, Columbus, and later conquistadors even when conquest also produced wealth, domination, and exploitation.
- European weapons, disease, missionaries, and diplomacy transformed Hawaii after contact, while Hawaiian rulers used monarchy, travel, Christianity, and foreign relationships to navigate that transformation.
- [[KamehamehaIV]] and [[QueenEmmaOfHawaii|Queen Emma]] cultivated British royal and Anglican ties; after losing their son and then her husband, Emma made hospital work, education, and church patronage central to her public role.
- Emma's defeat by Kalakaua in the 1874 royal election is presented as a contest partly aligned with British and American influence, while the later overthrow of the monarchy is linked to American commercial interests.

## Key Quotes

> “Moor's last sigh” - the romantic label attached to Boabdil's reported farewell view of Granada.

> “old queen” - the remembered title the episode gives Emma after her unsuccessful 1874 candidacy.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content was found. The episode reinforces the wiki's qualified use of “Reconquista” and its rejection of both idealized convivencia and simple permanent-conflict narratives.
- The episode's claim that religious belief should not be reduced to material interest complements rather than cancels evidence of extraction, forced conversion, expulsion, and commercial power.
- Boabdil's farewell scene and his mother's rebuke are romantic traditions rather than secure eyewitness history. The episode's compressed Hawaiian chronology, British-American contrast, motives in the 1874 election, and counterfactual that Emma might have kept Hawaii closer to Britain remain source-scoped.
