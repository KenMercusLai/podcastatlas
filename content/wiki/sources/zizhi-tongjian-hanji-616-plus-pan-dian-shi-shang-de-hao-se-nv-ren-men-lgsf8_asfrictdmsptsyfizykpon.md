# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》616 plus丨盘点史上的好色女人们

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] side episode contrasts later widow-chastity expectations with an episode-level picture of greater remarriage and divorce latitude in the [[WesternHanDynasty|西汉]], then turns from marriage norms to the way wealth, rank, and palace access shaped women's intimate choices. [[PingyangPrincessWesternHan|平阳公主]] and [[WeiQingHanGeneral|卫青]] supply the remarriage example; [[JingwuPrincessWesternHan|靖武公主]], [[ShanyinPrincessLiuChuyu|山阴公主刘楚玉]], [[LiuZiyeFormerDeposedEmperor|宋前废帝刘子业]], [[WuZetian|武则天]], [[XueHuaiyi|薛怀义]], [[ZhangYizhi|张易之]], [[ZhangChangzong|张昌宗]], and [[PrincessTaiping|太平公主]] carry the court-intimacy branch.

The durable synthesis is narrower than the title's ranking of “lustful women.” The source is most useful when it shows that female remarriage, desire, and sexual access were judged through changing institutions: ordinary marriage exit depended on period norms, while elite women could convert title and resources into unusual intimate agency. Its later palace stories are compressed, moralized, and sometimes disputed, so the wiki preserves them as evidence about [[FemaleMarriageAgencyHistoricalVariation|historically variable female marriage agency]] and [[CourtIntimacyAsPoliticalResource|court intimacy as a political resource]], not as a verified league table of sexual conduct.

## Key Claims

- The episode contrasts Song-Ming widow-chastity pressure and Cheng-Zhu moral discipline with a source-scoped picture of wider widow remarriage and female-initiated divorce in the Han period.
- It links marriage agency to status and material resources: imperial women could remarry or maintain male favorites because title, wealth, and household power changed the cost of acting on desire.
- [[PingyangPrincessWesternHan|Pingyang Princess]]'s marriage to [[WeiQingHanGeneral|Wei Qing]] is used to show that a widowed Han princess could remarry without the later chastity ideal serving as an absolute barrier.
- [[JingwuPrincessWesternHan|Jingwu Princess]] is presented through multiple marriages and later household scandal, though the episode compresses names, chronology, and contested allegations.
- The episode places male beauty culture and palace sexual disorder inside the instability of the Wei-Jin and Northern-Southern Dynasties rather than treating desire as detached from political breakdown.
- [[ShanyinPrincessLiuChuyu|Liu Chuyu]]'s complaint about unequal sexual access and [[LiuZiyeFormerDeposedEmperor|Liu Ziye]]'s grant of thirty male attendants become the sharpest case of rank converting into intimate privilege.
- The Wu-Zhou branch presents [[XueHuaiyi|Xue Huaiyi]], [[ZhangYizhi|Zhang Yizhi]], and [[ZhangChangzong|Zhang Changzong]] as favorites whose access to [[WuZetian|Wu Zetian]] carried political consequences.
- The host explicitly brings a female perspective to historical interpretation, but the second half also relies on sensational language and moral condemnation that can reproduce the gendered judgment it seeks to complicate.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. The episode's broad claims about Han remarriage, female-initiated divorce, noblewomen's titles, and Wei-Jin male beauty require wider legal and social evidence before becoming period-wide conclusions.
- The alleged incest between Liu Ziye and Liu Chuyu, the causal claim that Xue Huaiyi killed Princess Taiping's husband Xue Shao, the exact route by which Taiping introduced Zhang Yizhi, the burning of White Horse Temple, and the manner of Xue Huaiyi's death are preserved as episode claims rather than settled facts.
- Jingwu Princess's identity, husbands, children, and alleged relationship with Xue Kuang are compressed in the narration; the canonical page keeps the better-established identity map and treats the sexual charge as politically usable but unverified.
