# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》345-1｜汉武帝宝鼎悬案，至今仍是未解之谜

## Summary

This short [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode presents a ding reportedly excavated during [[HanWudi|汉武帝]]'s reign at Fenyin by a local spirit medium named Jin. Local officials connect the undeciphered object to [[XinyuanPingWesternHan|新垣平]]'s earlier promise that a lost Zhou ding would appear there; after an imperial inspection declares the vessel genuine, Han Wudi receives it ceremonially, accepts congratulations, and writes the “宝鼎歌.”

The durable synthesis is narrower than the title's mystery claim. The episode shows how discovery, official verification, inherited story, court reception, and royal authorship can convert an ambiguous artifact into [[AuspiciousOmenPolitics|祥瑞政治]]. It then retells the [[Jiuding|九鼎]] tradition—from Yu's casting and the transfer through Xia, Shang, and Zhou to loss in the Si River—to explain why a ding could operate as a symbol of sovereign authority. The supplied document stops during that legendary background and does not establish that the Fenyin vessel was one of the Jiuding or resolve its date, inscription, or archaeological identity.

## Key Claims

- A Fenyin spirit medium named Jin reportedly uncovers a large ding with unusual designs and writing but no identifying inscription during a local sacrifice.
- Local officials connect the find to Xinyuan Ping's earlier prediction that a Zhou ding would appear at Fenyin and report it upward.
- The episode contrasts [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]]'s deception by Xinyuan Ping with Han Wudi's decision to send inspectors before accepting the later vessel.
- After the inspection reportedly confirms that the object is genuine, Han Wudi receives it at Ganquan Palace, accepts court congratulations, and writes the “宝鼎歌.”
- Verification that a vessel is physically real does not by itself verify the court's Zhou-ding identification or the political meaning attached to it.
- The Jiuding tradition makes the ding a transferable sign of rule: the episode says Yu cast the vessels from regional tribute bronze and images, after which they passed from Xia to Shang to Zhou and were eventually lost in the Si River.
- The opening quotation from 王充 frames belief in spirits and the pursuit of blessing critically, while the host still distinguishes Han Wudi's inspection step from immediate credulity.
