# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》218｜贾谊为什么被称为千古第一政治奇才？（1）

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode reads [[JiaYi|贾谊]]'s memorial to [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] as a diagnosis of danger beneath apparent peace. The death of [[LiuChangHuainanKing|淮南王刘长]] supplies the immediate setting, but the argument moves beyond one prince: large kingdoms create enough territorial and political capacity for rulers of either different or shared surname to develop ambitions that personal virtue and kinship alone cannot reliably contain.

The proposed remedy is “众建诸侯而少其力”: divide Qi, Zhao, Chu, and other large kingdoms repeatedly among royal descendants, using smaller territories to make law and ritual more effective and rebellion less feasible. The episode presents this as preventive institutional design that can keep the state stable even under a weak or infant ruler, while its historical examples, exact memorial wording, and claim that the scheme would guarantee lasting peace remain source-scoped.

## Key Claims

- Jia Yi rejects the court's claim that the realm is already secure and compares the situation to someone sleeping above a fire burning beneath a pile of wood.
- He presents delayed reform as especially dangerous because a later court may have to destroy royal kin through violence after concentrated power has matured.
- The episode attributes rebellion risk primarily to institutional position and kingdom scale rather than to a fixed moral difference between different-surname and Liu-family kings.
- Changsha's survival is explained partly by its small population and limited power: constrained capacity leaves less room for political ambition.
- Counterfactual comparisons involving [[HanXin|韩信]], [[PengYue|彭越]], Fan Kuai, Li Shang, Zhou Bo, and Guan Ying argue that large grants can endanger otherwise meritorious men, while smaller titles might have reduced the threat posed by former kings.
- Jia Yi proposes dividing Qi, Zhao, Chu, and other large kingdoms among royal descendants until no further division is practical; where heirs are temporarily lacking, the court can reserve future kingdoms under centrally appointed administration.
- Smaller kingdoms are expected to be easier to govern through law and ritual, aligning royal houses with the emperor while reducing the territorial base for rebellion.
- The infant-emperor and empty-throne images make institutional resilience the test: a secure order should not depend entirely on the incumbent ruler's personal ability.

## Key Quotes

> “夫抱火厝之积薪之下而寝其上，火未及燃，因谓之安。” - the episode's image for mistaking delayed disaster for present security.

> “众建诸侯而少其力。” - the compact territorial remedy attributed to Jia Yi.

> “力少则易使以义，国小则亡邪心。” - the mechanism joining reduced capacity to legal and ritual restraint.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content is adopted. The episode expands the brief kingdom-partition proposal already recorded from Hanji 219 and complements, rather than replaces, the selective-buffer strategy recorded in Hanji 221.
- Institutional qualification: reducing kingdom scale may lower the capacity for rebellion, but the episode does not establish that small states are always loyal or that territory alone determines political behavior.
- Counterfactual qualification: claims that Han Xin, Peng Yue, Fan Kuai, Li Shang, Zhou Bo, or Guan Ying would have met different fates under different titles cannot be directly tested.
- Implementation qualification: reserving future kingdoms under centrally appointed administration could itself enlarge central discretion; the source does not examine succession disputes, local resistance, or administrative costs.
