# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》267｜实事求是，谁第一个提出来的？

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode links epistemic humility to [[ShishiQiushi|实事求是]], then locates the phrase in the [[HanShu|《汉书》]] description of [[LiuDeHejianKingWesternHan|河间献王刘德]]. It presents Liu De's acquisition, copying, collective collation, and presentation of ancient books after the [[QinBookBurningPolicy|秦代焚书令]] as a practical preservation system, while leaving the exact history of the phrase, individual texts, quantities, institutions, and transmission lines source-scoped.

## Key Claims

- Existing knowledge can become a rigid interpretive frame; the episode uses the hammer-and-nail metaphor and the Analects formula “知之为知之，不知为不知” to make honest acknowledgment of uncertainty the entry condition for inquiry.
- The host identifies “修学好古，实事求是” in the *Han Shu* biography of Liu De as the earliest source of the phrase and glosses it as establishing the facts and seeking what is correct.
- Liu De is described as Han Jingdi's second son, Liu Rong's younger brother, and a vassal king enfeoffed in 155 BCE who devoted much of his reign to collecting and organizing ancient books.
- The episode says he searched across Lu, Yan, Zhao, and Wei, paid heavily for books, and, when owners would not sell, had copies made while leaving an exemplar with the owner.
- Liu De reportedly brought Mao Chang, Guan Changqing, and other scholars together at Rihua Palace to edit and study the collected texts, turning royal resources into an organized collation project.
- The program credits him with presenting edited copies of the [[Shijing|《诗经》]], *Zuo Zhuan*, *Zhouguan*, *Liji*, *Shangshu*, *Mengzi*, and *Laozi* to the imperial court, and says the books numbered about one thousand sections.
- It also connects Liu De's court visit to the presentation of ritual music and wine, and reports praise from [[GongsunHong|公孙弘]] and [[DongZhongshu|董仲舒]].
- The host treats Liu De and comparable readers as important to classical continuity, but the episode closes by acknowledging that the historical record does not state the cause of his death soon after meeting Han Wudi.

## Key Quotes

> “知之为知之，不知为不知，是知也。” - the epistemic-humility principle used to open the episode.

> “修学好古，实事求是。” - the *Han Shu* appraisal around which the episode builds Liu De's scholarly method.

## Connections

- [[LiuDeHejianKingWesternHan|河间献王刘德]] - collector and learned vassal king whose book-seeking, copying, collation, and presentation anchor the episode.
- [[ShishiQiushi|实事求是]] - phrase interpreted here as both epistemic honesty and a fact-seeking method of textual collation.
- [[Shijing|《诗经》]] - one of the classics the episode specifically includes among Liu De's collected, edited, and presented texts.
- [[QinBookBurningPolicy|秦代焚书令]] - destruction-and-scarcity background used to explain the cultural stakes of recovering private books.
- [[HanShu|《汉书》]] and [[Shiji|《史记》]] - histories cited for Liu De's learning, conduct, and attraction of scholars.
- [[HanJingdi|汉景帝]] and [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] - Liu De's father and the emperor associated with his later book and ritual-music presentations.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content was adopted. The episode fills the acquisition, copying, editorial, and court-presentation mechanism that Hanji 268 part 1 leaves unspecified.
- The source itself leaves unresolved whether “实事求是” was Liu De's articulated principle or Ban Gu's later appraisal; its claim that the phrase originated here therefore remains source-scoped.
- The account that virtually all classical and Hundred Schools texts had disappeared, that Liu De personally traveled the named regions, that Rihua Palace housed more than one hundred scholars working continuously, and that his presented books numbered one thousand sections requires primary-source comparison.
- The exact manuscripts, received-text lineages, role of Mao Chang and Guan Changqing, dates and number of court presentations, ritual-music evaluation, origin of Xian County's name, and Liu De's cause of death remain episode-attributed.
- The broad claim that modern access to the listed classics is owed to Liu De should not be read as an exclusive or fully demonstrated transmission history.
