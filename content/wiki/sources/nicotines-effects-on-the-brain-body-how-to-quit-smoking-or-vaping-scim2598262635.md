# Nicotine’s Effects on the Brain & Body & How to Quit Smoking or Vaping

## Summary
This solo [[HubermanLab]] episode has [[AndrewHuberman]] separate nicotine pharmacology from the harms of smoking, vaping, dipping, and snuffing. It adds [[NicotineNeurobiologyAndDependence]] and extends [[ElectronicCigaretteRisk]], [[SmokingCessationSupport]], [[AcetylcholineFocusSupport]], and [[DopaminePeakTroughBaseline]] by linking receptor activation, delivery speed, attention, reward, withdrawal, and cessation support. Its practical synthesis is that rapid delivery strengthens reinforcement, harmful routes should not be mistaken for nicotine itself, and quitting is more likely to require structured behavioral and clinical support than willpower alone.

## Key Claims
- Nicotine activates nicotinic acetylcholine receptors, including alpha-4 beta-2 receptors discussed in relation to attention and appetite, and recruits dopamine, acetylcholine, and norepinephrine systems.
- Faster delivery produces faster nicotine and dopamine changes; the episode therefore treats vaping as especially reinforcing and concerning for young people.
- Nicotine can transiently increase alertness, motivation, focus, heart rate, blood pressure, vasoconstriction, appetite suppression, and metabolic rate, but these effects do not make it a generally safe cognitive enhancer.
- Smoking, vaping, dipping, and snuffing add route-specific toxicant, vascular, pulmonary, oral, nasal, cardiovascular, and cancer risks; lack of combustion does not mean lack of harm.
- Repeated nicotine exposure can produce tolerance, lower-baseline states, craving, and withdrawal, making cessation a homeostatic and learned-reinforcement problem rather than a simple failure of resolve.
- The episode discusses clinical hypnosis, bupropion, nicotine patches, gum, and nasal spray as cessation supports, while medication choice, contraindications, dosing, and combinations require qualified care.
