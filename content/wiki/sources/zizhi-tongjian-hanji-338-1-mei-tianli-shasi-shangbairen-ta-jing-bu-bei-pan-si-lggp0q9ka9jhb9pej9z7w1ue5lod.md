# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》338-1｜没天理！杀死上百人，他竟不被判死

## Summary

This short [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode recounts the fall of [[LiuPengliJidongKingWesternHan|济东王刘彭离]], a son of [[LiuWuLiangKingWesternHan|梁孝王刘武]] who is said to have spent twenty-nine years as king and to have gone out at night with servants and fugitives to rob and kill travelers. A victim's son eventually gets an accusation to the central court, investigation confirms more than one hundred identifiable killings, and officials request execution.

The durable tension is between verified atrocity and royal kinship. [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] does not impose the requested death sentence; the episode attributes his restraint to Liu Wu's service during the Rebellion of the Seven States and to family attachment. Liu Pengli is instead reduced to commoner status and exiled to Shangyong, while his kingdom is abolished and its territory becomes Dahe Commandery. The case therefore qualifies [[RuleOverKinshipPower|法度压过亲缘特权]] with a clear [[RoyalKinshipPenaltyMitigation|宗室亲缘减刑]] counterexample: punishment remains severe, but proximity and inherited merit change its ceiling.

## Key Claims

- Liu Pengli is presented as a son of Liang Xiao Wang Liu Wu and a Western Han vassal king whose father's rebellion-era service gives the family exceptional political credit.
- Over a twenty-nine-year reign, Liu Pengli allegedly goes out after dark with servants and fugitives to rob, kill, and take property for pleasure.
- The known violence becomes a local regime of fear: more than one hundred victims can be identified, yet residents are afraid to travel at night or accuse their king.
- A victim's son uses intermediaries and money to get a written accusation to the central authorities, showing the access barriers facing ordinary people against a territorial royal.
- Han Wudi first suspects slander against the imperial house, but an investigation confirms the accusation and officials request Liu Pengli's execution.
- Wudi refuses the requested death penalty; the episode attributes the mitigation to Liu Wu's service in the Rebellion of the Seven States and to royal kinship.
- Liu Pengli is stripped of kingship, reduced to commoner status, exiled to Shangyong, and loses his kingdom, whose territory is reorganized as Dahe Commandery.
- The opening discussion of ordinary lies and the closing butter-tea judgment story frame the political case as a moral warning that power may delay accountability without making wrongdoing disappear.

## Key Quotes

> “没天理！杀死上百人，他竟不被判死” - the episode title foregrounds the gap between the verified scale of killing and the final sentence.

> “要想人不知，除非己莫为” - the closing maxim turns the historical case into a warning about concealed wrongdoing.

- [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] - ruler who verifies the accusation and punishes Liu Pengli but refuses the officials' requested death sentence.

## Contradictions

- This is a direct qualification, not a factual contradiction, to [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-392-1-hanwudi-shoule-meimei-de-qian-weihe-reng-yao-shasi-qinwaisheng-lhey4utgx3njqea-suiqohjybrpa|Hanji 392-1]]: the same ruler later approves execution of a close nephew and son-in-law who killed one attendant, whereas Liu Pengli is spared after more than one hundred known killings. The current evidence supports selective, ruler-centered enforcement rather than a consistent kin-neutral standard.
- Transcript normalization: “刘鹏黎” is normalized to 刘彭离 because the Jidong kingship, Liang Xiao Wang parentage, twenty-nine-year reign, Shangyong exile, and Dahe Commandery outcome identify the figure.
- Source-scope caution: the episode's claim that Wudi spared Liu Pengli specifically because of Liu Wu's rebellion-era merit is an explanatory interpretation; the transmitted account directly states that Wudi could not bear to execute him but does not fully separate affection, precedent, dynastic credit, and political calculation.
- Procedural caution: the episode does not establish the full investigation record, identities of most victims or officials, governing homicide statutes, formal commutation mechanism, conditions of exile, or whether the opening and closing moral stories derive from the historical record.
