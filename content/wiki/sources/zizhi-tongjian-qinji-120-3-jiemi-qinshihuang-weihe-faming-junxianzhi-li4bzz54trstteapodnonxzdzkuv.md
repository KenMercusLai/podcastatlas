# 《资治通鉴·秦纪》120-3｜揭秘秦始皇为何发明郡县制

## Summary
This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode completes the anti-enfeoffment branch that [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-120-2-qinchao-di-yi-tao-vi-sheji-linian-lno-aeljks2vt1k3wjrizsanirtq|Qinji 120-2]] only began. After [[QinShiHuang|嬴政 / 秦始皇]] has unified the six states, [[QinWangWan|王绾]] proposes placing imperial sons over distant former [[YanState|燕国]], [[QiState|齐国]], and [[ChuState|楚国]] territory, while [[LiSi|李斯]] argues that renewed hereditary kingdoms would reproduce the violence Qin has just ended.

The episode's core institutional claim is [[CommanderyCountyAntiEnfeoffment|郡县制反分封定局]]: Qin's empire-wide commandery-county system is not just administrative novelty, but a way to preserve the results of unification by preventing conquered territory from becoming new semi-independent states. It distinguishes [[ZhouRoyalHouse|周王室]] enfeoffment under [[ZhouWenwang|周文王]] and [[ZhouWuwang|周武王]] from Qin's situation: early Zhou fiefs are described as authorization for armed expansion into only partly controlled space, whereas Qin would be dividing territory already brought under direct rule.

The source then uses later history as retrospective validation. [[XiangYu|项羽]]'s eighteen kingdoms, [[LiuBang|刘邦]]'s early Han same-surname kings, the Wu-Chu Seven States Rebellion, and [[HanWudi|汉武帝]]'s later weakening of princely states all become examples of why indirect hereditary rule can recreate separatism, while commanderies and counties become the durable template associated with "百代皆行秦政法."

## Key Claims
- Qin's post-unification problem is not only title, color, calendar, and legitimacy design; it also needs a territorial administration decision.
- Wang Wan argues from distance and governability: former Yan, Qi, and Chu regions are far from [[Xianyang|咸阳]], so imperial sons should be enfeoffed there to secure control.
- Li Si rejects that solution by reading Zhou enfeoffment as the source of later feudal fragmentation: kinship may bind one generation, but later descendants become strangers and attack one another.
- Li Si's alternative is to turn the whole empire into commanderies and counties, while rewarding royal sons and meritorious officials through tax revenue rather than territorial sovereignty.
- Qin Shi Huang accepts Li Si's position and frames renewed enfeoffment as planting the seed of future war.
- The episode argues that early Zhou enfeoffment was an expedient expansion mechanism, not proof that enfeoffment was a superior long-term system.
- Qin's situation differs because the land has already been conquered; dividing it afterward would surrender the direct-control gains produced by generations of Qin war.
- In the host's reading, rulers naturally prefer direct rule where capacity permits it, and Qin already has enough capacity to avoid indirect princely rule.
- Later examples from Xiang Yu, Liu Bang, and Han Wudi are used to support the long-run anti-enfeoffment argument.
- The episode says Qin divides the empire into thirty-six commanderies, each with a commandery governor, military commandant, and inspector.
- Source-scope caution: the transcript ends shortly after the three commandery offices and contains an unclear fragment about a measure that made it harder for local populations to cluster, so this page does not infer the full local-control discussion.

## Key Quotes
