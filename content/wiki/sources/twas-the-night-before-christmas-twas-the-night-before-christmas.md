# Twas the Night Before Christmas

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode, hosted by [[MalcolmGladwell]], investigates the long-running claim that [[HenryLivingstonJr]] rather than [[ClementClarkeMoore]] wrote [[AVisitFromStNicholas|“A Visit from St. Nicholas”]]. It first reconstructs [[MaryVanDusen]] and [[DonFoster]]'s stylistic case for Livingston, then follows [[SethKaller]]'s documentary and contextual rebuttal and concludes that the surviving evidence favors Moore.

The episode also places the poem inside [[ModernChristmasReinvention]]. Its domestic, child-centered gift-giver helps turn older public Christmas inversion into a private family ritual and fixes major features of [[SantaClaus]] in American memory. The broader lesson is methodological: [[LiteraryAuthorshipAttribution]] must combine style with provenance, chronology, comparison corpora, and disconfirming documents, while [[FalseBalanceControversyPersistence]] can keep a weak alternative alive after its evidentiary basis has eroded.

## Key Claims

- “A Visit from St. Nicholas” was published anonymously in the *Troy Sentinel* in 1823 and is conventionally attributed to Clement Clarke Moore.
- The poem helped establish the domestic Santa story, including a nocturnal household visit and eight named reindeer.
- Earlier Christmas customs could involve lower-status groups demanding food, drink, or hospitality; elite New York reformers redirected the festive inversion toward children and the household.
- Livingston descendants preserved recollections that he had read the poem, but Livingston made no public claim and the reported handwritten copy was said to have been destroyed.
- Foster argued from meter, vocabulary, punctuation, attitudes, and biography that Livingston's cheerful anapestic verse better matched the poem than Moore's writing.
- Kaller and other researchers challenged the family chronology, restored quotations presented without context, found verbal parallels in Moore's work, and identified Moore poems using child-oriented, Christmas, fairy, winter, and anapestic material.
- Foster's other disputed attributions do not decide this case, but they weaken an appeal to his authority; the positive Moore comparisons are more probative than a caricature of either candidate's personality.
- Repetition by journalists and cultural institutions can preserve the appearance of an evenly balanced controversy even when one side lacks comparable documentary support.
