# 492. The War on Beards: From Peter the Great to John Lennon (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode treats facial hair as a changing social code from medieval Europe to modern Britain. It joins religious identity, masculinity, medicine, military prestige, workplace discipline, consumer technology, celebrity, and counterculture through [[FacialHairAsSocialCode]], while [[PeterTheGreat|Peter the Great]]'s beard tax shows how grooming could become [[CoerciveWesternization|coercive state reform]]. The Crimean beard revival, Gillette razor, First World War shaving, and [[TheBeatles|Beatles]]' late-1960s facial hair show that symbolic meaning also depended on photography, safety, hygiene, mass production, and generational contrast.

## Key Claims

- A beard has no fixed meaning: across the episode it can signify worldly masculinity, Protestant identity, martial endurance, patriarchal authority, eccentricity, radicalism, or countercultural distance.
- Medieval and early-modern shaving was materially risky; barber-surgeons, infection danger, improved Sheffield steel, soaps, brushes, and later disposable blades helped determine which grooming norms were practical.
- Reformation and Puritan examples show that religious meaning could reverse: beards could mark Protestant difference, while radical Protestant sobriety could later favor shaving as a rejection of vanity and bodily waste.
- Georgian clean-shavenness linked the smooth face to civility, openness, commerce, and enlightened self-command, whereas the Crimean War and soldier photography helped restore the beard as a sign of hardiness and prestige.
- [[PeterTheGreat|Peter the Great]] personally shaved boyars after his 1698 return and later taxed beards, turning a visible Orthodox practice into a test of elite obedience and Westernizing authority.
- Victorian beard claims joined virility and health to gender anxiety, but germ theory later recoded facial hair as a possible disease reservoir and supported Edwardian clean-shaven uniformity.
- The First World War, military hygiene, office culture, and Gillette's disposable blades helped normalize shaving; interwar British beards were then read as eccentric, pacifist, socialist, artistic, or socially suspect.
- [[TheBeatles|The Beatles]] helped make facial hair newly legible within late-1960s youth culture, where it could combine masculine display, Victorian irony, spiritual exploration, and generational rebellion.
