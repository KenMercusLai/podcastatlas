# Regulating the tech sector in the AI era

## Summary

This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode has [[MaxVonThun|Max von Thun]] of [[OpenMarketsInstituteEurope|Open Markets Institute Europe]] explain why products from [[Apple]], [[Meta]], [[OpenAI]], and other technology companies can reach the [[EuropeanUnion|European Union]] later than the United States. He treats delay as a mixed phenomenon: companies may need time to comply with privacy, safety, and interoperability rules, but may also withhold launches as negotiating leverage against regulation they dislike.

The episode separates that near-term consumer cost from the wider question of European competitiveness. Von Thun argues that language and national-market fragmentation and divided capital markets matter more than recent regulation alone, while defending [[PrecautionaryTechnologyRegulation|precautionary technology regulation]] where AI harms may be severe or difficult to reverse. The source supports regulation–innovation compatibility but does not independently measure compliance costs, launch decisions, public opinion, or Europe’s technology gap.

## Key Claims

- Revamped Siri, Meta's Muse, and OpenAI's Dots were described as available in the United States while remaining largely unavailable in the EU.
- [[RegulatoryProductLaunchDelay|Regulatory product-launch delay]] can reflect genuine compliance work, corporate bargaining, or both rather than one universal cause.
- Von Thun says Apple's delayed AirPods live-translation release later arrived with interoperability allowing use with non-Apple devices.
- He argues that European public support for technology rules remained strong and grew after attacks on those rules by the [[DonaldTrump|Trump]] administration.
- He identifies language fragmentation, country-specific requirements, and divided capital markets as stronger explanations for Europe's technology gap than recent regulation by itself.
- EU rules apply to European and American companies operating in the market, although equal formal rules need not create equal compliance burdens.
- AI-agent escape and hacking reports, harmful chatbot attachment, and suicide cases are used to defend testing before wide release.
- Europe and the United States are presented as facing overlapping AI risks and as potential sources of policy evidence for one another.
