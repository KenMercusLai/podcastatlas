# 534. Emperors of Rome: Sex Secrets of the Caesars (Part 1)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode introduces [[Suetonius]] and *The Lives of the Caesars* through a new translation, using [[Nero]]'s death and [[OctavianAugustus|Augustus]]'s deathbed performance to show how biography joins autocracy, succession, spectacle, sex, violence, omens, and bodily detail. Suetonius emerges as both an archival court insider and a moralizing collector whose vivid organization made the early emperors physically intimate while helping establish durable templates for dynastic drama.

The episode also frames sex as political evidence within [[AncientRomanSexualStatusHierarchy|Roman sexual status hierarchy]]. Partner gender did not map cleanly onto modern orientation categories; for elite men, domination, submission, freedom, rank, and public reputation carried greater moral weight, while imperial privacy itself could generate suspicions that remain historically useful without becoming verified conduct.

## Key Claims

- Suetonius's twelve lives trace Rome from [[JuliusCaesar|Julius Caesar]] through the Julio-Claudians, the civil war of 69, and the Flavians, making biography a history of the transition from republic to autocracy.
- His authority combined archival and library work, court office under [[Hadrian]], personal and family recollection, material objects, argument with prior claims, and proximity to imperial power.
- Habits, appearance, food, jokes, marriage, sex, exercise, bodily functions, omens, violence, and death are political material in the biographies rather than detachable gossip.
- Augustus functions as the model Caesar because he converted violence into peace, dynastic fatherhood, divine association, public spectacle, and a carefully timed sequence of names and roles.
- Roman emperors had to appear before crowds, stage games and hunts, and survive public judgment, so theatrical performance was part of imperial legitimacy rather than only entertainment.
- Roman sexual accusation focused strongly on status inversion and submission: Caesar's alleged youthful relationship with the king of Bithynia was more damaging than his adulteries because it implied a passive role.
- Gossip can reveal Roman assumptions about privacy, masculinity, shame, and power even when a sensational allegation cannot be accepted literally.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with the current wiki was adopted. The episode supplies Suetonius's career and whole-collection frame, while the later Tiberius and Caligula episodes test particular allegations more strictly.
- Augustus's peace and paternal image coexist with the violence used to avenge Caesar; the episode treats performance as political skill rather than proof of insincerity.
- Suetonius's reported dismissal, North African family origin, the Vindolanda “Tranquilus,” omens, deathbed scenes, private motives, and individual sexual allegations remain uncertain or source-scoped.
- The Roman status model clarifies why accusations carried force but does not verify them, homogenize Roman practice, or endorse a hierarchy structured by freedom, domination, age, and rank.
