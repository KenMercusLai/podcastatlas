# 711. The Terror: Killing God (Part 5)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode places [[MaximilienRobespierre|Maximilien Robespierre]]'s [[CultOfSupremeBeing|Cult of the Supreme Being]] and its Paris festival at the center of his attempt to replace indefinite coercion with internalized revolutionary virtue. Designed by [[JacquesLouisDavid|Jacques-Louis David]], the ceremony joined mass participation, allegory, music, fire, landscape, and deist belief to the claim that universal rights required a transcendent moral guarantor.

The attempted exit from terror instead deepened political danger. The suspended [[ConstitutionOf1793|Constitution of 1793]], atrocities associated with [[JeanBaptisteCarrier|Jean-Baptiste Carrier]] and [[JosephFouche|Joseph Fouché]], and the [[LawOf22Prairial|Law of 22 Prairial]] exposed unstable boundaries between regulated terror, decentralized atrocity, and moral surveillance. Broad capital offences and Robespierre's unnamed warnings then gave threatened deputies a shared reason to unite before they became defendants themselves.

## Key Claims

- Robespierre treated belief in an all-seeing Supreme Being as the moral foundation for universal rights and as a possible substitute for permanent external coercion.
- David's Festival of the Supreme Being converted that theory into a mass civic spectacle of choirs, statues, fire, procession, and an artificial mountain.
- The festival's public success did not prevent atheist and de-Christianizing deputies from reading Robespierre's central role as priestly ambition or personal supremacy.
- The ratified Constitution of 1793 remained physically and politically suspended while emergency government continued, exposing the gap between democratic promise and revolutionary dictatorship.
- Robespierre opposed compulsory atheism and some provincial atrocities, but his distinction between virtuous state terror and cruelty did not provide reliable legal protection.
- Carrier's Nantes killings and Fouché's de-Christianization and mass executions in Lyon demonstrate how deputies on mission could combine ideological zeal, local power, and spectacular violence.
- Fouché survived recall by defending his record publicly, building political support, and turning Robespierre's intended discipline into an open factional contest.
- The Law of 22 Prairial removed defence counsel and ordinary evidentiary requirements, broadened “enemy of the people” offences, and left death as the only guilty sentence.
- Vague denunciations and rapidly expanding liability aligned deputies through anticipatory fear, accelerating the coalition that would threaten Robespierre in Thermidor.

## Key Quotes

> “Internalized surveillance mechanism.” - Dan Edelstein's description of the festival's all-seeing eye, as quoted by the episode.

> “It was not enough for him to be master; he also wanted to be God.” - Jacques-Alexis Thuriot's reported attack on Robespierre, as presented by the episode.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with Parts 1–4. The episode extends the sequence from factional elimination into political religion, the Great Terror, and the coalition forming against Robespierre.
- Robespierre's opposition to forced de-Christianization and some provincial atrocities qualifies a picture of undifferentiated repression without erasing his authorship role in the Law of 22 Prairial or his support for terror.
- The Festival of the Supreme Being may have been sincerely intended as a route away from emergency coercion while simultaneously increasing fears of Robespierre's personal supremacy; these are compatible political effects rather than mutually exclusive motives.
- Carrier's and Fouché's reported atrocities, casualty totals, private conversations, crowd estimates, Robespierre's emotional state, and claims about the happiest day of his life remain source-scoped.
- The episode presents the Law of 22 Prairial as largely written by Robespierre and introduced by Couthon; precise drafting responsibility and the law's full application require broader legal evidence.
