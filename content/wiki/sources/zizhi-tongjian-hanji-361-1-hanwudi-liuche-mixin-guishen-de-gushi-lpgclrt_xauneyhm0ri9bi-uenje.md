# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》361-1｜汉武帝刘彻迷信鬼神的故事

## Summary

This short [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode follows [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] from a mountain “万岁” report into coastal sacrifice, mass search for [[Penglai|蓬莱]], and [[GongsunQingFangshi|公孙卿]]'s giant-footprint story. The emperor doubts part of the evidence yet accepts a reported dog-leading elder's disappearance as divine, showing how ambiguous testimony can still produce ships, personnel, lodging, protected sacred space, and continuing search.

The closing branch moves through [[LiangfuMountain|梁父山]] to [[Taishan|泰山]], where the court builds and seals an altar, hides an imperial text beneath it, and restricts the summit rite to the emperor and [[HuoShanSonOfHuoQubing|霍嬗 / 霍子侯]]. The episode opens with bereavement and sudden death, so its broader interpretive frame connects imperial immortality seeking to mortality anxiety while keeping the ruler's inner motive unproven.

## Key Claims

- After mountain attendants report hearing three cries of “万岁,” Han Wudi expands the shrine, protects its vegetation, and assigns three hundred households' revenue to its sacrifices.
- During the eastern coastal tour, large numbers of extraordinary petitions and maritime immortal claims lead the emperor to add ships and send thousands toward Penglai.
- Gongsun Qing reports a giant figure who disappears and leaves a large footprint; other attendants report a dog-leading elder who asks to see the emperor and then vanishes.
- The episode says Han Wudi remains doubtful about the footprint but readily identifies the vanished elder as an immortal, stays by the sea, and dispatches thousands more searchers.
- At Liangfu, the emperor sacrifices to the “帝主” and has Confucian attendants wear deer-skin caps and join a cattle-shooting rite before the Taishan ceremony.
- At Taishan, the court builds a sealed altar with a secret imperial document, after which only Han Wudi and Huo Zihou ascend, spend the night, and perform a hidden heaven sacrifice.
- The opening grief discussion interprets immortality seeking through fear of loss and death, but the supplied summary does not establish that psychology as the sole cause of imperial policy.
