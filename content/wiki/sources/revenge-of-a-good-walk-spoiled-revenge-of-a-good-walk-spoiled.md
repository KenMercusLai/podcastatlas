# Revenge of A Good Walk Spoiled

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode revisits [[MalcolmGladwell]]'s 2017 argument that private Los Angeles golf clubs consume unusually large amounts of scarce urban land while benefiting from exceptional property-tax treatment and excluding the public. It connects golf's low user density, a 1960 constitutional valuation preference, Proposition 13, and the gradual turnover of member-owners to a wider claim about publicly subsidized private privilege.

Nearly a decade later, council member [[AdrinNazarian]] uses the episode to develop a proposed parcel tax on major clubs. The follow-up turns criticism into negotiation: rather than requiring every course to disappear, it asks whether [[LosAngelesCountryClub]] and similar institutions should provide land, access, or safer adjoining public space in exchange for continued advantages.

## Key Claims

- Private golf courses use roughly 200 acres each while accommodating relatively few people at once, making their opportunity cost unusually visible in land-scarce Los Angeles.
- A 1960 California constitutional provision values golf-course land by its current recreational use rather than ordinary “highest and best use,” and Proposition 13 later compounded the advantage for long-established clubs.
- The episode uses the Ship of Theseus to challenge the conclusion that a member-owned club never changes ownership when every membership share turns over gradually.
- Gladwell estimates that six major clubs occupy land worth at least $15 billion while collectively paying less than $1 million annually in property tax, but the supplied summary does not independently verify those figures.
- [[AdrinNazarian]]'s team proposes an approximately four-dollar-per-square-foot parcel tax as leverage for a public debate about taxation, land use, and access.
- [[SubsidizedPrivateLandReciprocity]] allows compromise: continued tax privilege could be exchanged for a public park, scheduled access, or a safer path beside a course.
- [[JournalismToPolicyLatency]] captures the episode's follow-up structure: a public argument may remain dormant before reaching an official who can convert it into an agenda and negotiation.
