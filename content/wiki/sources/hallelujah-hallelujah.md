# Hallelujah

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode uses [[ElvisCostello]]'s transformation of “The Deportees Club” into “Deportee,” Cézanne's repeated portraits, and the long evolution of [[HallelujahSong|“Hallelujah”]] to distinguish rapid, plan-led creation from discovery through repeated attempts. Its central [[ConceptualAndExperimentalInnovation]] argument is that an unsuccessful version may be an unfinished search rather than proof that the underlying work has no value.

The episode also develops [[IterativeCreativeReinterpretation]]: [[LeonardCohen]]'s prolonged writing and live revision supplied material that [[JohnCale]] selected and recombined before [[JeffBuckley]] transformed Cale's interpretation. The song's later recognition depended on both craft and a fragile chain of release, encounter, adaptation, circulation, and chance.

## Key Claims

- [[DavidGalenson]]'s distinction contrasts conceptual innovators who execute a comparatively clear idea with experimental innovators who discover their destination through trial, dissatisfaction, and revision.
- The distinction is most useful as a project-level heuristic; selected artistic examples do not establish two fixed and exhaustive kinds of creator.
- Costello materially changed melody, lyrics, arrangement, title, and performance when revisiting “The Deportees Club” as “Deportee.”
- Cohen reportedly spent years generating many verses for “Hallelujah,” recorded a little-noticed version, and continued changing the song in concert.
- Cale selected and combined material Cohen supplied, while Buckley subsequently built his guitar-and-voice interpretation from Cale's version.
- Experimental work is vulnerable because an early stage can be mistaken for a final failure, and another attempt may depend on institutions, collaborators, listeners, and coincidence.
- Returning to failed work can enable discovery when revision is diagnostic and transformative, but time and repetition do not guarantee quality or recognition.

## Key Quotes

> “perfectionism in a hurry” - the episode's phrase for incompatible ambitions pursued during the rushed recording of *Goodbye Cruel World*.
