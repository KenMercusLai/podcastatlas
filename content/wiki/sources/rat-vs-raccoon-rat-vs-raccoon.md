# Rat vs. Raccoon

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode begins with [[TorontoRaccoons|Toronto raccoons]] defeating expensive compost-bin defenses, then asks why psychology built so much human-behavior theory around laboratory rats. [[MichaelPettit]] recovers [[LawrenceCole]]'s early raccoon experiments, while [[KellyLambert]] explains how cheap housing, rapid breeding, docility, inbreeding, and repeatable behavior helped rats become the default model. The history supports [[ModelOrganismSelectionBias]] and [[ExperimentalTractabilityValidityTradeoff]]: a model can dominate because it fits an institution's methods even when its representativeness remains uncertain.

[[SuzanneMacDonald]] supplies the episode's reversal. Toronto raccoons perform poorly on some standard cognition measures but succeed through impulsive, persistent “search and destroy” exploration. That result extends [[AnimalIntelligenceModes]] by separating performance on human-designed tasks from ecologically effective behavior, while the rat-raccoon comparison remains a provocative metaphor rather than evidence that either species fully models people.

## Key Claims

- Early puzzle-box work led [[LawrenceCole]] to describe raccoons as curious, capable problem-solvers and possible models for human psychology.
- Behaviorism favored animals whose behavior could be conditioned, standardized, and repeated without attributing an inner mind to them.
- Rats displaced raccoons partly because they were smaller, cheaper, more docile, faster-breeding, easier to house, and more predictable in large samples.
- Laboratory inbreeding increased similarity and control, but also produced animals less representative of wild rats, other species, and complex human behavior.
- [[KellyLambert]]'s rat-driving work shows that a familiar model can still display learned flexibility, while her reflections question its default status.
- [[SuzanneMacDonald]] describes raccoons as action-first, destructive, and persistent rather than generally reflective or exceptional across standard tests.
- No single animal supplies a complete model of human conduct; orderly incentive response and impulsive system disruption can coexist in people.
