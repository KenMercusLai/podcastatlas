# 23. The 90s

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode asks whether the 1990s are distant enough to be history and uses the decade to examine how lived memory distorts period judgment. [[TomHolland]] and [[DominicSandbrook]] contrast a British and American memory of growth, Britpop, early [[TonyBlair|Tony Blair]], and post-Cold War confidence with genocide in Rwanda, war in Yugoslavia, post-Soviet collapse, and the uneven global experience hidden by nostalgia.

The decade is treated less as a sealed golden age than as a hinge whose apparent calm contained later transformations. The internet and digital economy, [[China]]'s rise, Russia's instability, American triumphalism, culture wars, religious persistence, and Britain's European choices all connect the 1990s to the twenty-first century.

## Key Claims

- [[RecentHistoryPerspective]] is difficult because living historians and audiences can mistake generational memory, familiar cultural detail, or personal experience for a representative account.
- The Western image of the 1990s as a [[GoldenAgeNarrative|golden age]] reflects growth, the Cold War's end, and the absence of later crises, but it obscures Rwanda, Yugoslavia, Russian collapse, and unequal experience.
- The decade works as a hinge between the Cold War and 9/11: later conflict over Russia, China, Islam, Europe, American politics, and culture was already visible within it.
- [[MassInternetAdoption]] and the digital economy may be the period's deepest long-run changes, while the decline of shared charts and television events shows mass culture beginning to fragment.
- Early Blair-era optimism and improved retrospective views of [[JohnMajor|John Major]] show how political reputations and memories of whole decades can reverse.
- Western secular confidence underestimated the resilience of religion and religious identity after the Cold War.
- Britain's decision not to join the euro is presented as a condition that made later [[Brexit]] more feasible, because leaving a currency union would have imposed a much higher exit cost.
