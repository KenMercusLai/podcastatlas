## Summary
This [[MarketplaceTech]] episode discusses [[OliviaCarville]]'s [[BloombergBusinessweek]] reporting that [[TikTok]] excluded about 10% of U.S. users, roughly 15 million people, from a 2021 safety update meant to reduce harmful recommendation loops around extreme dieting, self-harm, and suicide-related content. The episode uses [[ChaseNasca]]'s account, internal TikTok document review, and the dismissal of his parents' lawsuit under [[Section230]] to show why social-media accountability is shifting toward [[SocialMediaProductLiability]] and platform-design claims.

The source's main contribution is a concrete TikTok-specific bridge between [[FilterBubble]], [[PlatformSafetyABTesting]], [[AddictiveInteractionDesign]], and [[Section230DesignWorkaround]]. It shows that internal safety safeguards, experimentation, and trust-and-safety research can become central evidence when plaintiffs argue that harmful recommendation patterns were not accidental content exposure but product architecture.

## Key Claims
- In 2021, TikTok changed its algorithm to address harmful filter bubbles involving high-risk content such as extreme dieting and self-harm.
- Internal documents obtained by Bloomberg Businessweek said about 10% of U.S. users, roughly 15 million people, were deliberately excluded from the safety update.
- [[ChaseNasca]], a 16-year-old TikTok user who was in the excluded group, later died by suicide after receiving many sad, depressive, and suicide-related videos.
- TikTok's algorithm and trust-and-safety teams reviewed 7,563 videos Chase watched in the weeks, days, and hours before his death.
- In March 2023, TikTok's CEO testified before Congress about ByteDance, national security, and child safety, and Congress asked what TikTok knew about users who died by suicide after receiving harmful videos.
- TikTok told Bloomberg it was deeply committed to safety and said it had 40,000 trust-and-safety workers globally and more than $1 billion in safety investment.
- The reported A/B experiment held back a filter-bubble safeguard from some users, creating a tension between safety validation, engagement measurement, and user risk.
- Chase Nasca's parents filed a wrongful-death lawsuit, but TikTok successfully argued that [[Section230]] protected it from liability over user-posted content.
- The episode says newer lawsuits focus less on individual user content and more on whether platforms are addictive or dangerous by design.
- Carville says an internal document stated that the filter-bubble prevention strategy did not take effect on Chase's account "by design," a phrase that matters for product-liability claims.
- The episode raises the risk that companies may avoid internal safety research if the documents later surface in court, while still treating that research as part of the public-interest trust-and-safety function.

## Key Quotes
> "by design" - Carville's description of the internal document's account of why the filter-bubble safeguard did not apply to Chase Nasca's account.

> "Section 230" - the legal shield TikTok used when the Nasca lawsuit was dismissed.
