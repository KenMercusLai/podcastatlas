# 558. The Rolling Stones: Sex, Drugs and Rock ‘n’ Roll (Part 1)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode traces [[TheRollingStones|the Rolling Stones]] from Britain's small blues scene to chart success and a durable rock archetype. It centers [[BrianJones|Brian Jones]] as founder, follows the rise of [[MickJagger]] and [[KeithRichards]], and argues that postwar teenage spending and [[AndrewLoogOldham|Andrew Loog Oldham]]'s deliberate [[ManufacturedRebellionBranding|anti-Beatles branding]] made the band both a musical force and a marketable threat to adult respectability.

## Key Claims

- Rising wages, full employment, education, record players, dance halls, cinemas, and magazines helped form a [[PostwarTeenageConsumerMarket|postwar teenage consumer market]], with girls especially important to music spending and fandom.
- [[TheBeatles|The Beatles]] made guitar groups commercially credible, while Brian Epstein's respectable packaging created room for the Stones to occupy a more unsettling position.
- [[BrianJones]] organized the band around Chicago blues, booked gigs, handled money, and supplied its initial direction before touring, health problems, and resistance to original songwriting weakened his authority.
- [[MickJagger]] and [[KeithRichards]] reconnected through shared blues records, joined Jones's group, and became its creative center after beginning to write successful original songs.
- [[AndrewLoogOldham]] did not simply discover an authentic rebellion: he changed the lineup and presentation, encouraged abrasive behavior, and promoted the Stones as the group parents should hate.
- Hair, racialized coverage of white musicians playing Black American R&B, television, press complaints, and the 1965 service-station case magnified small differences or offences into generational conflict.
- The band's movement from blues covers to original hits such as “Satisfaction” helped establish a rock-group model that was aggressive, sensual, confrontational, and commercially scalable.

## Key Quotes

> “Would you let your daughter marry a Rolling Stone?” - publicity question crystallizing the band's manufactured threat to respectability.

> “The group parents love to hate.” - the managerial position used to differentiate the Stones from the Beatles.
