# 31.冰与火之歌：虽然从不自称历史

## Summary
This [[MihuanChishu|蜜獾吃书]] episode reads [[ASongOfIceAndFire|《冰与火之歌》 / A Song of Ice and Fire]] and [[GameOfThrones|《权力的游戏》 / Game of Thrones]] as fantasy built from dense but deliberately recombined historical and mythological material. It argues that [[GeorgeRRMartin|George R. R. Martin / 乔治·R·R·马丁]] borrows from the [[WarsOfTheRoses|Wars of the Roses / 玫瑰战争]], Norman conquest, crusades, Rome, Greece, Celtic and Norse materials, medieval geography, and religious history without claiming to reproduce any one real past. The episode's strongest contribution is [[HistoricalFantasySourceLayering]]: Martin's world feels historical because it layers institutions, family politics, legends, religions, diseases, and unreliable records rather than because it is a disguised history textbook.

## Key Claims
- [[ASongOfIceAndFire|《冰与火之歌》]] is presented as historically thick but not historical: the source repeatedly treats real events and civilizations as raw material for [[HistoricalFantasySourceLayering]], not as a one-to-one code.
- The episode compares the broad map logic of Westeros and Essos with medieval European worldviews, where unknown lands could be imagined through rumor, danger, monsters, climate fantasy, and limited geographic knowledge.
- Valyria is read partly through Rome, slave economies, mining, lost craft, and post-imperial successor cities, while Valyrian steel is compared with Damascus-steel legend as a lost-technology motif.
- The Children of the Forest, old gods, weirwoods, and First Men foreground [[CelticMythology]], [[DruidicKnowledge]], and layered settlement memory; later Andal and Targaryen arrivals make conquest history cumulative rather than clean.
- The source treats Targaryen conquest as a multi-source analogy: Roman Britain, Norman conquest, northern coloring, Egyptian royal incest, dragon power, and comet omen all overlap without becoming a single model.
- [[FictionalHistoriography]] is central to the episode's Martin reading: in-world histories are partial, legendary, and sometimes wrong, as shown by Samwell Tarly's questions about Night's Watch commanders and anachronistic knight stories.
- The [[WarsOfTheRoses|Wars of the Roses]] is the strongest political analogy, especially around Lancaster/Lannister name echoes, Henry VI, Margaret of Anjou, Richard of York, Edward IV, Warwick, the [[PrincesInTheTower|Princes in the Tower]], and the Tudor settlement.
- The episode uses Tywin Lannister, Robb Stark, Robert Baratheon, Ned Stark, and other figures to show that Martin often splits traits from one historical figure across multiple fictional characters.
- Religious and mythic material is also layered: old gods, the Seven, R'hllor, the Drowned God, Others, wargs, ravens, frost giants, and white animals draw on Christian, Zoroastrian, Gnostic, Celtic, and [[NorseMythology]] resonances.
- Later sections on greyscale, leprosy, Philip IV, the Knights Templar, Qarth, Constantinople, King's Landing, Paris, and London keep the same method: the source offers historically suggestive parallels while marking several as host speculation.

## Key Quotes
> "有历史支撑，但不是历史" - the source's core guardrail.

> "历史原型并不是一一对应" - the source's warning against simple mapping.

> "很多被相信的历史都掺了水" - the in-world memory problem the episode identifies in Martin.
