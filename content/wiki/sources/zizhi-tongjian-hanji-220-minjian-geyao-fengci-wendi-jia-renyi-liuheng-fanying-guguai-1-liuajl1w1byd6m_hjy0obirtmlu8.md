# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》220｜民间歌谣讽刺文帝“假仁义”，刘恒反应古怪（1）

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode complicates [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]]'s benevolent reputation through two linked governance problems. A decree restricts powerful relatives and senior officials from privately arresting commoners or imposing levies, but the episode treats the need for the order as evidence of elite abuse and questions both enforcement and the omission of marquises themselves. After [[LiuChangHuainanKing|淮南王刘长]] dies in exile, a popular song frames the event as intolerable conflict between brothers, turning royal punishment into a public legitimacy problem.

Liu Heng then grants marquis titles to Liu Chang's four sons, including [[LiuAn|刘安]]. [[JiaYi|贾谊]] warns that this apparent repair has two costs: it can make the court look as if it admits Liu Chang was wronged, and it gives aggrieved heirs status and resources that could support revenge. The episode closes with the 专诸 assassination story to illustrate the narrower danger of personal vengeance, while its claims about Liu Heng's illness, motives, policy implementation, and later outcomes remain source-scoped.

## Key Claims

- In 173 BCE, Liu Heng reportedly forbids mothers and wives of marquises, sons of kings, and officials of at least two thousand shi from privately arresting commoners or imposing levies.
- The order suggests that elite households and senior officials could use public coercive powers for private purposes, but the source does not establish prevalence or whether the decree was enforced.
- The episode identifies a possible coverage gap because the reported wording restricts relatives and officials without expressly naming the marquises themselves.
- After Liu Chang dies following exile, a popular song about cloth, grain, and two brothers presents the death as failed royal kinship rather than impartial punishment.
- Liu Heng answers the resulting reputation pressure by granting marquis titles to Liu Chang's four sons: Liu An, Liu Bo, Liu Ci, and Liu Liang.
- Jia Yi argues that rewarding the sons can publicly validate the accusation that Liu Chang was wronged and can supply future avengers with status, wealth, and territorial resources.
- The program says Liu Heng does not accept Jia Yi's warning, but the later rebellion histories do not prove that the 172 BCE grants alone caused the sons' subsequent choices.
- The closing account of Zhuan Zhu hiding a blade in a fish and killing King Liao of Wu gives concrete form to Jia Yi's warning about close-range revenge.

## Key Quotes

> “一尺布尚可缝，一斗粟尚可舂，兄弟二人不能相容。” - the popular song as rendered by the episode to criticize Liu Chang's death.

## Connections

- [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] - ruler whose anti-abuse decree and grants to Liu Chang's sons reveal both regulatory intent and reputation-driven risk.
- [[LiuChangHuainanKing|淮南王刘长]] - dead royal brother whose punishment becomes the subject of popular criticism and later family grievance.
- [[JiaYi|贾谊]] - adviser who distinguishes emotional or reputational repair from the political-security effects of granting rank and resources.
- [[LiuAn|刘安]] and [[LiuCiHengshanKingWesternHan|衡山王刘赐]] - sons whose early marquis grants precede later, separately evidenced conflicts with the center.
- [[LawEnforcementAgainstElitePrivilege|执法碰撞权贵特权]] - extended from later enforcement retaliation to an earlier decree attempting to restrict private elite coercion.
- [[ReputationalRepairSecurityTradeoff|声誉修复与安全风险权衡]] - concept for a concession that answers public criticism while strengthening actors with a durable grievance.
- 专诸 and 吴王阖闾 - assassination precedent used to illustrate the personal-revenge branch of Jia Yi's warning; neither receives a canonical page from this brief digression alone.

## Contradictions

- The episode says Liu Chang died by refusing food after exile, while Jia Yi's reported defense says the court had commuted death and that Liu Chang died from illness of his own. The source preserves both as competing political narratives rather than resolving the medical cause.
- The program attributes Liu Heng's illness to public pressure and interprets the grants as compensation; neither motive nor causal chain is independently established by the supplied text.
- The reported omission of marquises from the anti-abuse decree is a textual and enforcement concern, not proof that marquises legally retained an unlimited power to arrest or tax.
- Liu An's and Liu Ci's later conspiracy cases make Jia Yi's warning look prescient, but they do not prove that the initial marquis grants were sufficient causes of rebellion.
- The Chu revenge example in the episode appears to compress or misidentify figures around Wu Zixu, Prince Sheng, and King Ping; it is not used here as a settled genealogy or event sequence.
