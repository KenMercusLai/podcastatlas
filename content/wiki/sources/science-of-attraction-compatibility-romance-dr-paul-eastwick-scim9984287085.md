## Summary
This [[HubermanLab]] episode has [[AndrewHuberman]] interview [[PaulEastwick]] about attraction, mate selection, dating, attachment, social support, and relationship maintenance. Eastwick argues that marketplace-style ranking describes some first-impression or app contexts, but attraction often becomes [[IdiosyncraticAttraction|idiosyncratic]] as people share time, stories, support, responsiveness, and group settings. The episode qualifies simple gender, status, age, and similarity narratives by separating stated preferences from face-to-face attraction and by treating app filters, income, age gaps, sexual satisfaction, external attraction, and social support as context-dependent rather than formulaic.

## Key Claims
- [[IdiosyncraticAttraction]] captures Eastwick's core claim that consensus about desirability weakens as people know each other and particular pairs develop uniquely positive impressions.
- [[DatingAppMarketplaceDynamics]] describes why apps and first-impression contexts can concentrate attention around a small highly desired group, while ordinary acquaintanceship can reveal different attraction signals.
- [[RepeatedContactDating]] is the practical dating environment Eastwick favors: recurring groups, friends, activities, parties, labs, hikes, clubs, churches, and classes let people observe behavior before turning dating into an interview.
- [[PerceivedSimilarityInRelationships]] matters more than measured similarity in this source because people often use motivated reasoning to define what they have in common after attraction forms.
- [[RelationshipNeedDiversification]] is reinforced by Eastwick's claim that friends, family, social groups, and non-romantic support shape relationship resilience, while many men rely heavily on romantic partners for intimacy.
- [[SharedRelationshipNarrative]] names the in-jokes, private moments, partner-specific perceptions, breakup meanings, and life-story links that help love become more than a trait checklist.
- Sexual satisfaction, attachment security, age preference, financial status, and attraction to alternatives remain important but qualified; the source treats them as relationship-process variables rather than simple demographic rules.
