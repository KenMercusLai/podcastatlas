# 12 Days: Solomon Northup and Albert Camus

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode pairs two January 4 biographies: [[SolomonNorthup]] recovering his freedom in 1853 after twelve years of kidnapping and enslavement, and [[AlbertCamus]] dying in a car crash in 1960 with the unfinished manuscript of *The First Man*. Northup's story connects free Black citizenship, interstate abduction, abolitionist assistance, New York law, and the public force of a slave narrative; Camus's story connects [[Algeria]], Resistance journalism, anti-Stalinism, criticism of revolutionary violence, literary reputation, and [[CamusianAbsurdism]]. The pairing is biographical rather than causal, using two interrupted lives to examine moral imagination, political conviction, and historical memory.

## Key Claims

- [[SolomonNorthup]] was a freeborn Black New Yorker, farmer, family man, and violinist whose free status was legally unambiguous before criminals lured him to Washington, D.C., drugged him, and sold him into slavery in 1841.
- Northup's movement from William Prince Ford to John Tibeats and Edwin Epps shows that an enslaver's personal kindness could not remove the underlying injustice of ownership, sale, violence, and coerced plantation labor.
- Canadian abolitionist Samuel Bass helped Northup contact home despite legal danger, while [[NewYorkState|New York State]] law and Governor Washington Hunt supplied the public authority through which he regained freedom on January 4, 1853.
- [[FreeCitizenKidnappingIntoSlavery]] shows how formal free status could be defeated by criminal violence, interstate sale, racial hierarchy, and the evidentiary vulnerability imposed on a captive person.
- Northup's memoir *Twelve Years a Slave* strengthened abolitionist argument because Northern readers could follow a man from recognizable free citizenship into enslavement, a mechanism captured by [[SlaveNarrativeMoralIdentification]].
- The comparison with John Howard Griffin's *Black Like Me* suggests that some audiences recognize racial injustice more readily when a narrator crosses from a socially familiar position into conditions they had previously treated as distant.
- [[AlbertCamus]] is popularly associated with [[Existentialism]], but the hosts stress that he rejected that label and is better approached through [[CamusianAbsurdism]].
- Camus's Resistance work, anti-Stalinism, and criticism of revolutionary violence gave him a morally serious but increasingly isolated position within French left-intellectual life.
- Camus's pied-noir background and concern for his mother made the Algerian War a conflict between abstract justice and personal loyalty rather than a question the episode presents him as resolving cleanly.
- Camus died while travelling with publisher Michel Gallimard; the recovered manuscript of *The First Man* preserved an unfinished autobiographical return to childhood in [[Algeria]].

## Key Quotes

> "kind, noble, candid, and Christian" - Northup's remembered assessment of William Prince Ford, paired with the judgment that Ford remained blinded to slavery's wrong.

> "justice, but I love my mother more" - the attitude the hosts use to frame Camus's conflict over Algeria.
