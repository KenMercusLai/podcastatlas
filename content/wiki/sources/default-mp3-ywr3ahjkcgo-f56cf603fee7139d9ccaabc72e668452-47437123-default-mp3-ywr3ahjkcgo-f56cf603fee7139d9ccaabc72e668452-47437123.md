## Summary
This [[HiddenBrain]] episode has psychologist [[PhilipAtibaGoff]] argue that racial disparities in policing are more tractable when reform targets observable conduct, institutional routines, and risky situations rather than requiring proof of an officer's inner prejudice. Through the [[CenterForPolicingEquity]], foot-pursuit reform in Las Vegas, low-level traffic enforcement in Berkeley, and homelessness work in Minneapolis, the episode develops [[BehaviorFocusedPoliceReform]], [[PoliceContactReduction]], and a harm-reduction approach to public safety. It also extends [[MentalHealthCrisisPoliceResponse]] by showing how unequal access to care can turn a request for help into an armed police encounter.

## Key Claims
- [[PhilipAtibaGoff]] argues that disputes over whether an individual officer is good, bad, or personally prejudiced can crowd out measurable questions about what police do and which environments predict harm.
- The episode reports that Black people experience police use of force at about four times the rate of white people and says the disparity persists after controlling for crime and poverty; the supplied summary does not provide the underlying study design.
- [[BehaviorFocusedPoliceReform]] applies performance management to desired and undesired outcomes, using data to identify high-risk practices and hold institutions accountable for changing them.
- In Las Vegas, the [[CenterForPolicingEquity]] linked foot pursuits to elevated force risk; revised training reportedly asked officers to pause and wait for support, followed by an approximately 23 percent decline in force the next year.
- [[PoliceContactReduction]] asks whether an armed officer should initiate an encounter at all. Berkeley's proposed response to low-level traffic violations was to mail citations first rather than immediately conduct a stop.
- The episode extends [[MentalHealthCrisisPoliceResponse]] by contrasting privately purchased treatment with a resource-poor family's reliance on 911, whose available responders may include armed police but not the care actually needed.
- Minneapolis homelessness work treats shelter and warm sleeping space as direct violence-prevention infrastructure because housing can prevent the indoor-displacement calls that escalate into force.
- Goff distinguishes defended positions from underlying interests and reports common ground between police and activists around removing mental health, substance use, child welfare, school, and suicide-watch duties from routine police response.
- Harm reduction is presented as cumulative institutional change: a reform need not eliminate every tragedy to be valuable if it measurably prevents some injuries or deaths.

## Key Quotes
> “one step, one step, one step, and then a fall” - the supplied summary's translation of Goff's image for gradual changes accumulating into structural transformation.

> “I don't know” - Tracy Kazee's reported answer when asked whether police training systematically endangered young Black men, reframing a defensive dispute as an empirical question.

## Contradictions
- No settled contradiction found. The episode complements [[PoliceMisconductConcentration]] by showing that targeted action against repeat-risk officers and structural redesign of high-risk situations can coexist.
- The reported Las Vegas and Minneapolis changes remain qualified: the summary does not supply comparison groups or causal estimates, and Goff explicitly says he cannot determine how much his team's work caused Minneapolis's reported force decline.
- The episode's consensus frame does not resolve disputes about police budgets, coercive authority, legal accountability, or which emergencies still require armed response.
