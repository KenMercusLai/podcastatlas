# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》279｜推恩令为什么被称为千古第一阳谋？

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode explains [[TuiEnLing|推恩令]] as an inheritance-and-enfeoffment mechanism that lets a vassal king distribute territory among sons while progressively reducing the kingdom's concentrated power. Its durable contribution is conditional rather than celebratory: the measure aligns non-heir sons' immediate interests with centralization, but it becomes enforceable only after Emperor Wen's gradual partitioning and [[HanJingdi|汉景帝]]'s victory in the Rebellion of the Seven States have already shifted coercive advantage toward [[HanWudi|汉武帝]]'s court.

The episode uses [[LiuAn|淮南王刘安]] as evidence that princely risk has not disappeared. Yet its supplied summary does not name the proposing minister, and it does not independently establish the edict's exact text, implementation across kingdoms, or long-term effects. The familiar attribution to [[ZhuFuYan|主父偃]] is supported by the adjacent Hanji 285 note rather than made explicit in this episode summary.

## Key Claims

- The episode defines an “阳谋” as an openly visible strategy whose target still finds refusal costly; it calls the 推恩令 the best-known historical example.
- Liu An's court contacts, gifts, and Chang'an network are used to show that vassal kings remain a political concern in Wudi's reign.
- The policy lets kings request titles for additional sons and divide land among them, turning a formal extension of family favor into repeated territorial fragmentation.
- The mechanism recruits non-heir sons as beneficiaries: what weakens the royal house collectively can still improve each excluded son's position.
- Open refusal is costly because the central court can frame it as resistance to imperial command, but that pressure depends on the center already having superior power.
- Emperor Wen's “众建诸侯而少其力” partitions and Emperor Jing's suppression of the Seven States are presented as necessary preconditions rather than mere background.
- After the Seven States rebellion, the court is said to remove much of the kings' appointment, taxation, and governing power, making Wudi-era fragmentation less likely to trigger successful armed resistance.
- The episode therefore treats policy design, beneficiary incentives, legitimacy language, and coercive timing as a single system.

## Key Quotes

> “众建诸侯而少其力。” - the episode's formulation of the gradual partitioning approach associated with Jia Yi and Emperor Wen.

> “推恩。” - the benevolent public language through which territorial division is presented as favor to royal sons.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with the existing same-surname kingship lifecycle is adopted. The episode fills the missing mechanism between strong early kingdoms and the politically weak royal houses described in Hanji 638.
- Attribution caution: the supplied episode summary calls the proposer only “a minister.” Zhufu Yan's authorship is retained as an adjacent-source attribution, not silently imported as wording from this source.
- Chronology caution: the episode places the edict in Yuanshuo 2 and describes a spring first-month promulgation; the date and received wording require primary-text collation.
- Scope caution: “千古第一阳谋,” universal princely welcome, and “无解” are rhetorical judgments. The source does not survey every kingdom, resistance pattern, succession dispute, or implementation outcome.
- Causal caution: the policy's incentive design does not alone explain success. The episode itself says earlier partitions, the defeat of the Seven States, loss of princely governing power, and central military superiority are necessary conditions.
- Detail caution: Liu An's gifts, court contacts, daughter's role, imperial cane signal, kingdom powers, the handling of Liang and Qi, and post-rebellion administrative changes remain episode-attributed.
