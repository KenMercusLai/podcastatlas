# 158. Killer Fashion

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode with fashion historian [[AlisonMatthewsDavid|Alison Matthews David]] uses [[FashionVictimsBook|Fashion Victims]] to connect dangerous dress to material chemistry, restrictive design, workplace exposure, class, gender, and weak regulation. Its cases—from flammable tulle and crinolines to mercury felt, arsenical green, lead cosmetics, sandblasted denim, and toxic adhesives—show that the most severe risks often fell on workers even when consumers also faced harm.

## Key Claims

- Fashion hazards are not one category: garments and cosmetics can burn, poison, restrict movement, transmit occupational exposure, or shift environmental and health costs upstream.
- Risk often persisted after it was known because beauty, status, gender expectations, industrial profit, weak rules, and workers' limited bargaining power outweighed safety.
- Flammable stage costumes and crinolines joined new materials and silhouettes to gaslight and open flame, making fashion danger an occupational and domestic fire problem rather than mere wearer carelessness.
- Hobble skirts, narrow shoes, foot binding, high heels, and corsets show that discomfort and restricted movement could carry social meaning; the episode also cautions that wearers could experience some garments as supportive, stylish, or empowering.
- Mercury hatmaking, arsenical green, and lead cosmetics exposed makers more heavily and continuously than most wearers, while bans differed across countries according to politics, organizing, and industrial priorities.
- Modern consumer comfort does not end the pattern: sandblasting, neurotoxic adhesives, globalized garment labor, and hidden contaminants can relocate danger away from affluent buyers.

## Key Quotes

> "Fashion is the sister of death." — the episode's framing of Giacomo Leopardi's dialogue between fashion and death.

> "True fashion victims" — the episode's closing description of workers who absorb fashion's hidden labor and health costs.
