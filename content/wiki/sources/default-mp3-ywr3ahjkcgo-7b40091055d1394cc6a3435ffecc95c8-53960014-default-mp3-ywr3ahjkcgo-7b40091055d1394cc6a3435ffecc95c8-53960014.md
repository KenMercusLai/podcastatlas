# Did I Really Do That?

## Summary
This [[HiddenBrain]] episode has psychologist [[SaulKassin]] explain why innocent people can confess and why confession evidence is unusually difficult for jurors and investigators to discount. [[FalseConfessionPsychology]] distinguishes voluntary, compliant, and internalized false confessions and connects them to authority, isolation, exhaustion, misinformation, threatened loss, promised relief, and distrust of memory. [[PostConfessionEvidenceContamination]] shows that a confession can also reshape witness memory, forensic interpretation, alibi evidence, and the perceived meaning of later facts.

The practical reform is a transparent, hypothesis-testing investigation: record questioning from beginning to end, use open-ended prompts, protect undisclosed crime facts, and actively test alternatives to the initial guilt theory. The episode supports these claims with historical cases, wrongful convictions, laboratory studies, and a workplace example, but the supplied document is a structured summary rather than a transcript, case file, study report, or jurisdiction-specific legal guide.

## Key Claims
- [[FalseConfessionPsychology]] separates voluntary confessions made without direct police pressure, compliant confessions knowingly made for immediate relief or expected benefit, and internalized confessions in which a person temporarily comes to doubt their own memory.
- Confident accusation, authority, isolation, prolonged questioning, exhaustion, false evidence, threats, promises, and incremental admissions can shift attention from long-term consequences toward ending the immediate ordeal.
- The Central Park case illustrates why recording only a final statement is inadequate: apparently accurate details can come from prior disclosure, scene exposure, leading questions, or other contamination rather than independent knowledge.
- [[PostConfessionEvidenceContamination]] means a confession can alter the evidence environment itself by changing witness recollection, alibi support, informant behavior, and forensic interpretation.
- Observers in one cited experiment distinguished true from false inmate confessions at about chance level; police participants were described as less accurate yet more confident than students.
- Wrongful conviction also creates public-safety risk when investigators stop testing alternative suspects and the actual perpetrator remains free.
- Full recording, open-ended interviewing, protection of nonpublic facts, and active attempts to falsify the leading theory make confession evidence more assessable.
