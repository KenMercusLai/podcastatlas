# 577. The Irish War of Independence: The Violence Begins (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows the [[IrishWarOfIndependence|Irish War of Independence]] from Sinn Fein's 1918 electoral victory through the opening violence and escalation of 1919-1920. It treats the conflict as more than an IRA shooting war: [[DailEireann|Dail Eireann]] built courts, ministries, finance, and diplomacy while boycotts, strikes, propaganda, women's networks, intelligence, and local guerrilla action withdrew cooperation from British rule.

The episode begins with the coincidence of the First Dail and the Soloheadbeg ambush on 21 January 1919, refusing to let later ballads erase the killing of two Irish Catholic policemen or local revulsion. It then traces how attacks, policing collapse, British administrative failure, Black and Tan and Auxiliary reprisals, and uneven regional mobilization produced a conflict in which coercive capacity and political legitimacy moved in opposite directions.

## Key Claims

- Twenty-seven elected Sinn Fein MPs met in Dublin on 21 January 1919, declared independence, and began constructing a rival republican state rather than taking their Westminster seats.
- The Soloheadbeg attackers sought explosives and, in Paul Rouse's account, deliberately intended to kill and begin a war; their victims were Irish Catholic RIC constables James McDonnell and Patrick O'Connell.
- Republican pressure combined raids and attacks with police boycotts, Dail courts, fundraising, propaganda, international lobbying, labour action, and women's logistics and intelligence work.
- [[MichaelCollinsIrishRevolutionary|Michael Collins]] linked finance, intelligence penetration, and targeted violence, while [[EamonDeValera|Eamon de Valera]] used his American mission to seek recognition and raise funds without winning decisive US government support.
- British recruitment of Black and Tans and Auxiliaries increased coercive capacity but also brought reprisals, indiscipline, property destruction, and reputational damage amid weak and divided administration.
- IRA flying columns and intelligence networks developed unevenly across Ireland; the war remained highly localized rather than uniformly intense.
- The 1920 Government of Ireland Act established separate six-county and twenty-six-county Home Rule structures, but the southern arrangement fell far short of republican aims and the conflict continued toward Bloody Sunday.

## Key Quotes

> "woodenly stupid" - the phrase attributed to Sir Warren Fisher's criticism of British administration at Dublin Castle.

> "hedge war" - the episode's contrast between dispersed guerrilla conflict and the fixed positions of the Easter Rising.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with the current wiki was adopted. The episode supplies the opening phase for a conflict already represented through Bloody Sunday, truce, and treaty negotiation.
- The date 21 January 1919 works as a narrative beginning, but the Dail did not order the Soloheadbeg ambush and the episode does not present a single centrally coordinated start to the war.
- The episode rejects both a wholly sectarian account of the war and the claim that sectarian motives were absent; its discussion of Peter Hart also identifies serious interview-footnoting and terminology disputes.
- Claims about attackers' intentions, force totals, social composition, school and sporting radicalization, rape, British leaders' motives, local reprisals, and the strategic effects of labour or international opinion remain source-scoped pending specialist comparison.
- The Government of Ireland Act, rather than the later Anglo-Irish Treaty, created the two Home Rule structures that established Northern Ireland's six-county framework.
