# 328: Coronations: Sex, Holy Oil and Civil War (Part 2)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode traces English coronations from Edwy and [[WilliamTheConqueror]] through [[WilliamIII]] and [[MaryII]]. It treats [[BritishCoronationRitual|coronation]] not as an unchanged inheritance but as adaptable political and religious technology: anointing, oaths, crowns, processions, sacred oil, and public acclamation are repeatedly intensified or reinterpreted when conquest, usurpation, minority, confessional change, civil war, or dynastic replacement makes authority insecure.

The strongest synthesis joins ritual adaptation to narrative interpretation. Catholic and Protestant regimes revise the service without abandoning every older form, while later writers turn disorder, laughter, costume, broken objects, and other details into [[RetrospectiveCoronationNarrative|retrospective omens]] of a reign. Even [[OliverCromwell]]'s Protectorate borrows coronation-like robes, objects, oath, and acclamation, showing how regimes that reject kingship can still need its public language of legitimacy.

## Key Claims

- Coronations change with political need: vulnerable conquerors, usurpers, child kings, contested dynasties, and restored regimes often place unusual weight on anointing, repeat ceremonies, prophecy, or public spectacle.
- [[WilliamTheConqueror]] used Anglo-Saxon coronation and anointing to strengthen a conquered title, even as mistaken acclamation triggered fire and panic outside Westminster Abbey.
- Henry III's improvised childhood crowning and later Westminster ceremony, Henry IV's prophetic holy oil, and Henry VI's English and French coronations show ritual being repeated or enlarged where succession is insecure.
- Tudor and Stuart ceremonies expose [[ConfessionalCoronationAdaptation|confessional adaptation]]: inherited anointing and oath-taking survive while Catholic and Protestant rulers change clergy, oil, language, communion, and the theological meaning of royal authority.
- Elizabeth I's procession made Bible, descent, Englishness, virtue, and Protestant truth publicly legible while the abbey service retained ambiguity about Catholic elements.
- Shakespeare's Richard II and Henry V preserve a post-Reformation tension between sacred anointing and the personal capacity of the ruler.
- Cromwell's 1657 investiture used robes, Bible, sceptre, sword, oath, heralds, and acclamation without the royal title, demonstrating institutional borrowing across a regime break.
- The joint 1689 coronation of [[WilliamIII]] and [[MaryII]] rapidly legitimated the Glorious Revolution and embedded defense of the Protestant religion established by law in an oath inherited by later monarchs.

## Key Quotes

> "sacred balm" - the Shakespearean image used to express the claim that anointing cannot simply be washed from a king.

> "second Josiah" - Cranmer's Protestant model for the young Edward VI as a reforming biblical monarch.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The source strengthens Part 3's claim that coronation is both sacred rite and adaptable political performance rather than a static survival.
- The episode itself treats stories about Edwy, Ethelred, John, Charles I, prophetic oil, and other apparent omens as biased, retrospective, legendary, or uncertain rather than neutral evidence.
- Motives, reported dialogue, crowd reactions, exact ritual details, audience interpretation, the meaning of Shakespearean passages, and the causal effect of any ceremony on political legitimacy remain source-scoped.
