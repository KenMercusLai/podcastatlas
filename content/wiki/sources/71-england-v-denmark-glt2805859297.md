# 71. England v Denmark

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode uses the Euro 2020 semi-final between the [[EnglandNationalFootballTeam|England]] and Denmark national teams as a frame for [[AngloDanishRelations|Anglo-Danish relations]]. It argues that Scandinavian attack and settlement helped produce a more unified English kingdom, while conversion, assimilation, dynastic marriage, naval war, literary exchange, diplomatic disappointment, and wartime alliance repeatedly changed rivalry into kinship and back again.

The medieval half supplies the strongest institutional synthesis. The [[GreatHeathenArmy]], [[Guthrum]], the [[DanelawSettlement|Danelaw]], [[AlfredTheGreat|Alfred]], [[Aethelflaed|Æthelflæd]], [[Athelstan]], renewed tribute, and [[CnutTheGreat|Cnut]] show Danish pressure not merely damaging England but helping concentrate West Saxon power, stimulate fortified and fiscal government, and create a political field in which conquest could become shared Christian kingship.

## Key Claims

- Early medieval labels such as Dane, Northman, Jute, Goth, and Viking were overlapping and context-dependent; the episode often uses “Dane” broadly for Scandinavian raiders and settlers in England.
- The [[GreatHeathenArmy]] destroyed or subordinated Northumbria, East Anglia, and Mercia, leaving Wessex as the base from which Alfred's dynasty built a wider kingdom.
- Alfred's recovery, Guthrum's baptism, the [[DanelawSettlement|Danelaw settlement]], fortified towns, and later campaigns by Edward and [[Aethelflaed|Æthelflæd]] converted survival and negotiated coexistence into expansion.
- [[Athelstan]]'s conquest of Northumbria and later royal control over silver and currency are presented as parts of an unusually centralized English order accelerated by Danish pressure.
- Danish settlers converted and assimilated into English Christian society while leaving durable linguistic, place-name, and regional traces; cultural proximity coexisted with remembered difference.
- Under [[AethelredTheUnready|Æthelred]], repeated [[DanegeldEscalation|Danegeld]] failed to end attack, while [[SweynForkbeard|Sweyn]] and Cnut transformed raiding pressure into conquest and North Sea monarchy.
- The Cnut-and-the-waves story is interpreted as Christian humility about the limits of kingship, not as evidence that Cnut believed he could command the sea.
- [[AnneOfDenmark|Anne of Denmark]]'s marriage to [[JamesVIAndI|James VI and I]] joined the royal houses but did not erase marital conflict, Scottish-Danish ties, or later failed attempts to secure Danish aid for [[CharlesIOfEngland|Charles I]].
- British attacks on Copenhagen in 1801 and 1807 show strategic fear turning a culturally close relationship into naval coercion; the episode marks the 1807 bombardment as a dark and underremembered episode.
- [[HansChristianAndersen|Hans Christian Andersen]]'s disastrous visit to [[CharlesDickens|Charles Dickens]] provides a comic literary counterpoint to military and dynastic history.
- Danish merchant sailors who escaped occupation in 1940 operated with the Allies from Newcastle, turning an earlier imagined Danish claim on the city into a wartime community and shared memorial.
