## Summary
This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode reconstructs the sinking of [[RMSLusitania|RMS Lusitania]] by U-20 on 7 May 1915 as a human catastrophe, a strategic episode in Germany's submarine campaign, and a major turning point in American attitudes toward the [[FirstWorldWar|First World War]]. It follows the liner from its Cunard prestige and possible wartime conversion role through German war-zone warnings, the New York departure, the torpedo strike off Ireland, lifeboat chaos, propaganda battle, and [[WoodrowWilson|Woodrow Wilson]]'s diplomatic dilemma.

The episode's strongest wiki contribution is to show how [[UnrestrictedSubmarineWarfare]] made civilian passenger travel, contraband cargo, blockade logic, and neutral rights collide. [[RMSLusitania|Lusitania]] did not bring the [[UnitedStates|United States]] into the war immediately, but the sinking made later German submarine escalation politically legible as part of a pattern rather than as an isolated maritime disaster.

## Key Claims
- [[RMSLusitania|Lusitania]] was both a civilian passenger liner and a ship with wartime relevance: it had been subsidized for possible armed-merchant-cruiser conversion and carried cargo that included ammunition, shell components, fuses, metal, and industrial material.
- Germany's February 1915 war-zone declaration reflected a strategic effort to use U-boats against British trade, but it collided with older cruiser rules that expected warning, search, and passenger evacuation before sinking merchant ships.
- The episode treats the German newspaper warning before Lusitania's departure as real but late, dramatic, and practically limited for many passengers already preparing to board.
- U-20's attack killed roughly 1,194 people, including 128 Americans and many children; the speed of the sinking and severe list made most lifeboats unusable despite theoretical capacity.
- The hosts reject conspiracy theories that secret explosives or [[WinstonChurchill|Winston Churchill]] deliberately caused the disaster, while preserving the real ambiguity created by cargo secrecy, Room 40 intelligence limits, and wartime censorship.
- The sinking became a [[PassengerLinerAtrocityPropaganda|passenger-liner atrocity propaganda]] case: Germany defended it through cargo and military-use arguments, while Britain used it to reinforce the image of German barbarism.
- Wilson's protests, William Jennings Bryan's resignation, and Germany's temporary retreat from Atlantic unrestricted submarine warfare show that the sinking created diplomatic pressure before American belligerency.
- When Germany resumed unrestricted submarine warfare in 1917, the memory of Lusitania became part of the political and recruitment case for American intervention.
