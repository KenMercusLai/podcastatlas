# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》201｜女皇吕雉，创造古代最长寿的货币

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode places [[LuZhi|吕雉]]'s Gao Hou year-six “five-part coin” reform after the failure of an earlier eight-zhu standard. It uses [[GreshamsLaw|劣币驱逐良币]] to argue that lighter pod coins remained in payment while heavier official coins were hoarded, destroyed, or recast, forcing policy back toward a lighter standard.

The episode then asks whether failure came from unwillingness to prohibit private minting or inability to enforce the prohibition. It combines transmitted references to [[LiuBiWuKing|吴王刘濞]]'s copper-and-salt base with the excavated [[ErNianLuLing|《二年律令》]] money statute to argue that severe anti-minting law probably already existed, while central enforcement remained too weak to overcome profitable circumvention and regional power. This timing and causal synthesis remains episode-attributed rather than a settled reconstruction of early Han monetary history.

## Key Claims

- In Gao Hou year five, [[ZhaoTuo|赵佗]] reportedly adopts an imperial title and attacks [[ChangshaKingdom|长沙国]], while the court introduces one-year rotation for cavalry stationed on the northern frontier.
- In Gao Hou year six, Lu Zhi removes Lü Jia's royal territory, appoints [[LuChanWesternHan|吕产]] king of Lü, grants an amnesty, and faces continuing frontier pressure.
- The episode presents the “five-part coin” as a correction after an eight-zhu issue failed to displace lighter pod coins.
- It applies Gresham's Law: users spend the lower-value money while retaining or reminting the higher-value coin, so the heavier issue leaves everyday circulation.
- The host infers that a private-minting prohibition likely accompanied the earlier eight-zhu reform rather than appearing only with the later correction.
- The described Money Statute imposes death on unauthorized minters and material or circulation helpers, liability on people who know but do not report, and a rank-or-pardon reward for capture.
- Liu Bi's copper mountain, fugitive labor, minting, and salt production are used to show why a wealthy vassal kingdom could challenge central monetary control.
- The episode's final explanation is an enforcement-capacity gap: harsh law existed, but profit, weak implementation, continued light-coin circulation, and regional resource power defeated the first reform.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The source adds an earlier anti-minting and coin-circulation stage to the later private-minting and centralized five-zhu branches already in the wiki.
- The title says Lu Zhi created antiquity's longest-lived currency, but the supplied body neither identifies that long-lived issue clearly nor demonstrates its duration; the wiki does not adopt the title claim as fact.
- The date of Lu Zhi's prohibition on private minting remains disputed within the episode. The host favors Gao Hou year two from the excavated statute, but a Gao Hou year-six interpretation is also reported.
- “Gresham's Law” is used as an explanatory analogy. The episode does not establish every legal-tender, exchange-rate, weight, metal-content, or market condition needed for a strict economic-history application.
- The five-part coin's exact weight and relation to half-liang and pod coins, the destruction-and-recasting scale, legal wording, death and reporting rules, rewards, enforcement reach, and Liu Bi's activity remain source-scoped pending direct textual and numismatic comparison.
- The brief claims about Zhao Tuo's attack, frontier rotation, royal appointments, celestial anomaly, amnesty, raids, and Zhang Ao's posthumous title are not expanded into broader causal judgments here.
