# 22. Weird Wars

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] countdown has [[TomHolland]] and [[DominicSandbrook]] compare ten wars remembered as obscure, eccentric, or unusually named, from the First Sacred War and Canudos to the Emu War and Russian Civil War. The selection uses striking hooks to make unfamiliar conflicts accessible, but repeatedly returns to the real suffering hidden by comic labels, narrative distance, and later myth.

Its strongest synthesis joins [[HistoricalEntertainment]] to [[HistoricalEntertainmentEthics]]. “Weirdness” is not an inherent quality of war: it often arises when a conflict lies outside familiar national memory, when one vivid anecdote displaces structural causes, or when later retelling turns violence into a joke, legend, or political symbol.

## Key Claims

- The First Sacred War is presented through sacred authorization, siege, water denial, and a later story of hellebore poisoning, joining religious legitimacy to collective punishment and uncertain narrative detail.
- The War of Canudos and the War of the Triple Alliance show that unfamiliarity can conceal catastrophic mass death, dispossession, racial hierarchy, state violence, and contested casualty estimates.
- Al-Kahina's afterlife shows how a defeated leader can be reused by both colonial and anti-colonial narratives, making symbolic memory politically flexible.
- The [[AbyssinianExpedition1868|Abyssinian Expedition of 1868]] is framed as an imperial campaign whose extraordinary scale followed failed correspondence, hostage-taking, and diplomatic contempt; the episode compresses a branch treated much more fully elsewhere in the wiki.
- The War of Jenkins's Ear shows how a memorable personal anecdote and comic name can overshadow trade conflict, colonial rivalry, and the conflict's absorption into a larger European war.
- The Dutch-Portuguese War is presented as a plausible early “world war” because its commercial and imperial theaters connected South America, Africa, India, and the Pacific, though the label remains interpretive.
- The Emu War supplies the only substantially comic case because no humans died; even there, the episode uses failed machine-gun deployment as a parody of colonial military confidence rather than a template for treating human wars lightly.
- The Russian Civil War illustrates a different form of strangeness: enormous geography, proliferating forces, shifting alliances, foreign intervention, rail mobility, and simplified retrospective narration can make a vast catastrophe feel like a game map.

- The episode's entertainment format creates a productive tension rather than a resolved contradiction: memorable oddity attracts attention, while the hosts repeatedly acknowledge that all listed human conflicts involved serious suffering.
