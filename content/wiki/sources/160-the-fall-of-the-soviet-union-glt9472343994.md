# 160. The Fall of the Soviet Union

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode follows the [[SovietUnion|Soviet Union]] from [[MikhailGorbachev|Mikhail Gorbachev]]'s late-1980s reforms to his resignation on December 25, 1991. [[TomHolland]] and [[DominicSandbrook]] place economic scarcity, glasnost, televised political conflict, nationalism, and the weakening Communist Party inside an increasingly personal struggle between Gorbachev and [[BorisYeltsin]].

Its central mechanism is [[DualSovereigntyStateDissolution]]: once Yeltsin used an elected Russian presidency to make Russia a competing center of authority, reform of the union became inseparable from rivalry over who governed its largest republic. The failed August 1991 coup then produced [[FailedCoupAuthorityTransfer]], discrediting hardliners while giving Yeltsin the public and institutional leverage to ban the Russian Communist Party, eclipse Gorbachev, and join [[LeonidKravchuk]] and Belarusian leaders in ending the union.

## Key Claims

- Gorbachev's partial political and economic reforms created a dangerous intermediate condition: public criticism and electoral competition expanded while shortages, rationing, and institutional coordination worsened.
- The Congress of People's Deputies opened real political space and televised disagreement, but exposure of policy failure and suppressed history also weakened the authority of the existing system.
- Yeltsin converted personal rivalry with Gorbachev into an institutional challenge by making the Russian presidency and Russian sovereignty alternatives to union-level authority.
- Republican nationalism was not only popular identity politics; the episode argues that party and state-enterprise elites could also use it to preserve their own authority as Moscow weakened.
- Gorbachev's proposed Union Treaty attempted to preserve a decentralized federation, but hardliners' August coup destroyed the political position it was meant to stabilize.
- The coup failed because its leaders neither secured Gorbachev's resignation nor arrested Yeltsin or controlled communications and the streets; Yeltsin's resistance then shifted practical authority toward Russia.
- The Belavezha agreement is presented as an elite-led dissolution in which Yeltsin's determination to defeat Gorbachev and republican leaders' interests outweighed systematic planning for borders, minorities, and the post-Soviet order.
- Crimea, Donbas, Russian speakers outside Russia, public preferences, Western bailout proposals, economic statistics, and leader motives are raised as consequential but remain source-scoped in this account.

## Key Quotes

> "greatest geopolitical catastrophe of the century" - the episode's account of [[VladimirPutin]]'s later description of the Soviet collapse.

> "halfway house" - the hosts' description of reforms that had weakened the old system without establishing a workable replacement.

## Connections

- [[MikhailGorbachev]], [[BorisYeltsin]], and [[LeonidKravchuk]] - leaders whose competing authority and republican choices shaped the endgame.
- [[SovietUnion]] and [[Russia]] - union state and largest republic after Russia became a rival sovereign center.
- [[DualSovereigntyStateDissolution]] - mechanism linking parallel legitimacy, competing governments, and institutional breakup.
- [[FailedCoupAuthorityTransfer]] - mechanism by which an unsuccessful seizure of power accelerated the authority transfer its authors hoped to prevent.
- [[PostColdWarOrderRupture]] - longer afterlife of the unresolved borders, security expectations, minorities, and status loss discussed here.
- [[161-yeltsin-economic-chaos-and-president-putin-glt3048619920]] - continuation into post-Soviet economic transition, executive concentration, and Putin's rise.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode's elite-rivalry interpretation qualifies accounts of purely popular national liberation without showing that public nationalism or republican referendums were unimportant.
- The claim that only about 20% of Soviet citizens wanted dissolution is not treated as a direct measure of preferences after the August coup or of distinct republican electorates; referendum wording, timing, participation, and later political change require separate analysis.
- Yeltsin's revenge motive, Kravchuk's self-preservation, Gorbachev's private reasoning, the proposed Western rescue plan, casualty and output figures, and the counterfactual effect of different economic aid remain episode-attributed or source-scoped.
