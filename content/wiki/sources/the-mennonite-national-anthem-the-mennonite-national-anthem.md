# The Mennonite National Anthem

## Summary

[[RevisionistHistory]] closes its series on the [[MinnesotaStarvationExperiment]] through volunteer [[LesterGlick]], whose diaries and family testimony connect severe restriction to tuberculosis, social withdrawal, persistent hunger, weight cycling, and a changed relationship with food. Physiologist [[AbdulDulloo]] uses the study to explain [[PostStarvationBodyCompositionRecovery]]: fat can return faster than lean tissue during recovery, while the experiment remains unusually valuable because comparable controlled deprivation would be ethically and practically difficult.

Glick's Mennonite reading of Matthew 25 made feeding the hungry a religious duty and helped motivate his participation. The experiment ended his medical-school plan but informed a career in social-work education and practical generosity, illustrating [[HungerAsTotalDeprivation]] and the possibility of turning lasting injury into service without erasing the injury.

## Key Claims

- Glick experienced extreme weakness, hair loss, social withdrawal, food preoccupation, tuberculosis, and health and eating effects that his family regarded as lifelong.
- During recovery from severe restriction, fat may be restored faster than muscle, sometimes producing excess abdominal fat before lean tissue and function recover.
- The Minnesota experiment remains scientifically important because it combined controlled intake, detailed records, a recovery phase, and unusually disciplined voluntary participation.
- Modern starvation research faces both ethical limits on deliberately imposing harm and practical difficulty sustaining large calorie reductions over long periods.
- Glick's Mennonite faith framed participation as service to hungry strangers and made hunger a physical, emotional, social, and spiritual problem rather than a calorie count alone.
- Tuberculosis redirected Glick from medicine to social-work education, where he emphasized loving kindness, empathy, and material care.
- Glick's later food rituals, gardening, and cinnamon-roll sharing joined trauma residue to a continuing effort to give others sustenance.
