# Eating for Better Sleep & Foods that Improve Metabolic Health | Dr. Marie-Pierre St-Onge

## Summary
This [[HubermanLab]] episode has [[AndrewHuberman]] interview [[MariePierreStOnge]] about the reciprocal relationship between sleep, appetite, diet quality, meal timing, and cardiometabolic health. It develops [[SleepNutritionBidirectionality]] by joining controlled sleep-restriction findings on food reward and intake with diet-pattern, sleep-architecture, and earlier-versus-later eating evidence. The episode also extends [[AppetiteHormoneRegulation]] and [[CircadianEatingWindowAlignment]] while keeping sex-specific hormone findings, study effect sizes, functional-food results, and practical timing advice source-scoped.

## Key Claims
- [[SleepNutritionBidirectionality]] frames sleep and eating as a feedback system: short sleep can increase food reward and intake, while food quality and timing can alter later sleep and metabolic processing.
- [[AppetiteHormoneRegulation]] gains a sleep-state input: in one crossover sleep-restriction study, ghrelin increased in men, GLP-1 decreased in women, food-reward responses increased, and self-selected intake rose by about 300 calories.
- Severe restriction under tightly controlled feeding did not change every measured metabolic marker, whereas a six-week free-living reduction of about ninety minutes per night was associated with worse insulin sensitivity and higher blood pressure.
- Mediterranean- and DASH-style dietary alignment was associated with adequate sleep or fewer insomnia symptoms in cohort studies, but those associations do not by themselves establish that the diet caused better sleep.
- In an inpatient comparison, self-selected higher-calorie, higher-saturated-fat eating was followed by longer sleep onset and less slow-wave sleep; fiber, saturated fat, and refined carbohydrate intake tracked different sleep-architecture outcomes.
- [[CircadianEatingWindowAlignment]] is strengthened by a matched-food metabolic-chamber comparison in which later eating reduced fat oxidation and by weight-loss research associating earlier large lunches with better outcomes.
- Naps, sleep-apnea testing, fermented foods, MCT oil, ginger, snack oils, supplements, and industry-funded studies are discussed with outcome-specific limits rather than as universal protocols.
