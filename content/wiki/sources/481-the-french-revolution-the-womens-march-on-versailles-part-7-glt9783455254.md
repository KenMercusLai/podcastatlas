# 481. The French Revolution: The Women's March on Versailles (Part 7)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode presents the [[OctoberDays1789|October Days of 1789]] as the moment when unresolved constitutional argument, bread scarcity, rumor, radical journalism, and armed popular pressure made the Bourbon monarchy captive in Paris. Market women initiated the march to Versailles, the National Guard followed, palace violence nearly reached [[MarieAntoinette|Marie Antoinette]], and [[MarquisDeLafayette|Lafayette]] used balcony theatre to prevent an immediate massacre before the royal family and National Assembly moved to Paris.

Its central synthesis is that popular sovereignty had no agreed institutional form. The Assembly claimed to represent the nation, the king retained a suspensive veto, and Parisian crowds claimed the right to enforce revolutionary survival directly; the resulting [[CrowdSovereigntyRepresentationConflict|street-versus-representation conflict]] made both constitutional settlement and public order unstable.

## Key Claims

- The Declaration of the Rights of Man and the Citizen left unresolved the status of monarchy, women, enslaved people, property, and the institutional bearer of national sovereignty.
- Monarchiens influenced by Montesquieu and Britain wanted a second chamber and meaningful royal veto, while radicals treated those restraints as obstruction of the national will; the Assembly rejected an upper house and granted only a suspensive veto.
- [[LouisXVI|Louis XVI]]'s qualified response to the August decrees and Declaration made constitutional delay look like hostility, while rumors that the Assembly might be moved to Tours deepened fear of counterrevolution.
- Rising bread prices, milling disruption, and hunger converted constitutional uncertainty into [[RevolutionaryHungerStreetMobilization|street mobilization]], even though contemporaries often explained scarcity through unsupported aristocratic starvation plots.
- Press freedom enabled [[JeanPaulMarat|Jean-Paul Marat]] and other radical writers to present public life through hidden enemies, false patriots, and imminent betrayal.
- The Flanders Regiment banquet and reports that the tricolor had been insulted made royal military preparation and hostility to the Revolution seem immediate.
- On 5 October market women seized weapons and cannon, marched through rain to Versailles, entered the Assembly, and obtained promises of grain and the king's unqualified acceptance of the August decrees and Declaration.
- Lafayette followed with roughly 15,000 National Guardsmen because his troops insisted on marching, making him both commander and prisoner of the force he nominally led.
- The dawn palace invasion killed two bodyguards and nearly reached Marie Antoinette before Lafayette's balcony gestures secured temporary acclamation for the king, queen, and royal guards.
- The forced move to the Tuileries made the monarchy and Assembly physically dependent on Parisian politics and helped create the setting in which the Breton deputies' club became known as the [[JacobinClub|Jacobins]].

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with [[482-the-french-revolution-the-royal-family-escapes-part-8-glt3336151355]]. This episode supplies the coercive move to Paris, political distrust, and constitutional instability that precede the royal family's failed escape.
- The source acknowledges an evidentiary imbalance: elite and royal experiences are richly documented, while the voices and suffering of hungry Parisian women are less directly preserved.
- Bread scarcity was real, but claims that the king and aristocracy deliberately engineered starvation remain rumor rather than established coordination.
- The Flanders banquet reports, troop and crowd totals, exact responsibility for the dawn breach, private motives, dialogue, and eyewitness scenes remain source-scoped reconstructions.
- Lafayette's intervention likely reduced immediate violence, but that does not erase his inability to control the march or the coercion under which the royal family moved.
