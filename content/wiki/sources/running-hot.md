# Running Hot

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode follows producer [[BenNaddaffHafrey]] from neighborhood siren noise into a wider investigation of ambulance driving. Evidence presented by [[JonathanBayer]] and [[JeffJarvisEMS|Jeff Jarvis]] supports [[LightsAndSirensRiskCalibration]]: warning devices can matter in a narrow set of time-critical emergencies, but routine use adds crash, noise, hearing, and burnout costs while usually saving seconds or a few minutes.

The operational alternative is [[EmergencyDispatchTriage]]. Berrien County reduced lights-and-sirens responses by about half without increasing the reported rate at which lower-priority patients were later classified as priority one, while Jarvis reports a one-third reduction associated with a median response-time increase of six seconds. The episode links persistent overuse to firefighting inheritance, television's universal-urgency story, public expectations, and [[AlarmFatigue]].

## Key Claims

- Lights-and-sirens driving increases collision risk while usually producing small time savings; its benefit is concentrated in conditions such as cardiac arrest, choking, or respiratory arrest.
- Berrien County's former screening rules classified about half of calls as priority one, including complaints whose wording triggered overly broad urgency criteria.
- Bayer's reform treated lights and sirens as a high-risk therapy, retained them for genuinely time-sensitive cases, and removed about half of their previous use without a reported rise in lower-to-higher priority reclassification.
- Jarvis's analysis of 7.5 million records found lights and sirens used en route for about 86 percent of calls although only 6.9 percent resulted in a generously defined potentially lifesaving intervention.
- The 1970s television series [[EmergencyTVSeries|Emergency!]] helped legitimate paramedicine while repeatedly presenting emergency work as a race in which every response required lights and sirens.
- Fire-service routines persisted even as fewer than 4 percent of firefighters' 2023 calls were fires and most involved medical or rescue work.
- Reserving sirens for genuine urgency may reduce crashes, community noise, hearing damage, and desensitization, but the claim that selective use would make the public react more strongly remains untested.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. The episode argues against routine use, not against lights and sirens in cardiac arrest and other genuinely time-critical emergencies.
- Crash-risk, time-saving, intervention, decibel, call-share, and triage figures remain episode-attributed because the supplied document is a structured summary rather than the cited studies, ESO dataset, protocols, or hospital records.
- Berrien County's reclassification comparison is reassuring but does not establish that every dispatch system, geography, or patient population can adopt the same thresholds without harm.
- The causal influence attributed to *Emergency!* is plausible but not isolated from firefighting history, law, training, public demand, liability concerns, and other institutional incentives.
- The claim that rarer sirens would restore public responsiveness is explicitly a hypothesis rather than a demonstrated outcome.
