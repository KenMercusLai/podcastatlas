# 116. Alexander the Great Part 1

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode follows [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]] from his Macedonian formation under [[PhilipIIMacedon|Philip II of Macedon]] through accession, the destruction of Thebes, the Hellespont crossing, Granicus, Issus, Tyre, Egypt, the founding of [[Alexandria]], and the oracle at Siwa. It presents early success as cumulative: Philip supplied the army and political platform, while repeated victories widened Alexander's aims from a Greek-led punitive expedition into a claim on the whole [[AchaemenidEmpire]].

The episode also establishes the source problem developed in Part 2. Surviving narratives are late, Persian narrative testimony is largely absent from the discussion, and heroic scenes around [[Bucephalus]], the Gordian knot, Darius's family, and divine ancestry sit between recoverable history and [[AlexanderLegendFormation]]. Its strongest synthesis is that military adaptation, political theater, exemplary violence, and self-mythologizing advanced together rather than as separable parts of Alexander's rise.

## Key Claims

- Philip II united Macedon, funded and reorganized its army, subdued the principal Greek opposition at Chaeronea, and prepared the Persian invasion that Alexander inherited.
- Alexander's accession required elite support and renewed coercion; the destruction of Thebes made resistance visibly costly before the Asian campaign began.
- The Hellespont rituals, visit to Achilles's tomb, Gordian-knot story, and forced Delphic oracle present symbolic performance as part of command and legitimacy.
- Granicus and Issus converted a risky invasion into an expanding campaign, while Darius III's flight and abandoned family damaged royal prestige in the conqueror-side narrative.
- The siege and punishment of Tyre combined strategic removal of Persian naval bases with exemplary terror against resistance.
- Darius's settlement offers and Alexander's refusal mark the move from limited gains toward possession of the whole Persian imperial order.
- Egyptian coronation, the founding of Alexandria, and the private consultation at Siwa widened Alexander's claims through local kingship and divine association.
- Late sources, missing Persian perspectives, literary patterning, and disputed anecdotes require claim-level caution rather than either literal acceptance or wholesale dismissal.

## Key Quotes

> "the last of the Achaemenids" - Pierre Briant's formulation, repeated by the hosts to frame Alexander as a Persian imperial successor as well as a Macedonian conqueror.

> "pothos" - the episode's term for Alexander's yearning to move beyond an achieved limit.

## Connections

- [[AlexanderTheGreat]], [[PhilipIIMacedon]], and [[Olympias]] - conqueror, father and institutional predecessor, and mother within Macedonian dynastic politics.
- [[DariusIII]] and [[AchaemenidEmpire]] - defeated rival and imperial order Alexander increasingly sought to possess.
- [[AlexanderImperialSuccessionStrategy]] - transition from punitive invasion and territorial bargaining to whole-empire succession.
- [[AlexanderLegendFormation]] and [[HistoricalInquirySourceCriticism]] - framework and method for handling late, one-sided, and literary evidence.
- [[Bucephalus]], [[Aristotle]], and [[Alexandria]] - heroic mount, tutor tradition, and strategically placed Egyptian foundation.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. Part 1 complements Part 2 by showing that Achaemenid succession developed from an initially bounded invasion whose aims expanded through victory.
- Responsibility for Philip's assassination, the exact Granicus and Issus sequences, Darius's conduct, Alexander's treatment of the royal family, speeches, oracles, dreams, divine belief, and the Gordian-knot and Bucephalus stories remain disputed, literary, or source-scoped.
- Calling Alexander an Achaemenid successor does not erase Macedonian coercion, the destruction of Thebes, or the mass violence at Tyre.
