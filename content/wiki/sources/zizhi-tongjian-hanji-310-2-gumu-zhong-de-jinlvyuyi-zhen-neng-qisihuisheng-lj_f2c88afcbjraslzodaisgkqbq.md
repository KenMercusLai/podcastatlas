# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》310-2｜古墓中的金缕玉衣，真能起死回生

## Summary

This short [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode continues the Kang-tomb story by placing the reported wealth of [[LiuJiJiaodongKingWesternHan|胶东康王刘寄]] inside the wider economy of Western Han royal burial. It uses the excavated tombs of [[LiuShengZhongshanKingWesternHan|中山王刘胜]], the Xuzhou Shizishan Chu king, and [[LiuHeHaihunHou|刘贺 / 海昏侯]] as analogies showing that vassal rulers could receive extremely rich grave goods, while stopping short of archaeological proof about Liu Ji's own tomb.

The central object is the gold-thread jade suit: jade plaques joined with gold wire and presented as a high-status burial garment. The episode connects its material expense and rank differentiation to a belief that jade could keep essence from dispersing, preserve the body, and permit future return, making [[HanRoyalThickBurialAndJadeSuitBelief|汉代王侯厚葬与玉衣不朽信仰]] a junction of wealth, hierarchy, and imagined posthumous continuity rather than evidence that a body could literally return to life.

## Key Claims

- Western Han same-surname kings controlled territory and resources that could support large tomb projects and rich grave assemblages.
- The episode says many vassal kings selected and began constructing tombs early, then placed collected valuables in them at death.
- The reported robbery interrogation turns to whether the Kang tomb could plausibly contain exceptional wealth; experts in the episode answer by analogy with excavated royal tombs.
- The 1968 excavation associated with Liu Sheng and his wife is said to have yielded more than ten thousand objects, including gold-thread jade suits, the Changxin Palace Lamp, and a coiled-dragon bronze vessel.
- A gold-thread jade suit is described as jade plaques joined by gold wire and as a top-rank Han burial garment, with silver- and copper-thread variants assigned to lower noble ranks.
- The episode attributes to ancient belief the ideas that jade could prevent essence from dispersing, keep bones or the corpse intact, and support a future return to life.
- Shizishan and Haihun are used as additional analogies for royal burial wealth, but none of these excavations establishes the location, ownership, or contents of Liu Ji's tomb.

## Key Quotes

> “穿上玉做的衣服，可以使死者精气不散、尸骨不朽” - the episode's explanation of the belief attached to jade burial clothing.

> “金缕玉衣是汉代规格最高的一种丧葬殓服” - the status claim that organizes the object's rank symbolism.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with the canonical wiki was found. The source strengthens the general plausibility of wealthy Western Han royal burials but still provides no excavation evidence for Liu Ji's tomb.
- Identity normalization: the supplied summary's 刘继 is normalized to 刘寄 because the Jiaodong kingship, Kang title, relationship to Han Jingdi and Han Wudi, and direct continuation from Hanji 310-1 identify the established figure.
- The source is a compact popular-history summary rather than an excavation report. Object totals, “national treasure” counts, tomb and spouse terminology, exact jade-suit hierarchy, Shizishan terracotta and coin totals, Haihun gold wording, expert dialogue, and the prevalence or uniformity of the stated beliefs remain episode-attributed.
- Archaeological survival of rich goods supports the existence of elite burial wealth; it does not prove that jade prevented decay, enabled resurrection, or that the Kang-tomb proverb accurately describes one specific grave.
- The episode previews Guo Moruo and the Kang tomb but does not supply the promised excavation narrative; no claim about his role is adopted from the teaser.
