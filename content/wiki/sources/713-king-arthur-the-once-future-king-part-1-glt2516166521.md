# 713. King Arthur: The Once & Future King (Part 1)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode uses [[ThomasMalory|Thomas Malory's]] [[LeMorteDarthur|Le Morte Darthur]] to tell the end of [[KingArthur|King Arthur]]: Mordred's usurpation, Camlann, the return of Excalibur, the voyage to Avalon, and the unresolved promise that Arthur may return. It then separates that literary ending from the sparse Welsh notice of Arthur and Medraut at Camlann and from [[GeoffreyOfMonmouth|Geoffrey of Monmouth's]] influential elaboration of betrayal, battle, and Avalon.

The episode also extends [[ArthurianPoliticalLegitimation]] through Glastonbury Abbey's claimed discovery of Arthur and Guinevere and [[EdwardIOfEngland|Edward I's]] 1278 reburial of the supposed remains during his conquest of Wales. Malory's ruined Round Table is finally read against the [[WarsOfTheRoses|Wars of the Roses]], where betrayal, shifting allegiance, parliamentary action, and violence against survivors made Arthur's collapse legible as a fifteenth-century political tragedy.

## Key Claims

- Malory preserves competing endings: Arthur may have died, may have been taken to Avalon for healing, or may remain the king who will return in a future crisis.
- Excalibur's return to the lake completes a longer mortality arc in which Morgan le Fay had already removed the protective scabbard that prevented blood loss.
- The *Annales Cambriae* notice for 537 says that Arthur and Medraut fell at Camlann but does not identify their relationship, the victor, the battlefield, or the event's historical status.
- Geoffrey of Monmouth supplies the first extended account of Mordred's regency and seizure of power, locates the final battle by the River Camel, and sends the wounded Arthur to Avalon without resolving whether he recovers.
- Glastonbury Abbey's 1191 grave claim joined institutional finance and pilgrimage to an assertion that Glastonbury was Avalon, while the timing after the 1184 fire makes interested invention a serious possibility.
- Edward I's 1278 reburial appropriated Arthur as an English precedent for rule over a united Britain and challenged Welsh, Cornish, and Breton return traditions by displaying a body said to prove the king was dead.
- Malory's late-fifteenth-century institutions, weapons, betrayals, and condemnation of fickle English loyalties make Camelot's destruction a reflection on the Wars of the Roses as well as an inherited legend.
