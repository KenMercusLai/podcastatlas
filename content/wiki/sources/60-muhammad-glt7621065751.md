# 60. Muhammad

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode separates [[Muhammad]]'s highly probable existence from the much harder task of recovering a detailed life from the [[Quran|Qur'an]], later Muslim biographies, near-contemporary non-Muslim fragments, inscriptions, coins, and late-antique context. [[TomHolland]] acknowledges that secular non-belief is itself a standpoint, then argues for graduated confidence rather than either complete acceptance of later tradition or denial that Muhammad existed.

The episode's provisional reconstruction places Muhammad within a Jewish, Christian, and post-biblical late-antique world shaped by plague, Roman-Persian war, Arab frontier politics, apocalyptic expectation, and imperial collapse. Its strongest revisionist claims—that Muhammad may have led an invasion of Palestine, that some Qur'anic geography may lie north of Mecca, and that [[AbdAlMalik]] helped crystallize recognisable Islam—remain explicitly contested models rather than settled conclusions.

## Key Claims

- Early Syriac, Armenian, and other non-Muslim references make Muhammad's existence much harder to doubt than the details of the later traditional biography.
- The surviving narrative biographies are substantially later than Muhammad, so historians must compare them with the Qur'an, external fragments, material evidence, and late-antique context.
- The Qur'an is early, associated with someone called Muhammad, and historically indispensable, even though its origin, language, audience, transmission, and codification remain debated.
- Muhammad's movement is best contextualized within late-antique Jewish, Christian, Zoroastrian, Arab, Roman, and Persian worlds rather than as an eruption from an isolated desert.
- Holland provisionally retains Muhammad as merchant, prophet, community founder, and expansionary leader while disputing parts of the standard chronology and geography.
- The Roman-Persian war, plague, frontier integration, and imperial exhaustion created conditions in which Arab religious and military power could expand.
- Public inscriptions and coinage under Abd al-Malik show a recognisable Islamic imperial identity becoming clearer in the late seventh century, but do not prove that he invented Islam.

## Key Quotes

> “very hard to argue that Muhammad did not exist” — Holland's boundary between reasonable historical doubt and denial of Muhammad's existence.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The episode challenges parts of the traditional chronology, Meccan setting, conquest narrative, and account of Islam's immediately complete institutional form, but presents alternatives as contested reconstructions rather than established replacements.
- The Doctrina Jacobi's apparent chronology, Muhammad's possible leadership of an invasion of Palestine, the northern-geography hypothesis, the identity of the Qur'anic opponents, and the location of Ibn al-Zubayr's sanctuary remain source-scoped.
- Carbon dating constrains parchment age rather than the date of writing, and early fragments do not by themselves settle the Qur'an's complete transmission or codification history.
- Abd al-Malik's public religious program shows late-seventh-century consolidation but does not prove that Muhammad, Mecca, or the Qur'an were late inventions.
