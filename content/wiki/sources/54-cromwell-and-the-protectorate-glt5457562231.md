# 54. Cromwell and the Protectorate

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode with [[PaulLay|Paul Lay]] examines [[OliverCromwell|Oliver Cromwell]] as soldier, providential politician, Lord Protector, conqueror of [[Ireland]], and unusually reusable figure in later political memory. It follows the [[EnglishCommonwealth|English Commonwealth]] from the execution of [[CharlesIOfEngland|Charles I]] through Barebone's Parliament and the [[InstrumentOfGovernment1653|Instrument of Government]], then asks why a regime seeking “healing and settling” remained dependent on Cromwell and the army.

Its central synthesis resists both heroic and wholly exceptional-villain accounts. Cromwell helped make the prewar monarchy impossible to restore unchanged, but did not solve succession or constitutional legitimacy; documented atrocities at Drogheda and Wexford must be distinguished from later mythology without being excused; and Victorian liberal, Irish, republican, nationalist, and Brexit-era appropriations reveal how gaps in the record made his afterlife unusually contested.

## Key Claims

- Regicide was carried out by a relatively small coalition before its members had agreed what constitutional order should replace monarchy and the House of Lords.
- Cromwell's religion centered on discerning providence, but his habits and personality do not fit a simple joyless-Puritan stereotype; sparse early evidence also makes many biographical anecdotes insecure.
- Barebone's Parliament exposed the distance between millenarian reform, legal experimentation, army finance, and a workable political settlement.
- [[JohnLambert|John Lambert]] helped frame the 1653 [[InstrumentOfGovernment1653|Instrument of Government]], replacing king, Lords, and Commons with Lord Protector, Council of State, and Parliament.
- The Protectorate was authoritarian and army-backed but lacked the machinery of a modern totalitarian dictatorship; Cromwell repeatedly sought settlement while remaining indecisive about its final constitutional and succession form.
- [[RichardCromwell|Richard Cromwell]] inherited the protectorship without army support, exposing how little of his father's authority had been transferred from person to office.
- The [[Levellers]] offered a more democratic republican possibility, but Cromwell suppressed their Burford mutiny to preserve army discipline and state authority.
- In Ireland, documented killing at Drogheda and Wexford, especially of Catholic clergy, remains Cromwell's responsibility even when atrocity stories are corrected and his campaign is placed within a longer continuum of English violence.
- Cromwell's Westminster statue and later use by liberals, republicans, nationalists, and Brexiteers show historical memory repeatedly remaking him for new constitutional arguments.

## Key Quotes

> “healing and settling” - the governing objective Paul Lay uses to qualify a simple modern-dictator analogy.

> “man of blood” - the biblical judgment through which Charles I's opponents interpreted renewed civil war and royal duplicity.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction with existing wiki content was adopted. The episode reinforces constitutional instability and real Cromwellian violence while more strongly separating documented acts from mythologized atrocity stories.
- Calling the Instrument of Government the world's first written constitution, describing the Protectorate as not a dictatorship, and comparing the Irish conquest with the Norman Conquest are episode-attributed interpretations whose definitions and comparative scope remain contestable.
- Cromwell's motives, the degree of his control at Wexford, exact casualty figures, the representativeness of the Levellers, popular accommodation with the regime, and the causes of later constitutional change remain source-scoped.
- Cromwell's role in Jewish readmission is qualified by the episode's claim that conversion expectations, rather than modern humanitarianism, shaped his support.
