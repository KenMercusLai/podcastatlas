# 25. Empires

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode asks what empire is, why it has been such a persistent political form, and whether its coercion can be judged alongside the order, infrastructure, mobility, and identities it creates. [[TomHolland]] and [[DominicSandbrook]] move from Assyria, Persia, Greece, and the [[RomanEmpire]] through Arab, Ottoman, Chinese, Russian, British, and American cases to develop [[EmpireAsPoliticalOrder]] rather than a single civilizational model.

Its strongest contribution is a set of paradoxes. Empires can legitimize domination as moral order; conquered populations can appropriate imperial identities; biblical and classical traditions can generate [[AntiImperialTraditionsWithinEmpire|anti-imperial criticism]] from inside imperial worlds; and the modern nation-state system can itself spread through colonial power. The episode also extends [[AmericanImperialSelfDenial]] and rejects a fixed timetable of imperial decline, while its broad comparisons remain suggestive rather than comprehensive case studies.

## Key Claims

- Empire is defined through a dominant center or metropole exercising command over differentiated peripheral peoples and territories.
- Durable empire depends on more than violence: administration, roads, money, law, prestige, religious or moral purpose, and opportunities for identification can make rule appear preferable to disorder.
- The [[AchaemenidEmpire]] supplies an early example of conquest legitimated through truth, light, order, infrastructure, and remembered accommodation under [[CyrusTheGreat|Cyrus the Great]].
- [[RomanEmpire|Roman]] conquest was exceptionally violent, yet citizenship and Romanitas enabled some conquered peoples to appropriate an imperial identity and later defend its continuity.
- Nation-states are not treated as humanity's timeless default; European colonialism helped globalize the state form later used to dismantle empires.
- Western anti-imperial criticism draws partly on biblical memories of oppression and Greek and Roman criticism, so imperial traditions can help produce the standards later used against empire.
- China, Russia, the United States, and commercial corporations complicate claims that the modern world is post-imperial, but analogy does not make every large state or company institutionally equivalent.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction was adopted. The claim that empire was often a historical norm complements existing case studies but remains a comparative argument, not a universal law.
- The episode rejects a fixed two-century imperial lifespan, qualifying deterministic versions of [[ImperialRiseDeclineCycle]] while preserving the older moral trope that wealth and security can produce softness or overreach.
- The hosts' order-versus-coercion framing does not establish that subjects generally preferred empire, that benefits were evenly distributed, or that infrastructure morally offsets conquest, deportation, slavery, taxation, or cultural destruction.
- Claims about China, Russia, the United States, the European Union, and technology companies as empires depend on definition and should not collapse territorial rule, federal statehood, informal influence, and corporate market power into one category.
