# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》194｜史上第一起人彘之刑，虐杀先帝宠妃（1）

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode narrates the immediate aftermath of [[LiuBang|刘邦]]'s death: [[LiuYing|刘盈]] succeeds him, [[LuZhi|吕雉]] uses a brief separation between Liu Ying and [[LiuRuyi|刘如意]] to have the young Zhao king poisoned, and then has [[QiJi|戚姬 / 戚夫人]] mutilated and displayed as a “human swine.” Liu Ying's reported protest and decline make the atrocity both an act of palace revenge and a shock transmitted into the reigning emperor's capacity to govern.

The episode also qualifies its own total-withdrawal story. After saying Liu Ying stopped attending government and drank constantly, it later says he continued a measured seven-year reign with [[XiaoHe|萧何]] and [[CaoShen|曹参]], helping preserve the recovery associated with early Han Huang-Lao government. The durable synthesis is therefore not complete abdication but trauma, constrained authority, and uneven continued rule; exact ages, poisoning mechanics, injuries, chronology, psychological effects, and policy authorship remain source-scoped.

## Key Claims

- The episode places Liu Ruyi's death in a short protection gap: Liu Ying leaves for a hunt without him, after which Lu Zhi reportedly sends poisoned wine into his sleeping quarters.
- Liu Ying returns to find Liu Ruyi dead and is portrayed as grieving because the killing came from his own mother despite his effort to protect his younger brother.
- Lu Zhi then has Qi Ji mutilated, deprived of sight, hearing, and speech, and displayed in a pig enclosure as a “human swine”; the source uses the brevity of the transmitted formula to emphasize the violence concealed by compact historical prose.
- After being made to see Qi Ji, Liu Ying reportedly protests that the act is inhuman and says he can no longer govern, linking palace violence to trauma inside the sovereign household.
- [[ZhouChang|周昌]] reportedly responds to Liu Ruyi's death by claiming illness and withdrawing, marking the endpoint of the single-official protection arrangement established in Zhao.
- The host compares Lu Zhi's violence with Liu Bang's treatment of [[PengYue|彭越]] and argues that power struggle can convert punishment into cruelty pursued for emotional release.
- The episode later corrects an absolute withdrawal reading by saying Liu Ying still governed with restraint for seven years under Xiao He and Cao Shen; this creates a source-internal tension rather than proof of either uninterrupted rule or total incapacity.
- A closing anecdote about Lu Zhi giving water and food to an old man while farming is used to contest an innate-cruelty explanation, but it does not establish a complete causal account of her later violence.

## Contradictions

- The source internally says both that Liu Ying stopped attending government after the atrocity and that he continued a measured seven-year reign. The wiki records uneven or constrained continued rule rather than treating either absolute as settled.
- No settled contradiction with [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-194-shishang-diyiqi-renzhi-zhixing-nuesha-xiandi-chongfei-2-lmji5jplvzarhldoajaq-cvbupmf|Hanji 194 part 2]] is adopted. Part 1 supplies the event sequence and later self-qualification; part 2 develops the succession background, proportionality judgment, and disagreement over Liu Ying's duty.
- The title's “first human-swine punishment,” the actors' ages, exact date, poison administration, injury sequence, duration of survival, quoted protest, emotional states, and Zhou Chang's motive remain episode-attributed pending comparison with transmitted histories.
- The anecdote of Lu Zhi feeding an old man does not prove that she was originally gentle, and the host's claim that political pressure transformed her personality is interpretive rather than an exclusive causal explanation.
- The attribution of Huang-Lao government and the later Wen-Jing settlement to Liu Ying's own seven-year rule may compress ministerial continuity, imperial policy, and later dynastic outcomes into one retrospective claim.
