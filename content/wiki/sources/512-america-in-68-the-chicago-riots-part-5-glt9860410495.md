# 512. America in '68: The Chicago Riots (Part 5)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode reconstructs the August 1968 Democratic National Convention as a collision among a divided [[DemocraticParty]], anti-war organizers, Yippie political theatre, Mayor [[RichardJDaley|Richard J. Daley]]'s defense of civic order, and a police force primed to interpret provocation as a genuine threat. It argues that televised police violence and party disunity overshadowed [[HubertHumphrey|Hubert Humphrey]]'s nomination and helped make disorder, patriotism, and public presentation decisive issues in the [[UnitedStatesPresidentialElection1968|1968 election]].

The account refuses a simple morality play: it identifies repeated police brutality and collapsing accountability while also examining class resentment, fear after earlier urban unrest, inflammatory activist rhetoric, and the large contemporary audience that sided with police. Its central warning is that movements cannot control how jokes, symbols, or confrontational language will be interpreted outside their own political culture.

## Key Claims

- The convention crisis grew from overlapping conflicts over Vietnam, Johnson's withdrawal, Kennedy's assassination, Democratic succession, racial unrest, and the legitimacy of anti-war protest.
- Daley's political machine, personal investment in Chicago's reputation, and insistence on order made accommodation with protesters unlikely, while permit refusals and militarized preparation increased the chance of confrontation.
- Abbie Hoffman and Jerry Rubin treated Yippie protest as playful television spectacle, but authorities and many viewers read their threats, Viet Cong symbols, and attacks on patriotic imagery literally.
- Police repeatedly used clubs, mace, and tear gas against demonstrators, journalists, medical workers, and bystanders; removed badges and attacked cameras; and turned the Michigan Avenue confrontation into a nationally televised police riot.
- Humphrey won the nomination, but tear gas, Ribicoff's denunciation of "Gestapo tactics," Daley's response, and televised street violence made the convention look like a Democratic institutional collapse.
- Contemporary public opinion often favored the police, limiting the activists' ability to convert visible repression into broad political support and strengthening the environment for Nixon's law-and-order appeal.
- The later Chicago Seven prosecution transformed organizers and Yippie figures into defendants in another political spectacle, although convictions were ultimately reversed.

## Key Quotes

> "The politics of play" - the episode's description of the Yippies' attempt to use absurdity, happenings, and media attention as political action.

> "Festival of blood" - the Chicago Underground's warning that police violence could turn the planned gathering into disaster.

> "Gestapo tactics" - Senator Abraham Ribicoff's convention-floor description of police conduct in Chicago's streets.

## Contradictions

- The episode rejects any account that treats the violence solely as either unprovoked police brutality or activist insurrection. It describes extensive police misconduct while preserving the roles of threatening rhetoric, some protester violence, institutional fear, and public hostility to the demonstrators.
- NBC's later intercutting made Humphrey celebrations and street violence appear simultaneous when they were not, illustrating how a politically meaningful television narrative can exceed the chronology of the underlying footage.
- The source's crowd sizes, polling figures, police demographics, individual motives, quoted chants, and specific sequences are podcast-level claims rather than independently audited findings.
