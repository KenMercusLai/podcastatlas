# When You Need It To Be True

## Summary

This [[HiddenBrain]] episode places [[LeonFestinger|Leon Festinger]]'s observation of [[DorothyMartin|Dorothy Martin]]'s failed 1954 prophecy beside Liz's account of an online relationship with a fabricated man called Sergio. It argues that [[CognitiveDissonanceAndAccommodation|cognitive dissonance]], irreversible sacrifice, emotional need, social isolation, and intermittent reassurance can make contradictory evidence costly to accept. The comparison explains persistence without treating believers or fraud victims as simply unintelligent, while the two narrative cases cannot establish how common or universal the mechanism is.

## Key Claims

- Festinger predicted that failed prophecy would not necessarily dissolve the Seekers' belief because members who had sacrificed jobs, relationships, and reputation had made doubt personally expensive.
- Martin repeatedly reinterpreted failed pickup times and the absent flood as delay, training, or evidence that the group's faith had saved the world.
- Liz's longing to feel understood helped a purported Italian engineer build intimacy before avoiding video calls and introducing an urgent tax obstacle to a valuable Turkish contract.
- The scam combined [[RomanceScamEntrapment|romantic trust]] with [[AdvanceFeeFraud|advance-fee logic]]: a future relationship and financial resolution appeared to require immediate payments and material support.
- Friends' warnings initially threatened Liz's hope and relationship identity, while Sergio reframed scrutiny as jealousy and returned much of one payment, creating selective evidence of trustworthiness.
- A missed airport meeting briefly destabilized the relationship story, but a tragic explanation restored it; later spending, gifts, housing plans, and a second arrival promise deepened [[EscalationOfCommitment|commitment escalation]].
- The final confession ended the central deception but left grief, shame, self-blame, and residual emotional attachment, showing that accepting truth can carry its own psychological cost.

## Contradictions

- The source complicates any simple expectation that decisive falsification automatically corrects a belief: both the Seekers and Liz could reinterpret strong contrary evidence when accepting it threatened hope, identity, or sunk investment.
- It does not establish that every challenged belief becomes stronger, that friendship warnings always backfire, or that dissonance alone explains either case. The episode supplies two narrative illustrations rather than prevalence estimates, controlled comparisons, or a complete account of fraud susceptibility and recovery.
