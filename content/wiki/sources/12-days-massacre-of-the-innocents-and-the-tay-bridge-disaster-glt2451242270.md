# 12 Days: Massacre of the Innocents and the Tay Bridge disaster

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory|The Rest Is History]] episode pairs two December 28 commemorations. Its first half treats the [[MassacreOfTheInnocents|Massacre of the Innocents]] as a theologically meaningful Matthew narrative whose Moses and Exodus pattern, weak external corroboration, calendar tension, and fit with [[HerodTheGreat|Herod the Great's]] violent reputation must be distinguished from secure historical occurrence.

The second half reconstructs the [[TayBridgeDisaster|Tay Bridge disaster]] as a failure of Victorian engineering confidence: [[ThomasBouch]]'s celebrated bridge combined budget pressure, construction economies, and inadequate provision for high winds before collapsing with a train in a storm. [[WilliamMcGonagall]] then gave the catastrophe an enduring comic-literary afterlife through a poem whose forced rhyme and blunt moralizing sit uneasily beside total loss of life.

## Key Claims

- The Massacre of the Innocents appears only in Matthew among the canonical Gospels and is not mentioned by Josephus despite his extensive account of Herod.
- The narrative echoes Pharaoh's killing of Hebrew infants, Moses, flight into and out of Egypt, and Exodus, positioning [[Jesus]] within a biblical pattern even if the event is historically doubtful.
- Herod's killing of perceived dynastic threats made him a plausible narrative agent of infant murder, but character plausibility is not independent corroboration.
- Church tradition remembers the Innocents as early Christian martyrs, while the Coventry Carol helped carry the story into later war and civilian-suffering memory.
- The Tay Rail Bridge embodied Victorian engineering prestige before its central spans and a crossing train fell into the Tay during a storm on December 28, 1879, killing everyone aboard.
- The episode links the failure to cost pressure, construction economies, and inadequate wind-load provision under designer Thomas Bouch rather than treating the storm alone as a complete explanation.
- McGonagall's *The Tay Bridge Disaster* remained famous because its awkward diction, forced rhymes, and engineering moral generated comedy in tension with the tragedy it commemorated.

## Key Quotes

> “Rather be Herod's pig than Herod's child” — the episode's later joke attributed to Augustus, used to summarize Herod's violent family reputation.

> “The stronger we our houses do build, the less chance we have of being killed” — the poem's blunt construction lesson as recited and mocked by the hosts.
