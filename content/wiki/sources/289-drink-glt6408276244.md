# 289: Drink — Britain’s Empire of Booze

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode features drinks writer [[HenryJeffreys]] explaining the argument of [[EmpireOfBooze]]: Britain did not merely import prestigious alcoholic drinks but helped shape what champagne, claret, port, sherry, Marsala, and blended Scotch became. Wealth, shipping, taxation, diplomacy, bottle and barrel technology, consumer taste, and branding repeatedly connected British demand to production elsewhere.

The episode’s strongest contribution is [[BritishDrinkMarketFormation]], a framework for understanding drink styles as market and institutional products rather than timeless national traditions. It also extends [[WinePrestigeClassificationSystem]] by connecting strong English glass, London-facing Bordeaux innovation, champagne sweetness, château identity, and price premiums to changing consumer expectations and prestige narratives.

## Key Claims

- Strong English glass bottles helped make deliberately sparkling wine practicable by containing fermentation pressure, while later champagne marketing elevated a French invention story around Dom Pérignon.
- British demand helped move champagne toward dry brut styles and encouraged Arnaud de Pontac’s darker, selected, new-barrel Bordeaux for wealthy London buyers.
- Politics and fiscal policy shaped consumption: French claret carried Stuart and Jacobite associations, while the Glorious Revolution, wartime taxation, and the 1703 Methuen Treaty favored Portuguese port.
- Modern port and sherry emerged through a combination of stronger source wines, fortification, aging, blending, geographic regulation, merchant organization, and consumer demand rather than preservation needs alone.
- Marsala’s rise through British merchant and Royal Navy endorsement, followed by quality dilution and decline, shows how market access can create or erode category prestige.
- Blended Scotch gained ground when merchants softened and standardized whisky for the English market, cognac supply was damaged by phylloxera, and Scottish imagery supplied a powerful brand narrative.
- Historical taste differed materially from modern expectations: many older wines were sweeter, stronger, more oxidized, and less fresh before temperature-controlled fermentation.

## Key Quotes

> “Britain deeply influenced wine and drink history.” — the episode’s central argument as summarized in the source document.

> “British taste is presented as broad rather than uniform.” — qualification distinguishing mass demand for robust drinks from elite fashions such as dry champagne.

> “Festive drinks can be understood as historical artifacts as well as pleasures.” — the closing synthesis of the source document.
