# 682. South Africa: Mandela and the Death of Apartheid (Part 6)

## Summary

This [[TheRestIsHistory]] episode uses the [[RugbyWorldCup1995|1995 Rugby World Cup]] final at [[EllisPark]] to explain how [[NelsonMandela]] turned a formerly white and [[Afrikaners|Afrikaner]]-coded rugby symbol into a public ritual of post-[[Apartheid|apartheid]] reconciliation. The source presents [[SouthAfrica]]'s victory over New Zealand as historically important less for the result than for the way Mandela, [[Springboks|the Springboks]], and the crowd performed a new national settlement in front of the world.

The second half traces [[NkosiSikelelIAfrika|Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika]] from [[EnochSontonga]]'s 1897 Methodist hymn through its adoption by the [[AfricanNationalCongress|ANC]], its emotional opposition to [[DieStemVanSuidAfrika|Die Stem van Suid-Afrika]], and the later creation of a multilingual national anthem. The central synthesis is that sport and song became tools of [[SymbolicReconciliationStatecraft|symbolic reconciliation statecraft]] because Mandela refused a simple replacement politics and instead made former enemy symbols usable inside a shared national ceremony.

## Key Claims

- [[RugbyWorldCup1995|The 1995 Rugby World Cup final]] mattered as a national ritual because [[Springboks|the Springboks]] had long been a symbol of white South African and [[Afrikaners|Afrikaner]] identity.
- [[Apartheid]] is presented as a total social system that shaped housing, public space, relationships, education, opportunity, and sport.
- [[NelsonMandela]] treated rugby as a political tool after prison, learning Afrikaans, studying Afrikaner history, and using the [[Springboks]] to draw white South Africans into the new settlement.
- Mandela rejected demands to abolish the Springbok name and jersey, backing a "one team, one country" strategy with [[FrancoisPienaar]] instead.
- The image of Mandela wearing Pienaar's number-six Springbok shirt at [[EllisPark]] is framed as a decisive moment of [[SportAsReconciliationRitual|sport reconciliation ritual]].
- [[NkosiSikelelIAfrika|Nkosi Sikelel' iAfrika]] began as [[EnochSontonga]]'s Christian hymn, gained political force through black South African Christianity, and became the [[AfricanNationalCongress|ANC]] anthem in 1925.
- [[DieStemVanSuidAfrika|Die Stem]] and [[NkosiSikelelIAfrika|Nkosi]] carried opposed emotional meanings even though the source says their literal lyrics were less aggressive than their political associations.
- Mandela's refusal to scrap [[DieStemVanSuidAfrika|Die Stem]] turned anthem choice into a practical test of whether the new state could recognize people it did not yet fully represent.
- The final fused anthem used Xhosa, Zulu, Sesotho, Afrikaans, and English, making [[MultilingualNationalAnthemFormation|multilingual anthem formation]] a formal expression of post-apartheid compromise.
- Sontonga's rediscovered grave and Mandela's 1996 memorial link the hymn's neglected black Christian origin to its global visibility at the rugby final.

## Key Quotes

> "one team, one country" - slogan attached to Mandela's Springbok reconciliation strategy.

> "Nelson" - chant the source uses to mark the Afrikaner crowd's public turn toward Mandela.

> "Black Pearl" - nickname the source gives [[ChesterWilliams]], the Springboks' only non-white player in the squad.
