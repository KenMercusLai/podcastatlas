## Summary

This [[MarketplaceTech]] interview with historian [[JoeStreet]] interprets Hollywood's renewed interest in technology leaders as a cultural reckoning with Silicon Valley's economic and social power. It argues that the [[FounderBiopicNarrative]] makes complex institutions legible through a recognizable protagonist or villain, while technology companies' growing role in production and distribution creates a structural limit on criticism about those same companies.

## Key Claims

- Films about [[MarkZuckerberg]], [[SamAltman]], [[ElizabethHolmes]], and [[ElonMusk]] reflect a broader shift from technology optimism toward retrospective scrutiny of concentrated founder and corporate power.
- The [[FounderBiopicNarrative]] compresses institutional complexity into a story about an individual who overcomes obstacles, changes the world, and faces historical judgment.
- Hollywood's criticism of Silicon Valley has both a cultural dimension, reflecting public anxiety, and an industrial dimension, reflecting competition with technology companies that produce or distribute entertainment.
- Technology-company influence over distribution may constrain whether critical films reach audiences, extending [[FilmDistributionGatekeeping]] beyond buyer scarcity into conflicts involving platform power and subject matter.
- The 1970s paranoid-thriller cycle offers a qualified historical analogy for films translating public concern about surveillance and institutional power into genre.
- *The Internship* can be reread as an accidental dystopia about insecure work, corporate selection, and small-business dependence on Google's advertising system.

## Key Quotes

> “a contemporary Bond villain” — Street's shorthand for the cinematic legibility of Elon Musk as a powerful technology founder.

> “profoundly troubling” — Street's assessment of critical films facing distribution questions in a technology-shaped media system.
