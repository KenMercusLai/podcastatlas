# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》211｜文帝刘恒请谏，贾山献治乱策

## Summary

This [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode places [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]]'s early rule between consolidation and self-restraint. An order sending marquises back to their fiefs is publicly justified by supply burdens and local duties, while the host also interprets it as a way to weaken court-based noble networks. After an eclipse, Liu Heng asks officials to identify his faults and recommend worthy, upright, and outspoken people, reduces the guard force, limits corvée, and reallocates surplus court horses to the relay system.

The central memorial comes from [[JiaShanWesternHan|贾山]]. He argues that imperial power itself can frighten advisers into silence even when a ruler formally asks for criticism, contrasts Zhou-era preservation of popular capacity with Qin Shihuang's concentrated consumption and punishment of dissent, and warns that taking recruited remonstrants hunting turns a public counsel channel into imperial recreation. The episode says Liu Heng praises and accepts the advice, but its political motives, numerical claims, exact wording, and policy effects remain source-scoped.

## Key Claims

- Liu Heng orders marquises to return to their fiefs, with heirs sent in place of officeholders or specially retained nobles; the stated reasons are local supply burdens and failure to govern their territories.
- The host additionally interprets dispersal from Chang'an as a consolidation measure that reduces noble coordination, marriage alliances, and coup capacity, but this motive is inferred rather than quoted from the decree.
- After a solar eclipse, Liu Heng asks officials to criticize his faults, recommend 贤良方正 and 直言极谏 candidates, reduce labor burdens, and help ordinary people recover.
- The episode says he reduces the guard command and reallocates surplus court horses to the relay system, joining frugality to the severe material limits of the early Han state.
- Jia Shan argues that the ruler's authority can suppress candid speech even when the ruler looks receptive; a credible advice channel therefore requires more than issuing an invitation.
- His Zhou-Qin comparison links political survival to restrained ruler consumption, preserved popular capacity, and tolerance of criticism, while treating Qin's punishment of dissent as a cause of informational blindness.
- Jia Shan criticizes Liu Heng for taking recruited advisers hunting and proposes a role boundary: ordinary companions may share recreation, but major ministers and upright advisers should deliberate state affairs at court.

## Key Quotes

> “无事常如有事时，有事常如无事时。” - the episode's opening maxim for preparing against danger during apparent calm.

> “虽有尧舜之智，孟贲之勇，无所施也。” - Jia Shan's attributed warning that wisdom and courage cannot overcome a ruler who crushes criticism through power and intimidation.

## Contradictions

- No settled contradiction is adopted. The source strengthens Liu Heng's receptive and frugal profile while remaining compatible with later cases in which he rejects advice, delays structural reform, or favors particular courtiers.
- Motive boundary: the anti-coalition purpose assigned to the marquis-return order is the host's political reconstruction; the stated reasons concern supply burdens and territorial responsibility.
- Textual boundary: the episode says the received 《资治通鉴》 wording “其罢卫将军军” is sometimes transmitted without the final “军,” which could be misread as dismissing the general rather than reducing his troops; this note preserves the episode's correction without independently establishing the textual history.
- Historical-scope caution: dates, offices, eclipse terminology, troop and horse measures, early-Han poverty anecdotes, the Zhou count of roughly 1,800 states, Jia Shan's kinship to Guan Ying, memorial wording, hunting frequency, and the extent of Liu Heng's adoption require comparison with transmitted sources.
- Causal boundary: Zhou-Qin comparison is Jia Shan's normative diagnosis. It does not by itself prove that one variable—consumption, centralization, popular burden, or suppressed criticism—fully explains either dynasty's political outcome.
