# 《资治通鉴·汉纪》346｜千年王陵背后隐藏的惊天大秘密

## Summary

This short [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|芮淇讲透资治通鉴]] episode presents the excavation of [[DaqingdunHanTomb|大青墩汉墓]] as the material recovery of the lost [[SishuiKingdomWesternHan|西汉泗水国]]. Monumental scale, a wooden-chamber plan, outer storage compartments, bronze vessels, figurines, horses, gilded coffin fittings, and a high-status crossbow trigger establish a royal funerary setting, while burned or written “泗水王冢” labels on chamber boards make the cemetery attribution much more direct.

That identification does not settle the individual occupant. The episode compares the tomb against the six recorded kings of Sishui, but a silver turtle-knob seal reading [[LiuSuiSishuiSeal|刘绥]] does not match their transmitted names. The result is a case of [[ArchaeologicalInterpretationUnderSparseEvidence|archaeological interpretation under incomplete and conflicting evidence]]: the kingdom and royal-cemetery context can be more secure than the identity of the person buried in a robbed tomb.

## Key Claims

- The surviving twelve-meter mound, chamber scale, paired side chambers, outer storage structure, and rich furnishings are presented as evidence that Daqingdun had the rank of a vassal king's tomb.
- Figurines, wooden horses, bronze vessels, decorated coffins, gilded fittings, jewelry, incense equipment, and other objects reconstruct elite funerary display rather than a complete biography of the deceased.
- Multiple robbery openings, including one reaching the main chamber, show that the tomb context was disturbed and that missing or displaced identity evidence is possible.
- A decorated Han crossbow trigger is described as the excavation's outstanding weapon find, but its later “天下第一弩机” label is an expert or media evaluation rather than an inscriptional identity marker.
- Chamber boards marked “泗水王冢” and “王冢” provide direct textual evidence linking the monument to the Sishui royal cemetery.
- The episode narrows possible royal occupants by reign length and political fate, but tomb-building capacity cannot by itself identify one king.
- The silver turtle-knob seal reading “刘绥” conflicts with the transmitted list of Sishui kings and therefore opens an identity problem rather than proving that an otherwise unknown king occupied the tomb.
