# Hitler’s Olympics, Part 8: Vater, It Is to Be Fayetteville

## Summary

This [[RevisionistHistory]] episode reverses the usual account of Americans observing Nazi Germany by following [[HeinrichKrieger]], a committed Nazi and German law student who studied racial law in Arkansas and Washington. His research showed German jurists how the [[UnitedStates]] combined formal constitutional legitimacy with segregation, anti-miscegenation law, variable racial definitions, and judicial discretion.

The episode joins that [[TransnationalRacialLawBorrowing|cross-border legal borrowing]] to the 1936 Olympics. Warm treatment of Black American athletes in Berlin did not undo Nazi racism; it exposed how Olympic hospitality, American racial exclusion, and international prestige could coexist. [[LisaLindquistDorr]]'s Virginia case research also supports a second mechanism: [[DiscretionaryRacialDomination|Jim Crow's discretionary flexibility]] could preserve white control through inconsistent prosecution, punishment, leniency, and extralegal violence rather than one automatic rule.

## Key Claims

- [[HeinrichKrieger]] studied at the University of Arkansas School of Law, researched American racial law in Fayetteville and at the Library of Congress, and later supplied German legal officials with a systematic account of U.S. practices.
- Early Nazi jurists treated American immigration restriction, anti-miscegenation law, racial classification, reputation evidence, and judicial discretion as usable precedents while searching for a legal form of racial exclusion.
- American race law did not provide one coherent template: state definitions varied, and some Nazi jurists regarded the Arkansas one-drop rule as excessively severe.
- [[LisaLindquistDorr]]'s study of 288 Virginia accusations against Black men found outcomes ranging from extralegal killing and execution to short sentences, acquittal, pardon, or parole.
- The episode interprets Jim Crow's exceptions as part of its durability: racial, gender, class, labor, and local interests could be balanced without abandoning white supremacy.
- [[JonathanWiesen]] characterizes Krieger as admiring American racial consciousness while condemning lynching as disorderly and legally uncontrolled rather than rejecting racial violence itself.
- Black Olympians' positive treatment in Germany and [[JesseOwens]]'s enthusiastic reception showed Nazi observers that a state could celebrate Black achievement while denying Black citizens equality at home.
