# East India Company

## Overview
The East India Company appears in the source as the semi-governmental imperial institution that organized the invasion and occupation of [[Afghanistan]] during the [[FirstAngloAfghanWar|First Anglo-Afghan War]].

## Current Profile
[[88-the-first-anglo-afghan-war-glt5156371854]] describes the Company of the 1840s as no longer merely commercial: it governed territory, fielded armies dominated numerically by Indian sepoys, coordinated uneasily with regular British regiments, and made foreign policy through officials and political agents.

Its Afghan intervention exposes hybrid institutional weaknesses. Commercial-imperial power could mobilize a large expedition but still suffer distorted reporting, rival chains of command, poor maps, extravagant logistics, and political overconfidence.

## Key Characteristics
- Hybrid commercial, governmental, diplomatic, and military institution.
- Territorial power in India that did not yet directly border Afghanistan.
- Employer of an army in which Indian sepoys formed the majority of the expedition's troops.
- Institutionally distinct from regular British regiments, creating rivalry and coordination problems.
- Capable of regime change but unable to convert occupation into durable legitimacy.
- Exposed by the war's logistical and command failures.

## Evidence
- Institutional form: [[88-the-first-anglo-afghan-war-glt5156371854]] calls the 1840s Company a semi-governmental public-private partnership rather than a purely commercial body.
- Army composition: [[88-the-first-anglo-afghan-war-glt5156371854]] emphasizes the numerical importance of Indian sepoys.
- Operational failure: [[88-the-first-anglo-afghan-war-glt5156371854]] describes mapping, baggage, command, and cantonment failures.

## Qualifications
One war cannot stand for the Company's full history, commercial system, or rule across South Asia. The episode's public-private analogy is explanatory shorthand rather than a complete constitutional description.

## What Changed
- Established the Company as a hybrid governing and military actor through the Afghan intervention case.
