# Michel Foucault / 福柯

## Overview
Michel Foucault / 福柯 appears in the wiki as the philosopher used to explain surveillance, visibility, discipline, institutional power, and self-monitoring across workplace satire, platform-era youth culture, and retrospective interpretations of May 1968.

## Current Profile
The current sources use Foucault in applied rather than comprehensive doctrinal form. Across workplace comedy and platform sociology, visibility becomes disciplinary infrastructure: screens, open offices, restroom monitoring, searchable records, reports, and anticipated third-party judgment can make people regulate themselves even without a continuously present watcher. This joins [[OfficeSurveillancePrivacy]] to [[PlatformSelfDiscipline]] through a shared account of observation and internalized control.

The historical-reception layer places prisons, surveillance, repression, institutions, and “docile bodies” within the disputed cultural afterlife of [[May1968France|May 1968]], especially in the English-speaking world. The Part 1 source adds an important chronology check: Foucault was in Tunis during the Paris events and, in the hosts' account, his philosophy became more important to later memory than to the revolt's outbreak. He therefore appears as an example of retrospective interpretation, not as evidence that theory directly caused the uprising.

## Key Characteristics
- Functions in the wiki as the main philosophical reference for visibility as discipline.
- Is used satirically in a workplace setting where monitoring becomes literal office infrastructure.
- Is used sociologically in the youth-risk episode to explain self-discipline without a fixed watcher.
- Is used retrospectively to represent a possible intellectual afterlife of May 1968's challenge to authority.
- Was not presented as an on-the-ground intellectual organizer of the May outbreak and was in Tunis during the events.
- Bridges institutional surveillance, platform-native self-regulation, and cultural accounts of political defeat.

## Qualifications
All four sources use Foucault as an interpretive reference. The May 1968 episodes explicitly resist a simple causal story in either direction: Foucault did not organize the Paris outbreak, and the events are not shown to have directly caused his later work. The page does not attempt a full account of his philosophy, chronology, historical scholarship, or major works.

## What Changed
- Added the Tunis chronology and rejected Foucault as a primary causal driver of the May outbreak.
- Narrowed the May 1968 connection to retrospective reception and disputed intellectual afterlife.
