## Overview

Liu You / 刘友 is synthesized here as the Western Han Zhao king whom Hanji 202 part 1 says was summoned to Chang'an, isolated under guard, denied food, and buried with commoner rites after a Lü-family queen accused him of opposing Lü kingship. The part 1 body calls him only “赵王”; the adjacent part 2 recap identifies the previously starved Zhao king as Liu You, so the name is a transparent cross-episode normalization.

## Current Profile

Liu You's royal rank does not protect him from the household and regency network created by his marriage to a Lü-family woman. Part 1 says he does not favor the queen and instead favors other consorts. The queen then reports that he objected to people surnamed Lü becoming kings and intended to remove them after [[LuZhi|吕雉]]'s death.

The accusation moves a private marital conflict into the highest political channel. Liu You is summoned to Chang'an, refused an audience, confined in the Zhao residence, and cut off from outside support. Zhao officials who try to bring him food are themselves imprisoned and punished, after which he dies of starvation.

His reported commoner burial and final song extend punishment beyond physical death into royal status and political memory. The episode treats the song as his indictment of Lü rule, coerced marriage, and spousal accusation; the wiki preserves that as a transmitted, episode-attributed account rather than verified verbatim speech.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han Liu-family prince holding the Zhao kingship in the episode's Gao Hou year seven setting.
- Husband in a Lü-family political marriage whose private household remained connected to the regent's kin network.
- Accused opponent of Lü-family kingship, with the allegation reaching Lu Zhi through his queen.
- Confined royal whose access, communication, and food supply were reportedly blocked without an open trial.
- Victim of status degradation through reported commoner burial after death by starvation.

## Qualifications

The bounded evidence consists of two structured popular-history summaries, not a full biography or direct comparison with the Shiji, Hanshu, and Zizhi Tongjian texts. Part 1 does not itself name Liu You, and part 2 supplies only a retrospective identification; the page therefore keeps that normalization explicit. The truth of the queen's allegation, Liu You's precise words and intentions, the queen's identity and motive, the command chain, chronology, confinement procedure, final song, and burial status remain source-scoped.
