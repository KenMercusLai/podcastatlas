## Overview

阜陵王刘延 / Liu Yan appears across Hanji 747 and [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-762-lishi-gaosu-ni-nanren-youquan-neng-you-duo-huangtang-limdo2hfd8epcfkwwsrmzz9no0dj|Hanji 762]] as an Eastern Han imperial prince whose long career is repeatedly shadowed by prophecy and rebellion-linked accusations. He is kept distinct from [[LiuYanDongCommandery|Liu Yan of Dong Commandery]] and [[LiuYanLateHan|Liu Yan (late Han warlord)]].

## Current Profile

Hanji 747 now supplies an earlier Zhangdi-era punishment layer. Liu Yan and his son Liu Huang are accused of rebellion; [[EmperorZhangOfHan|Zhangdi]] cannot bear to execute him, so he demotes Liu Yan from Fuling king to Fuling marquis, leaves him with one county, and forbids officials from contacting him. The episode makes Liu Yan a test of same-surname royal-house discipline short of killing.

Hanji 762 then supplies the retrospective and death-notice layer. Liu Yan is presented as a son of [[LiuXiu|Emperor Guangwu]] and Consort Guo who is first enfeoffed as King of Huaiyang. Under [[EmperorMingOfHan|Emperor Ming]], he becomes implicated in prophecy and rebellion-associated writings. Emperor Ming spares his life but moves him from Huaiyang to Fuling, showing punishment without dynastic execution.

The pattern repeats under Zhangdi when Liu Yan and his son, named Liu Fang in Hanji 762, are accused again. Zhangdi again spares them, demotes Liu Fang, restricts retainers, and later restores Liu Yan's title and territory. Liu Yan dies after fifty-one years as king and receives the posthumous name Zhi.

## Key Characteristics

- Same-surname Eastern Han prince whose career spans repeated imperial reigns.
- Repeatedly associated with prophecy, forbidden writings, and rebellion suspicion.
- Receives limited penalties rather than execution under both Emperor Ming and Emperor Zhang.
- Hanji 747 case includes demotion to Fuling marquis, restriction to one county, and official-contact prohibition.
- Moves from Huaiyang to Fuling as part of a managed royal-house punishment.
- Serves as an example of royal-house discipline short of extermination.

## Qualifications

Hanji 747 names the accused son as Liu Huang, while Hanji 762 names the later accused son as Liu Fang. This page preserves the difference as source-scoped rather than silently harmonizing it. Hanji 762 summarizes earlier cases retrospectively while recording Liu Yan's death notice, so the page remains a compact royal-house profile and does not merge him with other figures named Liu Yan.
