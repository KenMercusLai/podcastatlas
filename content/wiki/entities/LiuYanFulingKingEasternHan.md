## Overview

阜陵王刘延 / Liu Yan appears across Hanji 742, Hanji 747, and [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-762-lishi-gaosu-ni-nanren-youquan-neng-you-duo-huangtang-limdo2hfd8epcfkwwsrmzz9no0dj|Hanji 762]] as an Eastern Han imperial prince whose long career is repeatedly shadowed by prophecy and rebellion-linked accusations. He is kept distinct from [[LiuYanDongCommandery|Liu Yan of Dong Commandery]] and [[LiuYanLateHan|Liu Yan (late Han warlord)]].

## Current Profile

Hanji 742 now supplies the earliest concrete punishment layer. Liu Yan is then Huaiyang king and is described as arrogant, lavish, and harsh toward subordinates. Accusers say he, Xie Yan, Han Guang, and others gathered dangerous men, fabricated prophetic charts, and conducted curse rituals. The court executes Xie Yan, Han Guang, Situ Xing Mu, and many implicated people, but [[EmperorMingOfHan|Emperor Ming]] judges Liu Yan's guilt lighter than the earlier Chu king Liu Ying case and moves him to Fuling with two counties instead of killing him.

Hanji 747 supplies the later Zhangdi-era punishment layer. Liu Yan and his son Liu Huang are accused of rebellion; [[EmperorZhangOfHan|Zhangdi]] cannot bear to execute him, so he demotes Liu Yan from Fuling king to Fuling marquis, leaves him with one county, and forbids officials from contacting him. The episode makes Liu Yan a test of same-surname royal-house discipline short of killing.

Hanji 762 then supplies the retrospective and death-notice layer. Liu Yan is presented as a son of [[LiuXiu|Emperor Guangwu]] and Consort Guo who is first enfeoffed as King of Huaiyang. Under [[EmperorMingOfHan|Emperor Ming]], he becomes implicated in prophecy and rebellion-associated writings. Emperor Ming spares his life but moves him from Huaiyang to Fuling, showing punishment without dynastic execution.

The pattern repeats under Zhangdi when Liu Yan and his son, named Liu Fang in Hanji 762, are accused again. Zhangdi again spares them, demotes Liu Fang, restricts retainers, and later restores Liu Yan's title and territory. Liu Yan dies after fifty-one years as king and receives the posthumous name Zhi.

## Key Characteristics

- Same-surname Eastern Han prince whose career spans repeated imperial reigns.
- Huaiyang king first punished under Emperor Ming after prophecy, curse, and dangerous-retainer accusations.
- Repeatedly associated with forbidden writings, rebellion suspicion, and royal-house discipline.
- Receives limited penalties rather than execution under both Emperor Ming and Emperor Zhang.
- Moves from Huaiyang to Fuling as part of a managed royal-house punishment.
- Hanji 747 case includes demotion to Fuling marquis, restriction to one county, and official-contact prohibition.
- Serves as an example of royal-house discipline short of extermination.

## Qualifications

Hanji 742 names the first accusers and punished associates but does not make Xie Yan, Han Guang, or Xing Mu durable standalone pages. Hanji 747 names the accused son as Liu Huang, while Hanji 762 names the later accused son as Liu Fang. This page preserves the difference as source-scoped rather than silently harmonizing it. Hanji 762 summarizes earlier cases retrospectively while recording Liu Yan's death notice, so the page remains a compact royal-house profile and does not merge him with other figures named Liu Yan.
