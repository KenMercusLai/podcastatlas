# Mona Lisa

## Overview
The Mona Lisa is the Leonardo portrait that [[667-the-mystery-of-the-mona-lisa-glt1735270133]] treats as both a Renaissance artwork and a modern cultural icon. The episode identifies the sitter as [[LisaGherardini|Lisa Gherardini]], wife of [[FrancescoDelGiocondo]], and explains the painting's fame through technique, biography, reproduction, theft, parody, museum display, and tourism.

## Current Profile
The source presents the painting as visually restrained but interpretively unstable: a seated woman turns toward the viewer in plain clothing, with no visible eyebrows, a translucent veil, an ambiguous smile, and a fantastical rocky landscape behind her. Its modern status is not explained by beauty alone. [[LeonardoDaVinci|Leonardo]]'s reputation, [[GiorgioVasari]]'s early account, improved photographic reproduction, Romantic writing, the 1911 theft, and the [[Louvre]] crowd ritual all accumulate around the object.

## Key Characteristics
- The painting is presented as an ambiguous portrait whose expression and landscape invite projection.
- The sitter identification is treated as substantially settled in favor of [[LisaGherardini|Lisa Gherardini]].
- The work's early artistic importance comes from pose, lifelike quality, and influence on later portraiture.
- Its modern fame depends on media circulation, literary mythmaking, theft, parody, and museum tourism.
- Its French patrimony is historically contingent: [[LeonardoDaVinci|Leonardo]] brought it to France and Francis I bought it.

## Evidence
- Ambiguous portrait effect - [[667-the-mystery-of-the-mona-lisa-glt1735270133]] describes the sitter's turned pose, plain clothing, lack of jewelry, blurred eyes and mouth, uncertain smile, and strange landscape.
- Identification - [[667-the-mystery-of-the-mona-lisa-glt1735270133]] says [[GiorgioVasari]] named Lisa del Giocondo and that Agostino Vespucci's 1503 note mentioning Leonardo and Lisa del Giocondo strongly supports that identification.
- Early artistic importance - [[667-the-mystery-of-the-mona-lisa-glt1735270133]] says Raphael saw the painting in Florence and that its pose became widely copied.
- Modern fame - [[667-the-mystery-of-the-mona-lisa-glt1735270133]] connects Romantic writers, better reproduction, the 1911 theft, Duchamp, Warhol, and Louvre visitor behavior.

## Qualifications
The source distinguishes strong sitter evidence from unresolved commission history. It does not settle why Leonardo kept the painting, whether he considered it finished, or whether aesthetic power alone would have produced its later fame.
