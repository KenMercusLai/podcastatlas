# William McGonagall

## Overview

William McGonagall was a Dundee hand-loom weaver, actor, and self-appointed poet remembered for verse judged comic, especially his 1880 poem on the [[TayBridgeDisaster|Tay Bridge disaster]], although how much of the comedy he understood remains unresolved.

## Current Profile

The bounded sources portray McGonagall as ridiculous, sad, persistent, and harder to interpret than the “worst poet” label suggests. With little formal education but strong enthusiasm for Shakespeare, he pursued acting and then experienced a poetic calling around age fifty. His walk to Balmoral after a fraudulent invitation, later hoaxes, and dubious honor can show grand self-misreading, but they also became material in a durable public legend.

His Tay Bridge poem secured the reputation he wanted in a form he may not have controlled. Forced rhymes, awkward meter, flat description, and a blunt recommendation for stronger construction made the poem comic precisely because the disaster killed everyone aboard. Yet the Cautionary Tales source adds [[FailurePerformanceAmbiguity]]: his chaotic Macbeth success, joke-like lines, costumes, crowd attraction, and reported pay can be read as partial or deliberate clowning as well as accidental comedy. Later performances in which audiences pelted him with food keep the ethical cost visible even if he exercised some agency.

## Key Characteristics

- Working-class Dundee weaver with limited schooling and intense Shakespearean enthusiasm.
- Performer remembered through anecdotes of theatrical self-importance.
- Late-blooming poet convinced of a calling and possible royal recognition.
- Writer whose technical weakness created durable comedy of uncertain intentionality.
- Public entertainment figure whose fame mixed agency, self-delusion, economic adaptation, mockery, and humiliation.

## Qualifications

These profiles follow comic podcast treatments and do not independently verify their theatrical, royal, travel, earnings, auction, or performance anecdotes. “Worst poet” is a reputation and comic judgment, not an objective literary ranking. Evidence that audiences laughed or paid does not prove McGonagall's private intention, and partial agency would not make food-throwing or organized humiliation harmless.
