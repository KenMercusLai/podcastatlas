# Kyle Harper

## Overview

Kyle Harper is a historian of the ancient world and global disease history who appears in the sources as a University of Oklahoma professor and the author of *The Fate of Rome* and [[PlaguesUponTheEarth|Plagues Upon the Earth]].

## Current Profile

Harper's contribution is a multi-causal environmental history extending from Roman transformation to the Columbian Exchange, Atlantic slavery, sanitation, cholera, influenza, and COVID-19. He treats climate evidence, pathogen ecology, ancient DNA, urban demography, and transport networks as additions to the historical archive, not replacements for texts or for political, military, and social explanation.

His central argument is that human success carries biological costs. Dense cities, long-distance exchange, plantations, grain supply, armies, roads, shipping, and animal or vector contact increase disease exposure; mortality can then disrupt food systems, labor, state capacity, settlement, and political expectations. These risks do not cancel the gains from technology or globalization, and disease remains one interacting cause among war, famine, violence, inequality, and institutional fragility.

## Key Characteristics

- Historian connecting ancient history with climate science, epidemiology, demography, and ancient DNA.
- Advocate of disease and climate as major historical forces within a plural causal model.
- Interpreter of Roman connectivity as both imperial capacity and pathogen infrastructure.
- Cautious about unknown pathogens, disputed mortality totals, and incomplete geographic evidence.
- Analyst of the Antonine, Cyprian, and Justinianic pandemics as distinct shocks rather than one undifferentiated plague story.
- Global disease historian connecting colonialism, slavery, transport, sanitation, and modern pandemic politics.

## Evidence

- Historical method - [[217-plague-and-the-decline-of-the-roman-empire-glt2583859060]] presents new environmental and biological evidence as an expanded archive for Roman history.
- Connectivity thesis - [[217-plague-and-the-decline-of-the-roman-empire-glt2583859060]] links cities, roads, ships, grain, armies, trade, animals, and migration to pathogen circulation.
- Multi-causal decline - [[217-plague-and-the-decline-of-the-roman-empire-glt2583859060]] repeatedly keeps politics, war, class, food provision, frontier pressure, and institutional change alongside disease.
- Evidentiary restraint - [[217-plague-and-the-decline-of-the-roman-empire-glt2583859060]] distinguishes certain *Yersinia pestis* identification from unresolved earlier pathogens and disputed mortality estimates.
- Global ecological range - [[147-disease-the-new-world-and-modern-pandemics-glt1023018457]] links New World demographic collapse, plantation disease ecology, cholera, influenza, and COVID-19 without reducing any case to one pathogen.
- Progress and risk - [[147-disease-the-new-world-and-modern-pandemics-glt1023018457]] presents sanitation, medicine, technology, and globalization as beneficial systems that also change pathogen opportunity.

## Qualifications

This profile is based on two podcast interviews rather than Harper's full scholarship. Claims about Roman life expectancy, pre-contact American populations, regional disease gradients, specific mortality levels, pathogen origins, migrant transmission, influenza origins, and the scale of plague or colonial effects remain the episodes' interpretations unless independently supported elsewhere.

## What Changed

- Expanded the profile from Roman environmental history to global disease, colonial, public-health, and globalization cases.
- Added his distinction between the gains from human connectivity and the pathogen opportunities those gains create.
