## Overview

The Florentine Republic / 佛罗伦萨共和国 was the formally republican but factionally unstable polity within which Medici dominance, Savonarolan moral authority, wider council participation, foreign pressure, and Machiavelli's public service succeeded one another.

## Current Profile

The earlier source makes Florence the setting that turns [[ThePrince|《君主论》]] into historically grounded analysis. [[NiccoloMachiavelli|Machiavelli]] served the republic after [[GirolamoSavonarola|Savonarola]] fell, organizing military affairs and campaigning against Pisa before Medici restoration ended his office.

The newer episode shows that republican form persisted under Medici dominance. This made [[PieroDeMedici|Piero]]'s inherited position awkward and his secret capitulation to [[CharlesVIIIOfFrance|Charles VIII]] intolerable to the Signoria. Expulsion, confiscation, and movement of art from family to civic space presented revolution as recovery of public authority.

The post-1494 settlement dismantled Medici machinery and created a Venetian-style grand council with wider male participation. Savonarola supported it but could not join because he was a clergyman and not Florentine. His power was [[InformalReligiousPoliticalAuthority|informal]], mediated through sermons, reputation, supporters, and sympathetic decisions.

The republic remained exposed to factional, military, papal, and economic pressure. It limited the boys' enforcement companies, stopped Savonarola preaching, survived his fall, and employed Machiavelli, but succumbed to a papal-backed Medici restoration in 1512.

## Key Characteristics

- Formally republican polity coexisting with strong family or religious influence.
- Governed through the Signoria and, after 1494, a wider grand council.
- Capable of rejecting concessions and expelling a dominant family.
- Used confiscation, public art, and inscriptions to stage legitimacy change.
- Constrained Savonarola while adopting parts of his program.
- Vulnerable to foreign armies, papal pressure, faction, crisis, and restoration.
- Setting of Machiavelli's public career and reflections on political durability.

### Wider participation and dual authority

### Institutional limits and restoration

## Qualifications

The sources explain Savonarola and Machiavelli rather than supply a complete constitutional history. Participation estimates, Signoria independence, factional balance, responsibility for coercive morality, and causes of the 1512 fall require specialist evidence. “Restoration” did not eliminate informal power or make participation universal.

## What Changed

- Added continuity between nominal republicanism under the Medici and the wider post-1494 council.
- Distinguished Savonarola's informal authority from civic office and state control.
- Added symbolic dispossession, limits on moral enforcers, papal pressure, and the 1512 restoration.
