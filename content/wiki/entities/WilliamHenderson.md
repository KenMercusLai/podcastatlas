# William Henderson

## Overview

William Henderson is a law professor whose paper on the under-theorized role of test-taking speed motivates [[puzzle-rush-puzzle-rush|Puzzle Rush]] and its critique of timed legal assessment.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Henderson as a former firefighter-paramedic who found the [[LawSchoolAdmissionTest|LSAT]] and law-school exams more time-pressured than emergency work. He asks whether the LSAT appears predictive partly because many first-year law courses reproduce its speeded conditions rather than because it captures legal ability in full.

His episode-reported analysis finds stronger prediction for timed in-class exams than for take-home exams and papers. He responds in his own teaching by using longer open-book exams, word limits, possible advance disclosure, and greater take-home weighting, treating assessment design as a choice that can change student rankings.

## Key Characteristics

- Questions whether test-taking speed has been mistaken for general legal merit.
- Separates prediction within similar timed formats from prediction across different forms of legal work.
- Uses assessment redesign as a practical response to a construct-validity problem.
- Preserves constraints such as word limits while relaxing severe time pressure.
- Treats changes in ordinal ranking as evidence that evaluation format helps define visible ability.

## Evidence

### Research question and reported finding

- [[puzzle-rush-puzzle-rush]] attributes to Henderson the comparison of LSAT scores with timed exams, take-home exams, and papers, with stronger prediction for the timed format.

### Teaching response

- [[puzzle-rush-puzzle-rush]] reports that Henderson increased in-class exam time, used open-book and take-home formats, retained word limits, and sometimes disclosed a possible question in advance.

### Rank sensitivity

- [[puzzle-rush-puzzle-rush]] describes a previously average-performing student earning an A on a take-home exam and connects the result to Henderson's conclusion that assessment methods can change ordinal rankings.

## Qualifications

The profile comes from a structured podcast summary rather than Henderson's paper, underlying data, syllabus, exam materials, or current biography. The reported associations do not establish that take-home work is a complete measure of legal ability, that time never matters in practice, or that one student's result generalizes. More generous timing can also change collaboration, research, resource-access, authorship, and comparability risks.

## What Changed

- Established a profile centered on format-sensitive predictive validity in legal assessment.
- Added Henderson's longer, open-book, and take-home exam redesign as an applied response.
- Preserved the distinction between questioning severe speed pressure and rejecting all constraints.
