# Twyla Tharp

## Overview
Twyla Tharp is an American dancer and choreographer whose public account of creativity joins disciplined routine, classical technique, bodily intelligence, internal standards, and continued artistic work across aging.

## Current Profile
In the supplied interview, Tharp rejects the romantic idea that creativity should wait for desire or inspiration. She prepares the body, fixes a schedule, brings material into real space, and uses repeated action to discover what the work requires. Her central metaphor is the [[CreativeSpine]]: an organizing focus that coordinates both body and composition. She treats training as vocabulary rather than stylistic imprisonment and moves repeatedly between immersion in the work and objective assessment of what is actually there.

Tharp also frames dance as service. A performance succeeds when it changes the audience's state for the better and creates a shared live experience, yet artistic standards cannot be outsourced to applause, scores, or accolades. Later-life work therefore requires both continued physical reach and acceptance of help, restriction, and altered independence.

## Key Characteristics
- Builds creative reliability through schedule, preparation, repetition, and work before motivation.
- Uses the spine as both a physical center and a compositional principle of coherence.
- Treats classical ballet training as a developed technical vocabulary that can be referenced without forcing every work into one exterior style.
- Tests judgment through the body, real space, real time, dancers, and the felt consequences of movement.
- Protects internal standards from competition scores, quick feedback, prestige, and accolades.
- Understands live dance as audience service, shared attention, and temporary community.
- Approaches aging through continued reach, adaptation, assistance, and commitment to worthy work.

Claims about other artists, ballet systems, telepathy, diet, health, audience embodiment, and the economics of dance remain her episode-specific judgments or anecdotes rather than universal conclusions.
