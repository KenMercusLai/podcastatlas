## Overview

晁错 / Chao Cuo is a Western Han official presented in the current evidence as a forceful advocate of reducing princely territory whose loyalty to central authority coexists with poor protection against the enemies, suspicions, and court resistance generated by his methods.

## Current Profile

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-233-chaocuo-bei-yaozhan-jielouchu-zhichang-zhong-de-daji-lql1pwomxc2t-e6x-cdityt1hwxq|Hanji 233]] does not portray Chao as a simple villain or innocent technician. He answers his father's warning by insisting that territorial reduction is necessary for court dignity and royal peace, but the policy concentrates royal hatred on its most visible advocate. Once the [[RebellionOfTheSevenStatesWesternHan|Seven States rebellion]] begins, his protection weakens further because he proposes that [[HanJingdi|景帝]] campaign personally while he remains in Chang'an and diverts attention toward prosecuting [[YuanAngWesternHan|袁盎]].

The episode's durable judgment separates substantive purpose from political survivability. Chao can be loyal, capable, and correct that strong kingdoms threaten the center while still misjudging authority boundaries, crisis priorities, colleague hostility, and his own exposure. Yuan's counterattack converts the rebels' “诛晁错” slogan into an imperial option, and Jingdi ultimately sacrifices Chao in an effort to remove both an external pretext and an internal source of resistance.

## Key Characteristics

- Centralizing official who presents territorial reduction as necessary to protect the court and ruling house.
- Visible policy advocate on whom princely resentment and the rebels' declared grievance become concentrated.
- Trusted insider whose personal-campaign proposal is presented as creating doubt about his judgment and intended role.
- Court rival who tries to prosecute Yuan Ang during the rebellion rather than keeping the emergency wholly focused on settlement and defense.
- Loyal and capable minister who fails to convert imperial favor into broader colleague support or protection.
- Political sacrifice whose execution removes a person and slogan but, as the adjacent sequel shows, does not end the rebellion.

## Qualifications

This profile currently rests on two compact popular-history source notes, with Hanji 233 supplying Chao's policy, court conflict, and execution and Hanji 234 supplying the failed peace result. The exact territorial measures, father's conduct, military advice, proposed prosecution, offices, audience sequence, impeachment, collective sentence, execution, imperial motive, court response, and mission result require primary-source comparison. “No boundaries” and “poor wisdom” are the host's modern interpretive frame; they should not replace institutional explanations involving princely autonomy, coercive centralization, court factions, wartime fear, and the emperor's need to preserve governability.

## What Changed

- Established Chao as a centralizing policy advocate whose substantive purpose and political survivability must be judged separately.
- Distinguished personal loyalty and ability from coalition support, crisis prioritization, and awareness of exposure.
- Recorded his execution as a political sacrifice that removes the declared grievance without demonstrating military settlement.
