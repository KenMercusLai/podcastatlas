## Overview

晁错 / Chao Cuo is a Western Han centralizing official whose current evidence joins a strategic case for early territorial reduction to the political exposure created by implementing it. His prediction that [[LiuBiWuKing|刘濞]] would rebel either way explains the policy's timing logic, while the later crisis shows how visible ownership, court isolation, and contested judgment make him expendable.

## Current Profile

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-231-hanjingdi-jiuhou-jing-zaojiu-wulong-chuanwei-shijian-2-lg4ybjhj0w8azd-h_krzhibc0clg|Hanji 231 part 2]] supplies the prewar rationale. Chao reportedly urges reduction under both [[LiuHeng|Emperor Wen]] and [[HanJingdi|Jingdi]], citing Liu Bi's bereavement, nonattendance, mining, minting, salt production, fugitive recruitment, and presumed revolt planning. His claim is not that削藩 avoids rebellion, but that delay permits a larger and better prepared one. The court then reduces land from Chu, Zhao, and Jiaoxi through offense-based cases; [[DouYingWesternHan|窦婴]]'s disagreement shows that apparent silence among other officials is not full consensus.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-233-chaocuo-bei-yaozhan-jielouchu-zhichang-zhong-de-daji-lql1pwomxc2t-e6x-cdityt1hwxq|Hanji 233]] follows policy into crisis. Chao defends reduction as necessary for court authority, but princely hatred and the rebels' slogan concentrate on him. His advice that Jingdi campaign personally and his effort to prosecute [[YuanAngWesternHan|袁盎]] weaken his protection. Yuan then converts “诛晁错” into an imperial option by proposing execution, pardon, and restoration of reduced territory.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-234-zhouyafu-zhanchang-dalian-hanjingdi-na-lai-de-danzi-li6iqhtuxgq5xwgbn_vb7lljgyxr|Hanji 234]] supplies the outcome test: Liu Bi does not withdraw after Chao's death. The current judgment therefore separates three questions. Chao offers a coherent timing argument about royal capacity, the chosen implementation helps trigger coalition mobilization, and his execution fails as a peace settlement. None of those findings alone settles whether every reduction was necessary, optimal, or inevitable.

## Key Characteristics

- Centralizing official who repeatedly argues for shrinking strong royal kingdoms.
- Strategist who frames the choice as earlier, smaller rebellion versus later, larger rebellion rather than peace versus war.
- Policy advocate who uses kingdom offenses to support successive reductions against Chu, Zhao, and Jiaoxi.
- Visible owner of a coercive program on whom royal resentment and the coalition slogan concentrate.
- Trusted insider whose crisis advice and attack on Yuan Ang further weaken court protection.
- Political sacrifice whose death removes a named grievance but does not end the rebellion.

## Qualifications

The profile rests on three popular-history notes. Chao's inevitability and scale prediction is a strategic counterfactual, not a demonstrable result. The exact memorials, royal conduct, copper and salt system, fugitive recruitment, offices, offenses, reductions, court debate, Dou Ying relationship, father's conduct, military advice, prosecution attempt, audience sequence, execution, and imperial motives require primary-source comparison. The host's workplace frame may illuminate exposure and coalition failure but cannot replace the institutional conflict between central authority and royal autonomy.
