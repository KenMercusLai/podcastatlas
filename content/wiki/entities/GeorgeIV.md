# George IV

## Overview

George IV appears as a British monarch and former prince regent who turned both [[Brighton]] and his coronation into extravagant fantasy landscapes while pleasure, marital conflict, expense, and public farce exposed the risks beneath royal spectacle.

## Current Profile

The episode presents George IV's delayed 1821 coronation as the survey's clearest fusion of grandeur and absurdity. Medieval and Elizabethan-looking dress, immense expense, music, procession, banquet, and public interest produced genuine magnificence, but oppressive heat, ceremonial awkwardness, melting wax, early-departing choristers, and violent competition for leftovers repeatedly punctured dignity.

The ceremony was also a marital and constitutional spectacle. George tried to exclude Caroline, supported proceedings against her, and denied her entry when she arrived without a ticket. His historical fantasy therefore did not restore an uncontested sacred order; it staged royal power alongside partisan opposition and personal humiliation.

The earlier Brighton setting adds a second arena for George's theatrical taste. Visits associated with gambling, cards, and fast living developed into sustained royal patronage and the Royal Pavilion, whose domes and cupolas turned architecture into an exoticized pleasure fantasy. Resort and coronation therefore express a common method: George used costly historical or foreign-looking spectacle to build environments around appetite and rank.

## Key Characteristics

- Used archaizing costume and ceremony to stage a highly theatrical version of monarchy.
- Presided over an extraordinarily expensive coronation whose scale became part of its notoriety.
- Tried to exclude Caroline of Brunswick after a bitter public marital conflict.
- Endured heat, heavy dress, awkward canopy movement, wax, litter, and banquet disorder that made grandeur inseparable from farce.
- Helped establish the episode's central contrast between fixed ritual content and unstable human performance.
- Royal patron whose gambling culture and Pavilion helped establish Brighton as a Regency pleasure resort.

## Qualifications

This remains a selective profile rather than a full account of George IV's regency, politics, patronage, marriage, or reign. The episodes' comic framing, architectural comparisons, motives, and modern cost conversion are interpretive, and Caroline's wider case is represented only through its immediate effect on the ceremony.

## What Changed

- Extended George's theatrical politics from coronation into Brighton's gambling culture and Royal Pavilion.
- Identified costly architectural fantasy as a shared feature of royal resort patronage and ceremonial self-display.
