# George IV

## Overview

George IV appears as a British monarch who turned coronation into extravagant antiquarian theatre while his conflict with Caroline of Brunswick exposed the cruelty and political risk beneath royal spectacle.

## Current Profile

The episode presents George IV's delayed 1821 coronation as the survey's clearest fusion of grandeur and absurdity. Medieval and Elizabethan-looking dress, immense expense, music, procession, banquet, and public interest produced genuine magnificence, but oppressive heat, ceremonial awkwardness, melting wax, early-departing choristers, and violent competition for leftovers repeatedly punctured dignity.

The ceremony was also a marital and constitutional spectacle. George tried to exclude Caroline, supported proceedings against her, and denied her entry when she arrived without a ticket. His historical fantasy therefore did not restore an uncontested sacred order; it staged royal power alongside partisan opposition and personal humiliation.

## Key Characteristics

- Used archaizing costume and ceremony to stage a highly theatrical version of monarchy.
- Presided over an extraordinarily expensive coronation whose scale became part of its notoriety.
- Tried to exclude Caroline of Brunswick after a bitter public marital conflict.
- Endured heat, heavy dress, awkward canopy movement, wax, litter, and banquet disorder that made grandeur inseparable from farce.
- Helped establish the episode's central contrast between fixed ritual content and unstable human performance.

## Qualifications

This is a coronation-centered profile rather than a full account of George IV's regency, politics, patronage, marriage, or reign. The episode's comic framing and modern cost conversion are interpretive, and Caroline's wider case is represented only through its immediate effect on the ceremony.

## What Changed

- Created a profile in which theatrical magnificence, marital exclusion, and administrative farce are parts of the same coronation politics.
