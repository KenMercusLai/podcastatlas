# Declaration of Independence

## Overview
The Declaration of Independence is the founding document through which [[ThomasJefferson|Thomas Jefferson]]'s language of equality, rights, liberty, and government by consent becomes central to American democratic identity.

## Current Profile
The episode treats the Declaration as both moral breakthrough and contradiction. Jefferson wrote language that later Americans would treat as universal democratic promise, but he and many other supporters were slaveholders. The removed anti-slavery passage matters because it shows Jefferson could name slavery's cruelty while still living from it. The Declaration therefore becomes the main textual anchor for the [[JeffersonianLibertyContradiction|Jeffersonian liberty contradiction]].

## Key Characteristics
- Founding document associated in the episode with equality, rights, liberty, and consent.
- Authored principally by [[ThomasJefferson|Thomas Jefferson]].
- Carried universal language despite support from slaveholding revolutionaries.
- Originally included anti-slavery language that Congress removed.
- Later benchmark against which Jefferson's own conduct at [[Monticello]] is judged.

## Evidence
- Authorship and ideals: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] identifies Jefferson as the writer of the Declaration's famous equality and consent language.
- Slaveholding contradiction: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] contrasts the Declaration's lofty reputation with the reality that Jefferson and other supporters were slaveholders.
- Removed passage: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] says Jefferson's original draft condemned slavery in strong moral terms before Congress removed the passage.
- Later memory: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] treats Jefferson as the founding father most associated with debate over America's original sin of slavery.

## Qualifications
The episode does not deny the Declaration's democratic force. Its qualification is that the document's moral power and the founding generation's exclusions must be interpreted together.
