# Wang Jingwei / 汪精卫

## Overview

Wang Jingwei appears as an anti-Qing revolutionary and senior Nationalist leader whose preference for accommodation hardened into a break with continued resistance, negotiation with Japan, and leadership of a Japanese-controlled Republic of China from Nanjing.

## Current Profile

[[235-china-and-world-war-ii-part-1-glt2114682484]] places Wang's later collaboration inside a revolutionary Nationalist career. He had attempted to assassinate a Qing prince, survived a death sentence, and became one of the movement's most prominent leaders, but by the 1930s he was more open than harder-line Nationalists to a settlement with Japan.

[[236-china-and-world-war-ii-part-2-glt5290070743]] compares Wang's later role to Laval or Pétain: he claimed that collaboration could spare Chinese lives and preserve order, but the regime installed in Nanjing in 1940 remained subordinate to Japanese power. His government did not present itself as abandoning Chinese nationalism; it claimed to be the authentic Republic of China and heir of Sun Yat-sen.

The episode also separates Wang's elite political decision from the mixed calculations of people living under occupation. Acceptance, accommodation, or work with local institutions could reflect coercion, property, routine, order, or survival without amounting to wholehearted ideological support.

## Key Characteristics

- He was a senior Nationalist figure before choosing collaboration with Japan.
- His earlier anti-Qing militancy and revolutionary prestige complicate a simple lifelong-collaborator portrait.
- He favored accommodation with Japan before his formal break from continued resistance.
- He left secretly in 1938 and became head of the Japanese-backed Nanjing regime in March 1940.
- He justified collaboration partly as a way to reduce bombing and civilian suffering.
- His regime competed with Chiang's government for the symbols and legitimacy of the Republic of China.
- He died in Nagoya in 1944, before the regime's collapse in August 1945.

## Evidence

- Revolutionary status and early accommodation: [[235-china-and-world-war-ii-part-1-glt2114682484]] recounts Wang's attempted Qing assassination, prominence within Nationalism, and greater openness to a Japanese deal.
- Political break: [[236-china-and-world-war-ii-part-2-glt5290070743]] describes Wang's 1938 flight, negotiations with Japan, and 1940 installation in Nanjing.
- Claimed rationale: [[236-china-and-world-war-ii-part-2-glt5290070743]] records the argument that cooperation could reduce bombing and save lives in occupied cities.
- Legitimacy claim: [[236-china-and-world-war-ii-part-2-glt5290070743]] says the regime claimed continuity with Sun Yat-sen and the Republic rather than open rejection of Nationalist identity.

## Qualifications

The episodes clearly identify the regime as Japanese-controlled while resisting a uniform account of everyone who lived or served under it. The comparison with European collaborators is interpretive, and the discussion of local attitudes does not establish the motives of every official or resident.

## What Changed

- Added Wang's anti-Qing revolutionary credentials and high standing within the Nationalist movement.
- Extended the collaboration story backward to his prewar preference for accommodation.
