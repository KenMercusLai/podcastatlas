# Sudan

## Overview

Sudan appears in the current evidence through two sharply separated crises: the 19th-century collision among Egyptian rule, British influence, slavery, Mahdism, and imperial intervention, and the contemporary foreign-backed war between the [[SudaneseArmedForces|SAF]] and [[RapidSupportForces|RSF]].

## Current Profile

The Gordon episode presents 19th-century Sudan as nominally Egyptian-ruled within an Ottoman framework but increasingly entangled with British financial and political influence. Slavery and ivory extraction, anti-slavery administration, local grievance, and [[MuhammadAhmad|Muhammad Ahmad's]] religious appeal converged in a revolt Gordon could not reduce to one cause. The [[SiegeOfKhartoum|siege of Khartoum]] then became a British story of failed intervention and martyrdom, although the episode supplies limited Sudanese perspective.

The contemporary source describes a different war whose external entanglement remains structurally important. The SAF controls much of the north and east after retaking [[Khartoum]], while the RSF retains much of the west, especially [[Darfur]]. Outside backing, drones, mass displacement, strategic interest in the [[RedSea]], gold, land, and fragmented armed coalitions make both outright victory and enforceable ceasefire difficult.

## Key Characteristics

- Historical setting where Egyptian rule, British influence, slave trading, abolitionism, and Mahdist religious politics intersected.
- Country whose capital's 1885 fall became a major object of British imperial memory.
- Contemporary arena of a foreign-backed civil war and humanitarian catastrophe.
- Territory divided among coalitions whose external patrons complicate war termination.
- Case showing that control of Khartoum does not alone settle national legitimacy or security.

## Qualifications

The sources cover different states, institutions, and wars separated by roughly 140 years; continuity should not be assumed from geography alone. The historical episode is heavily British-centered, while the contemporary report is time-sensitive and cannot settle disputed patronage, casualty estimates, or future territorial control. Neither provides a comprehensive Sudanese social, political, religious, or economic history.
