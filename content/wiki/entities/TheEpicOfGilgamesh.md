# The Epic of Gilgamesh / 吉尔伽美什史诗

## Overview

The Epic of Gilgamesh is a long-formed, fragmentary Mesopotamian tablet tradition whose king, city, friendship, grief, failed immortality, and written survival connect [[Uruk]]'s political memory to the human limits of heroic power.

## Current Profile

[[ep269-cong-manwei-yuzhou-dao-aodesai-weihe-zongyao-fanpai-yingxiong-shishi-ltklotheqpup6oamyflfzpxtipsw]] presents the epic not as a single authorial book but as a layered, edited, orally inflected, and incomplete tradition. Its emotional center is [[Gilgamesh]] becoming humanly intelligible when [[Enkidu]] dies. Grief makes him fear his own death, seek immortality, fail the sleep test, lose the rejuvenation plant to a snake, and return with the knowledge that kingly power cannot erase human limits.

The city's material and media prehistory comes into focus in [[519-the-worlds-first-city-glt2603135981]]. The poem's descriptions preserve Uruk's walls, gardens, clay workings, and Inanna's dwelling as cultural memory, while the city's early [[AdministrativeWritingInfrastructure]] supplies the deeper historical condition for a poem to survive in cuneiform. The accountant's recording medium becoming a poet's medium is therefore part of the epic's urban history, not merely a technological footnote.

## Key Characteristics

- Layered and fragmentary tablet tradition rather than one fixed authorial text.
- Centers friendship, grief, mortality, failed immortality, and finite kingship.
- Uses repetition and orally inflected form to intensify memory and emotion.
- Preserves Uruk as both royal setting and object of civic memory.
- Depends on the expansion of administrative writing into literary transmission.

## Evidence

- Textual formation and mortality arc: [[ep269-cong-manwei-yuzhou-dao-aodesai-weihe-zongyao-fanpai-yingxiong-shishi-ltklotheqpup6oamyflfzpxtipsw]] describes fragmentary formation, repeated grief, the sleep test, and the lost plant.
- Urban setting: [[519-the-worlds-first-city-glt2603135981]] highlights the epic's descriptions of Uruk's built and sacred landscape.
- Media history: [[519-the-worlds-first-city-glt2603135981]] connects early commodity recording at Uruk to cuneiform's later literary capacity.
- Comparative motifs: [[ep269-cong-manwei-yuzhou-dao-aodesai-weihe-zongyao-fanpai-yingxiong-shishi-ltklotheqpup6oamyflfzpxtipsw]] uses flood and snake parallels to raise, but not settle, transmission questions.

## Qualifications

The sources do not supply a full manuscript history or adjudicate every tablet, recension, date, translation, or reconstruction. Epic city descriptions are not transparent archaeological reports, and a possible historical Gilgamesh does not make the narrative biographical chronicle. Similar flood or snake motifs do not establish a single direction of borrowing. Uruk's early writing is a condition in the long media history of the epic, not evidence that the surviving composition was created with the first administrative tablets.
