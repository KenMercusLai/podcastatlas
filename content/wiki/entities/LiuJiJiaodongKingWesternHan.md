## Overview

胶东康王刘寄 / Liu Ji is a Western Han royal relative whose current profile joins implication in the aftermath of [[LiuAn|淮南王刘安]]'s rebellion case, preservation of his princely line after sudden death, and an unresolved later dispute over whether his still-unidentified tomb was exceptionally rich or politically reduced.

## Current Profile

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-316-1-ta-gong-yue-bai-wang-weihe-zhi-xia-baixing-yuelaiyue-qiong-lu_tr4m36rmz9sx38j4nvlmof5am|Hanji 316-1]] says Liu Ji knew something about Liu An's plan, did not promptly report it, and secretly prepared military equipment. Testimony reaches him after the Huainan case is exposed; fear and illness then precede his death before he designates an heir.

Royal kinship shapes the aftermath. The episode presents Liu Ji as a childhood companion and close maternal relative of [[HanWudi|汉武帝]]. Wudi's grief and pity are used to explain why the elder son Liu Xian succeeds in Jiaodong and the favored younger son Liu Qing receives the new Liu'an kingdom. The durable point is not exoneration but that suspicion did not extinguish the family line.

Three episodes build and then qualify the posthumous treasure story. Hanji 310-1 ties Liu Ji provisionally to “打开康王坟，山东不受贫,” and Hanji 310-2 uses other excavated Western Han royal graves to show that exceptional princely burial wealth was possible. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-311-wenxuejia-guomoruo-ruhe-niangjiu-kaogu-dabeiju-lpq9bozhrdxqn7tiotyx3j-epm9l|Hanji 311]] adds competing hypotheses: Jiaodong's wealth, a reported decade of construction, and collected valuables favor a rich tomb, while [[LiuKuanJibeiKingWesternHan|济北王刘宽]]'s reduced burial after political wrongdoing makes a sparse tomb plausible. Wudi's preservation of Liu Ji's family is counterevidence to a simple punitive-burial inference. No current source identifies or inventories the tomb.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han king of Jiaodong and close royal relative of Han Wudi.
- Implicated through testimony after the Huainan rebellion plot was exposed.
- Accused in the episodes of non-reporting and military preparation.
- Died after fear-associated illness before naming an heir in the source account.
- Left a line preserved through Liu Xian's Jiaodong succession and Liu Qing's Liu'an grant.
- Later proposed as the Kang king behind a Shandong treasure proverb, with rich- and sparse-burial hypotheses both unresolved.

## Qualifications

The four sources are short popular-history summaries, not court dossiers or excavation reports. They do not establish the full evidence against Liu Ji, whether military preparation amounted to a settled rebellion decision, the medical cause of death, exact genealogy, or formal succession procedure. 刘继 is normalized to 刘寄 from the joined Jiaodong, Kang-title, Huainan-case, Liu Xian, Liu Qing, and Liu'an identity chain. The proverb, other royal tombs, Jiaodong wealth, reported construction, and Liu Kuan analogy cannot prove Liu Ji's tomb location, identity, contents, or treatment. Guo Moruo's reported interest supplies no archaeological confirmation.

## What Changed

- Added explicit rich- and sparse-burial hypotheses rather than treating royal wealth analogies as one-directional evidence.
- Added Han Wudi's preservation of Liu Ji's family as counterevidence to a simple punitive-burial inference.
