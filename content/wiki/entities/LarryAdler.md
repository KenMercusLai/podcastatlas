# Larry Adler

## Overview
Larry Adler was an American-born harmonica virtuoso and celebrated storyteller whose recollections anchor [[a-polite-word-for-liar-a-polite-word-for-liar|A Polite Word for Liar]]. The episode presents him as unusually candid about embellishment while resisting the inference that his memory errors provide a simple reading of his character.

## Current Profile
The supplied source traces Adler from a Baltimore childhood and teenage performance breakthrough to international celebrity, friendship with [[JohnWeitz]], anti-McCarthy blacklisting, exile in London, and a final Royal Albert Hall performance. His public persona joined musical skill, wit, name-dropping, and a willingness to reshape anecdotes for comic or dramatic effect.

Adler admits that a desired or improved version can become the remembered version. His glider story supplies a documented example, and his final anecdote openly borrows a punchline from elsewhere. Yet his refusal to name people during the anti-communist campaign, despite bankruptcy and career loss, and his participation in a public reading supporting Salman Rushdie complicate any attempt to treat inaccurate recall as a complete moral diagnosis.

## Key Characteristics
- Internationally celebrated harmonica performer whose social world and stage career supplied abundant autobiographical material.
- Raconteur who openly acknowledged borrowing, improving, and eventually believing parts of stories.
- Remembered the Munich raid as an anticlimactic encounter without shooting, in conflict with Weitz's earlier violent account.
- Accepted major personal and professional costs rather than cooperate with anti-communist naming demands.
- Serves as the episode's main case for separating factual error, narrative appetite, candor, and moral character.

## Qualifications
The source is a structured summary of an interpretive podcast rather than a transcript, complete biography, blacklist archive, performance history, or forensic reconstruction of Adler's memories. Courage in one domain does not prove accuracy or eliminate vanity in another. His candor about embellishment supports the possibility of sincere reconstruction but does not establish that every discrepancy was unconscious.
