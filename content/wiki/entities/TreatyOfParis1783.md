# Treaty of Paris (1783)

## Overview

The Treaty of Paris of 1783 appears in the Franklin episode as the peace treaty with Britain that recognized American independence and completed Franklin's founding-document sequence.

## Current Profile

The sources treat the treaty as both Franklin's diplomatic closure and a strategic British settlement. Franklin helped negotiate recognition after French-backed victory, while the closing episode argues that generous terms could serve Britain by creating a viable United States as a counterweight to France and reopening an Anglo-American relationship grounded in language, culture, religion, and trade.

## Key Characteristics

- Peace treaty with Britain recognizing American independence.
- Diplomatic endpoint of the [[AmericanRevolution|American Revolution]] in the episode's account.
- One of the foundational documents Franklin signed.
- Continuation of Franklin's wartime diplomatic role after the French alliance.
- Settlement with terms the episode characterizes as generous and compatible with British strategic interests.

## Evidence

- Recognition endpoint: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says the 1783 treaty recognized American independence.
- Franklin's role: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says Franklin led negotiations with Britain and signed the Treaty of Paris.
- Foundational signature: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] identifies the peace treaty as one of the four foundational American documents Franklin signed.
- British calculation: [[350-the-triumph-of-george-washington-part-4-glt1619660676]] says generous terms could build the United States into a counterweight to France.
- Relational aftermath: [[350-the-triumph-of-george-washington-part-4-glt1619660676]] uses John Adams's later meeting with George III to illustrate an early turn toward renewed friendship.

## Qualifications

The sources remain high-level and do not cover boundaries, fisheries, Loyalist clauses, debts, Indigenous nations excluded from negotiations, or every negotiator in detail. The British counterweight interpretation is the episode's strategic reading rather than a complete account of ministerial motives.

## What Changed

- Added the British counterweight rationale and early Anglo-American reconciliation to Franklin's recognition endpoint.
