## Current Profile
Her public-education approach combines accessible examples with a functional clinical boundary. For ADHD, ordinary activity, intelligence stereotypes, and isolated high-performance moments give way to developmental history, age-relative attention and impulse control, learning, emotion, relationships, and daily function. For dreams, wishes, metaphor, memory, stress, bodily sensation, and personal context can support reflection but should not be treated as prophecies or fixed diagnostic signs.

Across both subjects, persistent distress or impairment matters more than the label alone. The sources identify her role and VOL.108 names an institutional affiliation, but they do not provide a complete professional biography; details beyond those episode notes remain open rather than inferred.

## Key Characteristics
- Uses ordinary experiences such as falling dreams, exam dreams, false awakenings, and creative dream records to make sleep and mental-health concepts accessible.
- Treats dream meaning as personal and contextual rather than universal, prophetic, or automatically pathological.
- Connects dream recall to sleep-stage timing without using dream quantity alone as a measure of sleep quality.
- Uses daytime impairment and subjective distress as practical escalation thresholds.
- Distinguishes sleepwalking from ordinary dreaming and routes it toward qualified clinical assessment.
- Separates age-typical activity and isolated high performance from persistent ADHD-related functional impairment.
- Treats family environment and treatment as individualized modifiers rather than sole cause or universal prescription.
