# Galileo Galilei / 伽利略

## Overview

Galileo Galilei / 伽利略 appears as an early-modern astronomer and instrument improver whose observations challenged inherited cosmology, and as a warning about the religious and political risks surrounding new science.

## Current Profile

The Descartes source uses Galileo's 1633 trial to explain why new cosmology affected publication strategy and intellectual survival. The Keating interview adds the observational method behind that danger: Galileo improved a refracting telescope, systematically directed it toward the night sky, and used lunar terrain, Jupiter's moons, and Venus to weaken the Earth-centered model.

His significance is not that one instrument delivered final truth. It is the combination of tool improvement, repeated observation, hypothesis pressure, iteration, and argument from public evidence. The episode also presents telescopic distance as a practical time advantage in Venetian military and commercial observation, while noting that Galileo's own explanation of tides was wrong.

## Key Characteristics
- Early systematic user of the telescope for astronomical observation.
- Instrument improver who linked optical capability to hypothesis testing.
- Observer whose lunar, Jovian, and Venus evidence pressured geocentric models.
- Historical warning about conflict among science, theology, and political authority.
- Scientist whose correct model-changing work coexisted with error, including his tide explanation.

## Qualifications

The sources do not provide a complete biography, priority history, technical optical reconstruction, or detailed account of the trial. The claim that Galileo was the first to use the telescope systematically on the night sky and the Venetian demonstration narrative remain episode-scoped. His evidence contributed to cosmological change but did not by itself settle every orbital or physical question.
