# James Bond

## Overview

James Bond is [[IanFleming]]'s fictional British Secret Service agent and the center of a globally successful novel-and-film franchise. In the current evidence he matters less as a realistic spy than as a changing fantasy of British class, consumption, danger, sexuality, and masculine authority.

## Current Profile

[[101-james-bond-glt6304120514]] distinguishes Fleming's Bond from his screen afterlives. The novels place reactionary mid-century assumptions, snobbery, sadomasochism, brand-conscious luxury, physical disgust, and suspicion of foreignness close to the character's foundations. Bond does little conventional intelligence collection and functions more like an assassin whose conspicuous travel and consumption make realism secondary to fantasy.

His Britishness is similarly constructed rather than simple. Bond combines establishment manners and institutional loyalty with outsider traits: he is half Scottish and half Swiss, looks “alien and un-English” in *Moonraker*, and has often been played by actors outside an uncomplicated English upper-class type. That mixture helps him sell elite British style without becoming only a clubland insider.

The films keep the character viable through [[BondMasculinityAdaptation]]. Connery could present drinking, misogyny, and sexual confidence as glamorous or comic; Moore could turn the premise toward irony and self-parody; Craig makes comparable traits signs of damage and conflict. The enduring profile therefore joins continuity of brand to repeated moral and tonal revision.

## Key Characteristics

- Represents a fantasy of decisive, dangerous, sexually powerful masculine agency.
- Carries upper-class British manners and institutional loyalty while retaining outsider toughness.
- Operates more as a conspicuous assassin-adventurer than as a realistic intelligence officer.
- Converts luxury goods, travel, drink, clothing, and confidence into consumer spectacle.
- Inherits reactionary assumptions about gender, race, class, sexuality, and physical difference from Fleming's novels.
- Survives on screen through tonal reinvention, from hard-edged glamour to irony and damaged introspection.
- Functions as a major post-imperial British cultural brand with unusual non-American global reach.

## Evidence

- Literary construction: [[101-james-bond-glt6304120514]] connects Bond to Fleming's wish fulfilment, wartime nostalgia, family insecurity, and adventure-story predecessors.
- British identity: [[101-james-bond-glt6304120514]] joins establishment style to mixed ancestry, outsider casting, and the character's “un-English” physical description.
- Consumer fantasy: [[101-james-bond-glt6304120514]] emphasizes luxury hotels, expensive drinks, special cigarettes, brand names, instant recognition, and implausible freedom of action.
- Cultural reach: [[101-james-bond-glt6304120514]] compares Bond with the Beatles as a maker of cool, self-aware post-imperial Britishness.
- Adaptive masculinity: [[101-james-bond-glt6304120514]] contrasts Connery, Moore, and Craig to show changing treatments of violence, drinking, womanizing, humor, and damage.

## Qualifications

The profile is bounded to one conversational cultural-history episode and does not catalogue every novel, film, actor, or production decision. Claims about audience attitudes, which traits are indispensable, whether a future Bond may change race or gender, and which actor or film is “best” remain judgments made in the discussion rather than settled franchise facts. Explaining the character's prejudices historically does not excuse them.

## What Changed

- Established Bond as a consumerist assassin fantasy rather than a realistic spy.
- Defined his durability through the tension between fixed British-masculine branding and repeated tonal revision.
- Added his role in converting post-imperial decline into exportable cultural style.
