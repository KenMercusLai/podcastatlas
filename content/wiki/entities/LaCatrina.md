# La Catrina

## Overview

La Catrina is the elegant female skeleton figure that became a major visual symbol of Mexico and [[DayOfTheDead|Dia de Muertos]], with a modern genealogy running from [[JoseGuadalupePosada|Jose Guadalupe Posada]]'s satire through [[DiegoRivera]]'s mural.

## Current Profile

The episode uses La Catrina to reject a direct line from ancient ritual to every modern skull costume. Posada's *La Calavera Garbancera* presented a skull in a fashionable French-style hat as social satire; Rivera later placed and named La Catrina in a panoramic history of Mexican society. Her festival afterlife therefore layers mortality symbolism, class satire, art history, nationalism, tourism, and performance.

## Key Characteristics

- Female skeleton presented with elite fashionable dress.
- Originates in Posada's *La Calavera Garbancera* in the episode's account.
- Carries an anti-elite and anti-imitation satirical charge.
- Renamed and canonized through Rivera's mural.
- Later absorbed into Dia de Muertos's public visual language.

## Evidence

- Satirical origin: [[264-mexico-day-of-the-dead-glt9022174156]] describes Posada's elegant skull woman as a critique of upper-class French-style aspiration.
- Mural afterlife: [[264-mexico-day-of-the-dead-glt9022174156]] links the figure's later name and prominence to [[DiegoRivera]].
- Festival symbolism: [[264-mexico-day-of-the-dead-glt9022174156]] contrasts this modern artistic genealogy with claims that skeleton costumes are straightforward ancient survivals.

## Qualifications

The figure's present meanings exceed the original satire, and modern participants need not be invoking Posada's precise political target. The episode supplies a genealogy of one dominant icon, not a complete history of Mexican skull imagery.

## What Changed

- Created a profile distinguishing La Catrina's modern artistic lineage from generalized ancient-origin claims.
