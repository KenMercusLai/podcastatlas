# Oliver Cromwell

## Overview

Oliver Cromwell appears here as the English commander whose 1649 campaign in [[Ireland]] became the principal symbol of seventeenth-century conquest, massacre, and Catholic dispossession.

## Current Profile

The source places Cromwell inside the Wars of the Three Kingdoms rather than treating his invasion as an isolated eruption. The 1641 Ulster rebellion and exaggerated English reports of Protestant killings intensified confessional fear; royalist resistance in Ireland then became a strategic problem for the English Commonwealth.

Cromwell arrived with about 12,000 troops and oversaw notorious violence at Drogheda and Wexford. Yet the episode also treats his later memory as a compression mechanism: Cromwell is a justified lightning rod for brutality, but the redistribution of land, death, transportation, and westward displacement formed a longer conquest and colonization process extending beyond his personal campaign.

## Key Characteristics

- English military commander in the Irish theatre of the Wars of the Three Kingdoms.
- Led a large 1649 invasion against royalist and Catholic resistance.
- Associated especially with mass killing at Drogheda and Wexford.
- Became a symbol of Catholic and Gaelic Irish dispossession and transportation.
- Functions in Irish memory as the personification of a broader colonial process.

- Campaign and violence: [[336-ireland-celts-conquest-and-cromwell-part-1-glt6280042699]] places his 12,000 troops, Drogheda, and Wexford at the center of the military account.

## Qualifications

This is a source-scoped Irish profile, not a full account of Cromwell's English political career, religious thought, Protectorate, or wider wars. The episode treats his reputation as grounded in real violence while warning that personalizing the whole transformation around Cromwell can hide its longer institutional chronology.
