# Robert Stephenson

## Overview

Robert Stephenson is presented in [[140-the-birth-of-the-railways-glt2177757841]] as the more formally educated son of [[GeorgeStephenson]], designer of the winning *Rocket* locomotive and a major civil engineer of Britain's expanding railway network.

## Current Profile

The episode contrasts Robert's Edinburgh education with his father's self-taught mining background while keeping their work connected. Robert designed *Rocket* for the 1829 Rainhill Trials, where the locomotive's efficiency and reported top speed helped establish the commercial credibility and public drama of the [[LiverpoolManchesterRailway]].

His later profile extends beyond locomotives. Tunnels, earthworks, embankments, viaducts, and bridges made railways into territorial systems, and the Kilsby Tunnel stands in the episode as a symbol of their scale. Robert also appears as a pragmatic voice worried by disorderly expansion and supportive of greater direction and standardization during the gauge and railway-mania era.

## Key Characteristics

- He joined a family tradition of practical engineering to university education.
- His design of *Rocket* made locomotive performance a public and commercial test.
- His work expanded from engines into tunnels, bridges, embankments, and network construction.
- He is presented as pragmatically concerned with reliable standards and coordinated growth.
- His career helps connect early colliery railways to national Victorian infrastructure.

## Evidence

- Education and family contrast: [[140-the-birth-of-the-railways-glt2177757841]] identifies him as George's more formally educated son and mentions study at Edinburgh University.
- Rainhill and *Rocket*: [[140-the-birth-of-the-railways-glt2177757841]] credits Robert with designing the locomotive that won the 1829 trials for the [[LiverpoolManchesterRailway]].
- Civil engineering: [[140-the-birth-of-the-railways-glt2177757841]] connects him to tunnels, earthworks, embankments, viaducts, bridges, and the Kilsby Tunnel.
- Governance and standards: [[140-the-birth-of-the-railways-glt2177757841]] says he became concerned about disorderly railway growth and associates the Stephenson standard gauge with four feet eight and a half inches.
