# Troy

## Overview

Troy is the Bronze Age site at Hisarlik in modern Turkey and the destroyed city of the Trojan War tradition, whose archaeological plausibility remains distinct from the historical truth of [[Homer]]'s narrative.

## Current Profile

The source places Troy near the entrance to the straits connecting the Aegean and Black Sea worlds, giving it plausible strategic importance. Excavation and possible Hittite correspondences bring the city into history, but neither the site nor names such as Wilusa, Alexandu, and Ahhiyawa verify a ten-year Greek siege, Helen's abduction, or the [[TrojanHorse]].

Troy's more secure history is also a history of reception. [[AlexanderTheGreat]] and [[XerxesI]] visited its heroic landscape; Romans traced themselves through [[Aeneas]]; medieval British tradition imagined Trojan ancestry; and [[MehmedII]] could place Ottoman conquest inside an Asia-versus-Europe succession story. The defeated city also supports sympathy for victims through [[Hector]] and [[GreekTragedy]], making Troy a reusable symbol of both heroic fame and purposeless ruin.

## Key Characteristics

- It joins a real, multiply layered archaeological site to an unverified epic war narrative.
- Its location gives strategic explanations of conflict some plausibility without establishing one historical cause.
- Possible Hittite name correspondences are suggestive but not decisive.
- Its heroic landscape became a destination for rulers performing geopolitical identity.
- Trojan ancestry could authorize Roman, British, and Ottoman claims to prestige or succession.
- Its destruction supports both victor-centered heroism and victim-centered accounts of war.

## Evidence

- Archaeological identity: [[6-troy-glt4742295987]] describes Hisarlik as the probable site of Troy while criticizing [[HeinrichSchliemann]]'s destructive and overconfident excavation.
- Bronze Age context: [[6-troy-glt4742295987]] connects the site to the straits and reports possible Wilusa, Alexandu, and Ahhiyawa correspondences.
- Political appropriation: [[6-troy-glt4742295987]] follows visits or ancestry claims associated with Alexander, Xerxes, Rome, medieval Britain, and Mehmed II.
- Defeat and suffering: [[6-troy-glt4742295987]] links Hector, Euripidean tragedy, and modern war comparison to sympathy for Troy as the losing city.

## Qualifications

- Archaeological Troy does not by itself prove the Trojan War described in later poetry.
- The source summarizes rather than adjudicates specialist debate over Hittite language, site layers, chronology, trade, and warfare.
- Ancestry and succession claims show Troy's political usefulness, not biological descent or continuous collective memory.
- Modern sympathy for Trojan victims should not be projected backward as the only ancient response; Greek tragedy already supplies an important ancient counterexample to a simple modern-versus-ancient divide.

## What Changed

- Created a bounded profile separating the archaeological site, epic city, and later political symbol.
- Added Troy's victim-centered and ancestry-based afterlives without treating them as evidence for the war's historicity.
