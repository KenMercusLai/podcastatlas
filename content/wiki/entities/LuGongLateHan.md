## Overview

鲁恭 / Lu Gong appears in the current wiki as a scholar-official under [[EmpressDowagerDengLateHan|Empress Dowager Deng]] whose profile now joins humane judicial procedure, moralized local governance, famine-era dismissal, and a boundary between teaching and public recommendation.

## Current Profile

Hanji 779 adds Lu Gong's positive early-regency layer. After becoming situ in 107 CE, he argues that the court's litigation calendar is harming farmers and distorting judgment. Light cases and already settled matters should not require summer interrogation that pulls people out of the fields, while serious capital cases should not be rushed into execution merely because officials face an eleventh-month deadline. The court accepts his proposed timing change. The episode also recalls his earlier Zhongmou governance as patient moral education rather than heavy reliance on punishment.

Hanji 782 then adds the later accountability and recommendation layer. Lu Gong is a famous scholar who had reached situ but is dismissed after Luoyang famine and the Three Excellencies' ritual admission of fault. The same source uses his students to clarify that teaching is not identical to public nomination: he may help students learn and assist selectively, but recommendation must answer to office fitness, not private teacher-student obligation.

## Key Characteristics

- Scholar-official whose current evidence spans local moral education, central justice reform, and high office.
- Situ who corrects litigation timing so judicial administration does not unnecessarily disrupt farming.
- Procedural reformer who distinguishes light-case handling from death-penalty review.
- Official removed during the 109 CE Luoyang famine accountability cycle under Deng's regency.
- Teacher who refuses to turn education automatically into mass recommendation.
- Appointment-boundary actor who separates private instruction from public office duty.

## Qualifications

Hanji 779's positive judicial evidence does not erase Hanji 782's later dismissal, and Hanji 782's dismissal does not prove personal corruption or incompetence. The bounded sources show an official whose humane procedural judgment and scholarly reputation coexist with a political-accountability system that removes senior ministers under famine pressure.
