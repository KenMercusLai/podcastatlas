# Persepolis

## Overview

Persepolis was an [[AchaemenidEmpire|Achaemenid]] ceremonial capital whose destruction under [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]] and reuse by [[MohammadRezaPahlavi|Mohammad Reza Pahlavi]] made it a layered site of imperial memory.

## Current Profile

[[154-the-most-disastrous-party-in-history-glt6506112996]] connects two spectacles separated by more than two millennia. In the ancient story, Alexander's company drunkenly burns the palace after Thais calls for revenge for Xerxes' destruction in Athens. In 1971, the shah stages an elaborate 2,500-year celebration associated with [[CyrusTheGreat|Cyrus the Great]], foreign dignitaries, luxury arrangements, and monarchical continuity. After the Iranian Revolution, the latter event becomes shorthand for elite excess and distance from Iranian society.

## Key Characteristics

- Monumental center of Achaemenid kingship and ceremonial power.
- Site whose burning became a story of conquest, revenge, drunkenness, and hubris.
- Stage for the shah's attempt to claim deep imperial continuity.
- Memory site whose later meaning was reshaped by revolutionary criticism.

## Evidence

- Ancient destruction: [[154-the-most-disastrous-party-in-history-glt6506112996]] recounts the source-qualified Alexander and Thais story.
- Modern commemoration: [[154-the-most-disastrous-party-in-history-glt6506112996]] describes the shah's 1971 anniversary celebration and foreign spectacle.
- Symbolic linkage: [[154-the-most-disastrous-party-in-history-glt6506112996]] ranks Persepolis first because one site carries two memories of imperial overreach.

## Qualifications

The episode does not settle whether the ancient burning was drunken accident, revenge, deliberate policy, or a later narrative construction. Its account of the 1971 celebration emphasizes retrospective symbolism rather than a full budget, policy, social, or revolutionary causal history.

## What Changed

- Established Persepolis as a layered memory site joining Achaemenid rule, conquest, monarchy, and revolutionary reinterpretation.
