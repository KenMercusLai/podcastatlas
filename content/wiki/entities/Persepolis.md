# Persepolis

## Overview

Persepolis was an [[AchaemenidEmpire|Achaemenid]] ceremonial capital whose destruction under [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]] and reuse by [[MohammadRezaPahlavi|Mohammad Reza Pahlavi]] made it a layered site of imperial memory.

## Current Profile

[[154-the-most-disastrous-party-in-history-glt6506112996]] connects two spectacles separated by more than two millennia. In the ancient story, Alexander's company drunkenly burns the palace after Thaïs calls for revenge for Xerxes' destruction in Athens. [[117-alexander-the-great-part-2-glt9707946822]] confirms the destruction at event level while broadening motive: deliberate Greek revenge, rejection of the Achaemenid dynasty, political theater, and drunken impulse remain possible and compatible in part.

In 1971, the shah stages an elaborate 2,500-year celebration associated with [[CyrusTheGreat|Cyrus the Great]], foreign dignitaries, luxury arrangements, and monarchical continuity. After the Iranian Revolution, the event becomes shorthand for elite excess and distance from Iranian society. The site therefore joins conquest-era ambiguity to modern struggles over imperial legitimacy.

## Key Characteristics

- Monumental center of Achaemenid kingship and ceremonial power.
- Site whose burning became a disputed act of conquest, revenge, dynastic rupture, drunkenness, and hubris.
- Stage for the shah's attempt to claim deep imperial continuity.
- Memory site whose later meaning was reshaped by revolutionary criticism.

## Evidence

- Ancient destruction: [[154-the-most-disastrous-party-in-history-glt6506112996]] recounts the source-qualified Alexander and Thaïs story, while [[117-alexander-the-great-part-2-glt9707946822]] keeps it beside deliberate political explanations.
- Modern commemoration: [[154-the-most-disastrous-party-in-history-glt6506112996]] describes the shah's 1971 anniversary celebration and foreign spectacle.
- Symbolic linkage: [[154-the-most-disastrous-party-in-history-glt6506112996]] ranks Persepolis first because one site carries two memories of imperial overreach.

## Qualifications

Neither episode settles whether the ancient burning was drunken accident, revenge, deliberate policy, or a later narrative construction around a secure destructive event. The 1971 account emphasizes retrospective symbolism rather than a full budget, policy, social, or revolutionary causal history.

## What Changed

- Broadened the burning from a single vivid party story into competing but partly compatible revenge, dynastic, political, and drunken explanations.

## Relationships

- [[AchaemenidEmpire]] - imperial polity for which Persepolis served as a ceremonial center.
- [[AlexanderTheGreat]] - conqueror associated with the palace's destruction.
- [[MohammadRezaPahlavi]] - shah who reused the site for modern dynastic legitimation.
- [[CyrusTheGreat]] - founder figure invoked by the 1971 celebration.
- [[DisastrousPartyPoliticalSymbol]] - framework connecting both spectacles to overreach and legitimacy loss.
- [[HistoricalMemoryContest]] - mechanism through which later regimes and audiences reinterpret the site.
