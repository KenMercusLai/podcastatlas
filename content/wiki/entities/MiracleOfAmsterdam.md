# Miracle of Amsterdam

## Overview

The Miracle of Amsterdam is a 1345 Catholic Eucharistic miracle tradition that the episode uses to explain how medieval [[Amsterdam]] became an important pilgrimage center before the city's later Protestant and commercial identities.

## Current Profile

According to the story retold in the episode, a dying man received the consecrated host, vomited it, and the host did not burn when placed in a fire. A church developed around the site, and the host was also said to survive later fires. Pilgrims, including the future emperor Maximilian, came to Amsterdam, and nearby street names retained traces of the sacred geography.

The tradition matters less here as proof of a supernatural event than as evidence for the city's medieval Catholic identity, pilgrimage economy, and later confessional rupture. After the 1578 Alteration, Protestant authorities ended the public miracle procession as they removed Catholic control of churches and civic government.

## Key Characteristics

- Eucharistic miracle tradition dated by the source to 1345.
- Centered on a consecrated host said to survive fire.
- Generated a church, pilgrimage traffic, sacred geography, and urban prestige.
- Demonstrates that medieval Amsterdam was strongly Catholic before the Alteration.
- Public commemoration was suppressed under Protestant rule after 1578.

- Pilgrimage significance: [[351-amsterdam-miracles-money-and-mud-part-1-glt4666881212]] describes a major pilgrimage center and Maximilian's 1489 visit.
- Confessional rupture: [[351-amsterdam-miracles-money-and-mud-part-1-glt4666881212]] says the Alteration ended miracle processions and repurposed Catholic institutions.

## Qualifications

The source recounts a devotional tradition, not independently verified evidence of a supernatural event. Its annual pilgrim estimate and claims about the host surviving later church fires remain source-scoped.
