# Madge Oberholtzer

## Overview

Madge Oberholtzer was an Indiana woman whose abduction and assault by Klan leader D. C. Stephenson, subsequent death, and preserved account led to his murder conviction and helped break the political power of the Indiana Ku Klux Klan.

## Current Profile

The episode places Oberholtzer at the moral and political center of the Klan's collapse. After meeting [[DCStephenson]] at an inauguration-related party, she was summoned by his secretary in March 1925, forced to drink, taken by train, raped, beaten, and bitten. She obtained mercury bichloride in a suicide attempt and later died from the combined consequences described in the source.

Her case exposed more than one leader's criminality. Stephenson's conviction contradicted the [[KuKluxKlan]]'s public claim to defend family morality; his failed effort to obtain a pardon then triggered disclosure of bribery records, widening the scandal into a political earthquake.

## Key Characteristics

- Her testimony and physical evidence made elite abuse prosecutable despite Stephenson's political power.
- Her death converted rumors of predation into a public criminal case.
- The case exposed the gap between Klan moral rhetoric and leadership behavior.
- Its political consequences depended on Stephenson's expectation that captured officials would protect him.
- Her historical significance should not reduce her to an instrument in the Klan's decline.

## Evidence

- Crime and death: [[657-the-ku-klux-klan-american-fascists-part-4-glt4147819855]] recounts the March 1925 abduction, assault, poisoning attempt, injuries, and April death.
- Trial consequence: [[657-the-ku-klux-klan-american-fascists-part-4-glt4147819855]] records Stephenson's second-degree murder conviction and life sentence.
- Political consequence: [[657-the-ku-klux-klan-american-fascists-part-4-glt4147819855]] connects the refused pardon, released documents, official corruption, and rapid Indiana membership collapse.

## Qualifications

This profile rests on one narrative source and does not reconstruct the complete trial record, medical evidence, Oberholtzer's life, or her own words. The episode's causal account of death and its description of the offenses should remain distinct from a full legal-history analysis. Her victimization and agency should not be subordinated to Stephenson's biography.

## What Changed

- Created a victim-centered profile connecting the criminal case to institutional exposure.
- Preserved the difference between the episode narrative and a complete legal record.
- Kept Oberholtzer's significance from being reduced to Stephenson's downfall.
