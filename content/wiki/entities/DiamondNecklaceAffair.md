# Diamond Necklace Affair

## Overview

The Diamond Necklace Affair was a 1784-1786 fraud and public trial in which [[JeanneDeLaMotte|Jeanne de la Motte]] deceived [[CardinalDeRohan|Cardinal de Rohan]] into obtaining an immense necklace supposedly for [[MarieAntoinette|Marie Antoinette]], who knew nothing about the scheme.

## Current Profile

The affair joined a confidence trick to an existing crisis of royal reputation. De la Motte used a queen lookalike, a nocturnal Versailles meeting, forged letters, and false couriers to turn Rohan's ambition into apparent evidence of royal approval. Jewelers desperate to sell a necklace originally intended for Madame du Barry accepted Rohan's mediation; de la Motte's associates then broke up the necklace and marketed its stones. When payment failed, Marie Antoinette pressed for investigation and [[LouisXVI|Louis XVI]] ordered Rohan's conspicuous arrest. Open proceedings and popular legal memoirs shifted attention from the fraud to whether the queen was the kind of person who might secretly have ordered the necklace. Acquittals, punishment, gossip, and later sexual slander made innocence politically weaker than plausibility.

## Key Characteristics

- Combined disguise, forged correspondence, status borrowing, and motivated belief into one fraud.
- Depended on multiple parties wanting the royal-purchase story to be true for different reasons.
- Became public through a royal investigation and conspicuous arrest that the monarchy could not later contain.
- Turned legal outcomes into reputational evidence: Rohan's acquittal was read against the queen despite her noninvolvement.
- Connected extravagant-consumption stories to sexual, xenophobic, anti-clerical, and anti-despotism attacks.
- Acted as an accelerant within deeper court, fiscal, subsistence, and institutional crises rather than as a sufficient cause of revolution.

## Qualifications

The source treats the affair as politically consequential without making it the cause of the [[FrenchRevolution|French Revolution]]. Many theatrical details derive from legal memoirs and later hostile accounts. The queen's innocence in this transaction must not be confused either with a blanket defense of monarchy or with her later documented political conduct after 1791.

## What Changed

- Created an event profile separating the proven fraud from the scandal's wider political interpretation.
- Located the affair in the cultural prehistory of revolution without treating scandal as a sufficient structural explanation.
