## Overview
郭伋 / Guo Ji is an Eastern Han official whose current wiki profile joins three source-bounded roles. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-705-1-diwang-moulue-liuxiu-ruhe-yi-sheling-nizhuan-caiyuan-fengyun-lq2m06p7eczj9ebysxpl84bkzzi|Hanji 705-1]] shows him as the publicly trusted candidate whose non-appointment to Da Sikong becomes a remonstrance issue for Zheng Xing. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-709-mian-dui-bangjia-an-guren-weihe-buguan-renzhi-sihuo-luknor8yw839qwxogeyzwluo6qe2|Hanji 709]] presents him as a trust-bearing anti-bandit official in [[YingchuanCommandery|颍川]], while [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-711-ta-zhu-liuxiu-da-xia-banbi-jiangshan-weihe-si-fa-biequ-liib38zub0-tgrbtycmlqvohv-w|Hanji 711]] later presents him as a Bingzhou governor warning [[LiuXiu|刘秀]] against overreliance on Nanyang appointments.

## Current Profile
Hanji 705-1 gives Guo Ji the reputation-before-office layer. Zheng Xing tells Liu Xiu that the public supports Guo Ji for Da Sikong and that delayed use of such a figure is producing rumor and uncertainty. Guo Ji is not shown acting in that scene, but his reputation functions as evidence in court advice: credible appointment signals matter because people are watching whether high office is filled by trusted talent.

Hanji 709 gives Guo Ji the local pacification layer. Facing bandit leaders and surrendered or captured men, he chooses conversation, release, and return to farming rather than immediate exemplary punishment. He then reports himself for this discretionary leniency. Liu Xiu does not punish him, and the episode says later bandit groups surrender because Guo Ji's word has become credible.

Hanji 711 gives the post-founding personnel layer. As Bingzhou governor passing through Luoyang, Guo Ji tells Liu Xiu that official selection should draw from the whole realm rather than leaning too heavily on Liu Xiu's Nanyang compatriots. The combined profile is not merely lenient or merely critical. Guo Ji is useful because he sees when credibility is the scarce resource: high appointments need public trust, surrendered bandits need believable leniency, and a national dynasty needs officeholding to be credible beyond the founding hometown circle.

## Key Characteristics
- Eastern Han official whose public reputation becomes personnel evidence before his later documented advisory scenes.
- Trust-based local pacifier who releases men to farm so surrender becomes believable.
- Discretionary official who self-impeaches after lenient handling, preserving institutional accountability.
- Personnel adviser who critiques Nanyang-heavy appointment patterns.
- State-capacity witness whose advice links appointment credibility, local order, and national staffing to public authority.
- Advisory actor whose criticisms are made inside Liu Xiu's governing relationship rather than as factional attack.

## Qualifications
This page remains bounded to the three listed source notes. Hanji 705-1 does not prove that Guo Ji should have been appointed Da Sikong; it proves that his public reputation could become evidence in remonstrance and rumor control. Hanji 709 does not prove that leniency is always better than punishment; it proves that Guo Ji's credibility could turn release into surrender pressure in one anti-bandit setting. Hanji 711 does not prove that every Nanyang appointment was poor; its durable claim is the structural warning that early trust networks can become an administrative constraint if they are not widened.
