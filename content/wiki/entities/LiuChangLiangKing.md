## Overview
梁王刘畅 / Liu Chang was an Eastern Han prince, described in the source as a son of [[EmperorMingOfHan|Emperor Ming]], whose private ritual language around becoming emperor triggered punishment from [[EmperorHeOfHan|Emperor He]]. He is distinct from modern [[LiuChang|刘畅]] and from [[LiuChangHuainanKing|淮南王刘长]].

## Current Profile
Liu Chang's current profile is limited to the Hanji 768 episode. During a ritual or prayer scene, his retainer Bian Ji voiced the taboo claim that Liu Chang should become Son of Heaven. Officials pressed for imprisonment, but Emperor He reduced Liu Chang's territory by two counties instead. Liu Chang then apologized and offered further reductions of his retinue and fief, which Emperor He declined, leaving a limited punishment in place.

## Key Characteristics
- Royal-house member whose status made imperial-ambition language politically dangerous.
- Punished for a court-sensitive speech act rather than for a fully developed rebellion in this source layer.
- Benefited from Emperor He's preference for limited containment over maximal punishment.
- Responded with apology and voluntary concession, helping close the case.
- Functions as an example of how royal privilege depends on avoiding the usurpation taboo.

- Taboo speech event: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-768-lishi-gaosu-ni-jidu-shi-shaxin-lgk8qoxvs5ax8oikgecrxqvispna|Hanji 768]] reports Bian Ji's statement that Liu Chang should become emperor and treats it as a serious breach for a prince.
- Limited imperial punishment: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-768-lishi-gaosu-ni-jidu-shi-shaxin-lgk8qoxvs5ax8oikgecrxqvispna|Hanji 768]] says officials sought imprisonment, while Emperor He chose to cut two counties from Liu Chang's kingdom.
- Case closure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-768-lishi-gaosu-ni-jidu-shi-shaxin-lgk8qoxvs5ax8oikgecrxqvispna|Hanji 768]] says Liu Chang apologized and offered larger reductions, but Emperor He declined the offer.

## Qualifications
The page does not yet establish whether Bian Ji's words reflected Liu Chang's settled intention, a retainer's flattery, or a ritualized political fantasy. The source emphasizes the taboo and the ruler's leniency more than Liu Chang's internal motives.
