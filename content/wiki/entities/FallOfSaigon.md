# Fall of Saigon

## Overview

The Fall of Saigon is the April 1975 collapse of South Vietnam and capture of its capital by North Vietnamese forces, reconstructed from the failed post-1973 settlement, the spring military rout, the refugee crisis, and the final U.S. Embassy evacuation.

## Current Profile

Part 1 makes the collapse a process rather than a single scene. The [[ParisPeaceAccords]] enabled American withdrawal without ending combat; Watergate and [[CongressionalWarPowersConstraint|congressional limits]] weakened the promised deterrent; and [[NguyenVanThieu|Nguyễn Văn Thiệu]]'s failed territorial contraction helped turn military retreat and refugee flight into a [[StateCollapseCascade]].

Part 2 then makes the endpoint visible. The embassy walls separated people protected by American evacuation capacity from Vietnamese civilians and allies left behind, while at the palace [[DuongVanMinh|Dương Văn Minh]]'s attempt to surrender power was answered with the claim that no power remained to surrender.

Its afterlife extends through postwar repression and displacement and into [[VietnamWarAmericanMemory]], where the final evacuation became an image of American defeat, guilt, and contested national honor.

## Key Characteristics

- It completed the military and political collapse of South Vietnam.
- Its speed emerged from treaty fragility, lost external support, retreat failure, refugee congestion, and enemy adaptation.
- Its final hours joined organized American extraction to chaotic exclusion of Vietnamese allies.
- The embassy and palace supplied contrasting symbols of abandonment and extinguished sovereignty.
- Immediate participant testimony carried shame as well as relief.
- Its consequences continued through imprisonment, economic breakdown, refugee flight, and political memory.

## Qualifications

The page reflects a Western-hosted narrative that explicitly acknowledges its limited Vietnamese perspective. Exact timings, dialogue, crowd behavior, casualty and detention totals, aid effects, and individual motives remain source-scoped. The sources do not settle whether different aid or bombing choices could have prevented the fall. The event ended South Vietnam's state power but did not end violence, displacement, regional conflict, or political argument over the war.

## What Changed

- Added the failed settlement, domestic U.S. constraint, strategic retreat, and refugee-driven collapse that preceded the final evacuation.
- Reframed the event as a cascade whose last hours exposed obligations accumulated during intervention.
