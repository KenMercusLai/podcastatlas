# German Invasion of Belgium (1914)

## Overview

The German invasion of Belgium began in August 1914 when Germany demanded military passage through neutral [[Belgium]] and attacked after Belgium refused, turning the [[SchlieffenPlan|Schlieffen Plan]] into an international-law, occupation, and propaganda crisis.

## Current Profile

[[594-the-first-world-war-the-invasion-of-belgium-part-1-glt4855256129]] presents the invasion as a product of strategic fear and deliberate choice. German planners believed the Entente's larger combined resources made a long two-front war dangerous, so the western armies were expected to move rapidly through Belgium, bypass French frontier defenses, threaten Paris, and then release forces for the east.

Belgian neutrality made that operational logic politically costly. Treaties had guaranteed Belgian independence, France avoided crossing first, and Belgium rejected Germany's demand for passage, railway use, and control of Liège and Namur. Britain could therefore combine law and defense of a smaller state with its existing concern about France and the Channel coast.

The invasion quickly became more than a maneuver campaign. Unexpected resistance at the [[BattleOfLiege1914|Battle of Liège]], timetable pressure, rumors of civilian shooting, collective punishment, executions, deportation, and Louvain's destruction joined military advance to occupation violence. Brussels was surrendered intact and entered on 20 August, but German operational momentum already carried severe diplomatic and reputational damage.

## Key Characteristics

- Began with an ultimatum demanding passage and strategic control from a neutral state.
- Converted a rapid-victory plan into a legal and diplomatic crisis that facilitated British intervention.
- Joined rail-based mass movement and strict marching timetables to fear of strategic delay.
- Met stronger Belgian resistance at Liège than German planners expected.
- Produced documented civilian reprisals alongside exaggerated and contested atrocity stories.
- Captured Brussels without destroying it while violence elsewhere damaged Germany's cultural reputation.

## Evidence

- Neutrality and ultimatum: [[594-the-first-world-war-the-invasion-of-belgium-part-1-glt4855256129]] connects the 1839 and 1870 guarantees, German demands of 2 August, Belgian refusal, and the 4 August invasion.
- Operational design: [[594-the-first-world-war-the-invasion-of-belgium-part-1-glt4855256129]] connects the Schlieffen Plan, more than 500 trains a day, three armies, and rapid-march targets to the attempt to defeat France first.
- Resistance and delay: [[594-the-first-world-war-the-invasion-of-belgium-part-1-glt4855256129]] follows the early attacks on Liège, capture of the city, and continued resistance of the surrounding forts.
- Civilian consequences: [[594-the-first-world-war-the-invasion-of-belgium-part-1-glt4855256129]] describes executions, deportations, collective punishment, and the destruction at Louvain while preserving disputed totals and stories.
- Brussels: [[594-the-first-world-war-the-invasion-of-belgium-part-1-glt4855256129]] says King Albert ordered the capital surrendered intact before German forces entered on 20 August.

## Qualifications

This is an opening-phase profile rather than a complete military or occupation history. Fear of encirclement and operational urgency explain German reasoning but do not legalize invasion or excuse reprisals. The source distinguishes documented atrocities from exaggerated propaganda and does not equate the 1914 violence with later Nazi racial extermination. Force numbers, casualty totals, village incidents, causal claims about British intervention, and counterfactual claims about delay remain source-scoped.
