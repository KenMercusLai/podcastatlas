# Hyde Parker

## Overview

Sir Hyde Parker was the overall British fleet commander during the 1801 [[BattleOfCopenhagen1801|Battle of Copenhagen]], with [[HoratioNelson]] leading the attack.

## Current Profile

Parker's profile is defined by caution before battle and the limits of command visibility during it. In March 1801 he commanded the Baltic fleet while Nelson, impatient to escape domestic crisis and return to action, served as deputy. Parker delayed at Yarmouth and hesitated before Copenhagen's formidable shallow-water defenses; after Nelson pressed for immediate action, Parker approved the attack and required him to lead it personally.

During battle Parker observed from farther from the main action as British ships grounded and losses mounted, then hoisted Signal 39 to discontinue. The source presents the order as a judgment intended to give Nelson a route out of a worsening battle, not simply as cowardice. Nelson did not repeat it, whereas [[EdwardRiou]] eventually obeyed and was killed during withdrawal. Parker therefore anchors a case in which a formal commander's caution and incomplete view, a subordinate's local judgment, and another subordinate's rank-based obligation produced divergent decisions.

## Key Characteristics

- Overall commander who delegated the Copenhagen attack to Nelson.
- Cautious planner who delayed departure and hesitated before Copenhagen's defenses.
- Distant observer whose view of the action differed from that of the ships engaged.
- Issuer of the disputed withdrawal signal.
- Example of how humane or cautious intent can produce dangerous ambiguity in battle.

## Qualifications

The bounded sources center Nelson and give limited access to Parker's reasoning. Descriptions of social delay and caution may be sharpened by the contrast with Nelson's impatience; the order's exact intent, Parker's information, how either delay or withdrawal would have unfolded, and later judgments of his command remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Extended Parker's profile from the disputed signal to his appointment, pre-battle caution, and delegation of the assault.
- Framed both delay and withdrawal through risk judgment, information limits, and delegated command.
