# The Oresteia

## Overview

The *Oresteia* is [[Aeschylus]]' 458 BCE tragic trilogy about [[Agamemnon]]'s murder, Orestes' retaliatory matricide, pursuit by the Furies, and trial at Athens.

## Current Profile

The episode reads the trilogy as a movement through competing claims that no private revenge can settle. [[Clytemnestra]] avenges Iphigenia by killing Agamemnon; Orestes obeys Apollo and avenges his father by killing his mother; the Furies then enforce the horror of matricide. Athena's court converts the sequence into public judgment, although the tied jury and divine deciding vote preserve the settlement's difficulty.

The trilogy also turns heroic myth into civic self-description. Athens appears able to contain inherited violence through institutions while still negotiating divine authority, kin obligation, gender, and the memory of war.

## Key Characteristics

- It presents revenge as morally intelligible but structurally self-renewing.
- It makes Clytemnestra a central agent rather than a marginal villain.
- It joins household, divine, and civic forms of justice.
- Its trial ending gives Athens a foundational place in the resolution.
- It adapts mythic history to address the political concerns of a later audience.

## Evidence

- Retaliatory sequence: [[458-helen-of-troy-a-family-of-blood-part-2-glt7255356131]] traces Iphigenia's sacrifice, Agamemnon's murder, and Orestes' matricide.
- Institutional ending: [[458-helen-of-troy-a-family-of-blood-part-2-glt7255356131]] describes Athena's court, the split jury, deciding vote, and acquittal.
- Civic interpretation: [[458-helen-of-troy-a-family-of-blood-part-2-glt7255356131]] reads the ending as authorizing Athenian democratic institutions through ancient myth.

## Qualifications

- “From revenge to law” is a useful synthesis, not a claim that divine force, hierarchy, or violence disappears.
- The episode summarizes the trilogy and does not capture every dramatic ambiguity or variant tradition.
- Its political interpretation is historically plausible but remains an interpretation rather than an explicit constitutional program.

## What Changed

- Established the trilogy as a model of retaliatory violence being redirected into difficult public adjudication.
