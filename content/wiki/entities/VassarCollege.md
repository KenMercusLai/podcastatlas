# Vassar College

## Overview

Vassar College is the private liberal-arts college used in [[food-fight-food-fight|Food Fight]] to examine the financial and competitive costs of expanding socioeconomic access.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Vassar as choosing financial aid, student diversity, teaching quality, and enrollment capacity over premium food, housing, and other visible amenities. Under [[CatherineHill]], its aid budget and Pell Grant share reportedly rose substantially, while the replacement of full-paying students with aid recipients reduced tuition revenue and increased reliance on the endowment.

That choice creates fragility rather than a simple moral victory. Vassar still needs affluent students through [[FullPayStudentCrossSubsidy]], yet competes for them against institutions able to spend more on the campus experience. Its position therefore grounds [[CollegeAccessAmenityCompetition]]: the sacrifices made to finance access can weaken the recruitment needed to sustain it.

## Key Characteristics

- Private liberal-arts college used as the episode's access-oriented comparison.
- Expanded financial aid and enrollment of Pell Grant recipients.
- Protects teaching quality and student capacity while limiting some amenity spending.
- Depends materially on full-paying students and endowment withdrawals.
- Treats socioeconomic diversity as part of educational quality rather than only a distributive goal.

## Evidence

### Access commitment

- [[food-fight-food-fight]] reports that Vassar's aid budget rose from about $25 million to roughly $60 million and its Pell Grant share reached about 23 percent.

### Financial dependence

- [[food-fight-food-fight]] reports that about 1,000 full- or near-full-paying students generated roughly $60 million of annual revenue and that losing even 50 would be damaging.

### Visible trade-offs

- [[food-fight-food-fight]] uses student criticism of food and administrators' comments about housing and facilities to show which sacrifices applicants can see.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to one argumentative podcast summary, not a complete institutional history or current assessment. Financial figures, aid shares, rankings, student complaints, budget constraints, and comparisons with Bowdoin remain episode-reported; dining quality does not independently measure educational quality, and the source does not audit every budget alternative.

## What Changed

- Established Vassar as a focused case of access spending financed through tuition cross-subsidy and endowment use.
- Made the recruitment feedback loop between fewer amenities and dependence on affluent students explicit.
