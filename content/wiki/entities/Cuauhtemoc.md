## Overview

Cuauhtemoc is the last ruler of the [[MexicaEmpire|Mexica Empire]], captured after Tenochtitlan's fall, tortured for information about gold, and later executed during [[HernanCortes|Hernan Cortes]]' Honduras expedition.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Cuauhtemoc as a defeated ruler subjected to exemplary colonial violence. Cortes's men burn his feet in an effort to find hidden gold; the reported image of his composure becomes part of his memory. Cortes later forces him to join the Honduras expedition and hangs him in February 1525 after receiving a report of an assassination plot.

The allegation remains unresolved because the sources are confused and contradictory. A final speech reported by Bernal Diaz and later claims about Cuauhtemoc's remains belong to his historical afterlife, not to an uncontested evidentiary record.

## Key Characteristics

- Final Mexica ruler associated with the last defense and aftermath of Tenochtitlan.
- Captive tortured for gold after military resistance had ended.
- Symbol of composure and dignity in later accounts of suffering.
- Political prisoner taken on Cortes's Honduras expedition.
- Executed on a conspiracy allegation whose factual basis remains uncertain.

## Evidence

- Torture after conquest: [[391-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-last-emperor-part-8-glt2984940365]] describes oil, fire, the search for gold, and the reported display of composure.
- Honduras captivity and execution: [[391-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-last-emperor-part-8-glt2984940365]] follows the disputed plot allegation and hanging in 1525.
- Contested afterlife: [[391-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-last-emperor-part-8-glt2984940365]] distinguishes Bernal Diaz's reported speech and a later grave claim from settled fact.

## Qualifications

The source cannot determine whether a plot against Cortes existed or was used as a pretext. The final speech is a transmitted literary account, and the identification of purported remains is treated as an elaborate hoax by one cited study. This page does not generalize from Cuauhtemoc's experience to every Indigenous response to conquest.
