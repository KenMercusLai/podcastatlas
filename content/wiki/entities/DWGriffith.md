# D. W. Griffith

## Overview

D. W. Griffith was the director of [[BirthOfANation|*The Birth of a Nation*]], a formally influential and commercially successful film that made [[ThomasDixonJr|Thomas Dixon Jr.'s]] racist Reconstruction mythology into national cinematic spectacle.

## Current Profile

The bounded episode holds Griffith's technical importance and political responsibility together. His 1915 adaptation helped establish feature-film narrative techniques while portraying the [[KuKluxKlan]] as heroic and Black citizenship as a threat. Its scale, White House exhibition, controversy, and protests by the NAACP made the film a major public event.

Griffith's relevance to the second Klan is not only attitudinal. The film amplified invented costumes and cross-burning imagery that [[WilliamJosephSimmons]] used in real initiation and public spectacle, illustrating a feedback path from entertainment into institutional practice.

## Key Characteristics

- Adapted Dixon's racist fiction into a landmark feature film.
- Combined cinematic innovation with white-supremacist historical distortion.
- Benefited from controversy and elite exhibition as well as mass commercial success.
- Provoked organized civil-rights opposition, including NAACP protest.
- Helped circulate symbols later adopted by the second Klan.

## Qualifications

The episode is not a complete account of Griffith's career, film technique, production, audience reception, or the censorship campaign. Technical influence does not make the film's politics incidental, while political condemnation does not by itself establish which innovations were uniquely Griffith's. The causal effect on Klan recruitment is presented as strong cultural influence, not an isolated experimental finding.

## What Changed

- Created Griffith as both a film innovator and a transmitter of white-supremacist mythology.
- Connected cinematic imagery to its later organizational adoption without reducing Klan growth to one film.
