# Wounded Knee Massacre

## Overview

The Wounded Knee Massacre was the 1890 killing of Big Foot's surrounded [[LakotaPeople|Lakota]] band by the U.S. Seventh Cavalry during a forced disarmament near Wounded Knee Creek.

## Current Profile

The sources present Big Foot's group as hungry, ill, frightened, and trying to reach Pine Ridge after [[SittingBull]]'s death. Nearly five hundred soldiers, including inexperienced Seventh Cavalry recruits and Hotchkiss guns, surrounded the group. During disarmament, soldiers struggled with Black Coyote, who was deaf, over his valued Winchester; an unclear discharge and shouted alarm led soldiers to open fire from positions encircling the camp.

The hosts classify the event as a massacre because women, children, boys at play, fleeing people, and many substantially disarmed refugees were killed, including by fire into a ravine. They also note that the circular firing pattern likely caused many U.S. casualties through friendly fire. [[AtrocityNarrativeReframing]] is central to the event's afterlife: early accounts and the Bureau of Indian Affairs used battle language, while General Nelson Miles condemned a criminal blunder and later memory increasingly centered Native victims.

Wounded Knee also became a climactic symbol of Native dispossession whose name gained moral force through later history writing and the 1973 occupation. [[MartyrdomHistoricalMemory]] captures that symbolic afterlife while preserving the difference between collective victims of mass violence and an individual who accepts death.

## Key Characteristics

- It began as a military containment and disarmament operation against a vulnerable refugee group.
- Fear, humiliation, revenge narratives, raw recruits, encirclement, and heavy weapons made a single discharge catastrophic.
- Civilian deaths and fire on fleeing people support the massacre classification.
- Friendly fire complicates simple casualty attribution without changing the asymmetry of the killing.
- Official exoneration and praise conflicted with contemporary military condemnation.
- Later remembrance shifted the dominant label and moral perspective from battle toward massacre.

## Evidence

- Preconditions and force imbalance: [[456-fall-of-the-sioux-the-massacre-at-wounded-knee-part-3-glt7996658277]] describes Big Foot's pneumonia, the group's deprivation, encirclement, Seventh Cavalry strength, and Hotchkiss guns.
- Trigger and killing: [[456-fall-of-the-sioux-the-massacre-at-wounded-knee-part-3-glt7996658277]] follows the struggle over Black Coyote's gun, the opening fire, friendly fire, the killing of children, and shooting into the ravine.
- Narrative conflict: [[456-fall-of-the-sioux-the-massacre-at-wounded-knee-part-3-glt7996658277]] contrasts early press and War Department praise with Miles's condemnation and later victim-centered memory.
- Corroboration and public memory: [[12-days-the-murder-of-thomas-becket-and-the-wounded-knee-massacre-glt4510547363]] repeats the disarmament trigger, women-and-children deaths, probable friendly fire, and battle-to-massacre shift, then connects Wounded Knee to later writing and the 1973 occupation.

## Qualifications

- The episode's casualty estimate is source-scoped; exact totals vary in historical accounts and require dedicated documentary comparison.
- The first accidental or contested gunshot explains the immediate trigger but does not by itself explain the scale or direction of the subsequent violence.
- Revenge motives attributed to Seventh Cavalry personnel are presented through context and survivor recollection, not as a uniform motive proven for every soldier.
- Calling the event a massacre does not deny that some Lakota fought back after shooting began or that U.S. soldiers died.
- Totals of roughly 300 Native people and 25 soldiers killed are source-scoped estimates rather than a resolution of disputed casualty counts.
- Calling Wounded Knee martyr-like describes its later symbolic use; it should not attribute a shared intention or chosen sacrifice to the people killed.

## What Changed

- Added Black Coyote's deafness as a communication factor in the immediate disarmament crisis.
- Strengthened corroboration for likely friendly fire and the shift from battle to massacre language.
- Added later historical writing and the 1973 occupation as carriers of Wounded Knee's symbolic power.
