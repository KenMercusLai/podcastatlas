# Lý Thường Kiệt / 李常杰

## Overview

Lý Thường Kiệt / 李常杰 was a Lý-dynasty Vietnamese commander whom the source presents as a court eunuch, an attacker of Champa and Song territory, and a continuing Vietnamese national hero.

## Current Profile

[[4-dongxifang-de-taijian-cong-supeisheng-dao-naersaisi-ltz5ibhvlh7g2ss-udowhi9ejho4|怪东西 episode 4]] uses Lý Thường Kiệt as one of its two strongest military counterexamples to the stereotype that eunuchs belonged only to domestic or ceremonial palace service. In the episode's account, he campaigned south against Champa and later used a preemptive strategy in the Song-Vietnam war, taking Qinzhou, Lianzhou, and Yongzhou before becoming a durable national-memory figure.

His profile therefore supports a bounded institutional claim: bodily or court status did not determine command competence. Military outcome still depended on strategy, forces, state backing, geography, and later historical memory, the same separation made by the episode's comparison with [[Narses]].

## Key Characteristics

- Lý-dynasty Vietnamese court and military figure identified by the source as a eunuch.
- Commander associated with campaigns against Champa.
- Advocate and executor of a preemptive offensive into Song territory.
- Conqueror, in the episode's account, of Qinzhou, Lianzhou, and Yongzhou.
- Later national hero whose reputation exceeds a simple palace-service identity.

## Evidence

- Cross-border command - [[4-dongxifang-de-taijian-cong-supeisheng-dao-naersaisi-ltz5ibhvlh7g2ss-udowhi9ejho4]] attributes southern and northern campaigns to Lý Thường Kiệt.
- Strategic agency - [[4-dongxifang-de-taijian-cong-supeisheng-dao-naersaisi-ltz5ibhvlh7g2ss-udowhi9ejho4]] presents the Song-border attack as deliberate preemption rather than passive palace service.
- Historical memory - [[4-dongxifang-de-taijian-cong-supeisheng-dao-naersaisi-ltz5ibhvlh7g2ss-udowhi9ejho4]] says he remains a Vietnamese national hero.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to one comparative podcast summary. Exact dates, personal status, titles, campaign sequence, force levels, territorial effects, Song and Vietnamese interpretations, and the formation of national-hero memory require specialist verification; the comparison with Narses concerns role range, not equivalent states or wars.
