# Yorktown

## Overview

Yorktown is the 1781 Virginia campaign and siege where American and French forces trapped Cornwallis and made continued British recovery of the thirteen colonies politically untenable.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Yorktown as the convergence of [[GeorgeWashington|George Washington]]'s preserved army, French land support, and decisive French naval control. Cornwallis's earlier victories did not protect an exposed coastal position once relief by sea failed. His October 19 surrender did not instantly end all imperial fighting, but it convinced British leaders that the North American war could not be recovered on acceptable terms.

## Key Characteristics

- Culmination of Britain's southern strategy in Virginia.
- Joint American-French encirclement rather than a solely American victory.
- Naval blockade that prevented British relief or evacuation.
- Surrender driven by disease, hunger, siege pressure, and strategic isolation.
- Decisive political blow even though Britain retained New York and Charleston and the wider war continued.
- Memory site exposing the contradiction between independence and re-enslavement.

## Evidence

- Encirclement: [[350-the-triumph-of-george-washington-part-4-glt1619660676]] says Washington and French forces trapped Cornwallis while the French fleet blocked relief by sea.
- Surrender conditions: [[350-the-triumph-of-george-washington-part-4-glt1619660676]] identifies disease, hunger, blockade, and siege as making Cornwallis's position untenable before the October 19, 1781 surrender.
- Strategic effect: [[350-the-triumph-of-george-washington-part-4-glt1619660676]] says Britain still held major ports but London treated the defeat as unrecoverable.
- Moral aftermath: [[350-the-triumph-of-george-washington-part-4-glt1619660676]] says Washington soon ordered people who had escaped slavery to join Britain rounded up and returned to enslavers.

## Qualifications

The source gives a strategic overview rather than a full operational history. It does not detail troop numbers, siege engineering, the Chesapeake naval battle, unit-level action, or all command decisions, and formal peace followed nearly two years later.
