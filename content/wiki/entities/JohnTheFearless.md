# John the Fearless

## Overview

John the Fearless is the Burgundian duke whose pressure on the Armagnacs aided English expansion and whose murder at Montereau transformed the factional balance.

## Current Profile

John's lands in Burgundy and the Low Countries, including Flanders, gave him political weight and an interest in trade with England. He competed with [[LouisOfOrleans|Louis of Orléans]] for influence during [[CharlesVIOfFrance|Charles VI's]] incapacity, arranged Louis's 1407 murder, and publicly justified it as tyrannicide. Royal pardon did not settle the feud; Parisian mobilization, Armagnac reaction, and alternating negotiations with England made his alignment tactical and unstable.

John and his son Philip did not join the main French force approaching Agincourt, although Burgundian relatives and retainers did. The source suggests that an Armagnac defeat could have benefited him, but preserves this as inferred motive rather than settled explanation.

John later maneuvered between negotiation with [[HenryVOfEngland|Henry V]] and reconciliation with the Dauphin. His forces captured towns and Paris, indirectly giving Henry strategic room in [[Normandy]] while intensifying French civil violence.

At Montereau, John was killed by the Dauphin's servants during a controlled meeting. Regardless of whether misunderstanding or treachery caused the attack, blame attached to the Dauphin and moved John's son [[PhilipTheGood|Philip the Good]] toward England.

## Key Characteristics

- Burgundian faction leader competing with the Armagnacs for control of France.
- Prince whose territorial and commercial position connected Burgundy, Flanders, France, and English wool interests.
- Organizer and defender of Louis of Orléans's murder.
- Major prince absent from Agincourt despite participation by some Burgundian kin and retainers.
- Conditional interlocutor and captor of Paris whose shifting posture reduced pressure on Henry's invasion.
- Would-be reconciler killed during negotiations with the Dauphin.
- Posthumous cause of a stronger Anglo-Burgundian alignment.

## Qualifications

The sources do not settle every motive behind Louis's murder, why John stayed away from Agincourt, or whether his later killing resulted from a planned betrayal or a confrontation triggered by his movement toward his sword. His relationship with England was tactical and shifting rather than a simple permanent alliance, and the source's account of the wool trade does not reduce all Burgundian policy to commerce.
