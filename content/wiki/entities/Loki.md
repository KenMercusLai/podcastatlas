# Loki / 洛基

## Overview

Loki / 洛基 is a Norse trickster and threat figure whose surviving mythic relationships, destructive role, comic ingenuity, and modern superhero rewriting make him a compact case of changing divine identity.

## Current Profile

[[ep269-cong-manwei-yuzhou-dao-aodesai-weihe-zongyao-fanpai-yingxiong-shishi-ltklotheqpup6oamyflfzpxtipsw]] distinguishes Edda material from the [[MarvelCinematicUniverse|漫威宇宙]]. Marvel's sibling relationship with [[Thor]] simplifies older kinship in which Loki belongs closer to [[Odin]]'s sworn-brother generation, showing how modern media can preserve a name and trickster function while revising family, motive, and audience sympathy.

The older threat profile makes Loki father of Hel, Fenrir, Jormungandr, and Sleipnir, an agent in Balder's death, and a punished figure beneath serpent venom. Yet his mischief and ingenuity also make Norse stories move. The surprise tournament loss to Anubis measures modern audience reception, including a jokingly proposed anti-Marvel reaction, rather than historical religious weight [[58-the-world-cup-of-gods-part-1-glt8884399326]].

## Key Characteristics

- Trickster whose ingenuity can solve crises while producing new danger.
- Parent of destructive, underworld, world-serpent, and exceptional-horse figures in the episode's account.
- Agent in Balder's death and punished enemy within the Ragnarok sequence.
- Mythic figure whose kinship and moral framing change substantially in Marvel adaptation.
- Entertaining narrative catalyst as well as malignant threat.

## Qualifications

Both sources are synthetic podcast discussions rather than direct editions of Norse texts. The Eddas are already layered and Christian-era witnesses, individual myths have variant forms, and “god,” “trickster,” or “villain” can conceal differences among cult, story, and later reception. Tournament voting and proposed voter motives are playful evidence only.

## What Changed

- Added Loki's destructive family, Balder role, punishment, and narrative-catalyst function.
- Reframed modern popularity and backlash as reception history rather than continuity of worship.
