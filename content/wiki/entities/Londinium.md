# Londinium

## Overview

Londinium was the Roman settlement at modern [[London]], established after the AD 43 conquest at a strategically useful Thames crossing and developed into a port, road hub, commercial center, military node, and provincial capital of [[RomanBritain]].

## Current Profile

The city's initial advantage was geographic rather than monumental. The lowest practical bridge point on the Thames joined river access to roads and continental movement; a bridge probably existed by AD 47, and an IOU dated 8 January AD 57 records an early commercial community. The episode says Londinium likely replaced Colchester as provincial capital by the end of the first century.

The [[BoudiccanRevolt]] burned the settlement around AD 60, but rebuilding produced an increasingly substantial civic landscape. A large forum and basilica supported trade, assembly, law, and administration; a fort and late-second-century wall expressed military capacity and capital status; the Walbrook [[Mithras|Mithraeum]] shows a later religious and associational layer. The port, roads, writing tablets, imported people, and preserved everyday objects present the city as a connected colonial and commercial center rather than a purely Italian enclave.

Its survival is fragmentary. Wet Walbrook deposits preserved organic objects, but later fires, demolition, stone reuse, abandonment, and rebuilding obscured the Roman plan. The late city appears to have contracted before Roman rule ended, and modern London's street pattern largely reflects later development. Roman Londinium is consequently recovered through archaeology beneath offices, markets, Guildhall, and a car park rather than through an intact monumental center.

## Key Characteristics

- River crossing and port whose geography concentrated roads, trade, military access, and administration.
- Early literate commercial settlement documented by first-century writing tablets.
- Provincial center rebuilt after destruction during Boudicca's revolt.
- Civic city with a forum, basilica, fort, wall, port, temples, and mixed population.
- Archaeological environment where waterlogging preserved rare organic material.
- Urban landscape now dispersed beneath later buildings and a non-Roman street plan.
- Late Roman city whose declining population and built intensity preceded post-Roman abandonment and westward settlement.

## Qualifications

The page derives from a walking-tour episode rather than a complete archaeological history. A bridge by AD 47, the date at which Londinium became capital, causes of individual fires, population composition, and the pace of late decline remain approximate or interpretive. The Number One Poultry skulls cannot securely identify victims or trophy-taking, the London Stone cannot be securely tied to the governor's headquarters, and burial within the walls does not prove that the unknown late Roman girl was Christian.
