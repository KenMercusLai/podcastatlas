# Battle of Loos

## Overview

The Battle of Loos is the September 1915 Western Front offensive that [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]] uses to show the tactical failure of British gas, the continuing deadlock of trench warfare, and the private grief attached to [[JohnKipling]]'s death.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Loos as a cautionary case in technological escalation. British forces used thousands of chlorine cylinders despite warnings about the wind, and much of the gas blew back into their own lines. Masks fogged, conditions were poor, and the battle ended with heavy casualties for both sides without solving the broader Western Front problem.

Loos also becomes a memory case through the Kipling family. [[RudyardKipling]] had used connections to help his short-sighted son [[JohnKipling]] gain a commission after rejection. John went missing on the second day of Loos after losing his glasses in the mud, and the episode frames Rudyard's later grief as a private version of public patriotic pressure.

## Key Characteristics

- British chlorine-gas offensive south of Lille in 1915.
- Example of new technology failing under bad wind, poor protection, and rigid planning.
- High-casualty battle that did not break Western Front stalemate.
- Personal grief case through John Kipling's death and Rudyard Kipling's responsibility.
- Links patriotic recruitment culture to family-level loss.

## Evidence

- Gas failure: [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]] records the British use of 5,000 chlorine cylinders, warnings about wind, gas blowing back, and fogged masks.
- Casualties: [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]] gives about 60,000 British and 25,000 German casualties.
- Kipling case: [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]] follows John Kipling's commission, disappearance, and Rudyard Kipling's later grief.
