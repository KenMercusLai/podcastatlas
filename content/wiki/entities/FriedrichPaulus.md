# Friedrich Paulus

## Overview

Friedrich Paulus was the German Sixth Army commander at the [[BattleOfStalingrad]], where he remained inside the encircled pocket under Hitler's hold order and surrendered from the Univermag headquarters in January 1943.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Paulus less as an autonomous battlefield hero or villain than as a commander operating under direct political constraint. After [[OperationUranus]] closed the pocket, he relayed Soviet surrender offers to headquarters but did not receive permission to accept them or break out. The promotion to field marshal near the end was understood as pressure to die rather than surrender, because no German field marshal had previously been captured alive.

Paulus nevertheless surrendered. Soviet negotiator Fyodor Lashkin reportedly found him gaunt, unshaven, and diminished amid the dead and dying at the Univermag. In captivity, he later turned against National Socialism, appeared as a witness at the Nuremberg trials, and lived in East Germany.

## Key Characteristics

- Commander of the German Sixth Army during the Stalingrad campaign.
- Remained in the pocket after Hitler rejected withdrawal and surrender.
- Depended on an airlift and relief effort that failed to sustain or reach the army.
- Forwarded Soviet surrender terms while radio communication with headquarters continued.
- Interpreted promotion to field marshal as a suicide signal but did not comply.
- Surrendered at the Univermag and later opposed National Socialism in Soviet custody.

## Qualifications

This source does not provide a full command biography or assess Paulus's earlier role in planning and conducting the German campaign. Political constraint does not remove his responsibility as Sixth Army commander or the invading army's participation in Nazi war and occupation. His inner interpretation of the promotion, exact surrender dialogue, condition, and post-capture motives remain episode-attributed.

## What Changed

- Established Paulus as a constrained but responsible commander whose refusal of the implied suicide expectation became part of Stalingrad's surrender narrative.
