## Overview

施丹 / Shi Dan is synthesized as a Western Han elder official whose profile now has five layers: early-reign restraining remonstrance, learned ritual opposition, confidential-memorial exposure, post-title-settlement punishment, and later retrospective restoration. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-568-hewu-duanjian-gudai-zheqi-panjue-zhide-dangdai-xueyixue-ljd2ojjmsla46no-9m2q802syxlp|Hanji 568]] supplies the first current layer: after [[HeWuWesternHan|何武]] is removed, Shi Dan becomes Da Sikong and repeatedly warns [[HanAidi|汉哀帝]] not to rush personnel, kinship, and policy changes during the mourning and succession-settling period. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-575-gudai-dachen-xiemi-xiachang-you-duo-can-lkb_o6qht3sinbtms5gh1rixizm6|Hanji 575]] then shows him opposing Aidi's elevation of the Dingtao parental line because imperial ritual order cannot have two supreme centers, before he becomes removable after a secret memorial leaks and his currency advice appears inconsistent. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-579-zoujin-kexue-guyao-shi-yao-ma-lof7pvrnlzv2lmbzjshbx7vi2c2n|Hanji 579]] and [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-580-huigu-sizhou-shuishi-nide-kaoshan-liid3tymhojmajkyeqoq6vryprhz|Hanji 580]] preserve the later attack on his title and fief after Aidi accepts the Dingtao title settlement. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-626-waren-fenmu-wangmang-jing-ruci-sangxinbingkuang-lotwk6rbtl5pvbrnlfrysu5ol6rz|Hanji 626]] then shows [[WangMang|王莽]] recoding the same anti-Fu stance as merit after the posthumous prosecution of [[FuTaihouWesternHan|傅太后]] and [[DingJiWesternHan|丁姬]].

## Current Profile

Hanji 568 shows Shi Dan as a restraining adviser at the beginning of Aidi's reign. After He Wu is removed and Shi Dan is appointed Da Sikong, he warns that ancient rulers in mourning delegated government and did not quickly change the former ruler's way. His criticism targets Aidi's early elevation of Ding and Fu relatives, removal of prior officials, frequent policy changes, and failure to pause before rearranging the power field. The same source makes Shi Dan's advice morally earnest but politically fraught: the host says his repeated direct memorials are moving, while also warning that good intentions can produce bad consequences in factional politics.

Hanji 575 shows the substance of the later ritual opposition that later sources only summarize. Shi Dan argues that if Aidi has become Chengdi's successor and inherited the ancestral temple, he cannot separately honor the Dingtao birth-parent line with officials, vehicles, garments, or a capital temple in ways that rival the main imperial hierarchy. The issue is not whether Aidi may feel filial affection, but whether that affection can rewrite [[AdoptiveLineageRitualPolitics|adoptive-lineage ritual obligation]].

The same source shows why principled ritual argument is not enough to protect him. Aidi becomes angry after the memorial. Shi Dan then gives and forgets an earlier answer on proposed currency reform, which lets opponents call him inconsistent. More seriously, he has a subordinate write a confidential memorial, the subordinate copies and circulates it, and Ding/Fu-linked accusers argue that a sealed submission to the emperor has effectively reached common people. The Tingwei escalates the leak into "great irreverence"; Shen Xian, Que Qin, and Tang Lin defend Shi Dan as a rare learned elder and former imperial teacher, but Aidi removes him and later grants only guannei marquis status after Tang Lin's plea.

Hanji 579 gives the later title-settlement sequel. Zhu Bo and Zhao Xuan argue that Dong Hong had originally proposed changing the titles, that Shi Dan's earlier impeachment suppressed the proposal and harmed Aidi's filial duty, and that Shi Dan should lose title and fief now that Dong Hong has been restored. Aidi approves. Hanji 580 then treats Shi Dan's title-and-fief removal as the preceding case before Zhu Bo and Zhao Xuan turn the same punitive logic against Wang Mang.

Hanji 626 later says Shi Dan had previously opposed Fu Taihou and had been removed from office and stripped of ducal standing. After Wang Mang's 5 CE campaign against Fu Taihou and Ding Ji, Shi Dan is summoned to Chang'an, made a guannei marquis, restored to his former income, later made Yiyang marquis, and then dies soon afterward.

Shi Dan therefore functions both as a confidential-remonstrance casualty and as a posthumous-merit-vindication case. His later reward does not come from a new achievement; it comes from Wang Mang recoding an earlier stance as correct once the court officially condemns Fu Taihou's old status.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han elder official and former imperial teacher whose learning does not protect him from court punishment.
- Early Aidi-era Da Sikong who repeatedly advises restraint during mourning and succession settlement.
- Ritual-order opponent of Aidi's attempt to elevate the Dingtao parental line into near-imperial status.
- Confidential-memorial casualty whose subordinate-copy leak becomes punishable as "great irreverence."
- Target of Zhu Bo and Zhao Xuan's post-title-settlement cleanup after he had opposed Dong Hong's proposal.
- Example of Wang Mang turning posthumous ritual correction into living political sorting.
- Restored and re-titled shortly before his death, illustrating how later power can reverse memory while sincere remonstrance remains politically risky.

## Qualifications

Hanji 568 supplies Shi Dan's early Da Sikong appointment, repeated advice, and the host's good-intentions warning, but not the complete text of every memorial, Aidi's full response, or Shi Dan's later tactical calculations. Hanji 575 supplies Shi Dan's ritual argument, currency-advice inconsistency, leaked-memorial case, removal, and partial mercy, but not the complete received text of his memorials, the subordinate's identity, the full legal file, or Tang Lin's broader career. Hanji 579 gives the immediate Zhu Bo/Zhao Xuan accusation but not Dong Hong's full original memorial, Shi Dan's full impeachment text, or the complete legal formula for reducing him. Hanji 580 only names Shi Dan as the immediately preceding punishment case before the Wang Mang attack. Hanji 626 does not reconstruct the full earlier debate in which Shi Dan opposed Fu Taihou. The page therefore treats him as source-scoped evidence for early restraint advice, ritual opposition, secrecy failure, removal, and later retrospective reward, not as a complete biography or independent judgment on every stage of the dispute.
