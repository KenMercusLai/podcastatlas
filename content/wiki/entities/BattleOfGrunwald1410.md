# Battle of Grunwald / Tannenberg (1410)

## Overview

The Battle of Grunwald, also discussed as Tannenberg in the bounded source, was the 1410 victory of a Polish-Lithuanian coalition over the [[TeutonicOrder|Teutonic Order]].

## Current Profile

The coalition formed through the Polish-Lithuanian dynastic and confessional settlement around Jogaila and Jadwiga and included forces from a politically and ethnically varied Lithuanian realm. The battle killed the Teutonic grand master and many senior members, making it a severe institutional and military shock.

Victory did not end the war or the order. The coalition failed to capture [[MalborkCastle|Malbork]], peace followed, and the order's decline unfolded through later wars, debt, mercenary dependence, internal opposition, and territorial reorganization. Sienkiewicz later made the battle a climax of Polish national memory, giving the event a modern afterlife distinct from its medieval coalition politics.

## Key Characteristics

- Major Polish-Lithuanian coalition victory over the Teutonic Order.
- Battle involving a politically and ethnically mixed force rather than two homogeneous modern nations.
- Severe leadership loss for the order.
- Tactical and symbolic victory that did not secure Malbork or immediately destroy the opposing state.
- Later anchor of Polish literary and national memory.

## Qualifications

Force composition, casualty totals, tactical sequence, names, and the precise relationship between battle and later decline require specialist verification. “Tannenberg” must not be confused with [[BattleOfTannenberg1914|the 1914 battle]], whose naming carried its own politics of historical memory.

## What Changed

- Established the 1410 battle as a distinct canonical event.
- Separated immediate military victory from delayed institutional decline.
- Added the gap between a mixed medieval coalition and later national memory.
