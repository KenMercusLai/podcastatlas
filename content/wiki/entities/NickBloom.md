# Nick Bloom

## Overview
Nick Bloom is a [[StanfordUniversity]] economist whose work-from-home research appears in the wiki's workplace-evidence and family-formation branches.

## Current Profile
Bloom's workplace research is job- and outcome-specific: some work benefits from in-person presence, many roles fit hybrid arrangements, and some can produce more remotely. The randomized [[Ctrip]] experiment in [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b9cf0c3dfc0e043244126a9eac2eb0f8-49651044-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b9cf0c3dfc0e043244126a9eac2eb0f8-49651044]] grounds that view in higher call-center output, lower attrition, lower facility cost, isolation, voluntary return to the office, and a promotion penalty. It also separates emergency pandemic conditions from designed hybrid work and treats choice, schedule intensity, home space, connectivity, and task coordination as part of the intervention.

In evidence-resistant management debates, listening and respect are necessary for persuading executives whose office-formed intuitions conflict with research. Across both workplace sources, Bloom resists using either physical presence or one productivity statistic as a complete measure of organizational success. [[would-you-trust-an-economist-with-your-economy]]

The household branch asks whether flexibility changes family formation. Bloom reports in [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-58f17fc3725ef3102277ae962324f7dd-59875813-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-58f17fc3725ef3102277ae962324f7dd-59875813]] that couples in which both partners work remotely at least weekly have larger families on average, especially through additional children, and estimates a large post-pandemic U.S. birth contribution. He also acknowledges conflicting findings and the possibility that family-oriented people select remote-compatible jobs.

## Key Characteristics
- Segments remote-work evidence by job, workflow, and outcome.
- Treats productivity, retention, promotion, isolation, coordination, and preference as distinct outcomes.
- Combines data with listening in evidence-resistant management debates.
- Studies workplace flexibility as a possible family-logistics mechanism.
- Keeps association, selection, and causation distinct when evidence conflicts.

## Evidence
- Job heterogeneity and persuasion: [[would-you-trust-an-economist-with-your-economy]] presents Bloom's remote, hybrid, and in-person distinctions and his trust-repair approach.
- Randomized workplace experiment: [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b9cf0c3dfc0e043244126a9eac2eb0f8-49651044-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b9cf0c3dfc0e043244126a9eac2eb0f8-49651044]] reports higher Ctrip call-center output, lower quits and costs, substantial return-to-office choice, and lower promotion rates after performance adjustment.
- Boundary conditions: [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b9cf0c3dfc0e043244126a9eac2eb0f8-49651044-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b9cf0c3dfc0e043244126a9eac2eb0f8-49651044]] distinguishes routine individual tasks from creative or large-team coordination and documents unequal home-space and connectivity access.
- Fertility association: [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-58f17fc3725ef3102277ae962324f7dd-59875813-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-58f17fc3725ef3102277ae962324f7dd-59875813]] reports Bloom's international couple-level association and U.S. estimate.
- Conflicting result: [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-58f17fc3725ef3102277ae962324f7dd-59875813-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-58f17fc3725ef3102277ae962324f7dd-59875813]] cites a working paper finding a slight decrease among married, college-educated women working from home.

## Qualifications
The wiki's evidence comes from three podcast summaries rather than the underlying studies. The Ctrip result is unusually strong workplace evidence but still concerns volunteers doing measurable call-center work within one company; it does not establish the same effect for creative, collaborative, or involuntary home work. Productivity, promotion, attrition, fertility effect sizes, and causal claims remain population- and study-specific, and remote-compatible job selection may account for part of the observed fertility association.

## What Changed
- Added randomized evidence separating output and retention gains from isolation, promotion, and coordination costs.
- Clarified that hybrid design includes worker choice, schedule intensity, workspace, connectivity, and task fit.
