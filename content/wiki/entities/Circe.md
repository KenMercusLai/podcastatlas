# Circe

## Overview

Circe is the island enchantress who turns Odysseus' men into pigs, delays the voyage for a year, and then sends [[Odysseus]] toward the underworld.

## Current Profile

In [[687-the-odyssey-hero-of-the-trojan-horse-part-1-glt2393125423]], Circe moves the Odyssey from hostile-coast danger into magic and suspended time. She drugs a scouting party, transforms the men into pigs while their minds remain human, is overcome only after Odysseus receives divine help from Hermes, and later becomes a guide who tells him he must consult [[Tiresias]] before going home.

## Key Characteristics

- Circe combines threat, erotic delay, restoration, and guidance rather than serving only as a monster.
- Her pig transformation preserves human consciousness inside animal form, making altered embodiment part of the danger.
- Odysseus' victory over her depends on divine assistance as well as courage.
- A year on her island becomes another test of [[HomericNostos]] because the crew must remind Odysseus that home is still waiting.
- Her final advice turns her from obstacle into navigator for the underworld, Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis, and Helios sequence.

## Evidence

- Enchantment threat: [[687-the-odyssey-hero-of-the-trojan-horse-part-1-glt2393125423]] says Circe drugs the scouts and turns them into pigs with human minds.
- Divine-aided victory: [[687-the-odyssey-hero-of-the-trojan-horse-part-1-glt2393125423]] has Hermes give Odysseus moly so Circe's magic cannot overpower him.
- Delay and reminder: [[687-the-odyssey-hero-of-the-trojan-horse-part-1-glt2393125423]] says Odysseus and the crew remain with Circe for a year before the men remind him of Ithaca.
- Navigation role: [[687-the-odyssey-hero-of-the-trojan-horse-part-1-glt2393125423]] has Circe send Odysseus to Tiresias and later warn him about the Sirens, Scylla, Charybdis, and Helios' cattle.

## Qualifications

The source notes that Homer treats Odysseus' sexual relationship with Circe as a nonissue for a heroic man. This page records that as source-scoped ancient-world framing rather than a modern moral endorsement.
