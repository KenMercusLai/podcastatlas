# Aurangzeb

## Overview

Aurangzeb was a Mughal prince and emperor who prevailed in the succession war triggered by [[ShahJahan]]'s illness in 1657. The current bounded profile joins his coercive seizure of the throne and treatment of his father to a later source's argument that his territorial overexpansion and war spending weakened the [[MughalEmpire|Mughal Empire]] after his death.

## Current Profile

[[362-the-taj-mahal-love-and-death-glt2445608477]] contrasts Aurangzeb with Dara Shukoh, Shah Jahan's preferred heir, whom the episode characterizes as liberal, multicultural, and Sufi. Aurangzeb's military and political victory included Dara's humiliation and execution and the removal of rival brothers.

After taking power, Aurangzeb imprisoned Shah Jahan at Agra Fort for eight years. When his father died, Shah Jahan was buried beside [[MumtazMahal]] in the [[TajMahal]] without the grand procession Jahanara reportedly wanted. Aurangzeb therefore enters this knowledge branch primarily as the actor who transformed Shah Jahan's final years and the monument's burial history.

[[75-the-east-india-company-glt7809915873]] extends the profile from succession to imperial capacity. Dalrymple argues that Aurangzeb expanded too far and spent too heavily on war, leaving the posthumous empire vulnerable as Maratha, Jat, and Sikh power rose. The episode uses that weakening, followed by Nadir Shah's invasion, to explain the fragmented landscape in which European trading companies could acquire political power.

## Key Characteristics

- Mughal prince who converted an imperial illness into a successful succession bid.
- Military and political victor over his brothers, including Dara Shukoh.
- Ruler who had Dara executed and confined Shah Jahan at Agra Fort.
- Successor whose decisions shaped Shah Jahan's death and burial at the Taj Mahal.
- Expansionist ruler whose wars are presented as straining imperial finances and reach.
- Predecessor to a fragmentation process that created openings for regional powers and European companies.

## Evidence

- Succession victory: [[362-the-taj-mahal-love-and-death-glt2445608477]] describes the struggle after Shah Jahan's 1657 illness and Aurangzeb's defeat of his brothers.
- Coercive consolidation: [[362-the-taj-mahal-love-and-death-glt2445608477]] reports Dara's humiliation and execution and Shah Jahan's eight-year confinement.
- Burial consequence: [[362-the-taj-mahal-love-and-death-glt2445608477]] says Shah Jahan was buried beside Mumtaz without the requested grand procession.
- Imperial overreach: [[75-the-east-india-company-glt7809915873]] attributes later Mughal weakening partly to Aurangzeb's overexpansion and war expenditure.
- Posthumous consequence: [[75-the-east-india-company-glt7809915873]] connects that weakening to rising Maratha, Jat, and Sikh power and, after Nadir Shah's invasion, to Company opportunity.

## Qualifications

The sources supply two narrow interpretive slices, not a full account of Aurangzeb's long reign, administration, campaigns, regional politics, patronage, or religious policy. The contrast with Dara and the overextension thesis are episode characterizations; neither should be expanded into a complete moral or monocausal judgment about Mughal decline.
