# Miletus

## Overview

Miletus appears in the wiki as the wealthy Ionian city whose elite instability, failed expansion politics, and destruction anchor the [[IonianRevolt]].

## Current Profile

The episode presents Miletus as rich, sophisticated, and intellectually important, but politically fragile under Persian-backed tyranny. [[Histias]] is removed to Darius's court, [[Aristagoras]] takes over, and local social pressure plus the failed Naxos expedition make revolt a way out of immediate danger.

Miletus also becomes the revolt's trauma point. After the Ionian fleet is destroyed near Lade in 494 BC, the city is stormed and its people are killed, violated, enslaved, castrated, or deported in the episode's account. A later Athenian tragedy about the city's fall is said to upset the audience so deeply that the playwright is fined.

More than a century later, [[dchha74-mania-for-subjugation-iv-dchha74-mania-for-subjugation-iv]] presents the city resisting [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]] despite his liberation rhetoric. His smaller fleet reaches the harbor islands first and prevents the larger Persian fleet from helping effectively. Alexander rejects neutrality and a naval battle, takes the city by land, kills defenders, spares citizens, and recruits a group of mercenaries whose courage impresses him. The episode uses the siege to show that local faction, survival, and strategic interest do not reduce to a simple Greek-versus-Persian alignment.

## Key Characteristics

- Miletus is treated as the leading Ionian city in wealth, sophistication, and intellectual importance.
- Persian-backed tyranny makes its local politics dependent on imperial favor.
- Aristagoras's failed Naxos scheme turns Miletus into the revolt's launch point.
- The city's destruction becomes a warning to Athens about Persian punishment.
- Athenian emotional reaction to Miletus's fall links Ionian suffering to mainland Greek fear.
- Later resistance to Alexander exposes the limits of a simple liberation narrative.
- Harbor control lets a smaller fleet support a land siege without accepting a full naval battle.

## Qualifications

The page records two podcast reconstructions of Miletus across different campaigns. It does not independently verify casualty details, sexual violence claims, deportation scale, the full literary context of the Athenian tragedy, the later siege sequence, or Alexander's motives in sparing citizens and recruiting mercenaries. “Liberation” remains a political claim rather than a neutral description of local allegiance.

## What Changed

- Extended Miletus from the Ionian Revolt to its later resistance against Alexander.
- Added harbor control and avoided naval battle as the mechanism behind the Macedonian siege.
- Qualified the Greek-liberation frame through local faction and survival politics.
