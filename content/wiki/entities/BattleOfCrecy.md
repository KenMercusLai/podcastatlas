# Battle of Crécy

## Overview

The Battle of Crécy was Edward III's 26 August 1346 victory over [[PhilipVIOfFrance|Philip VI]], where an outnumbered English army used selected ground, archery, obstacles, and disciplined deployment to defeat repeated French attacks.

## Current Profile

After crossing the Seine and Somme during a destructive Normandy campaign, [[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]] stopped near the Forest of Crécy and organized three divisions. [[EdwardTheBlackPrince|Edward of Woodstock]] held the forward line, another division supported him, and the king remained in reserve. Archers and field cannon occupied the wings, while trenches, spikes, and wagons strengthened the position.

The episode explains the French defeat as a cascading coordination failure. Rain, missing shields, and English range weakened Genoese crossbowmen; French cavalry then rode through their retreat and attacked into dense missile fire and obstacles. Horses, knights, and successive formations accumulated in disorder, turning chivalric aggression into a killing field.

The prince's exposed command and Edward's reported instruction that he should “win his spurs” became part of heroic royal memory. The episode nonetheless makes the wider significance tactical: projectile weapons, preparation, and command discipline outweighed numbers and conventional mounted prestige.

## Key Characteristics

- English battle fought from ground selected and prepared in advance.
- Integrated use of archers, men-at-arms, obstacles, reserve divisions, and early field cannon.
- Genoese crossbow failure worsened by rain, missing shields, range disadvantage, and friendly hostility.
- Repeated French cavalry attacks losing coherence against the English position.
- Foundational episode in the Black Prince's chivalric reputation.
- Tactical victory followed by the siege of Calais rather than immediate conquest of France.
