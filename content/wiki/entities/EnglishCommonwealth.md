# English Commonwealth

## Overview

The English Commonwealth was the crownless regime created after the 1649 execution of [[CharlesIOfEngland|Charles I]], later reshaped under [[OliverCromwell]]'s Protectorate and ended by the 1660 Restoration.

## Current Profile

The episode presents the Commonwealth as a sequence of improvised settlements rather than one settled constitution. Monarchy and the House of Lords were abolished, but authority remained divided between a purged House of Commons and the army that had made the rupture possible. Cromwell's personal authority temporarily contained that conflict without resolving representation, religious government, succession, or civilian control.

The regime mixed experiment with coercion. It permitted some radical and religious openings, built administrative and military capacity, and advanced union and parliamentary claims, yet also taxed heavily, expelled legislators, controlled worship, reconquered Ireland, and enabled dispossession. Its institutions collapsed after Cromwell's death, but parts of its political and state-building inheritance outlived it.

## Key Characteristics

- Republic formed after regicide without a fully agreed successor constitution.
- Depended on an unstable alliance between Parliament and the army.
- Passed through Commonwealth and Protectorate forms under changing leadership.
- Combined religious and political experimentation with coercive government.
- Expanded military, administrative, and overseas state capacity.
- Failed to establish durable succession or shared constitutional legitimacy.

- Authority and coercion: [[333-the-republic-of-britain-life-under-cromwell-glt3360112790]] traces Parliament–army conflict, Cromwell's expulsions, taxation, worship policy, and military rule.
- Collapse and legacy: [[333-the-republic-of-britain-life-under-cromwell-glt3360112790]] follows succession failure and Restoration while preserving longer institutional consequences.

## Qualifications

“English Commonwealth” compresses several constitutional phases and a wider three-kingdom problem. The episode is a broad social and political interpretation, not a complete institutional chronology, and its use of “Republic of Britain” should not imply an uncontested modern British state form.

## What Changed

- Created a profile distinguishing constitutional failure from the regime's longer institutional legacy.
