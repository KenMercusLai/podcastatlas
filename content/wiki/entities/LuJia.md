## Overview

陆贾 / Lu Jia is an early Han persuader, envoy, and civil-governance advocate. Across the current evidence he participates in tactical surrender efforts, fails in one hostage negotiation, helps turn [[ZhaoTuo|赵佗]] toward Han recognition twice under different emperors, and uses [[Xinyu|《新语》]] to argue that conquest methods cannot by themselves govern a settled realm.

## Current Profile

Qinji 138 places Lu Jia with [[LiShiqi|郦食其]] in [[ZhangLiang|张良]]'s Yaoguan plan. Their inducement makes Qin generals willing to negotiate, but Zhang Liang warns that soldiers may not follow their commanders' private preference. Persuasion here creates tactical vulnerability rather than a self-sufficient settlement.

Hanji 166 gives Lu Jia a failed mission to [[XiangYu|项羽]] for peace and the return of [[LiuTaigong|刘太公]] and [[LuZhi|吕雉]]. [[HouGongChuHan|侯公]] later succeeds, keeping Lu Jia's role distinct from the final [[HonggouSettlement|鸿沟议和]] broker.

Hanji 176 and 185 add Lu Jia's civil-order role. Through [[Xinyu|《新语》]], he tells [[LiuBang|刘邦]] that winning the realm on horseback does not mean it can be governed on horseback; ritual, learning, and civil administration must follow military victory.

Hanji 184 and 185 give the first [[Nanyue|南越]] mission. Liu Bang sends Lu Jia with seal and tally after recognizing Zhao Tuo as king. Lu Jia combines Zhao's Central Plains origin, kin and graves, Han's victory, family risk, possible military action, and a dignity-preserving title to make submission cheaper than war.

Hanji 209 makes Lu Jia a repeat envoy under [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]]. Before dispatch, Liu Heng repairs Zhao Tuo's ancestral graves, honors relatives, and reportedly withdraws a border force. Lu Jia then carries a letter that offers southern autonomy and restored exchange, emphasizes war's human and material cost, and refuses a two-emperor order. The source ends before Zhao Tuo's answer, so it establishes Lu Jia's role in delivering the renewed settlement design rather than its completed outcome.

## Key Characteristics

- Persuader whose speech operates inside broader military, political, and recognition packages rather than independently of power.
- Envoy whose missions include both failure and success, preventing a simple image of universally decisive eloquence.
- Repeat Nanyue negotiator serving Liu Bang's first recognition and Liu Heng's later reconciliation attempt.
- Practitioner of face-saving coercion through identity, kinship, relative power, titles, and bounded autonomy.
- Advocate for replacing conquest habits with ritual, learning, and civil governance after victory.

## Qualifications

These podcast notes do not constitute a complete biography or primary-text dossier. The Yaoguan persuasion creates an attack opening rather than a durable negotiated surrender; the Xiang Yu mission fails; and the success of the Liu Bang-era Nanyue mission depends on title recognition and credible coercion as well as speech. Hanji 209 stops before Zhao Tuo responds, and its hostage interpretation of relatives and graves is the host's inference. Exact speech, chronology, envoy authority, and the division between Liu Heng's policy design and Lu Jia's own contribution require textual comparison.
