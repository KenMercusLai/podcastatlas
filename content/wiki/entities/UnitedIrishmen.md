# United Irishmen

## Overview

The United Irishmen were an Irish reform and later revolutionary organization that sought parliamentary reform, Catholic emancipation, and an Irish parliament before helping organize the 1798 rebellion.

## Current Profile

The sources place the organization within the American and French revolutionary transformation of the 1780s and 1790s. Founded in October 1791 with [[WolfeTone|Theobald Wolfe Tone]] among its central figures, its initial program crossed confessional lines and aimed at parliamentary reform and Catholic representation rather than immediate republican insurrection.

Suppression drove the organization underground in 1795 and toward French support. Wolfe Tone sought aid in Paris and became a French officer and citizen; weather stopped the 1796 expedition, and the 1798 rebellion mobilized about 50,000 rebels across several regions but ended with roughly 30,000 deaths. The movement's transformation illustrates how reform, sedition, and treason could become unstable categories when British authority and an Irish self-determining republic made rival sovereignty claims. Defeat did not erase its significance: it helped define a modern revolutionary nationalism while the British response produced the [[ActOfUnion1801|Act of Union]].

## Key Characteristics

- Founded in 1791 around parliamentary reform and Catholic representation rather than immediate republican revolt.
- Attempted a cross-confessional alliance of Catholics, Protestants, and dissenters.
- Became a secret revolutionary organization after suppression in 1795.
- Sought French military help through Wolfe Tone and other contacts.
- Helped organize the regionally dispersed and extremely lethal 1798 rebellion.
- Marks the emergence of a modern republican and anti-monarchical nationalism in the source.

- Revolutionary turn: [[336-ireland-celts-conquest-and-cromwell-part-1-glt6280042699]] connects suppression, clandestine reorganization, Wolfe Tone's French mission, and the failed 1796 expedition.
- Rebellion and aftermath: [[336-ireland-celts-conquest-and-cromwell-part-1-glt6280042699]] supplies the source-scoped mobilization and death figures and links 1798 to the Union response.

## Qualifications

The sources stress that later Catholic-nationalist and Protestant-unionist identities had not yet become absolute: Protestant dissenters were central to the United Irish project, and later Irish nationalism continued to include Protestant leaders. The exact boundary between protected reform, sedition, treason, and armed republicanism changed over the organization's life. Mobilization and casualty totals are episode-level estimates rather than independently adjudicated figures here.

## What Changed

- Added the October 1791 founding context and sharpened the distinction between initial reform and later clandestine republican war.
- Connected the turn to French aid with the wider problem of contested allegiance.
