# Benjamin Franklin

## Overview

Benjamin Franklin is presented as a printer, journalist, inventor, scientist, diplomat, and founding figure whose public persona helped turn colonial grievance into international support for the [[AmericanRevolution|American Revolution]].

## Current Profile

The episode's Franklin is neither a static patriotic icon nor only a charming polymath. He begins inside Boston Puritan discipline and British imperial loyalty, becomes famous through printing, moral journalism, practical invention, and electrical science, then breaks with Britain after the Hutchinson letters, the Cockpit humiliation, and the outbreak of war. His decisive contribution comes in France, where celebrity, costume, salons, and Enlightenment symbolism become tools for securing the [[FrancoAmericanAlliance1778|Franco-American alliance]]. The profile remains morally qualified because Franklin owned enslaved people, wrote a pro-slavery defense in 1770, and only late in life turned publicly toward abolition.

## Key Characteristics

- Self-made colonial printer and journalist whose authority came from work, wit, thrift, and public usefulness.
- Practical Enlightenment figure associated with electricity, invention, weather, swimming, bifocals, and the [[RoyalSociety]].
- Former loyal Englishman whose break with Britain made him a representative case of [[ColonialLoyaltyRupture|colonial loyalty rupture]].
- Founding participant in the [[ContinentalCongress|Continental Congress]], the [[DeclarationOfIndependence|Declaration of Independence]], the French treaty, the peace treaty with Britain, and the [[UnitedStatesConstitution]].
- Diplomat whose charm, scientific fame, fur-cap image, and salon performance made [[DiplomaticPersonaAsStrategicAsset|persona a strategic asset]] in France.
- Pragmatic constitutional actor who accepted imperfect settlement when he thought no better agreement was available.
- Morally contradictory founder whose late abolitionist petition and satire do not erase earlier slaveholding and pro-slavery writing.

## Evidence

- Self-made public authority: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] describes Franklin's escape from apprenticeship, rise in Philadelphia printing, newspaper dominance, autobiography, and maxims of industry and frugality.
- Science and Enlightenment reputation: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] links Franklin's lightning experiment, inventions, weather and Gulf Stream curiosity, David Hume's praise, and election to the Royal Society.
- Political break: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] follows Franklin from admiration for George III through the Hutchinson letters, Cockpit attack, return to America, and independence stance against William Franklin's loyalism.
- Founding documents: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says Franklin signed the Declaration, the French treaty, the peace treaty with Britain, and the Constitution, and made the famous "self-evident" edit to Jefferson's draft.
- French diplomacy: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says Franklin's Paris mission used celebrity, costume, Voltaire symbolism, and salon sociability to help secure French military and financial backing.
- Slavery qualification: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says Franklin owned enslaved people, wrote an anonymous 1770 defense of slavery, petitioned Congress against slavery in February 1790, and published a final anti-slavery satire.

## Qualifications

The episode is interpretive biography, not a complete archival life of Franklin. It emphasizes personality, public image, and diplomatic effect; Franklin's private motives, the exact weight of his persona in French policy, and his inner moral change on slavery remain source-scoped. The slavery evidence is not treated as redeemed by the late petition; it remains part of the current profile.
