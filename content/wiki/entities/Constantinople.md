# Constantinople

## Overview

Constantinople was the eastern Roman capital founded by [[ConstantineTheGreat|Constantine the Great]] as a new Rome and developed into a wealthy, Christian, strategically placed center of imperial government.

## Current Profile

The source treats Constantinople's durability as the product of geography plus infrastructure. Its position between Europe and Asia supported movement toward the Danube and Persian frontiers; waterworks overcame an early constraint; and the Theodosian Walls made the city exceptionally defensible. By the sixth century it was presented as a vast, cosmopolitan commercial center rather than evidence of Roman decline.

Its political form was deliberately Roman: emperor, palace, Senate, people, imported statues, and Hippodrome reproduced and transformed institutions of the older capital. The Hippodrome was especially important because it brought ruler and populace into direct contact and made the Blues and Greens channels for entertainment, patronage, violence, theology, and political pressure.

Across its later history, Constantinople survived seventh-century territorial collapse and Arab sieges, remained the center of a population that called itself Roman, and acquired Christian and Muslim apocalyptic roles. The Crusader sack of 1204 was catastrophic but not final because Byzantine rule returned. [[MehmedII]]'s capture in 1453 became the dominant symbolic end of Byzantium even though Trebizond survived until 1461, and later claimants treated possession, protection, or memory of the city as evidence in competing Roman successions.

## Key Characteristics

- Strategically located capital linking European, Asian, Danubian, and eastern-frontier concerns.
- Fortified and supplied metropolis whose walls and aqueducts supported unusual durability.
- Deliberate new Rome that reproduced imperial, senatorial, civic, and monumental forms.
- Christian capital and rival religious center as well as a Roman political city.
- Cosmopolitan commercial center with a very large sixth-century population.
- Hippodrome polity in which popular factions mediated contact between emperor and crowd.
- Long-lived symbolic capital whose sieges, sack, recovery, and conquest generated rival Roman and apocalyptic claims.

## Qualifications

This profile depends on two popular-history episodes and concentrates on the city's foundation logic, sixth-century setting, and high-level political afterlife. Population estimates, rankings among world cities, factional organization, motives assigned to Constantine, siege interpretation, apocalyptic traditions, and Ottoman succession claims remain source-scoped. Durability should not be confused with uninterrupted stability or immunity from conquest, and 1453's symbolic weight does not erase Trebizond's survival until 1461.

## What Changed
