# John II of France

## Overview

John II of France is the French king whose alienation of nobles and defeat at [[BattleOfPoitiers|Poitiers]] gave [[England]] immense bargaining power but also exposed how a captive monarch and an ungovernable kingdom could make a victorious settlement impossible to implement.

## Current Profile

The earlier episode presents John as kingly in appearance but insecure, obstinate, and politically damaging. His execution of the returning Constable of France, promotion of Charles of Spain, confiscation of [[CharlesTheBad|Charles the Bad's]] Norman lands, and bloody arrest of rivals in Rouen deepen noble distrust. His sponsorship of the Order of the Star seeks military renewal, but its refusal-to-retreat ideal adds rigidity at Poitiers.

John raises a great army and brings his sons and crown jewels against the trapped Black Prince. He rejects papal mediation, accepts dismounted combat to avoid another Crécy, and advances with the final line after earlier formations lose coordination. He is captured with his son Philip, many nobles, and the jewels.

John then appears as a prestigious captive living lavishly in London while France fragments under the Dauphin, Charles the Bad, Parisian revolt, the [[Jacquerie]], and [[FreeCompaniesHundredYearsWar|free companies]]. His 1358 Windsor terms promise a huge ransom and extensive sovereign concessions to [[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]], but France lacks the money and political order needed to carry them out.

The episode says John later offers still wider concessions, which the Dauphin and Estates General reject. After the [[TreatyOfBretigny|Treaty of Brétigny]], he returns voluntarily to English captivity when treaty obligations cannot be fulfilled and dies in 1364, allowing the Dauphin to become [[CharlesVOfFrance|Charles V]].

## Key Characteristics

- French king whose favoritism and coercion intensify noble opposition.
- Patron of chivalric reform through the Order of the Star.
- Overconfident commander captured at Poitiers and held in London.
- Diplomatic asset whose ransom and concessions exceeded France's immediate capacity.
- Lavish captive whose courtly consumption contrasts with disorder in France.
- Signatory to proposed settlements that depended on absent fiscal and political stability.
- Voluntary returnee to captivity when treaty obligations failed.
