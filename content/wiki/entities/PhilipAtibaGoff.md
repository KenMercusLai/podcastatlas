## Overview

Philip Atiba Goff is a psychologist and co-founder of the [[CenterForPolicingEquity]] whose work in the supplied [[HiddenBrain]] episode reframes racial disparities in policing as measurable problems of behavior, institutional design, resource distribution, and situational risk.

## Current Profile

Goff's distinctive contribution is methodological. He does not require reformers to establish an officer's private prejudice before calling a racially unequal outcome racism or trying to change it. Instead, he asks which practices predict force, which encounters are unnecessary, which public needs have been displaced onto armed police, and which outcomes an agency can measure and manage.

The episode presents him as a coalition builder without implying that all interests align. His positions-versus-interests distinction identifies common ground around reducing killings, racial disparities, and inappropriate police duties, while leaving budget, authority, and accountability disputes visible. His preferred trajectory is cumulative harm reduction rather than a promise that one intervention will solve policing.

## Key Characteristics

- Moves analysis from unverifiable inner motive toward observable conduct and institutional outcomes.
- Treats racial disparity as compatible with both individual agency and structural causation.
- Uses performance data to identify high-risk practices and evaluate reforms.
- Seeks shared interests between police, communities, and activists without claiming full consensus.
- Favors prevention and contact reduction over relying only on punishment after harm.
- Presents incremental harm reduction as a possible route to deeper structural change.

## Qualifications

This profile rests on one structured podcast summary rather than Goff's publications, datasets, institutional records, or a comparative evaluation of the reforms discussed. Reported disparity and force-reduction figures remain episode-scoped. Coalition language should not be read as proof that police, affected communities, and activists agree on budgets, coercive authority, responsibility, or the proper endpoint of reform.
