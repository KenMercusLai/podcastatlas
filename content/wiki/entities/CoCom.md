# CoCom / 巴黎统筹委员会

## Overview
CoCom, commonly rendered in Chinese as 巴黎统筹委员会 or 巴统, was a U.S.-led multilateral export-control body used during the Cold War to coordinate restrictions on strategic goods and technology.

## Current Profile
[[2b2e96d8aea7-2b2e96d8aea7]] presents CoCom less as a permanent security consensus than as a negotiated alliance. Its restrictive lists and approval rules mattered, but member compliance also depended on aid, foregone trade, domestic employment, and whether the United States appeared to reserve profitable exceptions for itself. The episode uses this institutional history as context for [[AIExportControls]], while acknowledging that contemporary model and compute controls are not simply CoCom restored.

## Key Characteristics
- Coordinated member restrictions on military, atomic, dual-use, computing, radar, precision-machine, and strategic-material exports.
- Applied stricter China controls for a period through a separate China-focused mechanism.
- Relied on member agreement and domestic enforcement rather than a single supranational authority.
- Faced recurring pressure from members that valued exports and employment more than uniform restriction.
- Lost its original strategic basis after the Soviet collapse and was succeeded by a looser arrangement.

## Evidence
Alliance design and scope:
- [[2b2e96d8aea7-2b2e96d8aea7]] describes a U.S.-led body formed around 1949, a restrictive-list system, and controls spanning military and dual-use technology.

Commercial and political limits:
- [[2b2e96d8aea7-2b2e96d8aea7]] uses British and Japanese pressure, U.S. exceptions, and the [[ToshibaMachine]] case to argue that member incentives weakened uniform enforcement.

Institutional succession:
- [[2b2e96d8aea7-2b2e96d8aea7]] places CoCom's end after the Soviet collapse and connects it to the later [[WassenaarArrangement]].

## Qualifications
The page records one podcast's historical account rather than a primary-document reconstruction. Dates, list sizes, voting rules, China-specific machinery, exceptions, and enforcement outcomes need corroboration before they are treated as a complete institutional history.
