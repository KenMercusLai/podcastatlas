# Chile

## Overview
Chile appears in the wiki as a Latin American democracy broken by the 1973 military coup, a copper and infrastructure node in current great-power competition, and part of a climate-sensitive regional fishmeal supply cluster.

## Current Profile
The historical sources present Chile's 1973 breakdown as a sequence rather than a sudden foreign-controlled event. [[370-the-1973-chilean-coup-allende-nixon-and-the-cia-part-1-glt6405070034]] adds Chile's democratic self-image, copper dependence, [[SalvadorAllende]]'s reforms, inflation and shortage, institutional confrontation, extensive U.S. interference, and [[ReneSchneider]]'s defense of constitutional succession. [[371-the-1973-chilean-coup-general-pinochet-seizes-power-part-2-glt3740766328]] follows collapsing command restraint into the coup and [[AugustoPinochet]]'s personal dictatorship and sustained repression.

The contemporary sources show different forms of strategic and ecological exposure. [[latin-lessons-the-donroe-doctrine-boost-6a4233e26b6690f46aae4e3d]] places copper and a proposed Chile-Hong Kong cable inside U.S.-China competition, while [[shangye-xiaoyang-45-eerninuo-yueda-yu-yuegui-1000164352]] places Chile with Peru and Ecuador in a fishmeal cluster vulnerable to fisheries and El Nino disruption.

## Key Characteristics
- Chile's democratic tradition and military non-intervention norm constrained an early coup route but did not survive prolonged polarization, institutional conflict, foreign pressure, and command turnover.
- The 1973 coup ended Allende's elected government and opened the way to Pinochet's personal dictatorship and state terror.
- Copper gives Chile a durable role in critical-minerals investment and geopolitical competition.
- Infrastructure proposals such as an undersea cable can become objects of U.S.-China strategic rivalry.
- Regional fishmeal concentration links Chilean supply conditions to global aquaculture-feed costs.
