# League of Corinth

## Overview

The League of Corinth was the Macedonian-led settlement created after the [[BattleOfChaeronea]] to restrain war among Greek member states, preserve the new order, and organize an expedition against the [[AchaemenidEmpire]].

## Current Profile

[[PhilipIIMacedon|Philip II]] used military victory as the foundation for a federated peace. Members were expected to avoid conflict with one another and act collectively against violations, while Philip held the leading command and directed the Persian war. Sparta remained outside. The arrangement gave Greek participation and Panhellenic language to a hierarchy created by Macedonian force.

Philip's assassination exposed how much the system depended on the monarch's personal authority. [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander]] restored the league through rapid movement, negotiation, and credible force before campaigning north. When [[Thebes]] later revolted, a council of member states supplied formal collective authorization for destruction and enslavement. That vote spread responsibility but did not remove Alexander's power to choose a less severe outcome.

The league therefore combined real coordinating institutions with unequal control. It could reduce interstate war and present Persia as a shared external project, yet it also legalized coercion against members and converted Macedonian supremacy into collective procedure.

## Key Characteristics

- Post-Chaeronea alliance and peace settlement led by Macedon.
- Required members to preserve the internal order and act against violations.
- Organized a Panhellenic campaign against Persia under Macedonian command.
- Excluded Sparta and did not erase the unequal power behind member consent.
- Depended initially on Philip's personal coalition and required reconfirmation after his death.
- Supplied collective legal cover for the punishment and destruction of Thebes.

## Qualifications

The page is grounded in three structured popular-history summaries rather than a constitutional study of the league. Member autonomy, council procedure, the balance of imposed and voluntary support, Philip's intended Persian settlement, and the exact legal force of the vote against Thebes require fuller evidence. Collective authorization is not equivalent to equal decision power or moral exculpation.
