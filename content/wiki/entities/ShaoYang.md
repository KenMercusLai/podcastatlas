# 邵阳 / Shao Yang

## Overview
邵阳 / Shao Yang is the Chinese American local elected official interviewed in [[404-shaoyang-yiwei-huaren-shiyiyuan-qinlide-meiguo-difang-zhengzhi-f96bdc7a-c667-4594-8719-2070d24f8231]] about [[FremontCalifornia|Fremont]], school-board politics, city council work, California electoral constraints, and Chinese American political participation.

## Current Profile
The source presents Shao as a Chinese immigrant who moved from science and technology work into local public office. He describes leaving Hefei for U.S. graduate study in 1990, receiving a chemistry PhD at Harvard, becoming a Christian, naturalizing as a U.S. citizen in 2002, and developing a habit of close voter-guide reading before running for office.

Shao's political profile is local, conservative-leaning, and formally nonpartisan. He first ran unsuccessfully for school board in 2012, won a seat on the [[FremontUnifiedSchoolDistrict]] board in 2014, later served on the [[FremontCalifornia|Fremont]] city council and as vice mayor, and ran unsuccessfully for California Assembly against [[AlexLeeCalifornia|Alex Lee]]. He frames officeholding as representation work for parents, Chinese American voters, and moderate or nonpartisan residents who may otherwise be ignored.

The episode is also a self-reflection on political identity. Shao says he has done the work of a politician without seeing ambition or party advancement as his core reason for service. His wife's question about who he is without office becomes the source's strongest boundary against reducing public value to title, election result, or status.

## Key Characteristics
- Chinese immigrant and former scientist whose public-service narrative runs through faith, voting, court interpreting, school-board work, and city council service.
- Conservative-leaning but long nonpartisan local officeholder who is wary of party identity.
- Treats voting, meeting attendance, and candidacy as representation infrastructure for Chinese Americans and parents.
- Uses school-board budget decisions, counselor staffing, curriculum review, and parent notice as concrete governance cases.
- Reads city council work through transparency, public materials, open meetings, voter accountability, and local compromise.
- Treats state-level California politics as much more party-constrained than local nonpartisan office.
- Reflects on the personal cost of politics, negative campaigning, identity pressure, and humility after entering government.
