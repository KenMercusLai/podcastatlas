## Overview

Liu Qiang appears across Hanji 720, Hanji 728, Hanji 732, and Hanji 733-1 as a displaced former-heir figure whose survival depends on turning succession loss into controlled royal honor. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-720-donghan-kaiguo-taizi-weihe-fanfu-da-cizhi-baogao-lslizspo8rywnf3cebyhincdhvz0|Hanji 720]] now supplies the missing root layer: after [[GuoShengtong|Guo Shengtong]] is deposed, Liu Qiang repeatedly asks to leave the crown-prince position, and [[LiuXiu|Guangwu]] makes him Donghai King while replacing him with Liu Yang, the later [[EmperorMingOfHan|Mingdi]]. Hanji 728 then shows later favor and fief-distance management, Hanji 732 shows him rejecting an anonymous succession provocation, and Hanji 733-1 shows his final illness, deferential memorial, and frugal burial.

## Current Profile

Hanji 720 gives Liu Qiang the crown-prince withdrawal layer. After Guo Shengtong's deposition, the source says Liu Qiang becomes uneasy because his mother's status had been his support. Zhi Yun advises that remaining in an unstable heir position is dangerous and does not help him serve his mother, so Liu Qiang repeatedly asks through trusted attendants and royal kin to resign and retire to a fief. Liu Xiu hesitates for years, then in 43 CE makes Liu Qiang Donghai King and makes Liu Yang crown prince, renaming him Liu Zhuang.

The same source preserves the institutional objection through Yuan Hong's comment. Yuan Hong argues that crown-prince status is meant to settle order and should not be changed if the heir has no grave fault. Liu Qiang's withdrawal therefore solves a family danger without making the original succession change institutionally clean.

Hanji 728 gives Liu Qiang the later Guangwu-era honor layer. In 52 CE, Liu Xiu changes Lu King Liu Xing's fief to Beihai and merges the former Lu territory into Donghai. The source says Liu Qiang is very proper in ritual conduct, and Liu Xiu treats him exceptionally by expanding his fief and granting huben guards, cavalry, and ritual-music equipment. The same episode later lists Liu Qiang among the princes sent back to their fiefs after the prince-guest disaster.

Hanji 732 gives Liu Qiang an accession-risk role. After [[LiuXiu|Guangwu]] dies, [[LiuJingGuanglingKingEasternHan|Liu Jing]] sends an anonymous letter arguing that Liu Qiang had been wrongly displaced as heir and that Queen Guo had been wrongly removed. Liu Qiang does not take the bait: he panics, secures the messenger figure, seals the letter, and reports the matter to Mingdi.

Mingdi's response confirms why Liu Qiang is treated as a stabilizing royal kinsman. The emperor keeps Liu Jing's role private, has Liu Qiang leave the capital first, and then reassures the Guo/Liu Qiang branch. Liu Qiang's loyal report lets the new reign contain a dangerous grievance without making the former heir into a rival center.

Hanji 733-1 then emphasizes Liu Qiang's final memorial rather than a full earlier biography. During his illness, Mingdi sends imperial envoys, physicians, and princes to attend him. Liu Qiang thanks the emperor, asks to return his fief to commandery status, and urges Mingdi to care doubly for the empress dowager. Mingdi weeps, mourns deeply, and sends gifts beyond ordinary funeral practice, but then orders burial goods kept simple because Liu Qiang had been known for modesty and frugality.

## Key Characteristics

- Established crown prince whose security collapses after his mother's deposition.
- Uses repeated resignation to turn forced succession loss into morally legible withdrawal.
- Recast as Donghai King when Liu Yang / Liu Zhuang becomes crown prince.
- Receives exceptional Guangwu-era honor without becoming an unbounded capital presence.
- Sent back to his fief after the prince-guest disaster, making honor compatible with territorial distance.
- Royal kinsman who refuses to let later grievance language become rebellion.
- His final memorial, fief-return request, and frugal burial extend the same self-limiting profile.

## Qualifications

This page now covers Liu Qiang's resignation and replacement, but it remains source-bounded rather than a full biography. Hanji 720 presents his motive as a survival and filial calculation after Guo Shengtong's deposition; that is stronger than a pure virtue reading, but still an interpretation of compressed chronicle material. Yuan Hong's criticism should stay attached to the synthesis: Liu Qiang's peaceful withdrawal reduced conflict without proving that replacing an established heir was sound institutional practice. Queen Guo's death notice and many details of Liu Qiang's fief administration remain outside the current evidence.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 720 as Liu Qiang's root succession layer: Guo Shengtong's deposition, repeated crown-prince resignation, Donghai King settlement, and Liu Zhuang's installation.
- Reframed the profile around withdrawal as self-protection plus political cover, not only later royal honor and containment.
- Preserved Hanji 728 as preferential treatment and post-guest-disaster fief-return evidence.
- Preserved Hanji 732 and Hanji 733-1 as the loyal former-heir and frugal final-memory layers.
