# Battle of Poltava / 波尔塔瓦战役

## Overview

The Battle of Poltava in 1709 was the decisive battlefield reversal of the [[GreatNorthernWar|Great Northern War]]. An exhausted Swedish army under [[CharlesXII|Charles XII]] attacked [[PeterTheGreat|Peter the Great]]'s fortified, numerically superior, artillery-supported Russian force; broken coordination and overwhelming firepower destroyed Swedish field power and altered the Baltic balance.

## Current Profile

The campaign episode treats Poltava first as a lure: after abandoning the Moscow route, losing the relief column at [[BattleOfLesnaya|Lesnaya]], and finding [[Baturin]] destroyed, Charles besieges a small fortress in hopes of drawing Peter into the decisive battle he still believes Sweden can win. Severe winter losses, rotten equipment, wet powder, missing allies, and Charles's shattered foot instead make the eventual encounter increasingly favorable to Russia. Swedish officers then fail to strike while Peter's army is crossing the Vorskla, surrendering the last clear chance to fight only part of the Russian force.

The battle episode treats Poltava as the endpoint of those accumulated disadvantages rather than a single unlucky engagement. The attack still relies on surprise and disciplined infantry closing rapidly without artillery support, but newly built redoubts, rival commanders, dispersed units, and failed communication disrupt the plan before the main clash.

Roos's separated battalions were destroyed, Lewenhaupt drifted away from the main force, and Rehnskiöld waited for units that did not return. Peter then brought his army out of the fortified camp. The surviving Swedish infantry briefly struck the Russian line, but artillery, reserves, widened gaps, and encirclement caused collapse. The defeat made Charles a fugitive, encouraged Sweden's enemies to re-enter the war, and secured Russia's great-power and Baltic trajectory.

## Key Characteristics

- It concentrates prior logistical attrition, winter exposure, numerical inferiority, injury, and alliance weakness into one battle.
- The siege is intended to compel a decisive encounter, but delay gives Peter time to cross and fortify.
- The Swedish plan depends on surprise, speed, cohesion, and close combat because artillery and material parity are absent.
- Redoubts and divided command turn a high-risk maneuver into fragmented local actions.
- Russian artillery, reserves, and improved infantry translate defensive preparation into battlefield decision.
- Its political effect exceeds the immediate casualties by reversing coalition expectations and imperial credibility.

## Evidence

- Pre-battle disadvantage: [[565-the-great-northern-war-revenge-of-the-cossacks-part-2-glt1223770243]] connects Russian supply denial, Lesnaya, Baturin, winter, failed alliances, equipment decay, and Charles's wound to the siege and attack decision.
- Missed opportunity: [[565-the-great-northern-war-revenge-of-the-cossacks-part-2-glt1223770243]] describes the failure to attack while Russia crossed the Vorskla; [[566-the-great-northern-war-slaughter-on-the-steppes-part-3-glt4679437587]] follows the fully assembled forces into battle.
- Coordination failure: [[566-the-great-northern-war-slaughter-on-the-steppes-part-3-glt4679437587]] traces the changed redoubt plan, Roos's isolation, Lewenhaupt's displacement, and Rehnskiöld's uncertainty.
- Russian battle system: [[566-the-great-northern-war-slaughter-on-the-steppes-part-3-glt4679437587]] describes fortified positions, cannon, infantry depth, reserves, and the opening of Peter's camp for the main engagement.
- Geopolitical result: [[566-the-great-northern-war-slaughter-on-the-steppes-part-3-glt4679437587]] links the Swedish collapse to Charles's flight, renewed coalition war, Russian influence in Poland, and Saint Petersburg's security.

## Qualifications

The account is a narrative synthesis from two consecutive episodes, with source-scoped troop totals, casualty estimates, dialogue, command motives, siege purpose, and near-miss anecdotes. Poltava is decisive in their geopolitical interpretation, but the war continues until 1721; battlefield victory does not by itself explain the entire imperial transition.

## What Changed

- Added the siege-as-lure interpretation and the missed chance to attack Russia during its river crossing.
- Made Lesnaya, Baturin, winter, equipment failure, absent allies, and Charles's wound explicit preconditions of the battle.
