# Siege of Khartoum

## Overview

The Siege of Khartoum was the 1884-1885 Mahdist encirclement and capture of [[Khartoum]], ending with [[CharlesGeorgeGordon|General Gordon]]'s death shortly before the British relief force arrived.

## Current Profile

The episode treats the siege as the endpoint of an unclear political mission rather than an inevitable last stand. Gordon reached Khartoum to evacuate civilians under one reading of his orders, held the governor-general's authority under another, and refused to save himself while abandoning others. He organized defenses and public reform gestures as [[MuhammadAhmad|Mahdist]] forces closed routes and supplies.

The city fell on 26 January 1885 after attackers exploited a weakness. Gordon was killed, many inhabitants were massacred, and British steamers arrived two days later. That narrow interval became crucial to the story's afterlife because it allowed logistical and political failure to be narrated as the preventable betrayal of a Christian hero.

## Key Characteristics

- Grew from a mission whose inquiry, evacuation, and governing purposes were never stably aligned.
- Pitted Gordon's improvised defense against a religious-political movement he only partly understood.
- Combined civilian evacuation and reform gestures with worsening hunger, bombardment, and isolation.
- Ended with the city's capture, mass killing, and Gordon's uncertain death.
- Became a foundational scene of Victorian imperial martyr memory because relief arrived immediately too late.

- Mission and arrival: [[142-general-gordon-and-the-siege-of-khartoum-glt6227664313]] follows Gordon's changing mandate and 18 February 1884 arrival.
- Defense and deprivation: [[142-general-gordon-and-the-siege-of-khartoum-glt6227664313]] describes evacuation, fortification, river chains, bombardment, isolation, and hunger.
- Fall and delayed relief: [[142-general-gordon-and-the-siege-of-khartoum-glt6227664313]] dates the fall to 26 January 1885 and the British steamers' arrival to two days later.

## Qualifications

The account is British- and Gordon-centered. It does not independently establish casualty totals, the decisive military breach, internal Khartoum politics, Sudanese civilian experience, the Mahdist command structure, or the precise circumstances of Gordon's death. “Betrayal” describes the later public frame, not a settled causal verdict.

## What Changed

- Created an event profile distinguishing the siege itself from the later martyr narrative attached to it.
