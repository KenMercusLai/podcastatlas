# Zhao Chongguo / 赵充国 (Western Han)

## Overview
Zhao Chongguo is synthesized as a Western Han frontier commander whose source arc begins with a high-risk breakout under [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] and matures into strategic restraint backed by credible force, patient state-building, and costly truth-telling under later rulers. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-377-1-liling-qingzhan-xiongnu-hanwudi-weihe-buyuan-ta-dang-qianfeng-lttki-nkmyzvt3q1wxomv0-hpmmk|Hanji 377-1]] adds the early-career layer: he leads more than one hundred soldiers out of an encirclement around [[LiGuangliWesternHan|李广利]], is wounded more than twenty times, and receives promotion after Wudi inspects his injuries. Later sources show that bravery developing into disciplined deterrence, coalition splitting, field reconnaissance,屯田, infrastructure, surrender management, and truthful reporting.

## Current Profile
Hanji 377-1 supplies the earliest current action in Zhao's profile. During Li Guangli's 99 BCE return from the Tianshan region, the Han force is encircled and short of food. Zhao, acting as a sima in the episode, leads more than one hundred elite soldiers through the siege so the larger force can follow. Wudi later views Zhao's more than twenty wounds and promotes him. This is a direct-combat survival episode, not yet the low-loss frontier strategy that dominates his later profile.

Hanji 402-2 supplies the earliest currently attested counsel scene. When Xiongnu prepares to attack Wuhuan, Zhao tells Huo Guang that Wuhuan has been harming Han borders and that Xiongnu punishment of Wuhuan can work to Han's advantage. His advice is restraint, but it is not softness toward Wuhuan; it is a refusal to spend Han force protecting an unreliable dependent group at the wrong moment.

In Hanji 689-3, Zhao is already in his seventies when Qiang groups influenced by [[Xiongnu]] plan to attack Shanshan and Dunhuang and cut Han communications with the Western Regions. Under [[EmperorXuanOfHan]], he volunteers for the campaign, and the source links his operation to an omen-worded command formula that makes the [[FiveStarsOutOfTheEastBrocade]] intelligible as more than decorative text.

Hanji 443 makes the strategic style explicit. When a Xiongnu chanyu brings more than one hundred thousand cavalry near the Han border, Xuan sends the seventy-eight-year-old Zhao with forty thousand elite cavalry. Zhao does not chase battle glory. He holds a tight defensive posture until Xiongnu hesitation, the chanyu's illness, and internal succession fracture change the field. The episode reads this as "不战而屈人之兵" and "贵谋贱战": Zhao's force creates pressure, but the final result comes through political fracture, defection, and the later institutional work of [[ZhengJiWesternHan|郑吉]] and the [[WesternRegionsProtectorate]].

Hanji 435, Hanji 436, and Hanji 439 fill in Zhao's managerial and administrative profile. Hanji 435 presents the crisis as a frontier-governance failure before it becomes Zhao's campaign: Yi Qu Anguo permits Xianling Qiang to cross the Yellow River without upward reporting, then kills more than thirty Qiang leaders he suspects, provoking wider rebellion and a failed Han response. Zhao's first answer to Emperor Xuan is disciplined humility inside confidence: no one is more suitable than himself, but "百闻不如一见" means he will inspect the terrain before promising a plan. His early field conduct already shows the later pattern. He stages a night crossing, refuses to chase a small Qiang cavalry group when his own army is tired, scouts Four-Look Gorge before passage, improves soldier food, and releases Diao Ku with a message that Han will punish only the guilty and reward those who separate from rebels.

In Hanji 436, Emperor Xuan first sends Zhao Xin Wuxian's proposal to attack Qiang groups in July with thirty days of grain, seizing livestock and families before withdrawing. Zhao's refusal is not a general dislike of battle. He argues that the march load would reduce combat ability, that Qiang forces could infer Han timing from grain limits, and that attacking coerced or neutral groups would help Xianling turn a loose alliance into a grateful, mutually committed bloc. He therefore asks Xuan to keep the original strategy: target the Xianling leaders, pardon or reassure coerced groups, and use active defense to make the enemy come on Han terms. Xuan initially orders the attack anyway, but Zhao's second memorial persuades him to accept Zhao's plan.

Hanji 437 shows the same reasoning in field action before the final court acceptance. When Xianling Qiang flee across the Huangshui after abandoning wagons, livestock, families, and children, Zhao rejects an urgent chase and orders slow pursuit, because desperate fugitives will keep running if not pressed but may turn into a death fight if cornered. He then forbids destruction of settlements and grazing in Qiang fields, feeds and releases a surrendering chief, and lets that chief carry the court's no-punishment message back to his people. The episode also makes Zhao's upward-risk ethic explicit through Zhao Ang's attempted warning: Zhao treats personal safety through obedience as disloyal if the order would worsen the frontier and state cost. His memorial asks to withdraw cavalry, hold key points with 10,281 soldiers, use pre-cut timber for posts, irrigation, and more than seventy bridges, and turn guarded farming into grain accumulation at Jincheng.

Hanji 438 focuses on how Zhao defends that memorial under renewed imperial questioning. Emperor Xuan asks when the war will actually end, and Zhao answers by arguing that imperial forces should win with minimal damage rather than chase visible killing. He presents the Qiang as already losing fertile land, pasture, cohesion, and morale; points to surrender and persuasion as signs of internal fracture; and lists twelve advantages of leaving infantry for屯田 while withdrawing costly cavalry. The episode's management analogy is not evidence about Han offices, but it captures Zhao's communication method: affirm the ruler's goal, show seriousness, supply concrete data and risks, and offer an alternative plan rather than simply saying no.

Hanji 439 shows the same logic after the immediate tactical dispute has become a settlement program. Facing repeated questioning from Emperor Xuan and shifting court opinion, Zhao argues that the [[XianlingQiang|先零羌]] are already near collapse through hunger, cold, land loss, weak horses, family constraints, and defection. He rejects an attack that cannot certainly end the problem, because it would tire Han forces, fail to stop small raids, and cheapen state prestige. The same source makes his personal-risk ethic explicit: obeying an order could shield him from suspicion, but he treats that as disloyal if it harms the ruler and state. The accepted plan leaves more than ten thousand troops in the 河湟 field for屯田, pacification, road work, bridges, relay posts, and eventual surrender management.

Hanji 440 shows the same public-duty ethic continuing after the campaign's apparent success. Zhao asks to withdraw屯田 forces only after reporting the scale of Qiang surrender, death, and remaining fugitives; then, when a friend urges him to attribute the achievement to Xin Wuxian and Xu Yanshou for self-protection, Zhao insists that military affairs require an honest record for the ruler and later generations. Xuan accepts Zhao's account, removes Xin Wuxian from the broken-Qiang command, restores Zhao as rear general, and later follows Zhao's initial warning that Xin Tang is unsuitable for Qiang affairs because of drunkenness and impulsiveness. The episode's darker layer is that correct advice does not dissolve resentment. Xin Tang eventually receives the post, abuses Qiang people while drunk, and helps trigger renewed rebellion; Xin Wuxian's side then secretly accuses Zhao's son Zhao Ang of leaking secrets, and Zhao Ang dies in prison. Zhao's profile therefore gains a family-vulnerability qualification: accurate frontier judgment and loyal reporting can still create enemies inside the court.

## Key Characteristics
- Zhao's source arc moves from a wounded breakout leader opening an escape route for Li Guangli to a later counselor practicing strategic nonintervention when Han protection would reward an unreliable frontier actor.
- His Southern Qiang campaign answers a coordinated Qiang-Xiongnu threat to Han-Western-Regions communications and supplies the military background for the brocade's five-star and "讨南羌" interpretation.
- Zhao's Xiongnu standoff emphasizes deterrence, patience, and refusal to turn every advantage into immediate attack.
- His Qiang campaign begins with accurate early diagnosis of alliance risk and with field decisions grounded in terrain, fatigue, scouting, and troop morale.
- His Qiang tactical reasoning separates principal culprits from coerced allies, while his field conduct applies that logic through slow pursuit, settlement protection, and trust-building treatment of submitters.
- His Qiang policy shows how withholding battle can be active command when paired with farming, infrastructure, surrender, local order, and explicit cost-risk explanation to the ruler.
- His post-campaign conduct shows that truthful reporting and personnel warnings can protect frontier order while damaging rival commanders' credit and appointment interests.

## Qualifications
This page is still not a full biography of Zhao Chongguo. Hanji 377-1 supplies a compact valor-and-promotion scene; its force size, wound count, office title, casualty ratio, and exact breakout sequence remain source-scoped. Hanji 402-2 adds a compact counsel scene, not a complete Wuhuan campaign history; Wuhuan naming, exact Xiongnu intelligence, and the policy consequence of Zhao's advice remain source-scoped. Hanji 689-3 is campaign-and-artifact centered, and the brocade-to-campaign connection remains plausible inside the episode's interpretation rather than proven. Hanji 443 reads Zhao's Xiongnu result as decisive strategic success, but the broader later history of Xiongnu conflict shows this was suppression, fracture, and frontier-order building rather than literal disappearance of the Xiongnu problem. Hanji 435 is an origin-and-initial-operations note; Yi Qu Anguo, Yang Yu, Diao Ku, the named Qiang branches, exact geography, and reported troop or casualty numbers remain source-scoped. Hanji 436 is tactical and source-scoped around one debate; Qiang branch names, the July date conversion, and exact supply assumptions need primary-history checking before being generalized. Hanji 437 is management-lesson heavy; casualty, livestock, wagon, timber, bridge, troop, and land-allocation figures remain source-scoped, and the enterprise-management analogy should not be treated as evidence about Western Han institutions. Hanji 438 is also management-lesson heavy; the twelve-point ordering, surrender counts, one-year timeline, cavalry-cost comparison, and "first precious屯田 document" evaluation remain source-scoped. Hanji 439 is management-lesson heavy; exact force figures, surrender counts, construction details, and received-text chronology remain source-scoped unless checked against primary histories.
