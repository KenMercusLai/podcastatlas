# Memphis Sanitation Strike

## Overview

The Memphis sanitation strike was a 1968 labor and civil-rights campaign by roughly 1,500 sanitation, sewer, and drainage workers seeking union recognition, better pay, better hours, and equal dignity.

## Current Profile

The strike began after two workers were crushed in a rubbish truck on February 1. Minister James Lawson connected the workers' demands to civil rights through the slogan “I Am a Man,” and [[MartinLutherKingJr|Martin Luther King Jr.]] treated Memphis as a concrete local version of the economic-justice campaign he wanted to take to Washington.

King's first Mason Temple speech generated enormous support, but the March 28 demonstration collapsed into property destruction, looting, and clashes with police. His decision to return turned the strike into both a test of nonviolent credibility and the setting for his final speech and assassination. The dispute was settled about a week after his funeral under White House pressure, giving the workers a posthumous campaign victory.

## Key Characteristics

- Labor dispute triggered by dangerous working conditions and worker deaths.
- Combined union demands with a claim to racial and human dignity.
- Became a local test case for King's national economic-justice strategy.
- Produced both a major mass meeting and a march that collapsed into violence.
- Drew King back to Memphis despite threats and staff concern.
- Ended in a settlement after King's assassination.

## Qualifications

The current source centers King and the assassination sequence more than the workers' organization, city bargaining, union structure, or the strike's longer institutional consequences. Crowd sizes, the March 28 sequence, responsibility for violence, and the exact effect of White House pressure remain source-scoped.
