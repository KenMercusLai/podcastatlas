## Overview

汉献帝 / Emperor Xian of Han, personal name Liu Xie, is the late-Han ruler whose recognized title repeatedly survives the destruction of his practical command. His evidence runs from contested childhood succession through forced enthronement, military captivity, flight, Cao-controlled court government, household purge, abdication, and continued status as Shanyang Gong.

## Current Profile

Liu Xie's political life is defined by the gap between [[MingqiLegitimacy|名器合法性]] and coercive capacity. [[DongZhuo|董卓]] selects and installs him, Li Jue and Guo Si fight over his person, and later protectors control his route, food, lodging, seals, and court. Yet the title remains powerful enough to constrain alternative sovereign projects, legitimate offices and campaigns, and create a [[LegitimacyWindowSeizure|合法性窗口抢占]] for [[CaoCao|曹操]].

The Xu court stabilizes Liu Xie's material setting without restoring sovereignty. Hanji 1150 extends the pattern from captured movement into captured household: independent adviser [[ZhaoYanYilangLateHan|赵彦]] is killed, [[DongGuirenLateHan|董贵人]] and her unborn child cannot be saved, and [[FuShouEmpress|伏皇后]] is removed with her family exposed to punishment. At the same time, the emperor's title, palace attendants, family, and potential heirs remain enough of a danger to produce [[CapturedSovereignResidualThreat|受制君主的残余威胁]] for Cao Cao.

His abdication to [[CaoPi|曹丕]] converts accumulated Cao-family control into a new dynasty, but does not end Liu Xie's recognized household. Hanji 1150 presents the Shanyang settlement, posthumous imperial honor, and continuing fief as an unusually durable predecessor arrangement; its medical and Yuntai Mountain anecdotes remain source-scoped.

## Key Characteristics

- Preferred but not publicly designated son whose throne begins through Dong Zhuo's coerced replacement of Liu Bian.
- Recognized sovereign whose title retains legal and strategic value even when soldiers control his body and court.
- Repeated captive, refugee, and bargaining asset under Dong Zhuo's successors and later escort coalitions.
- Capable of narrow administrative judgment, ritual concern, and refusal, but unable to enforce those decisions against organized force.
- Central legitimacy resource in Cao Cao's relocation to Xu and opposition to unsupported imperial claimants.
- Controlled sovereign whose advisers, spouses, and children are purged while his residual title still worries the controller.
- Abdicated predecessor whose fief, household status, and posthumous honor continue under Cao Wei.

## Qualifications

The page synthesizes a long podcast sequence rather than a critical biography. Many sources use literary dialogue, modern management analogies, and inferred motives. Exact dates, casualty and household counts, secret intentions, and physical scenes remain source-scoped. Hanji 1150's medical-service narrative and broad comparison with later fallen dynasties require separate corroboration. The evidence supports a ruler with residual title effects and narrow judgment, not an independent sovereign secretly exercising balanced power.
