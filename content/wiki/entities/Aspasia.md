## Overview

Aspasia was a Milesian woman associated with Pericles in fifth-century BCE Athens and remembered, through sharply contested traditions, as an intellectual, rhetorician, hetaira, and political influence.

## Current Profile

[[97-top-ten-mistresses-glt8855582591|Top Ten Mistresses]] places Aspasia high in its ranking because her possible intellectual influence is large even though the evidence is unusually unstable. As a woman from Miletus living in a society that sharply restricted citizen women, she is presented through the comparatively sociable role available to some hetairai. Comic and hostile sources connect her to prostitution, Periclean policy, and even the Peloponnesian War, while philosophical traditions associate her with rhetoric, Socrates, Pericles' funeral speech, and possibly the Diotima figure in Plato.

The profile therefore rests less on a recoverable list of actions than on a source problem: hostile satire and admiring philosophical attribution both testify to a powerful reputation without establishing authorship or causal control.

## Key Characteristics

- Woman from Miletus who entered the social and intellectual world of classical Athens.
- Partner of Pericles situated outside the conventional status of an Athenian citizen wife.
- Figure associated with rhetoric, conversation, and philosophical instruction.
- Target of sexualized comedy and political blame.
- Potentially major intellectual influence whose specific contributions remain uncertain.

## Evidence

- Social position: [[97-top-ten-mistresses-glt8855582591]] contrasts restrictions on Athenian women with the more sociable and intellectual role available to hetairai.
- Hostile tradition: [[97-top-ten-mistresses-glt8855582591]] reports comic accusations involving prostitution, Pericles, and responsibility for war.
- Intellectual tradition: [[97-top-ten-mistresses-glt8855582591]] connects Aspasia with Socrates, the funeral oration, and a possible relation to Plato's Diotima.

## Qualifications

The episode does not resolve Aspasia's legal or occupational status, the historical content of comic attacks, her authorship of speeches, her teaching relationship to Socrates or Pericles, or any link with Diotima. Sexualized hostility does not prove political power, while philosophical attribution does not prove authorship. The current page preserves the scale of her reputation without turning it into recovered biography.
