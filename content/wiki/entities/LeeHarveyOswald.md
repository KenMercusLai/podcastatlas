# Lee Harvey Oswald

## Overview

Lee Harvey Oswald was a former U.S. Marine and Soviet defector whom the episode identifies as the killer of [[JohnFKennedy|John F. Kennedy]] and police officer J. D. Tippit.

## Current Profile

The episode constructs a cumulative profile rather than relying on one decisive clue. It links a lonely and unstable childhood, early Marxism, Marine marksmanship, failed Soviet defection, conflict with Marina Oswald, workplace difficulty, Cuba activism, a rifle bought under the A. J. Hidell alias, and the attempted shooting of Edwin Walker to a search for dignity and historical significance through politics and violence.

For the assassination itself, the hosts emphasize opportunity and conduct: Oswald worked in the Texas School Book Depository before the motorcade route became public, made an unusual weeknight visit to retrieve a long package, was linked to the sixth floor and rifle, left immediately, collected a revolver, killed Tippit, resisted arrest, and lied during interrogation. The source treats this chain as stronger than intelligence-agency explanations, but it remains a podcast synthesis rather than a complete forensic adjudication.

## Key Characteristics

- Socially isolated and resentful figure whose politics became central to identity and self-worth.
- Marxist and pro-Cuba activist whose Soviet experience disappointed rather than ended his ideological ambitions.
- Marine-trained marksman with the skills the episode considers sufficient for the assassination.
- Violent actor linked by the source to the Walker attempt and Tippit killing before and after Kennedy's murder.
- Book Depository employee whose accidental workplace access created a late opportunity to attack the motorcade.
- Suspect whose flight, resistance, possession of a revolver, and false statements are treated as consciousness-of-guilt evidence.

## Qualifications

The episode's psychological narrative is interpretive: childhood assessments, resentment, Marxism, envy, and desire for historical importance do not by themselves prove murder. Some claims depend on later testimony, host selection, and compressed treatment of disputed ballistic or trajectory questions. The page records the source's cumulative case without treating personality profiles or political ideology as substitutes for event-specific evidence.
