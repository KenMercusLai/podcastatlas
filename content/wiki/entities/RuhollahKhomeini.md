# Ruhollah Khomeini

## Overview

Ruhollah Khomeini was a Shiite scholar, exiled anti-Shah organizer, and revolutionary leader who returned to [[Iran]] in 1979 and became the Islamic Republic's founding supreme authority. The bounded sources connect clerical and bazaar networks, cassette sermons, [[ShiiteRitualRevolutionaryMobilization|Shiite revolutionary mobilization]], anti-Western economic identity, [[ClericalRepublicDualAuthority|clerical-republican state design]], coercive gender rules, the [[IranHostageCrisis|Iran hostage crisis]], and revolutionary consolidation.

## Current Profile

[[iran-protests-and-sanctions]] presents Khomeini's founding era as the historical layer behind [[IranSanctions]] and [[RevolutionaryEconomicSelfReliance]]. Revolution, the hostage crisis, the first U.S. asset freeze, and constitutional suspicion of foreign concessions made investment and openness questions of sovereignty as well as economic technique.

The journalistic confrontation branch comes from [[77-ta-shi-ziyou-de-yegou-ta-shi-falaqi-685710187]] and [[OrianaFallaci|Oriana Fallaci]]'s Qom interview. Expectations that a critic of [[MohammadRezaPahlavi]] might be sympathetic gave way to confrontation over enforced dress, segregation, and temporary marriage, making Khomeini a case for [[JournalisticIndependenceAgainstFaction]] and [[ProtectionAsControl]].

Khomeini's formation and organizational base joined scholarly authority from clerical education in Qom to conflict over the White Revolution and legal immunity for American military personnel, letting him combine religious traditionalism with anti-colonial nationalism. Exile in Najaf did not isolate him: recorded sermons circulated by cassette through clerical and bazaar-linked networks. Shiite memory of Karbala, martyrdom, and forty-day mourning then supplied a political language the Shah and U.S. officials failed to understand in [[636-revolution-in-iran-fall-of-the-shah-part-1-glt5083873634]].

The revolutionary ascent in [[637-revolution-in-iran-rise-of-the-ayatollah-part-2-glt7243579163]] begins with Paris giving Khomeini access to global media while his broad anti-Shah message let different audiences underread *velayat-e faqih*. His Muharram appeal joined Karbala, martyrdom, and resistance to tyranny; after returning to Tehran he rejected Shapour Bakhtiar, formed a Revolutionary Council, appointed [[MehdiBazargan|Mehdi Bazargan]], and placed republican promises beneath a superior claim of Islamic authority.

[[ep251-yilang-kunju-sishinian-zhanlue-xuanze-de-daijia-yu-xianshi-lp8vyonyugtd2ozymgof-mim9zkd]] presents him as a revolutionary strategist concerned with the Muslim world's political direction and credits him with combining clerical authority with technical bureaucracy. The later Iran-series episodes add the hostage-crisis sequence: [[638-revolution-in-iran-the-hostage-crisis-part-3-glt6844236579]] says he was initially surprised, then endorsed the embassy occupation after recognizing its popularity and value against Bazargan's moderates; [[639-revolution-in-iran-death-in-the-desert-part-4-glt6288433592]] follows prolonged captivity, failed rescue, and later wartime hardening.

## Key Characteristics

- Founding revolutionary authority who joined Shiite religious legitimacy to modern political mobilization and Iranian nationalism.
- Scholar-organizer who used exile, cassette circulation, clerical authority, and bazaar-linked networks to remain politically present inside Iran.
- Exile leader who used global media and broad anti-monarchical language while keeping clerical guardianship politically underrecognized.
- Anti-imperial leader whose regime treated foreign economic dependence as a political danger.
- Institutional designer associated with a dual clerical and administrative state structure.
- Leader who used or benefited from the hostage crisis as a tool of consolidation and anti-American mobilization.
- Enforcer of social and gender controls challenged directly by Oriana Fallaci.

## Qualifications

The sources are podcast interpretations rather than a complete archival biography. They do not settle Khomeini's exact birth year, the reach and effect of cassette networks, how much his Paris presentation concealed, how different coalition groups understood his program, whether U.S. contact could have changed the outcome, whether he knew of the embassy plan in advance, how independent the student organization was, or how much the hostage crisis caused consolidation. The Fallaci episode is sympathetic to her challenge but does not settle every later controversy around her politics. Claims about strategic horizon, administrative authorship, political formula, motives, dialogue, crowd behavior, and counterfactual alternatives remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Added the Paris media, Muharram-Ashura mobilization, return, rival-government, and army-neutrality sequence behind Khomeini's rise.
- Grounded the clerical-republican state design explicitly in *velayat-e faqih* and the subordination of opposition to religious authority.
- Preserved uncertainty over coalition understanding, counterfactual U.S. contact, advance knowledge of the embassy seizure, and distributed responsibility.
- Added his Qom formation, anti-colonial breakthrough, Najaf cassette network, cleric-bazaar base, and the earlier forty-day protest cycle.
