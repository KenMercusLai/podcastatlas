# Byzantine Empire / 拜占庭帝国

## Overview

The Byzantine Empire appears in the wiki as both the eastern Roman power that reconquered North Africa and held [[Ceuta]] as a remote frontier, and the eleventh-century imperial system whose armies, court ranks, wealth, and succession politics shaped [[HaraldHardrada|Harald Hardrada]].

## Current Profile

The Ceuta source presents Byzantium as the bridge between Vandal North Africa and Islamic conquest. Justinian's armies defeated the Vandals in 533-534, after which Ceuta became a far-western outpost that reportedly held until the early eighth-century advance.

The Harald source reveals a different imperial scale. Byzantine fleets and armies campaigned against corsairs, Armenians, Sicilian powers, Bulgars, and other rivals; the [[VarangianGuard|Varangian Guard]] incorporated foreign warriors and could reward service with command, rank, and wealth. At Constantinople, dynastic legitimacy around Zoe, succession from Michael IV to Michael V, popular revolt, monastic refuge, blinding, and restoration show that military institutions were inseparable from palace and urban politics. Harald's later use of Byzantine experience and treasure in Norway demonstrates how imperial resources could be converted into power far beyond the empire.

## Key Characteristics

- Eastern Roman state capable of reconquest and far-flung frontier defense from North Africa to the eastern Mediterranean.
- Multi-theater military system that incorporated foreign specialists through the Varangian Guard.
- Court hierarchy able to convert foreign service into rank, wealth, and portable prestige.
- Dynastic and urban political order in which popular legitimacy, palace intrigue, military allegiance, monasteries, and ritual status could decide succession crises.
- Imperial center whose people, money, and practices affected political formation outside Byzantine territory.

## Qualifications

The current page remains source-bounded and discontinuous: one source covers Ceuta from late antiquity into Islamic conquest, while the other focuses on Harald's eleventh-century service and the crisis of 1042. Harald's exact itinerary, promotions, treasure, court involvement, imprisonment, and escape are reconstructed from evidence of unequal reliability and should not be generalized into a complete account of Byzantine military or political institutions.

## What Changed

- Expanded the profile from a North African frontier power to an eleventh-century military and court system.
- Added foreign-service incorporation, imperial rank, and wealth as mechanisms with political effects outside the empire.
- Added the 1042 succession crisis as evidence for the interaction of dynasty, city, military, and ritual legitimacy.
