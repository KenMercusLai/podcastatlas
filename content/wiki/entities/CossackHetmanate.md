# Cossack Hetmanate / 哥萨克酋长国

## Overview

The Cossack Hetmanate, also called the Zaporozhian Host in the episode, was the military-political order that emerged from the 1648 Khmelnitsky rebellion and later entered a vassal relationship with the Russian tsar. During the [[GreatNorthernWar|Great Northern War]], its incomplete autonomy made [[IvanMazepa|Ivan Mazepa]]'s office both powerful and precarious.

## Current Profile

The episode places the Hetmanate in a frontier society shaped by Polish-Lithuanian rule, Ruthenian peasantry, Jewish towns, landholding elites, steppe warfare, and Cossack communities with a distinct corporate identity. Its relationship with Moscow is neither full independence nor simple administrative absorption: the hetman must deliver loyalty and military service while defending local privileges and authority.

Mazepa's 1708 defection exposes that unstable arrangement. His inability to bring most Cossacks immediately to Charles, followed by the destruction of [[Baturin]] and Russian control of movement, shows how patronage, coercion, divided allegiance, and military timing limit the polity's room for maneuver.

## Key Characteristics

- It grows from a frontier military society into a territorial political order after the Khmelnitsky rebellion.
- Its autonomy is constrained by vassalage to the Russian tsar and wartime service demands.
- The hetman mediates between imperial patrons, officers, local communities, and competing confessional and social interests.
- Internal allegiance is divisible; Mazepa's decision does not automatically carry the whole Cossack body with him.
- Baturin's destruction demonstrates how imperial violence can close an autonomy bid before it becomes a broad mobilization.

## Qualifications

The episode provides a compressed background designed to explain Mazepa's choice, not a complete social or constitutional history. Terminology, territorial boundaries, relations among different Cossack groups, peasant and town perspectives, Jewish experience, and the political meaning of vassalage require broader sources.
