## Current Profile
The source positions Yan as the specialty-side counterpart to emergency physician [[LuXiaoEmergencyPhysician|卢骁]]. His contribution is not to dismiss early emergency judgment but to deepen it: reconstruct whether unconsciousness preceded trauma, examine consciousness and limb function, use imaging and history to localize disease, and distinguish a condition that resembles stroke from cervical or vascular causes.

He also explains why urgency does not automatically make immediate surgery safest. An anticoagulated head-injury patient may need coagulation correction or platelets because uncontrolled intraoperative bleeding can defeat the purpose of rapid intervention. His public-education boundary is similarly cautious: searchable information can clarify principles, but individual outcomes and clinical judgment cannot be copied from a single case.

## Key Characteristics
- Prioritizes causal and anatomical clarification after emergency stabilization.
- Uses consciousness, orientation, limb movement, chronology, recent procedures, and imaging as linked diagnostic evidence.
- Treats cross-specialty disagreement as an opportunity to reconcile rescue urgency with procedure-specific feasibility and harm.
- Emphasizes plain-language explanation when families ask why surgery or another intervention cannot occur immediately.
- Describes neurosurgical independence as the result of prolonged supervised practice rather than short-term information acquisition.
- Values younger clinicians' information access and technical learning while preserving the role of accumulated clinical events.
