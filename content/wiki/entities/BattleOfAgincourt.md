# Battle of Agincourt

## Overview

The Battle of Agincourt is the 25 October 1415 English victory whose tactical interaction, French destruction, prisoner killing, religious interpretation, and political afterlife connect battlefield history to national myth.

## Current Profile

The battle's location and the armies' condition emerged from [[CampaignLogisticsBattlefieldSelection|campaign logistics]]. Harfleur's delay and disease weakened the English, while French defense of the Somme forced them inland beyond an eight-day food plan. Once Henry crossed at unguarded fords, the French could stop dispersing along the river and concentrate ahead of the road to Calais.

The tactical source explains the result as [[TerrainConstrainedCombinedArms|an interaction]] among wet ploughed clay, narrowing woods, English archers and stakes, a thin defensive line, failed French cavalry, crowding, exhaustion, and disrupted French command. The French were not simply ignorant of English tactics: their original combined-arms plan had logic, but terrain and aristocratic precedence displaced missile troops and weakened execution. Exact force ratios remain disputed even though the English are presented as materially outnumbered.

The battle killed members of the French royal family, high command, aristocracy, and local Picard governing class while sending hundreds or perhaps thousands into captivity. It also included Henry's order to kill prisoners when he feared renewed attack. The sources keep this act, looting, stripped bodies, grief, shame, and the Duke of Orleans's long imprisonment beside the English triumph.

For Henry, the victory validated tactics and kingship under divine favor. Its London celebration strengthened [[LancastrianLegitimacy]] and helped create a durable national myth that exceeded the popular resonance of earlier English victories.

## Key Characteristics

- English victory emerging from the interaction of ground, archery, stakes, formation, timing, and French coordination failure.
- Confrontation produced by siege delay, English supply limits, French river denial, and post-crossing concentration.
- Catastrophic loss of French military, aristocratic, and administrative leadership despite disputed army and casualty totals.
- Much smaller English loss credited partly to Henry's command decisions and the constricted battlefield.
- Battlefield followed by captivity, looting, grief, and memorial poetry.
- Controversial English killing of prisoners during a perceived renewed threat.
- Victory interpreted through divine favor, saintly symbolism, propaganda, and public ceremony to stabilize the Lancastrian regime and build national myth.

## Qualifications

Army, prisoner, casualty, arrow, route, and timing estimates vary and are not independently audited here. William Bardolph's pre-battle warning conveys alarm but not reliable French totals; the battle source preserves English numerical inferiority without settling whether the ratio was near two-to-one or substantially smaller. National myth should not erase French suffering, the prisoner killings, or the practical and contingent campaign choices behind victory.

## What Changed

- Added the siege, supply, river, route, and concentration chain that selected the battlefield and weakened the English before deployment.
- Distinguished contemporary alarm about French numbers from reliable force estimates.
