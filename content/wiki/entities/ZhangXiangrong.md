# Zhang Xiangrong / 张向荣

## Overview

Zhang Xiangrong / 张向荣 appears in the bounded sources as the author of [[XiangruiBook|《祥瑞》]] and as a Chinese-department graduate discussing the history, curriculum, limits, enduring value, and reading practices of humanities education.

## Current Profile

The Wang Mang source uses Zhang's book to reconstruct late Western Han legitimacy through Confucian politics, auspicious omens, consort-kin power, reform, and dynastic collapse. The Weird History episode shifts from his historical writing to educational autobiography. Zhang distinguishes Chinese-department research and theory from writer training, describes how literary theory once estranged him from reading, and explains how history, Confucian learning, and Western philosophy later helped restore an integrated intellectual life.

The new episode title identifies Zhang as Lu's co-speaker on learning classical Chinese and modern languages. Its classical-reading branch favors annotated editions, interest-led entry, tolerance of unresolved words, broad cultural knowledge, and extensive reading over conspicuous archaizing or examination-centered grammar labels. This extends the educational autobiography into a practical account of [[ClassicalProseReading|classical-prose reading]]. The supplied internal summary calls this speaker 张大白, however, so the attribution remains explicitly qualified.

Across the three sources, Zhang's profile is interpretive rather than narrowly disciplinary. He is concerned with how inherited intellectual systems are reorganized by institutions, how moral ideals can become political technologies, and how reading across fields and periods can repair the fragmentation produced by formal specialization.

## Key Characteristics

- Writes intellectual and political history through legitimacy, Confucian ideals, omens, and institutional consequences.
- Resists flat portraits of Wang Mang as either pure usurper or modern reformer.
- Describes Chinese-department education as research, theory, criticism, linguistics, and textual study rather than author training.
- Uses personal educational experience to defend interest, cross-disciplinary learning, and revisable life paths.
- Treats literature's influence as difficult to quantify but consequential for judgment and ways of living.
- Treats classical-Chinese competence as contextual reading and cultural accumulation rather than performance through obscure diction.

## Qualifications

This page is bounded to one podcast discussion of Zhang's book and two Weird History education or language conversations. It does not establish a complete bibliography, career history, representative account of Chinese departments, or validated classical-Chinese curriculum. Historical claims attributed to *Xiangrui*, retrospective accounts of university experience, reading recommendations, and claims about grammar or memorization remain source-scoped. The episode 23 title names Zhang Xiangrong while its internal summary names Zhang Dabai; this page records only the title-grounded attribution and does not merge those identities.
