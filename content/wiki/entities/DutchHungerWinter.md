# Dutch Hunger Winter

## Overview

The Dutch Hunger Winter is represented as a World War II famine caused by a Nazi blockade, during which doctors gathered data on pregnant women and later followed exposed families.

## Current Profile

The episode treats the famine as a natural experiment: the exposure was not ethically assigned by researchers, yet its timing and follow-up allowed later study of health, hospitalization, labor-market, weight, and gene-methylation outcomes. This gives the case evidentiary value without making the underlying catastrophe a research intervention.

Its findings cannot simply be transferred to the [[MinnesotaStarvationExperiment]]. Prenatal exposure, adult experimental restriction, wartime conditions, duration, severity, and available follow-up differ. Claims that effects extend across generations are especially consequential and require stronger support than the structured episode summary provides.

## Key Characteristics

- Originated in imposed wartime famine rather than deliberate research exposure.
- Included data gathered from pregnant women and later family follow-up.
- Supports longitudinal inquiry into health and socioeconomic consequences of severe malnutrition.
- Is described as showing gene-methylation differences and possible later-generation effects.
- Differs materially from controlled starvation in healthy adult male volunteers.

## Qualifications

The page relies on one structured podcast summary rather than famine records, cohort methods, individual studies, effect sizes, replication evidence, or a review of epigenetic inheritance. A natural experiment reduces the ethical problem of deliberate assignment but not confounding, selective survival, measurement, historical, or causal-inference limits. “Intergenerational” and “transgenerational” should not be treated as interchangeable without study-specific evidence.

- Created a bounded profile separating the famine's historical harm from its later use as observational evidence.
