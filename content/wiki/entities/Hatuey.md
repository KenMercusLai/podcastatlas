## Overview

Hatuey was a Taino resistance leader remembered for opposing Spanish expansion from Hispaniola into Cuba and for stories that made gold and forced conversion symbols of colonial violence.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Hatuey as a leader pursued during [[DiegoVelazquezDeCuellar|Diego Velazquez de Cuellar]]'s conquest of Cuba. In the best-known traditions, he warned Cuban Tainos that Spaniards worshipped gold and later refused baptism before execution because he did not want an afterlife among Spaniards.

These stories preserve a sharp Indigenous moral indictment, but the episode does not establish their wording or exact historicity. Hatuey's secure significance in this bounded source is therefore dual: he represents organized resistance to the [[SpanishCaribbeanExpansion|Spanish Caribbean expansion chain]], and his remembered speech exposes how conquest joined material extraction to religious claims.

## Key Characteristics

- Taino leader who resisted Spanish movement from Hispaniola into Cuba.
- Political opponent pursued during Velazquez's conquest campaign.
- Figure associated with a critique of Spanish fixation on gold.
- Executed captive whose reported baptism refusal challenged the conquerors' Christian legitimacy.
- Historical person whose best-known words survive as morally powerful but source-uncertain traditions.

## Evidence

- Resistance and pursuit: [[384-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-adventure-begins-part-1-glt8046611029]] places Hatuey in the violent conquest of Cuba led by Velazquez and his associates.
- Gold critique: [[384-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-adventure-begins-part-1-glt8046611029]] recounts the remembered warning that Spaniards treated gold as a god.
- Execution and conversion: [[384-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-adventure-begins-part-1-glt8046611029]] presents his alleged refusal of baptism while explicitly preserving uncertainty about the story's exact form.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to one retrospective podcast episode. It does not independently establish Hatuey's route, following, campaign chronology, execution circumstances, exact words, or the transmission history of the gold and baptism stories. Their evidentiary uncertainty does not erase the documented colonial setting of violence, forced labor, dispossession, and conversion pressure.
