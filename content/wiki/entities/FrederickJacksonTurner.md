# Frederick Jackson Turner

## Overview

Frederick Jackson Turner was an American historian whose 1893 frontier thesis argued that successive frontiers helped shape American democracy, freedom, self-reliance, violence, and difference from Europe.

## Current Profile

[[42-the-wild-west-glt7464073840]] presents Turner less as the final authority on western history than as a durable interpreter of American national identity. His thesis made the frontier a formative national process, but the episode qualifies its single moving line with multiple frontiers, border zones, Indigenous expansion and conflict, migration, exchange, and the racial and economic diversity of western labor.

## Key Characteristics

- Formulated the frontier thesis in 1893.
- Linked frontier experience to democracy, freedom, self-reliance, violence, and American distinctiveness.
- Converted western expansion into a broad explanation of national character.
- Remains influential as historiography even where the model is empirically simplified.

## Evidence

### Frontier interpretation

- [[42-the-wild-west-glt7464073840]] summarizes Turner's claim that the frontier formed American political culture and difference from Europe.

### Later qualification

- [[42-the-wild-west-glt7464073840]] contrasts a single frontier line with multiple contact zones, peoples, economies, and competing expansions.

## Qualifications

The source offers a conversational summary rather than Turner's full text or a survey of later scholarship. It does not establish how much weight should be assigned to the frontier relative to slavery, Indigenous sovereignty, state institutions, capitalism, immigration, region, or transnational comparison.
