# Seven Years' War

## Overview

The Seven Years' War was an overlapping global conflict in North America, Europe, the Atlantic, West Africa, the Caribbean, and India that shifted imperial power toward Britain while creating fiscal, territorial, and political burdens that helped destabilize the postwar order.

## Current Profile

The sources now treat the war as a set of connected but not centrally synchronized struggles. Fighting opened in the North American backcountry before formal declarations; Prussia and allied armies tied down French power in Europe; British fleets, blockade, and overseas expeditions operated across the Atlantic; and company commanders pursued a partly autonomous contest in Bengal. Britain's [[FiscalMilitaryState|fiscal-military state]] joined low-cost public borrowing to naval supply, Prussian subsidy, continental coalition, and expedition finance, allowing global reach without withdrawing from the European balance of power.

The result was a decisive but unstable imperial expansion rather than a clean triumph. Britain gained Canada and its [[BeaverFurTrade|beaver trade]], Caribbean and West African positions bound to slavery, and a stronger [[EastIndiaCompany|East India Company]] position after a Plassey campaign dependent on Indian financiers, elite conflict, and bribery. It also inherited debt, Quebec's French Catholic population, western-security obligations, Native resistance, and a standing military burden. Removing France made colonists less dependent on British protection while Whitehall tightened settlement, customs, and revenue policy; France's recovery spending and Britain's postwar taxation therefore made the conflict part of the causal background to both the [[AmericanRevolution|American]] and [[FrenchRevolution|French Revolutions]].

## Key Characteristics

- Overlapping global war whose theaters were connected by rivalry and resources but retained local actors and command dynamics.
- European coalition struggle in which Prussia and British-allied forces helped shield Britain's overseas strategy.
- Maritime and financial war sustained by blockade, public credit, subsidy, and expedition logistics.
- Imperial transfer that expanded British power in Canada, the Caribbean, West Africa, and India.
- Conflict entangled with Native alliances and resistance, enslaved labor, slave trading, Indian finance, and company corruption.
- British victory that removed an external threat while enlarging debt, territory, troop requirements, and administrative burdens.
- Precondition for the [[ProclamationLine1763|Proclamation Line of 1763]], colonial taxation crisis, and wider revolutionary fiscal strain.

## Qualifications

These popular-history accounts remain strongest on British strategy and North American consequences. The wider synthesis gives Indian and Native actors less independent attention than British policymakers, commanders, and institutions. Causal claims about later Anglophone power, Prussia's future, and two revolutions identify important pathways rather than sufficient causes; fiscal totals, negotiated preferences, motives, and the relative weight of finance, coalition warfare, and battle remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Reframed the war from a mainly North American postwar hinge into an overlapping global conflict with European, maritime, Caribbean, West African, and Indian theaters.
- Added the fiscal-military mechanism joining public credit, subsidy, blockade, naval power, and expedition capacity.
- Qualified imperial victory through slavery, company corruption, Native resistance, governance cost, and revolutionary blowback.
