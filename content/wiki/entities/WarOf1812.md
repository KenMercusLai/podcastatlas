# War of 1812

## Overview

The War of 1812 appears in the wiki as the conflict setting for [[StarSpangledBanner|The Star-Spangled Banner]] and as a North American war whose memory diverges across the United States, Britain, and Canada.

## Current Profile

The sources use the war both to explain the anthem's battlefield scene and to expose asymmetrical Anglo-American attention. They describe causes including British trade restrictions, impressment, American ambitions toward Canada, conflict involving Native American allies of Britain, and American national-unity hopes. Britain treated the conflict as secondary while fighting Napoleon; failed U.S. invasions of Canada and the later Chesapeake campaign culminated in the burning of Washington and the September 1814 attack on [[FortMcHenry]] that [[FrancisScottKey]] turned into poetry.

The anthem source also makes the war part of slavery history. British commanders used escaped enslaved people as guides, informants, and soldiers, while American slaveholders feared the political and military meaning of armed Black men in British service. The rivalry episode adds the memory outcome: Washington's destruction and abandoned U.S. war aims did not prevent Fort McHenry and the post-treaty Battle of New Orleans from supporting a national story of survival or defiance.

## Key Characteristics

- Anglo-American war remembered unevenly in Britain, the United States, and Canada.
- Conflict with causes including trade restrictions, impressment, Canada ambitions, frontier conflict, and national-unity politics.
- Canadian identity case because failed U.S. invasions and defense against the United States became foundational in the source's account.
- Chesapeake campaign setting for the burning of Washington and the attack on Baltimore.
- Example of asymmetrical attention because Britain treated the war as secondary to Europe while it became nation-making memory in North America.
- Immediate historical context for the Fort McHenry flag scene and the anthem poem.
- Slavery and freedom conflict because escaped enslaved people reached British lines and some fought in the [[ColonialMarines]].

## Evidence

- Causes and memory: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] lists trade restrictions, impressment, Canada, Native American alliances, and U.S. unity as causes while calling the war obscure in Britain and ambiguous in U.S. memory.
- Canada branch: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says failed American invasions helped make the war foundational in Canadian identity.
- Chesapeake campaign: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] follows British action from coastal attacks to Washington and then Baltimore.
- Power and memory asymmetry: [[263-usa-vs-england-the-200-year-rivalry-glt7546211559]] contrasts Britain's Napoleonic priority with later American emphasis on Fort McHenry and New Orleans.
- Washington and settlement: [[263-usa-vs-england-the-200-year-rivalry-glt7546211559]] recounts the 1814 British advance, destruction of public buildings, and the hosts' interpretation that it pressured Madison toward terms and abandonment of original aims.
- Anthem setting: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says the bombardment of [[FortMcHenry]] on 13-14 September 1814 supplied the rockets, bombs, dawn, and flag imagery.
- Slavery branch: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says British forces welcomed escaped enslaved people and organized some male runaways into the [[ColonialMarines]].

## Qualifications

This page remains source-scoped to two popular-history episodes. It does not cover the full chronology, the Treaty of Ghent in detail, Indigenous diplomacy and loss, U.S. domestic politics beyond the host summaries, or the wider Canadian and British military record. The relationship between battlefield outcomes, treaty terms, and later national claims of victory remains qualified rather than settled here.

## What Changed

- Reframed the war as both an anthem origin and a case of unequal British-American attention.
- Added Washington, New Orleans, and selective national memory while preserving uncertainty over victory claims.
