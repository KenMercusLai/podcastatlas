# King John

## Overview

King John was king of England from 1199 to 1216 and the ruler compelled to accept [[MagnaCarta|Magna Carta]] during baronial rebellion in 1215.

## Current Profile

The episode portrays John as a ruler whose military and political failures interacted. Loss of much of the Angevin inheritance, defeat at Bouvines, and sharply increased extraction left him unable to satisfy the barons whose resources he needed. His arbitrary exercise of authority and reputation for bad faith made the resulting fiscal conflict a crisis of trust and lawful rule.

The settlement at Runnymede was not a voluntary constitutional gift. Armed rebels holding London forced John to concede revenue limits, legal protections, and an enforcement council empowered to act against him. His repudiation and renewed war show both why the 1215 text failed immediately and why later reissues under his son, rather than John's compliance, secured its survival.

## Key Characteristics

- English monarch ruling from 1199 until his death in 1216.
- Lost major continental territories and suffered the politically damaging defeat at Bouvines.
- Raised revenue aggressively in pursuit of continental recovery.
- Exercised royal authority in ways opponents experienced as arbitrary and untrustworthy.
- Accepted Magna Carta under armed pressure and then failed to observe the settlement.

## Evidence

- Military and fiscal failure: [[62-magna-carta-glt5613809659]] connects territorial loss and Bouvines to exceptional extraction from England.
- Crisis of rule: [[62-magna-carta-glt5613809659]] presents civil war, rebel control of London, and the armed meeting at Runnymede as consequences of distrust as well as taxation.
- Settlement and repudiation: [[62-magna-carta-glt5613809659]] describes the twenty-five barons' enforcement power, John's noncompliance, renewed war, and the charter's later reissues.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to one public-history episode centered on Magna Carta, not a complete assessment of John's reign. The episode distinguishes classical tyranny or rule by personal will from modern mass-murdering dictatorship, and its taxation multiple, character judgments, site traditions, and weighting of fiscal versus constitutional causes remain source-scoped.
