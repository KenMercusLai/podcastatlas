# The Birth of a Nation

## Overview

*The Birth of a Nation* is [[DWGriffith|D. W. Griffith's]] 1915 adaptation of [[ThomasDixonJr|Thomas Dixon Jr.'s]] Reconstruction fiction, remembered in the bounded source as both a technically foundational film and a powerful work of white-supremacist propaganda.

## Current Profile

The film turned [[LostCauseMyth|Lost Cause mythology]] into emotionally forceful mass cinema. It represented Black citizenship and Reconstruction as social catastrophe while making the [[KuKluxKlan]] heroic. NAACP protest and public controversy did not prevent major commercial success or exhibition at the Wilson White House.

Its organizational afterlife is central here. Cross-burning and visual Klan conventions presented as historical tradition were fictional constructions that [[WilliamJosephSimmons]] adopted during the second Klan's 1915 relaunch. The film therefore acted not only as memory propaganda but as a repertoire from which a new movement built ceremony and identity.

## Key Characteristics

- Joined feature-film innovation to a racist account of Reconstruction.
- Gave Lost Cause mythology national emotional and visual reach.
- Attracted both organized civil-rights protest and elite attention.
- Achieved commercial success despite or partly through controversy.
- Supplied invented ritual imagery later performed by the second Klan.

## Qualifications

The page is based on one synthetic episode, not a full film history or reception study. Aesthetic importance and racist political function can coexist, but claims about precise audience effects, direct recruitment, the White House screening, and the source of each visual convention require specialist evidence. The film amplified conditions favorable to revival; it did not alone create the second Klan.

## What Changed

- Created the film as both a cultural artifact and an organizational resource for the second Klan.
- Distinguished technical influence from moral or historical legitimacy.
