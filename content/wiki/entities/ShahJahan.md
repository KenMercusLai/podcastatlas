# Shah Jahan

## Overview

Shah Jahan was a Mughal emperor and the patron of the [[TajMahal]], commissioned after the death of his favored wife [[MumtazMahal]]. The source profile holds personal grief, formal kingship, architectural ambition, succession violence, imprisonment, and posthumous burial together.

## Current Profile

Born Prince Khurram, Shah Jahan married Arjumand Banu Begum, later titled Mumtaz Mahal, within the politically influential family of Nur Jahan. The episode describes the marriage as unusually close: Mumtaz accompanied him on campaigns and bore fourteen children, seven of whom survived to adulthood. Her death on 17 June 1631 during childbirth prompted a conspicuous period of mourning and the project that became the Taj Mahal.

The monument also served royal ideology. Through [[MonumentalDynasticLegitimation]], Timurid and earlier Mughal funerary precedents, immense refinement, and [[TajMahalParadiseSymbolism]], Shah Jahan presented himself as an ideal king as well as a bereaved husband. His illness in 1657 triggered war among his sons; [[Aurangzeb]] prevailed, confined him at Agra Fort for eight years, and buried him beside Mumtaz after his death.

## Key Characteristics

- Mughal ruler presented as more religiously orthodox than Jahangir but not as a fundamentalist.
- Husband whose documented attachment to Mumtaz and visible mourning underpin the Taj Mahal's emotional interpretation.
- Architectural patron who used funerary design to join commemoration, lineage, piety, and perfect kingship.
- Emperor whose highly formal court made his withdrawal in grief politically conspicuous.
- Defeated father in a succession war, imprisoned by Aurangzeb and buried beside Mumtaz.

## Evidence

- Marriage and grief: [[362-the-taj-mahal-love-and-death-glt2445608477]] describes Mumtaz's campaign travel, fourteen births, death in 1631, and Shah Jahan's withdrawal and mourning dress.
- Architectural patronage: [[362-the-taj-mahal-love-and-death-glt2445608477]] connects the Taj Mahal to earlier Mughal and Timurid tombs, paradise imagery, dynastic legitimacy, and the ideal king.
- Final years: [[362-the-taj-mahal-love-and-death-glt2445608477]] follows the 1657 illness, succession struggle, eight-year imprisonment, death, and burial.

## Qualifications

Court histories praising the marriage were also products of an imperial court, although the episode treats the grief as genuine. Emotional motive and political self-presentation are not mutually exclusive. The “Black Taj” is not accepted as an evidenced plan, and the source does not provide a complete assessment of Shah Jahan's wider reign.

## What Changed

- Established a profile joining marital grief to monumental and dynastic self-presentation.
- Qualified the “Black Taj” story and retained succession violence as part of the architectural patron's legacy.
