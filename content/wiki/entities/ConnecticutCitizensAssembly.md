## Overview

The Connecticut Citizens' Assembly was a 100-resident deliberative body convened to recommend reforms to Connecticut's property-tax system. More than 200,000 invitations produced about 1,500 positive responses, from which organizers used demographic configurations and a lottery process to compose the group.

## Current Profile

The assembly combined four weekends, two online sessions, participant compensation, expert instruction, self-authored discussion rules, and collective voting. It produced about ten recommendations, including a 1% tax on annual income above $1 million, consolidation of some school services, and interstate tolls intended to reduce or eliminate vehicle taxes. Its experience supports the feasibility of informed citizen deliberation but does not establish policy effectiveness because the body could recommend rather than legislate.

## Key Characteristics

- It used random selection within demographic composition rather than election or open self-selection alone.
- It treated policy education and procedural rule-making as prerequisites for recommendation.
- It brought homeowners, renters, parents, non-parents, and residents from different regions into one process.
- It produced concrete tax, revenue, and service-sharing proposals despite ideological disagreement.
- It had advisory status and therefore depended on the comptroller and legislature for publication and implementation.

## Qualifications

- The supplied source is a journalistic episode summary, not the assembly's selection protocol, full report, minutes, or legislative record.
- Some members disputed whether recommendations had been changed without adequate discussion, including reduction of a proposed high-earner tax from 4% to 1%.
- The episode ends before legislative consideration, so it cannot show whether the recommendations changed taxes, services, budgets, or public trust over time.
- Participant friendship and learning are reported observations rather than controlled measures of polarization or policy competence.

## What Changed

- Added the assembly as a concrete case of sortition-based fiscal deliberation.
- Recorded both its learning capacity and its lack of direct implementation power.
- Preserved the recommendation-revision and publication-delay disputes as limits on procedural legitimacy.
