# Joseph-Ignace Guillotin

## Overview

Joseph-Ignace Guillotin was a French physician and social reformer whose proposal for equal, mechanical execution gave the guillotine his name despite his preference for abolishing capital punishment.

## Current Profile

The source presents Guillotin as a practical Enlightenment reformer rather than the machine's sole inventor. A former Jesuit trainee involved in poverty, orphan, foundling, and public-health work, he helped identify the Tennis Court meeting place in June 1789 and later argued that rank should no longer determine execution method. Ridicule pushed him away from implementation, leaving [[AntoineLouis|Antoine Louis]] and [[TobiasSchmidt|Tobias Schmidt]] to turn the proposal into machinery.

## Key Characteristics

- He preferred abolition but pursued harm reduction when abolition lacked Assembly support.
- He joined penal equality to the proposal for mechanical beheading.
- His wider reform work concerned poverty, children, and sickness among the poor.
- His name became inseparable from a machine he did not personally design or build.

- [[505-the-french-revolution-the-shadow-of-the-guillotine-part-3-glt1620539569]] describes his October-November 1789 proposals for equal punishment and swift decapitation by a simple mechanism.

### Political and social-reform context

- [[505-the-french-revolution-the-shadow-of-the-guillotine-part-3-glt1620539569]] connects him to the Tennis Court, medicine, Jesuit formation, and committees serving vulnerable people.

## Qualifications

- The source does not make Guillotin the technical inventor; Louis supervised design and Schmidt built the machine.
- Supporting a less painful method while preferring abolition still placed him inside an institution of state execution.

## What Changed

- A canonical profile now separates Guillotin's reform proposal from the machine's construction and later Terror symbolism.
