# Kevin Cokley

## Overview
Kevin Cokley is a psychologist represented here through a [[HiddenBrain]] discussion of the [[ImpostorSyndrome|impostor phenomenon]], identity, achievement, institutional climate, and the boundary between motivating and harmful self-doubt.

## Current Profile
Cokley combines research interpretation with his own academic experience. He describes beginning a faculty role with a thin publication record, feeling conspicuous as one of relatively few Black professors, and managing dress and speech to project authority. Later accomplishment did not eliminate doubt, so he uses publication and citation records as deliberate evidence against selective self-criticism rather than assuming recognition will automatically become self-trust.

His practical stance is neither confidence-only reassurance nor celebration of relentless work. People should test doubt against evidence, supervisors should normalize fallibility, and schools should help students treat failure as a bounded event. At the same time, [[CalibratedSelfDoubt]] can preserve humility and preparation only while health, rest, opportunity, and identity remain protected.

## Key Characteristics
- Prefers “impostor phenomenon” because it describes an experience without implying a clinical syndrome.
- Connects personal self-doubt to competitive environments, social identity, stereotypes, praise, and institutional belonging.
- Uses objective achievement records to counter selective attention to errors and minimized success.
- Encourages leaders to model fallibility so mistakes do not automatically signal non-belonging.
- Treats hard work as one possible response to doubt while warning that over-preparation and work without rest can become unhealthy.
- Preserves both sides of confidence calibration: excessive doubt can paralyze capable people, while insufficient doubt can harden into hubris.

## Qualifications
This profile is bounded to one structured episode summary rather than Cokley's complete scholarship, publications, clinical views, or institutional work. The reported group findings and causal explanations require the underlying studies, and his personal strategy of responding through intensive preparation is not a universal recommendation. Public examples and the John Henry story are explanatory illustrations, not diagnostic or causal evidence.
