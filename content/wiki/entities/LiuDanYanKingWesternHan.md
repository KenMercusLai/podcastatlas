## Overview

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-399-hanwudi-tuogu-dachen-shengsi-neidou-mubiao-gandiao-huoguang-lgiwuhupmd0dox-oaoxjfj-smlw5|Hanji 399]] then shows his coalition-building role: Liu Dan sends repeated gifts and envoys to Chang'an, and his name becomes the mask for a forged memorial accusing Huo of possible treason. ## Current Profile

The combined sources now show Liu Dan as more than a later legal label. Hanji 390-2 and Hanji 393-1 make his claim psychologically and politically earlier than the formal anti-Huo coalition. As Han Wudi's third son, he begins from a marginal position behind Liu Ju and Liu Hong, but their deaths turn his senior surviving-son status into an imagined claim. His request to enter Chang'an as a filial palace guard is punished as a succession move, so after Han Wudi dies he is already primed to read edict form, seal packaging, regent control, Han Zhao's youth, and even Han Zhao's paternity as signs of usurpation rather than ordinary succession.

Hanji 399 presents him as a remote royal claimant who cultivates Chang'an partners through gifts, jewelry, horses, and repeated envoy contact before conspirators use a memorial in his name to attack Huo Guang. Hanji 400-1 then presents him as a claimant who still sees himself as plausible successor material: he stresses that he is the late emperor's eldest surviving son, interprets Chang'an unrest as evidence that Huo Guang is vulnerable, and believes vacancies or weakness among senior officials make the moment favorable. His own Yan chancellor Ping warns him that unreliable partners and past failure should make him cautious, but Liu Dan orders preparation instead.

The same episode also shows Liu Dan's vulnerability inside the coalition. [[ShangguanAnWesternHan|上官安]] secretly plans to kill him after using him and then install [[ShangguanJieWesternHan|上官桀]], so Liu Dan is both claimant and disposable instrument. Once the conspiracy is exposed through Yan Cang, Yang Chang, and [[DuYannianWesternHan|杜延年]], Liu Dan considers immediate rebellion but is dissuaded by his chancellor. Han Zhao's formal rebuke leaves him no political exit; he holds a farewell feast and uses the royal seal cord to kill himself. Hanji 402-1 then shows the downstream legal afterlife: the rebellion remains the context for Sang Hongyang's execution and the harsher treatment of rebellion-adjacent actors.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han Yan king and seniority claimant whose succession grievance begins before Han Wudi's death, hardens after the child-emperor accession, and turns into a later attack on Han Zhao's birth legitimacy.
- Remote coalition-builder whose gifts and envoys connect Yan ambition to Chang'an anti-Huo actors before his name is used as the mask for a forged memorial against Huo Guang.
- Anti-Huo rebel who joins a plan to kill Huo Guang and depose Han Zhao.
- Ambitious interpreter of political signs, treating senior lineage, Chang'an unrest, and official vacancies as opportunity.
- Poor coalition reader whose supposed allies plan to use and eliminate him.
- Suicide case after imperial rebuke, while his son Liu Jian is spared death and reduced to commoner status.
- Charge background for Sang Hongyang's execution and later legal-political case expansion.

## Qualifications

This page is bounded to Hanji 390-2, Hanji 393-1, Hanji 399, Hanji 400-1, and Hanji 402-1. It does not reconstruct Liu Dan's full lineage, Liu Hong's biography, the complete Wugu affair, the primary-source edict and seal procedure, the full rumor tradition around Han Zhao's birth, complete earlier conspiracy with Liu Ze, every Yan-state official, the full legal procedure against his household, or later treatment of Liu Jian beyond these episodes. Hanji 400-1 frames Han Zhao's edict as forcing Liu Dan into a political dead end; it does not separately prove every private motive inside Liu Dan's final decision.
