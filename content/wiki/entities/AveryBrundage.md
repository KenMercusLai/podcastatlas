# Avery Brundage

## Overview

Avery Brundage was an Olympic official represented in the current source as the clearest personal symbol of the [[InternationalOlympicCommittee]]'s contested political-neutrality doctrine.

## Current Profile

[[81-modern-olympics-part-2-glt4846758176]] connects Brundage to three moral crises. Before Mexico 1968, the state killed student protesters; the episode reports his dismissive claim that he had not seen the events because he was at the ballet. After Tommie Smith and John Carlos protested on the medal podium, Brundage supported their Olympic exclusion. After eleven Israeli athletes and officials were killed during the Munich 1972 hostage crisis and failed rescue, he insisted that the Games continue.

The resulting profile is not simply administrative resolve. In the episode's interpretation, Brundage shows how an institution can invoke separation of sport and politics selectively: political violence surrounding the Games need not stop them, while athletes' political expression can draw punishment.

The earlier Berlin material adds a more ambivalent role. [[hitler-s-olympics-part-9-a-plague-on-both-your-houses-hitler-s-olympics-part-9-a-plague-on-both-your-houses|Hitler’s Olympics, Part 9]] uses Brundage to represent soft idealism: compromise may preserve an international institution and its athletic stage, but it also allows an oppressive host to appropriate Olympic peace language and prestige. This does not reverse the later critical profile; it explains the continuity value that made his position institutionally durable.

[[hitler-s-olympics-part-7-long-jump-tall-tale-hitler-s-olympics-part-7-long-jump-tall-tale|Hitler’s Olympics, Part 7]] connects that institutional continuity to athlete dependence. After [[JesseOwens]] left a post-Berlin tour for paid opportunities, Brundage's amateur regime banned him from amateur sport even though sporting organizations had profited from unpaid athlete appearances. In 1968, Olympic officials then used Owens's stature in an effort to obtain apologies from Tommie Smith and John Carlos after their podium protest. The pair's refusal exposes the same selective neutrality: athlete labor and symbolic usefulness were welcomed, while professional self-support and political expression could draw punishment.

[[hitler-s-olympics-part-5-the-amateur-s-hour-hitler-s-olympics-part-5-the-amateur-s-hour|Hitler’s Olympics, Part 5]] makes the Berlin role less ambivalent. In 1934 Brundage investigated discrimination through Nazi interpreters and in officials' presence, discounted Jewish representatives' reports of exclusion and violence, and accepted formal assurances without equal training conditions. His antisemitic correspondence, description of the boycott as Jewish sabotage, IOC ambition, and later appointment lead the episode to reject simple deception. [[MargaretLambert]] then supplies the concrete test: the regime coerced her candidacy, denied equal preparation, and excluded her after record-level performance.

## Key Characteristics

- Associated with strict defense of Olympic continuity.
- Used or embodied a doctrine separating sport from politics.
- Responded punitively to the Mexico 1968 podium protest.
- Is portrayed as morally inadequate toward repression before Mexico 1968 and murder at Munich 1972.
- Illustrates the selective application of institutional neutrality.
- Represents compromise that preserved Olympic continuity while accepting politically controlled athlete access and risking authoritarian appropriation.
- Enforced amateur and protest boundaries that increased institutional control over athletes, while his Berlin inspection failed to secure equal conditions for Jewish competitors.

## Qualifications

The page is bounded to four episode accounts and does not reconstruct Brundage's complete career, motives, precise legal authority, role in every Berlin or Mexico decision, or wider archival record. “Soft idealism” is the finale's interpretive category, while Part 5 argues for knowing accommodation; the tension is retained rather than flattened. The sources find no explicit bargain tying his IOC seat to American participation and do not show that Brundage alone designed or executed every eligibility and disciplinary action.

## What Changed

- Connected economic eligibility and political discipline as forms of athlete dependence.
