# Liverpool and Manchester Railway

## Overview

The Liverpool and Manchester Railway is presented in [[140-the-birth-of-the-railways-glt2177757841]] as the intercity line whose 1829 Rainhill Trials and 1830 opening converted railway engineering into a major commercial and public event.

## Current Profile

Business communities in Liverpool and [[Manchester]] wanted more effective movement of goods and passengers between two industrial centers. The Rainhill Trials made locomotive choice a test of efficiency and commercial viability, and [[RobertStephenson]]'s *Rocket* won with a reported top speed of twenty-nine miles per hour.

The opening made the railway's promise and danger visible together. The Duke of Wellington attended, while Liverpool MP William Huskisson was struck by *Rocket* after leaving his train and later died. The episode argues that the fatal accident did not stop adoption and may have intensified public fascination by dramatizing unprecedented speed and mechanical power.

## Key Characteristics

- It was conceived as an intercity freight-and-passenger connection between major industrial centers.
- The Rainhill Trials tested locomotive performance for commercial service.
- Robert Stephenson's *Rocket* won the trials.
- Its 1830 opening was staged as a major national public occasion.
- William Huskisson's fatal accident attached danger and publicity to the railway's public image.
- It stands in the episode as the point when the industrial railway became a mass cultural fact.

## Evidence

- Commercial purpose: [[140-the-birth-of-the-railways-glt2177757841]] links the project to Liverpool and Manchester business demand for moving freight and passengers.
- Locomotive trial: [[140-the-birth-of-the-railways-glt2177757841]] describes the Rainhill competition, *Rocket*'s victory, and its reported twenty-nine-mile-per-hour speed.
- Public opening: [[140-the-birth-of-the-railways-glt2177757841]] dates the opening to 1830 and identifies the Duke of Wellington as an attendee.
- Fatality and reception: [[140-the-birth-of-the-railways-glt2177757841]] recounts Huskisson's collision and death, then argues that the railway nevertheless became an enormous public success.

## Qualifications

The source is a narrative survey and does not supply a complete corporate, financial, engineering, or operating history. The speed record, exact accident dynamics, Huskisson's intentions, and the claim that the fatality aided publicity remain source-scoped or interpretive.
