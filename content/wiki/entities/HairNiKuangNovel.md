## Overview

《头发》 is a [[NiKuang|倪匡]] novel in the [[WesleyNiKuang|卫斯理]] series that turns human origins, hair, religion, death, and immortality into parts of one extraterrestrial exile-and-return system.

## Current Profile

The bounded episode presents the novel as the series' return after a six-year interruption and as the beginning of a more explicitly science-fictional middle period. A Nepalese disappearance, a corpse whose death is uncertain, shared dreams, stolen sacred objects, a hidden underground device, and consciousness entering another body progressively reveal that death can be a transfer event and that some minds may return to an advanced world.

The central reversal makes humanity neither a naturally fallen species nor the direct product of benevolent gods. Advanced beings manufactured replacement bodies so their minds could survive bodily death; those bodies developed autonomous consciousness, rebelled, and were exiled to Earth after the original civilization disabled hair's information and control functions. Four emissaries coded as Allah, Buddha, Christ, and Dao then attempt to guide selected humans back. The hosts admire the compression of theology into speculative machinery but argue that it flattens distinct religions and leaves selection authority unexplained.

Its sharpest ethical move is Wesley's recognition that the supposed sinners were created by the very civilization now judging them. The episode sees the ending as a partial retreat from that insight because Wesley soon collaborates with the creators on removing earthly evil and entertains mass death as a possible cost. The novel therefore remains powerful as a provocation about creation, autonomy, salvation, and immortality even where its science, theology, and moral logic do not cohere.

## Key Characteristics

- Builds revelation through disappearance, ambiguous death, shared dreams, sacred objects, and a hidden transport device.
- Reimagines hair as a disabled knowledge store, communication channel, and machine interface.
- Treats soul, reincarnation, possession, and paradise as consciousness-transfer and screening mechanisms.
- Recasts four religious traditions as imperfect interventions by extraterrestrial emissaries.
- Makes autonomous humanity the product of bodies manufactured for another species' immortality project.
- Turns creator hypocrisy into its strongest moral accusation but weakens that critique in the resolution.
- Uses a six-year time difference inside the story to mirror the series' reported publication hiatus.

## Qualifications

This profile is based on one conversational retelling rather than direct comparison of editions or the complete novel. Publication history, exact plot sequence, religious identifications, quotations, time mechanics, authorial intent, and judgments about Wesley's culpability remain source-scoped. The fictional functions attributed to hair, thought waves, consciousness, death, and interstellar transport are not scientific claims, and the episode's religious criticism is itself informal rather than specialist theology.

## What Changed

- Established the novel as a distinct entity linking a mystery structure to technologized religion and consciousness transfer.
- Identified creator responsibility, rather than the hair mechanism alone, as the episode's strongest current ethical judgment.
