# János Kádár

## Overview

János Kádár was a Hungarian communist whose opening profile in the 1956 story combines complicity in László Rajk's destruction, victimization by the same Stalinist system, and elevation to party leadership during the uprising's unresolved first phase.

## Current Profile

[[719-revolution-in-the-soviet-empire-the-hungarian-uprising-part-1-glt4398850202]] introduces Kádár through the purge of [[LaszloRajk|László Rajk]]. Kádár reportedly reassured his friend that a false confession would spare his life, after which Rajk was executed. The regime then arrested, tortured, and imprisoned Kádár himself, making his early profile morally and politically double: participant in coercion and victim of it.

During the opening of the [[HungarianRevolution1956]], Kádár replaced Ernő Gerő as party leader after the Kossuth Square massacre. The episode ends before his role is resolved, so the current profile does not project his later choices backward into the uncertain first days of the uprising.

## Key Characteristics

- Operated inside the Hungarian communist leadership during the Stalinist purges.
- Helped secure Rajk's cooperation through a false assurance that his life would be spared.
- Was subsequently arrested, tortured, and imprisoned by the same regime.
- Embodies the way purge systems can consume both collaborators and targets.
- Replaced Gerő as party leader during the uprising's opening crisis.
- Remains politically unresolved at the source's endpoint.

## Evidence

- Complicity and victimization: [[719-revolution-in-the-soviet-empire-the-hungarian-uprising-part-1-glt4398850202]] connects Kádár to Rajk's false reassurance and to his own later arrest, torture, and imprisonment.
- Crisis promotion: [[719-revolution-in-the-soviet-empire-the-hungarian-uprising-part-1-glt4398850202]] places his replacement of Gerő after the Kossuth Square massacre and before the revolution's outcome was settled.

## Qualifications

This source supplies only an opening profile. It does not cover Kádár's later alignment, government, repression, or longer rule, and those subjects cannot be inferred from his early victimization or appointment. Dialogue, motives, responsibility for Rajk's confession, and Kádár's intentions on taking the party leadership remain source-scoped.
