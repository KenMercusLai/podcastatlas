# The 120 Days of Sodom

## Overview

*The 120 Days of Sodom* is an unfinished novel written by [[MarquisDeSade|the Marquis de Sade]] during his imprisonment in the Bastille in 1785.

## Current Profile

The episode treats the novel as more than a catalogue of sexual and physical cruelty. Four libertines destroy the bridge to an inaccessible castle, isolate participants and victims, and arrange narrated and enacted abuse in an escalating schedule. That closed architecture turns appetite into a system: wealth, hierarchy, seclusion, classification, repetition, and control remove outside restraint and make the work a disturbing model of organized domination.

## Key Characteristics

- Written in 37 days near the end of 1785 while Sade was imprisoned in the Bastille.
- Organized around four libertines who create an inaccessible and internally controlled castle world.
- Uses storytellers, servants, selected participants, and victims as differentiated roles within a planned hierarchy.
- Escalates toward extreme violence and murder through deliberate classification and sequence.
- Combines obscenity with [[SadeanNaturalism|anti-Christian and nature-based argument]] rather than presenting violence as untheorized impulse.
- Became a later reference point for thinking about sadism, systems of torture, and totalitarian organization.

## Qualifications

- The episode avoids most explicit content and supplies interpretation rather than a full textual analysis.
- Structural resemblance to later totalitarian violence does not establish that the novel caused or directly predicted a specific regime.
- Calling the work darkly funny, boring, revolting, or unreadable records the hosts' responses rather than settled literary judgment.
- The episode centers the author and intellectual legacy more than the victims represented within the work.

## What Changed

- Established the novel as a closed organizational model of domination rather than only a notorious obscene text.
- Separated later totalitarian analogy from a claim of direct historical causation.
- Made the episode's strong ethical and evidentiary qualifications explicit.
