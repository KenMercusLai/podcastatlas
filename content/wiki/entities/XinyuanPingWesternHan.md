## Overview

新垣平 / Xinyuan Ping is a Western Han fangshi whom the current sources present as converting claims of privileged access to qi, omens, and sacred objects into a new temple, rewards, and influence over [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]]'s ritual decisions before an accusation and investigation expose the scheme.

## Current Profile

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-225-2-weishenme-shuo-xinyuanjieyi-de-zuxian-shi-zhongguoren-lmk2n0ved6gt9tgoxs1qb2s0kuc3|Hanji 225-2]] now supplies the entry stage. Xinyuan Ping comes from Zhao, claims skill in observing qi, and says a sacred object with five-colored vapors appears northeast of Chang'an. Liu Heng responds by ordering a Five Emperors temple north of the Wei River. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-226-zhuangshen-nonggui-de-xinyuanping-shi-ruhe-bei-hanwendi-shipo-de-ljxpb9_fn-jmatlg621chtclqn3j|Hanji 226]] continues the same authority path through sacrifice, office, rewards, and access to discussions of institutions and major rites. Xinyuan Ping then arranges a stronger form of apparent verification: after predicting a precious object outside the palace, an associate presents a jade cup inscribed “人主延寿.” Prediction and proof therefore come from the same controlled scheme.

The later claims scale upward. A reported second solar culmination is linked to a restart of the reign count and public feasting, while a supposed Zhou鼎 at Fenyin prompts temple construction in anticipation of an object that has not appeared. The source does not establish the mechanism behind the solar report, so only the staged cup can be described confidently as a concrete prop-based fraud within this bounded evidence.

His fall begins not with independent imperial detection but with an unnamed petitioner's accusation. The episode says a廷尉 investigation confirms fabrication, after which Liu Heng executes Xinyuan Ping and exterminates his three kin groups. The case changes the patron as well as removing the claimant: Liu Heng reportedly loses interest in calendar, color, and spirit projects and stops personally visiting the altars.

The unfulfilled Fenyin-ding claim later acquires an afterlife. When a physical ding is reportedly excavated there under [[HanWudi|汉武帝]], local officials use Xinyuan Ping's old prediction to identify the find as possibly Zhou. The later find does not vindicate his fraud or establish that the vessel was a Zhou ding; it shows that a discredited claim can survive as an interpretive frame and shape how later evidence is reported and received. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-345-1-hanwudi-baoding-xuanan-zhijin-rengshi-weijie-zhimi-lhlqnresx40rqnl1pjyzvxuj-sge|Hanji 345-1]]

## Key Characteristics

- Fangshi who claims exclusive ability to read qi, sacred objects, and auspicious signs.
- Court entrant whose first qi-reading claim produces a Five Emperors temple before the later staged proof cycle.
- Court entrepreneur who converts unverifiable interpretation into office, wealth, ritual construction, and policy access.
- Stager of a prediction-and-confirmation loop through the prearranged jade cup.
- Escalating claimant whose signs move from a temple to chronology, public feasting, and dynastic-symbolic projects.
- Fraud defendant exposed through accusation and investigation rather than through the ruler's unaided skepticism.
- Discredited claimant whose unfulfilled Fenyin-ding story remains available to classify a later physical discovery under Han Wudi.

## Qualifications

The profile rests on three popular-history podcast notes rather than a primary-source dossier; their overlap is narrative continuity, not independent confirmation. They do not independently establish the signs, dates, rewards, ritual details, solar mechanism, confession, legal procedure, punishment scope, or the later ding's date and identity. Hanji 226's title “被汉文帝识破” compresses a sequence that the episode itself begins with external denunciation. Hanji 345-1 names [[ZhangShizhiWesternHan|张释之]] in its retelling, while the earlier supplied material renders the廷尉 as “张世芝”; the investigative identity and added interrogation detail remain source-scoped. Ancient Chinese use of the surname 新垣 does not establish Aragaki Yui's genealogy, and sharing it with [[XinyuanYan|新垣衍]] does not demonstrate kinship.

## What Changed

- Added the later afterlife of Xinyuan Ping's Fenyin-ding prediction as an interpretive frame for a physical discovery.
- Distinguished later discovery of a real vessel from validation of Xinyuan Ping's fraud or the vessel's claimed Zhou identity.
- Preserved the unresolved廷尉 name and role boundary across the retellings.
