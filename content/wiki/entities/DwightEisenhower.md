# Dwight D. Eisenhower

## Overview

Dwight D. Eisenhower was the American president whose opposition to the secret British, French, and Israeli attack during the [[SuezCrisis|Suez Crisis]] converted United States financial and diplomatic power into a forced ceasefire by its allies.

## Current Profile

The first Suez episode presents Eisenhower as opposing an Anglo-French attack because it could widen war, alienate Arab governments, and strengthen Soviet influence. He preferred negotiated guarantees for canal navigation and rejected the British assumption that alliance solidarity would ultimately outweigh those risks.

When Israel attacked and Britain and France issued their coordinated ultimatum without informing Washington, Eisenhower treated the deception as both a strategic danger and an alliance breach. His administration backed ceasefire action at the United Nations and withheld the financial cooperation Britain needed to defend sterling and obtain short-term credit and an IMF loan. It also considered oil sanctions and threatened actions against American sterling reserves. Soviet intervention threats led him to alert the Sixth Fleet and prepare for wider conflict, joining coercion of allies to containment of escalation.

This episode therefore shows American supremacy operating through institutions, markets, credit, and reserve currency power as much as armed command. Eisenhower's success strengthened the United States' regional role, but it did not eliminate the later costs or contradictions of deeper American involvement.

## Key Characteristics

- Opposed an Anglo-French attack as dangerous to regional alignment and Cold War stability.
- Reacted strongly to allied deception and refused to ratify a military fait accompli.
- Used the United Nations and financial leverage together rather than attacking his allies militarily.
- Balanced pressure on Britain and France with preparation against possible Soviet intervention.
- Emerged from Suez with American strategic hierarchy over Britain made unmistakable.

## Qualifications

These two episodes treat Eisenhower mainly through Suez and do not provide a balanced account of his presidency, Middle East policy, covert interventions, reelection politics, or the later Eisenhower Doctrine. The claim that he might have accepted a faster fait accompli is retrospective and source-scoped. Financial leverage forced allied withdrawal, but this case alone does not establish a general rule that American coercion was lawful, consistent, or stabilizing in other crises.

## What Changed

- Created a profile of Eisenhower's Suez diplomacy, alliance coercion, and escalation management.
- Identified finance, reserves, credit, and multilateral institutions as instruments of American strategic supremacy.
