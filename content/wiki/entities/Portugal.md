# Portugal

## Overview
Portugal appears in the wiki as an Iberian polity whose maritime empire and Catholic networks shape early-modern global history and whose twentieth-century colonial crisis helps explain its transition from dictatorship to democracy.

## Current Profile
The sources now show Portugal across four contexts. The Ceuta episode gives the origin-direction layer: after completing its mainland [[IberianReconquista|Reconquista]] in 1249, Portugal turns toward North Africa and the Atlantic, with the 1415 conquest of [[Ceuta]] becoming the conventional opening of [[PortugueseMaritimeExpansion]]. The Japan episode shows the downstream Asian trade and Jesuit branch, while the Elizabethan episode shows Portugal's empire folded into [[PhilipII|Philip II]]'s Spanish monarchy. The modern episode then shows imperial retention reversing direction: the [[PortugueseColonialWars|colonial wars]] weaken the [[EstadoNovoPortugal|Estado Novo]], politicize officers, and help produce the [[CarnationRevolution]] and an uncertain but ultimately democratic transition.

## Key Characteristics
- Completes its mainland Reconquista earlier than Castile and turns expansion toward North Africa and Atlantic routes.
- Captures Ceuta in 1415 under [[JohnIOfPortugal|John I]], making the city a symbolic first step in later Age of Discovery memory.
- Builds an Asian Catholic maritime presence that helps structure early modern Japan-Europe contact.
- Becomes part of Philip II's enlarged Iberian monarchy in the Elizabethan sources.
- Retains African colonies into the twentieth century at mounting fiscal, military, and political cost.
- Moves from authoritarian collapse through revolutionary instability toward pluralist democracy.

## Evidence
- Ceuta and expansion evidence: [[496-yiminchao-beihou-de-xiuda-cong-gudian-shidai-manzu-duhai-yibiliya-shoufu-shidi-yundong-dao-putaoya-dahanghai-lu8pawnkiselmmrgubrey4pq-4fw]] says Portugal's post-1249 constraints pushed attention toward North Africa and Atlantic islands, and that the 1415 capture of Ceuta is traditionally treated as the opening of the Age of Discovery.
- Ceuta qualification: [[496-yiminchao-beihou-de-xiuda-cong-gudian-shidai-manzu-duhai-yibiliya-shoufu-shidi-yundong-dao-putaoya-dahanghai-lu8pawnkiselmmrgubrey4pq-4fw]] says the hosts qualify whether Portugal already possessed a global conquest plan in 1415 and note that Portuguese rule damaged Ceuta's old commercial ecosystem.
- Asian maritime evidence: [[14-wushi-weilian-dahanghai-shidai-de-riben-he-xifang-555771285]] presents Portugal as one of the Catholic Iberian maritime powers active in Asian trade, Macau-related contact, and [[Jesuits|Jesuit]] missionary networks before [[WilliamAdams|William Adams / 三浦按针]] arrived in [[Japan]].
- Confessional trade evidence: [[14-wushi-weilian-dahanghai-shidai-de-riben-he-xifang-555771285]] says Iberian Catholic influence made English and Dutch Protestant sailors politically suspect in Japan.
- Iberian Union evidence: [[694-elizabeth-i-vs-the-catholics-a-treacherous-conspiracy-part-4-glt1627515087]] says Philip II's acquisition of Portugal and its empire made England's strategic environment more dangerous after [[FrancisDrake]]'s return.
- Dictatorship and war evidence: [[273-portugal-the-carnation-revolution-glt3297148398]] links late Estado Novo weakness to political repression, stalled reform, economic strain, and the African wars.
- Revolutionary-transition evidence: [[273-portugal-the-carnation-revolution-glt3297148398]] follows the officer coup, social occupations, nationalization, regional backlash, military factionalism, and the moderate turn of November 1975.

## Qualifications
The sources emphasize different periods and uses of Portugal. The Ceuta source treats 1415 as a conventional starting point but resists making it a fully planned global project. The Japan source treats Portugal as part of a Catholic maritime field; the Elizabethan source treats Portuguese empire mainly through its absorption into Spanish power. The Carnation Revolution source is a brisk, personality-led metropolitan narrative, so its budget shares, foreign intervention claims, political anecdotes, and treatment of African decolonization remain source-scoped.

## What Changed
- Added the twentieth-century shift from authoritarian imperial retention to colonial-war crisis.
- Distinguished the peaceful April coup from the unstable revolutionary process and later democratic consolidation.
- Preserved the early-modern expansion, Asian trade, and Iberian Union branches as separate historical contexts.
