# Portugal

## Overview
Portugal appears in the wiki as an Iberian polity whose commercial-crusading maritime empire shapes early-modern global history and whose twentieth-century colonial crisis helps explain its transition from dictatorship to democracy.

## Current Profile
The sources now show Portugal across connected imperial and political contexts. The Ceuta episode gives the origin-direction layer: after completing its mainland [[IberianReconquista|Reconquista]] in 1249, Portugal turns toward North Africa and the Atlantic, with the 1415 conquest of [[Ceuta]] becoming the conventional opening of [[PortugueseMaritimeExpansion]]. The Golden Age episode follows [[BartolomeuDias]] around the Cape, [[VascoDaGama]] to India, and [[AfonsoDeAlbuquerque]] into a [[PortugueseFortifiedMaritimeNetwork|fortified Asian network]]. It treats spices, dynastic prestige, Christian mission, and anti-Muslim warfare as a single [[PortugueseCrusadingCommercialExpansion|commercial-crusading formation]], not separate motives.

That system made a small kingdom a global maritime power but depended on extreme voyage attrition and organized coercion. The Japan episode shows the downstream Asian trade and Jesuit branch. [[SebastianOfPortugal|Sebastian's]] failed Moroccan crusade then creates the succession crisis through which the Elizabethan episode finds Portugal's empire folded into [[PhilipII|Philip II's]] Spanish monarchy.

The Salazar episode shows later imperial contraction producing a melancholic and preservative national frame. Poverty, saudade, Catholicism, fado, football, and the claim to a “pluri-continental nation” surround the rise of the [[EstadoNovoPortugal|Estado Novo]], whose wartime neutrality and postwar anti-communism supported durability while repression and colonial war remained central. The Carnation Revolution episode then shows imperial retention reversing direction: the [[PortugueseColonialWars|colonial wars]] weaken the regime, politicize officers, and help produce the [[CarnationRevolution]] and an uncertain but ultimately democratic transition.

## Key Characteristics
- Completes its mainland Reconquista earlier than Castile and turns toward North Africa and Atlantic routes, with the 1415 capture of [[Ceuta]] becoming a symbolic first step in later Age of Discovery memory.
- Builds an Asian Catholic presence through fortified ports and trading nodes, joining the spice trade and royal ambition to Christian universalism and anti-Muslim warfare.
- Becomes part of Philip II's enlarged Iberian monarchy in the Elizabethan sources.
- Retains African colonies into the twentieth century at mounting fiscal, military, and political cost.
- Develops an authoritarian order that joins Catholic conservatism and administrative stability to repression and resistance to rapid modernization.
- Uses or is represented through Fátima, fado, and football while their meanings exceed the regime, then moves from authoritarian collapse through revolutionary instability toward pluralist democracy.

## Evidence
- Ceuta and expansion evidence: [[496-yiminchao-beihou-de-xiuda-cong-gudian-shidai-manzu-duhai-yibiliya-shoufu-shidi-yundong-dao-putaoya-dahanghai-lu8pawnkiselmmrgubrey4pq-4fw]] says Portugal's post-1249 constraints pushed attention toward North Africa and Atlantic islands, and that the 1415 capture of Ceuta is traditionally treated as the opening of the Age of Discovery.
- Ceuta qualification: [[496-yiminchao-beihou-de-xiuda-cong-gudian-shidai-manzu-duhai-yibiliya-shoufu-shidi-yundong-dao-putaoya-dahanghai-lu8pawnkiselmmrgubrey4pq-4fw]] says the hosts qualify whether Portugal already possessed a global conquest plan in 1415 and note that Portuguese rule damaged Ceuta's old commercial ecosystem.
- Asian maritime evidence: [[14-wushi-weilian-dahanghai-shidai-de-riben-he-xifang-555771285]] presents Portugal as one of the Catholic Iberian maritime powers active in Asian trade, Macau-related contact, and [[Jesuits|Jesuit]] missionary networks before [[WilliamAdams|William Adams / 三浦按针]] arrived in [[Japan]].
- Confessional trade evidence: [[14-wushi-weilian-dahanghai-shidai-de-riben-he-xifang-555771285]] says Iberian Catholic influence made English and Dutch Protestant sailors politically suspect in Japan.
- Cape-route evidence: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] follows Dias's 1488 return and da Gama's 1498 arrival at Calicut as the route's decisive validation.
- Maritime-network evidence: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] links Cannanore, Cochin, Goa, Malacca, and wider Asian diplomacy to a fort-and-trading-post model.
- Violence and motive evidence: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] joins royal spice ambition and Christian mission to massacres, forced conversion, intimidation, and attacks on pilgrims and civilians.
- Succession evidence: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] connects Sebastian's 1578 defeat and death without an heir to Philip II's takeover in 1580.
- Iberian Union evidence: [[694-elizabeth-i-vs-the-catholics-a-treacherous-conspiracy-part-4-glt1627515087]] says Philip II's acquisition of Portugal and its empire made England's strategic environment more dangerous after [[FrancisDrake]]'s return.
- Dictatorship and war evidence: [[273-portugal-the-carnation-revolution-glt3297148398]] links late Estado Novo weakness to political repression, stalled reform, economic strain, and the African wars.
- Salazar-era evidence: [[230-portugal-football-fado-and-fascism-part-4-glt9147423732]] connects post-imperial decline, First Republic instability, Salazar's fiscal rise, cultural symbolism, wartime neutrality, and colonial conflict.
- Classification and culture evidence: [[230-portugal-football-fado-and-fascism-part-4-glt9147423732]] distinguishes reactionary authoritarianism from fascist mobilization and uses [[PortugueseCulturalSymbolPolitics|Fátima, fado, and football]] to analyze national identity and regime appropriation.
- Revolutionary-transition evidence: [[273-portugal-the-carnation-revolution-glt3297148398]] follows the officer coup, social occupations, nationalization, regional backlash, military factionalism, and the moderate turn of November 1975.

## Qualifications
The sources emphasize different periods and uses of Portugal. The Ceuta source treats 1415 as a conventional starting point but resists making it a fully planned global project. The Golden Age source is a brisk Portuguese-centered narrative whose casualty figures, motive claims, royal boasts, and limited African and Asian perspectives remain source-scoped. The Japan source treats Portugal as part of a Catholic maritime field; the Elizabethan source treats Portuguese empire mainly through its absorption into Spanish power. The two modern episodes are likewise personality-led metropolitan narratives. Comparative regime judgments, repression figures, cultural-causation claims, budget shares, foreign intervention claims, and limited treatment of African decolonization remain source-scoped.

## What Changed
- Extended the early-modern branch from Ceuta through the Cape route into a fortified Indian Ocean network.
- Integrated navigational achievement with voyage attrition, coercion, and crusading-commercial motive.
- Added Sebastian's Moroccan defeat as the succession bridge into Philip II's Iberian monarchy.
