# Michael Stokes Paulsen

## Overview

Michael Stokes Paulsen is a constitutional-law scholar whose co-authored state-subdivision argument anchors [[divide-and-conquer-divide-and-conquer|Divide and Conquer]].

## Current Profile

Paulsen treats Article IV, Section 3 as a live legal rule rather than a historical curiosity. With [[VasanKesavan]], he argues that its final consent condition reaches both state subdivision and state combination, uses [[WestVirginia|West Virginia]] as a historical test, and applies the interpretation to the advance consent in [[Texas]]'s 1845 admission resolution.

His practical conclusion remains conditional. Texas may have a path to divide through state action because Congress consented in advance, but he also believes [[UnitedStatesCongress|Congress]] could withdraw that permission before subdivision was completed.

## Key Characteristics

- Uses text, grammar, drafts, constitutional history, and founding-era records together.
- Treats punctuation as evidence within a wider interpretive argument rather than as a self-sufficient answer.
- Uses West Virginia's statehood to test the reach of Article IV's consent requirement.
- Applies the resulting interpretation to Texas's 1845 admission terms.
- Distinguishes legal permission from the timing and politics needed to exercise it.

## Evidence

### Article IV interpretation

- [[divide-and-conquer-divide-and-conquer]] attributes to Paulsen and Kesavan the conclusion that the consent clause governs both subdivision and combination.

### Historical and present applications

- [[divide-and-conquer-divide-and-conquer]] connects their interpretation first to West Virginia's constitutional formation and then to Texas's possible creation of as many as five states.

### Revocation boundary

- [[divide-and-conquer-divide-and-conquer]] reports Paulsen's view that Congress could revoke its earlier permission before Texas finished acting.

## Qualifications

The bounded source is an episode summary, not Paulsen and Kesavan's article, the complete historical record, or a judicial decision. The page records their attributed interpretation without treating Texas subdivision, continuing consent, congressional revocation, or the required implementation process as settled law.
