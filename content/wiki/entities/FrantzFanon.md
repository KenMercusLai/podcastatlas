# Frantz Fanon

## Overview

Frantz Fanon appears as a Martinique-born anti-colonial thinker whose work in and identification with [[Algeria]] connected colonial psychology, revolutionary violence, Black identity, and [[PanAfricanism]].

## Current Profile

The episode opens with Fanon's 1958 Accra appeal to treat Algeria, Rhodesia, and Angola as parts of one African liberation struggle. His Caribbean birth and French education make him a transnational figure rather than a simple representative of one national tradition. *The Wretched of the Earth* is presented as foundational to later thought about colonial violence and liberation.

Fanon's strongest and most contested claim in the source is that colonialism is violent in its structure and therefore can be answered adequately only through violence. The Algerian case gives that argument historical force, but the episode does not independently establish its moral or strategic necessity.

## Key Characteristics

- Martinique-born writer and activist closely identified with Algerian liberation.
- Transnational bridge among Caribbean, European, and African anti-colonial networks.
- Advocate of a Pan-African frame joining geographically separate liberation struggles.
- Theorist of colonial violence and revolutionary response.
- Later intellectual influence is represented principally through *The Wretched of the Earth*.

## Evidence

- Pan-African role: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] opens with Fanon's 1958 Accra speech linking Algeria to struggles elsewhere in Africa.
- Intellectual formation: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] places his Martinique birth, French context, and Algerian commitment inside the hybrid networks of decolonization.
- Violence thesis: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] attributes to Fanon the judgment that colonial violence required violent anti-colonial response.

## Qualifications

This is a source-bounded introductory profile, not a complete account of Fanon's psychiatry, wartime service, writings, FLN work, political development, or later reception. The episode explains why his violence thesis became influential but does not settle its ethics, effectiveness, or applicability across different colonial systems.
