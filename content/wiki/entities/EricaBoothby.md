# Erica Boothby

## Overview
Erica Boothby is the psychologist featured in [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-1dfa7e5aca191bd78c9a8028baf43cb1-50295125-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-1dfa7e5aca191bd78c9a8028baf43cb1-50295125|Mind Reading 2.0: How Others See You]], where she explains systematic errors in how people infer others' attention, judgments, and enjoyment of conversation.

## Current Profile
Boothby's account begins with a collaboration conversation she judged poorly even though an outside observer heard mutual connection. She turns that discrepancy into a research program on [[LikingGap]] and related [[SocialAttentionAsymmetry|social-attention asymmetries]]: private access to one's own insecurity and mistakes is much richer than access to another person's hidden interest, charity, or continuing thoughts.

Her practical stance is calibrated inquiry. Recognizing the bias, consulting a third party, asking respectfully, approaching the other person with curiosity, and voicing sincere positive thoughts can improve the information available without assuming that every social forecast should become optimistic.

## Key Characteristics
- Studies how people evaluate conversations and infer what others think of them.
- Uses differences between participant, partner, and observer perspectives to identify social-cognitive errors.
- Treats self-criticism as potentially informative but costly when it is mistaken for another person's judgment.
- Connects social uncertainty to guardedness, indirect signaling, and missed opportunities for relationship repair.
- Recommends curiosity, outside perspective, direct communication, and expressed appreciation as bounded corrective practices.

## Qualifications
This profile is based on one structured podcast summary rather than Boothby's publication record or complete body of research. The summary does not provide full study methods, samples, effect sizes, cultures, replications, or clinical evidence. Its childhood findings do not establish the trajectory through adolescence and later adulthood, and the proposed social-anxiety and narcissism relationships remain hypotheses or early signals rather than settled conclusions.
