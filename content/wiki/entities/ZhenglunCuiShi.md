## Overview

《正论》 / Zhenglun is the late-Han political essay attributed in [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] to [[CuiShiLateHan|Cui Shi]]. The wiki treats it as a source-mediated statecraft text: after Cui Shi declines to answer the court's policy questions, Zhenglun becomes the vehicle for criticizing late-Han complacency, silent ministers, and formulaic governance.

## Current Profile

Zhenglun's current profile is diagnostic. It argues that a ruler must identify the political disease before choosing the remedy, because methods that were suitable in one era can fail or become harmful in another. The episode uses Confucius' different answers to different rulers, the contrast between Emperor Xuan and Emperor Yuan, and the medicine-and-food metaphor to explain the text's claim that strict law and moral teaching are conditional instruments.

The essay is not recorded here as a simple Legalist manifesto. Hanji 837 closes with Sima Guang's qualification: by late Han, the problem was not that the law lacked harshness in the abstract, but that powerful people escaped enforcement while ordinary people remained exposed to severity. Zhenglun therefore enters the wiki as a temporary corrective text against soft elite impunity, bounded by the larger principle of strict-lenient balance.

## Key Characteristics

- Cui Shi-authored political essay introduced after his withdrawal from Emperor Huan's palace policy examination.
- Critique of complacent rulership, failed orders, ministerial silence, and governing by inherited formulas.
- Diagnostic statecraft text that asks whether the political condition calls for strict enforcement, lenient repair, moral teaching, or another remedy.
- Uses harsh-law language as medicine for disorder while preserving the idea that peaceful conditions require moral nourishment rather than coercive excess.
- Interpreted through Sima Guang's qualification as a corrective against late-Han weak enforcement and elite impunity, not as universal praise of severity.

## Evidence

- Origin and authorship: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] says Cui Shi returns home from Luoyang and writes Zhenglun after declining the court's direct policy questions.
- Anti-complacency critique: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] presents the essay as warning that rulers mistake temporary calm for durable order and ministers stay silent to protect themselves.
- Context-sensitive method: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] uses Confucius' different answers and the Xuan/Yuan contrast to show Zhenglun's rejection of mechanical imitation.
- Punishment and teaching metaphor: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] explains that law can be medicine for disorder while moral teaching functions like nourishing food for settled order.
- Sima Guang boundary: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] says Sima Guang accepts the immediate corrective but notes that existing Han penalties were already severe.

## Qualifications

The page describes Zhenglun through Hanji 837's presentation, not through a full independent reading of the extant or reconstructed text. Its title is disambiguated from [[ZhenglunYuShuofu|《争论与说服》 / How Minds Change]], an unrelated modern book already present in the wiki. The essay's harsh-law language should be read with its diagnostic and Sima Guang qualifications rather than treated as a blanket policy program.
