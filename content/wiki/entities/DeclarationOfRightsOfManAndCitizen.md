# Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen

## Overview

The Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen was the 17-article statement approved by the National Assembly on 26 August 1789 that defined liberty, equality, property, security, resistance to oppression, expression, and national sovereignty as foundations of the new French order.

## Current Profile

The source treats the Declaration as both rupture and inheritance. It legally attacked privilege, arbitrary punishment, censorship, corporate sovereignty, and sacred monarchy while drawing on American revolutionary precedent, Enlightenment debate, natural-right language, and older Christian moral assumptions. Its claim to speak universally gave it lasting power, but its placement of sovereignty in the nation and its failure to resolve gender, slavery, property, monarchy, and colonial application created a durable [[UniversalRightsNationalSovereigntyTension|universalism-sovereignty tension]].

## Key Characteristics

- Declared men free and equal in rights.
- Named liberty, property, security, and resistance to oppression as natural and imprescriptible rights.
- Located sovereignty in the nation rather than king, Church, estate, or corporation.
- Protected presumption of innocence, religious opinion within public-order limits, and free communication.
- Used universal and sacred language while leaving major political and social exclusions unresolved.

## Qualifications

- The source does not provide a complete article-by-article legal history.
- Universal wording did not automatically confer equal political power on women, enslaved people, colonized subjects, or propertyless citizens.
- American, Enlightenment, classical, and Christian genealogies can overlap; the episode's emphasis on Christian inheritance remains interpretive.
- Later global constitutional influence does not mean later declarations reproduced the French text without substantial change.

## What Changed

- Created a profile distinguishing the Declaration's universal moral claim from the national authority charged with enforcing it.
- Made unresolved exclusions and the text's mixed intellectual inheritance part of the current judgment.
