# Abu Hanifa

## Overview

Abu Hanifa is presented as an early Muslim jurist associated with Kufa and the legal tradition later known as the Hanafi school.

## Current Profile

The episode uses Abu Hanifa to show that Islamic jurisprudence was not merely the repetition of ruler-issued commands. Reason could be treated as a God-given means for identifying the purposes of divine law when applying authoritative sources to legal questions.

He also appears comparatively: schools associated with Malik ibn Anas, al-Shafi'i, and Ahmad ibn Hanbal developed different methods and later regional profiles. In that setting, Abu Hanifa is one figure in a plural scholarly field rather than the sole architect of Islamic law.

## Key Characteristics

- Early jurist associated with Kufa and Abbasid-era legal formation.
- Name attached to the Hanafi school of jurisprudence.
- Presented as giving structured reason a legitimate role in legal judgment.
- Example of scholarly authority operating alongside and partly independently of rulers.
- Participant in a wider field of competing legal methods and schools.

## Evidence

- **Legal method:** [[379-baghdad-the-arabian-nights-part-4-glt8529456010]] says Abu Hanifa treated reason as a divine gift usable in discerning God's purposes.
- **Comparative position:** [[379-baghdad-the-arabian-nights-part-4-glt8529456010]] places him beside Malik, al-Shafi'i, and Ibn Hanbal as founders or namesakes of major Sunni legal traditions.
- **Authority structure:** [[379-baghdad-the-arabian-nights-part-4-glt8529456010]] uses jurists such as Abu Hanifa to explain why the state applied law it did not wholly create.

## Qualifications

The current profile derives from a compressed popular-history discussion rather than a study of Abu Hanifa's surviving doctrine, students, later school formation, or scholarly disputes. Regional descriptions of legal schools are broad and source-scoped.
