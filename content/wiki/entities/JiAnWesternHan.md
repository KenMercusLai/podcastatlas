## Overview

汲黯 / Ji An is a Western Han official presented as upright, clean, unwilling to flatter rank, willing to confront [[HanWudi|汉武帝]], and capable of governing through accumulated public reputation. His current evidence joins protection of civilians during [[HunyeKingWesternHan|浑邪王]]'s surrender reception, public-purpose remonstrance about ritual and personnel, equal-standing etiquette toward [[WeiQingHanGeneral|卫青]], a warning about [[ZhangTangWesternHan|张汤]], attempted recoding of criticism as an imperial offense, the social cost of dismissal, and successful Huaiyang service ending in office. The sources frame his distinctive political value as corrective access and [[MoralAuthorityConstrainsFormalPower|道德威望约束权力]]: Wudi finds the criticism uncomfortable yet regulates his own conduct around a reputation and candor that court agreement cannot replace.

## Current Profile

The earliest current confrontation arises during Hunye King's surrender reception. When Wudi cannot obtain enough borrowed horses and threatens the Chang'an magistrate, Ji offers his own life rather than let the local official bear responsibility for popular resistance. He then argues that accepting surrender does not justify exhausting state resources, compelling civilians to serve the newcomers, or applying frontier prohibitions without regard to changed place and status. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-314-1-xiongnu-wang-guijiang-wudi-weihe-nusha-500-hanren-ljb-ocshiauhvyra88aykkqlxkak|Hanji 314-1]] supplies this reception and merchant-case layer.

The same episode says more than five hundred Han merchants are condemned to death after trading with surrendered Xiongnu in Chang'an. Ji contrasts those sentences with the soldiers, money, and popular labor consumed by war and argues that protecting a narrow legal “leaf” while injuring the state's own “branch” reverses public purpose. His remonstrance does not depend on denying the value of surrender; it demands that external credibility remain accountable to domestic burden and contextual justice. The source does not independently establish that every sentence was executed or that Ji's later removal was retaliation.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-314-2-shisha-de-hanwudi-juran-baorong-tade-cuo-lof7jgitcm-qyuw_afgjv5erjpr5|Hanji 314-2]] revisits that punishment from the ruler's security logic, specifying ironware as the sensitive trade good and presenting severe punishment as a nationwide deterrent against military supply reaching the Xiongnu. This does not erase Ji's legal objection: the joined record keeps strategic sensitivity separate from whether merchants in Chang'an could reasonably understand exchange with already surrendered people as frontier smuggling.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-318-jian-hanwudi-woshi-nide-pobiren-ljw371ou_jca8esjwuqngsdccaga|Hanji 318]] extends the same public-purpose method. Ji argues that ancestral-temple music should honor predecessors and educate the people rather than magnify one ruler's delight in a “divine horse.” He also challenges a personnel contradiction: vigorous recruitment cannot sustain government if valued officials are readily executed, because capable people are finite. The episode does not prove that the music was performed or that every punished official was blameless.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-1-men-ke-luoque-de-youlai-ni-zhidao-ma-lkoajhp2b_k32aurnk8rs-ukxaib|Hanji 333-1]] pairs Ji with [[ZhengDangshiWesternHan|郑当时]] as worthy senior ministers. Their authority and reputation attract visitors while they hold power; after dismissal removes usefulness and, in the episode's telling, money, previous associates stop coming.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-2-ta-ai-da-huangdi-de-lian-weihe-neng-shanzhong-lhsygwstd8wysfxfgdbfkkyokscl|Hanji 333-2]] supplies the next career layer. Han Wudi recalls Ji after private minting and official-popular conflict trouble Huaiyang. Ji asks to remain close to court as a zhonglang who can correct the ruler's omissions, but Wudi insists that Ji's reputation itself can settle the locality. The episode says Ji restores order, becomes a kingdom chancellor, and dies in office seven years later.

Before departure, Ji warns [[LiXiWesternHan|李息]] that Zhang Tang blocks advice, decorates error, tracks imperial preferences, and expands punitive government. Li's fear keeps the warning private. The joined profile therefore treats Ji's frankness as a consistent test of public purpose across surrender, law, ritual, personnel, and administration. He survives not because he stops criticizing, but because clean conduct, useful reputation, and local capacity preserve value even after direct advisory access is denied.

Hanji 288 supplies a more direct courtroom version of that conflict. Ji reportedly argues that Zhang cannot strengthen inherited institutions or settle the people, but instead disorders earlier law and traps debate in technical wording. Zhang's command of specialist terminology defeats Ji at the level of clause-by-clause argument without answering Ji's public-purpose objection. This adds a method boundary to Ji's profile: moral and political principle can expose what punitive legality is doing while still lacking the technical leverage to defeat it inside the legal forum.

Hanji 314-2 makes that relationship explicit: Wudi is said to send Ji away because direct criticism is hard to bear, then recall him because he cannot dispense with its corrective value. It also supplies a source-attributed early career sketch and says Ji's recorded exchanges seek no private favor. The claim that Wudi became unrestrained after Ji's death is a useful warning about dependence on a single critic, but the current evidence cannot prove that counterfactual or reduce Wudi's later governance problems to one missing adviser.

Ji's court etiquette supplies a mechanism behind that corrective standing. When other officials defer heavily to the newly powerful Wei Qing, Ji refuses to bow and argues that a great general benefits from keeping an equal-standing guest. Wei reportedly accepts the claim and repeatedly seeks Ji's advice. Wudi's informality before Wei Qing and [[GongsunHong|公孙弘]] is then contrasted with his unwillingness to receive Ji while bareheaded or sitting improperly; in the culminating anecdote, Wudi avoids the audience and approves Ji's request through an attendant rather than let Ji witness him without proper form. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-294-1-wudi-bu-pa-quanchen-bu-pa-xiongnu-weihe-dudu-pa-ta-ljpqul0y4ep-pyufaow-vsqwp9ir|Hanji 294-1]] supplies both comparisons.

This does not show Ji holding more formal power than general or emperor. It adds a narrower claim: consistent public principle can make powerful observers regulate their own behavior because the critic's judgment is credible. The episode's language of imperial “fear” remains an interpretation of ritual caution, not direct access to Wudi's private emotion.

Ji's candor also survives when the target controls the rhetorical outcome. He first accuses [[GongsunHong|公孙弘]] of abandoning a privately agreed ministerial position after reading Wudi's reaction; Gongsun replies that those who understand him call him loyal, shifting the argument away from the agreement itself. Ji later accuses him of using coarse bedding and one-dish meals to perform virtue despite holding high office. Gongsun acknowledges the practice and an interest in reputation, invokes Guan Zhong and Yan Ying to separate lifestyle from governing ability, calls Ji a close friend, and tells Wudi that only such loyalty makes criticism audible. The exchanges support Ji's role as a credible truth-teller while showing that candor can be reframed into the criticized official's own loyalty, competence, and humility performance without resolving either accusation. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-273-jian-gan-dui-hanwudi-weisha-dui-buying-gongsunhong-1-lisjfpgbgiuqjv27w8tucc1js1jv|Hanji 273 part 1]] [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-286-canbei-wujie-qiannian-zhumaichen-xiuqi-de-fanzhuan-zhenxiang-2-lrgdpir3ez-hk_eptcq8q3-pzozr|Hanji 286 part 2]]

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-273-jian-gan-dui-hanwudi-weisha-dui-buying-gongsunhong-2-lg5ktrwoi1xsx9xqlucin_dvv35d|Hanji 273]] supplies the sharper retaliation claim behind that contrast. After Ji again separates ancestral ritual from Wudi's pleasure in exceptional horses, the episode says Gongsun labels the intervention slander of sacred institutions and recommends clan punishment. Wudi refuses, so the scene shows a proposed criminalization rather than an imposed sentence. The same source attributes Ji's later Huaiyang appointment partly to Gongsun's timing, while Hanji 333-2 emphasizes Wudi's genuine need for Ji's governing reputation. The joined profile therefore preserves both displacement and public-service explanations instead of treating outward office as proved revenge.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han senior official presented as upright, clean, and willing to confront the ruler on public matters rather than seek private favors when prestige, ceremony, or punishment harms public purpose.
- Defends a threatened local magistrate and merchants against burdens or penalties he regards as context-blind and disproportionate.
- Counts war deaths, fiscal expenditure, civilian labor, and forgone compensation when evaluating the reception of surrender.
- Draws visitors while powerful and loses them after dismissal and impoverishment.
- Seeks to remain near Han Wudi so he can correct omissions and participate in court deliberation.
- Is assigned to Huaiyang because the emperor believes his reputation can stabilize strained local governance.
- Maintains equal-standing etiquette toward Wei Qing and challenges both Zhang Tang's technical legality and Gongsun Hong's austere image from public principle, even when specialists or political tacticians answer through technical defeat, rhetorical absorption, proposed punishment, or outward appointment.

Cost accounting for surrender:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-314-1-xiongnu-wang-guijiang-wudi-weihe-nusha-500-hanren-ljb-ocshiauhvyra88aykkqlxkak|Hanji 314-1]] has Ji place reception rewards and service beside prior war deaths, expenditure, and uncompensated households.

Public-purpose remonstrance:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-318-jian-hanwudi-woshi-nide-pobiren-ljw371ou_jca8esjwuqngsdccaga|Hanji 318]] says Ji rejects using ancestral-temple music to celebrate one horse and warns Wudi that scarce talent cannot be killed without limit while the court seeks worthy people.

Status reversal:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-1-men-ke-luoque-de-youlai-ni-zhidao-ma-lkoajhp2b_k32aurnk8rs-ukxaib|Hanji 333-1]] says visitors are numerous while Ji An has authority and disappear after he loses office and money.

Court access and outward assignment:

- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-2-ta-ai-da-huangdi-de-lian-weihe-neng-shanzhong-lhsygwstd8wysfxfgdbfkkyokscl|Hanji 333-2]] says Ji gives Li Xi a detailed warning about Zhang's rejection of advice, alignment with imperial desire, legal manipulation, and punitive authority.

Corrective value despite ruler discomfort:

Etiquette and moral constraint:

Criticism retained through rhetorical absorption:

Proposed criminalization and contested displacement:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-273-jian-gan-dui-hanwudi-weisha-dui-buying-gongsunhong-2-lg5ktrwoi1xsx9xqlucin_dvv35d|Hanji 273]] says Gongsun Hong answers Ji's horse-song criticism by proposing clan punishment for “诽谤圣制,” which Wudi rejects, and attributes the later Huaiyang appointment partly to Gongsun's intervention.

- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-314-2-shisha-de-hanwudi-juran-baorong-tade-cuo-lof7jgitcm-qyuw_afgjv5erjpr5|Hanji 314-2]] claims that Ji's recorded exchanges with Wudi all concern public affairs rather than requests for himself.

Local service and final outcome:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-2-ta-ai-da-huangdi-de-lian-weihe-neng-shanzhong-lhsygwstd8wysfxfgdbfkkyokscl|Hanji 333-2]] says Ji stabilizes Huaiyang, receives promotion, dies in office seven years later, and remains valued after death.

## Qualifications

Hanji 333-1 explicitly warns that names and details were not textually collated and transcribes Ji An as “济暗/齐暗”; Hanji 314-2 transcribes him as “几岸”; Hanji 294-1 repeatedly renders him “己案.” The page normalizes these forms to 汲黯 from the joined biographical chain. The ten sources do not establish his full biography, ancestry, education, exact office sequence, wealth, every exchange with Wudi, the mechanics of governance by reputation, or the exact family appointments. Hanji 288 does not establish the full debate, precise laws at issue, verbatim dialogue, or whether Ji's inability to answer technical clauses means legal ignorance, genre contrast, or retrospective moral framing. Hanji 294-1 does not independently establish the exact etiquette, chronology, number of consultations, Wudi's dress or posture, the “武帐” setting, or whether imperial avoidance expresses fear, respect, embarrassment, or retrospective moralizing; “公孙洪” is normalized to 公孙弘, while likely “庄助” remains unlinked pending direct collation. Hanji 273 part 1 and Hanji 286 part 2 do not establish the private ministerial agreement, material details of Gongsun's lifestyle, Ji's exact words, whether austerity was deceptive, or whether Gongsun's responses were sincere rather than tactical; rhetorical success does not prove Ji's accusations false. Hanji 273 part 2 repeats the horse-song and Huaiyang arcs but does not establish Gongsun's hidden motive, the exact “诽谤圣制” charge, the legal possibility of clan punishment, or whether outward appointment was retaliation rather than genuine deployment of Ji's local authority. Hanji 314-1 does not establish the exact frontier statute, whether all merchant death sentences were executed, or that Ji's later removal was retaliation; its legal, fiscal, and numerical details remain source-scoped. Hanji 314-2's ironware specification, public-affairs-only claim, removal-and-recall account, and posthumous causal link also remain episode-attributed. Hanji 333-2 supports Ji's warning before Zhang's later fall but not access to Zhang's private intent. Hanji 318 does not establish the horse's miraculous nature, completed ancestral-temple performance, the merits of every punished official, or a full severity-to-shortage causal chain. Claims that clean officials are structurally excluded or that virtue automatically protects descendants remain host interpretations.

## What Changed

- Added Ji's accusation that Gongsun broke private ministerial coordination after reading Wudi's response.
- Expanded the austerity confrontation while preserving the distinction between Gongsun's rhetorical victory and the truth of Ji's charge.
- Kept the agreement, exact dialogue, lifestyle motive, loyalty judgment, and promotion effects source-scoped.
