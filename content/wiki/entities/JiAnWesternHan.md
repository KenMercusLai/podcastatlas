## Overview

汲黯 / Ji An is a Western Han official presented as upright, clean, willing to confront [[HanWudi|汉武帝]], and capable of governing through accumulated public reputation. His current evidence joins protection of civilians during [[HunyeKingWesternHan|浑邪王]]'s surrender reception, public-purpose remonstrance about ritual and personnel, a warning about [[ZhangTangWesternHan|张汤]], the social cost of dismissal, and successful Huaiyang service ending in office. The sources frame his distinctive political value as corrective access: Wudi finds the criticism uncomfortable and repeatedly sends him outward, yet continues to use a reputation and candor that court agreement cannot replace.

## Current Profile

The earliest current confrontation arises during Hunye King's surrender reception. When Wudi cannot obtain enough borrowed horses and threatens the Chang'an magistrate, Ji offers his own life rather than let the local official bear responsibility for popular resistance. He then argues that accepting surrender does not justify exhausting state resources, compelling civilians to serve the newcomers, or applying frontier prohibitions without regard to changed place and status. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-314-1-xiongnu-wang-guijiang-wudi-weihe-nusha-500-hanren-ljb-ocshiauhvyra88aykkqlxkak|Hanji 314-1]] supplies this reception and merchant-case layer.

The same episode says more than five hundred Han merchants are condemned to death after trading with surrendered Xiongnu in Chang'an. Ji contrasts those sentences with the soldiers, money, and popular labor consumed by war and argues that protecting a narrow legal “leaf” while injuring the state's own “branch” reverses public purpose. His remonstrance does not depend on denying the value of surrender; it demands that external credibility remain accountable to domestic burden and contextual justice. The source does not independently establish that every sentence was executed or that Ji's later removal was retaliation.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-314-2-shisha-de-hanwudi-juran-baorong-tade-cuo-lof7jgitcm-qyuw_afgjv5erjpr5|Hanji 314-2]] revisits that punishment from the ruler's security logic, specifying ironware as the sensitive trade good and presenting severe punishment as a nationwide deterrent against military supply reaching the Xiongnu. This does not erase Ji's legal objection: the joined record keeps strategic sensitivity separate from whether merchants in Chang'an could reasonably understand exchange with already surrendered people as frontier smuggling.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-318-jian-hanwudi-woshi-nide-pobiren-ljw371ou_jca8esjwuqngsdccaga|Hanji 318]] extends the same public-purpose method. Ji argues that ancestral-temple music should honor predecessors and educate the people rather than magnify one ruler's delight in a “divine horse.” He also challenges a personnel contradiction: vigorous recruitment cannot sustain government if valued officials are readily executed, because capable people are finite. The episode does not prove that the music was performed or that every punished official was blameless.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-1-men-ke-luoque-de-youlai-ni-zhidao-ma-lkoajhp2b_k32aurnk8rs-ukxaib|Hanji 333-1]] pairs Ji with [[ZhengDangshiWesternHan|郑当时]] as worthy senior ministers. Their authority and reputation attract visitors while they hold power; after dismissal removes usefulness and, in the episode's telling, money, previous associates stop coming.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-2-ta-ai-da-huangdi-de-lian-weihe-neng-shanzhong-lhsygwstd8wysfxfgdbfkkyokscl|Hanji 333-2]] supplies the next career layer. Han Wudi recalls Ji after private minting and official-popular conflict trouble Huaiyang. Ji asks to remain close to court as a zhonglang who can correct the ruler's omissions, but Wudi insists that Ji's reputation itself can settle the locality. The episode says Ji restores order, becomes a kingdom chancellor, and dies in office seven years later.

Before departure, Ji warns [[LiXiWesternHan|李息]] that Zhang Tang blocks advice, decorates error, tracks imperial preferences, and expands punitive government. Li's fear keeps the warning private. The joined profile therefore treats Ji's frankness as a consistent test of public purpose across surrender, law, ritual, personnel, and administration. He survives not because he stops criticizing, but because clean conduct, useful reputation, and local capacity preserve value even after direct advisory access is denied.

Hanji 314-2 makes that relationship explicit: Wudi is said to send Ji away because direct criticism is hard to bear, then recall him because he cannot dispense with its corrective value. It also supplies a source-attributed early career sketch and says Ji's recorded exchanges seek no private favor. The claim that Wudi became unrestrained after Ji's death is a useful warning about dependence on a single critic, but the current evidence cannot prove that counterfactual or reduce Wudi's later governance problems to one missing adviser.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han senior official presented as upright, clean, and willing to confront the ruler on public matters rather than seek private favors when prestige, ceremony, or punishment harms public purpose.
- Defends a threatened local magistrate and merchants against burdens or penalties he regards as context-blind and disproportionate.
- Counts war deaths, fiscal expenditure, civilian labor, and forgone compensation when evaluating the reception of surrender.
- Draws visitors while powerful and loses them after dismissal and impoverishment.
- Seeks to remain near Han Wudi so he can correct omissions and participate in court deliberation.
- Is assigned to Huaiyang because the emperor believes his reputation can stabilize strained local governance.
- Gives Li Xi a specific pre-departure warning about Zhang Tang's political and legal conduct.

Cost accounting for surrender:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-314-1-xiongnu-wang-guijiang-wudi-weihe-nusha-500-hanren-ljb-ocshiauhvyra88aykkqlxkak|Hanji 314-1]] has Ji place reception rewards and service beside prior war deaths, expenditure, and uncompensated households.

Public-purpose remonstrance:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-318-jian-hanwudi-woshi-nide-pobiren-ljw371ou_jca8esjwuqngsdccaga|Hanji 318]] says Ji rejects using ancestral-temple music to celebrate one horse and warns Wudi that scarce talent cannot be killed without limit while the court seeks worthy people.

Status reversal:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-1-men-ke-luoque-de-youlai-ni-zhidao-ma-lkoajhp2b_k32aurnk8rs-ukxaib|Hanji 333-1]] says visitors are numerous while Ji An has authority and disappear after he loses office and money.

Court access and outward assignment:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-2-ta-ai-da-huangdi-de-lian-weihe-neng-shanzhong-lhsygwstd8wysfxfgdbfkkyokscl|Hanji 333-2]] says Ji asks to stay near the emperor as a zhonglang, but Wudi sends him to Huaiyang because his reputation can settle official-popular conflict.

Warning about Zhang Tang:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-2-ta-ai-da-huangdi-de-lian-weihe-neng-shanzhong-lhsygwstd8wysfxfgdbfkkyokscl|Hanji 333-2]] says Ji gives Li Xi a detailed warning about Zhang's rejection of advice, alignment with imperial desire, legal manipulation, and punitive authority.

Corrective value despite ruler discomfort:

Public rather than private purpose:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-314-2-shisha-de-hanwudi-juran-baorong-tade-cuo-lof7jgitcm-qyuw_afgjv5erjpr5|Hanji 314-2]] claims that Ji's recorded exchanges with Wudi all concern public affairs rather than requests for himself.

Local service and final outcome:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-2-ta-ai-da-huangdi-de-lian-weihe-neng-shanzhong-lhsygwstd8wysfxfgdbfkkyokscl|Hanji 333-2]] says Ji stabilizes Huaiyang, receives promotion, dies in office seven years later, and remains valued after death.

## Qualifications

Hanji 333-1 explicitly warns that names and details were not textually collated and transcribes Ji An as “济暗/齐暗”; Hanji 314-2 transcribes him as “几岸.” The page normalizes both to 汲黯 from the joined biographical chain. The five sources do not establish his full biography, ancestry, education, exact office sequence, wealth, every exchange with Wudi, the mechanics of governance by reputation, or the exact family appointments. Hanji 314-1 does not establish the exact frontier statute, whether all merchant death sentences were executed, or that Ji's later removal was retaliation; its legal, fiscal, and numerical details remain source-scoped. Hanji 314-2's ironware specification, claim that all recorded interactions concern public business, account of repeated removal and recall, and causal link from Ji's death to Wudi's later excesses also remain episode-attributed. Hanji 333-2 supports Ji's warning before Zhang's later fall but does not prove access to Zhang's private intent. Hanji 318 does not establish the horse's miraculous nature, whether the composition entered ancestral-temple performance, the merits of every punished official, or a complete causal link from severity to talent shortage. Claims that clean officials are structurally excluded or that virtue automatically protects descendants remain host interpretations.

## What Changed

- Extended Ji's public-purpose remonstrance from ritual and personnel into surrender reception, civilian burden, and contextual law.
- Added his willingness to substitute his own life for a threatened magistrate and his cost accounting for war and incorporation.
- Clarified why Wudi retains an uncomfortable critic: Ji's candor supplies corrective value that compliant access cannot replace.
- Added the episode's public-affairs-only characterization while qualifying its completeness and the claimed posthumous causal effect.
