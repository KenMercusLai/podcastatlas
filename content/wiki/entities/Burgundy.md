# Burgundy

## Overview

Burgundy is the late-medieval composite state whose Valois dukes joined a French duchy to territories in the [[LowCountries|Low Countries]] and came close to establishing a kingdom between [[France]] and the [[HolyRomanEmpire|Holy Roman Empire]].

## Current Profile

The polity began from the Duchy of Burgundy around Dijon but expanded through dynastic marriage, inheritance, purchase, diplomacy, and conquest. [[PhilipTheBold|Philip the Bold's]] marriage into [[Flanders]] brought access to exceptionally wealthy cloth cities; later dukes accumulated a discontinuous northern and southern realm whose wealth could support independent diplomacy and an elaborate court.

Its power remained structurally ambiguous. The dukes were French princes, territorial rulers in imperial space, commercial partners of England, and aspiring sovereigns without a royal crown. [[PhilipTheGood|Philip the Good]] exploited this position through changing alliances, while [[CharlesTheBold|Charles the Bold]] tried to close the territorial gap by force.

Charles's death at Nancy and lack of a male heir ended the project at its most exposed point. France recovered the original duchy, but [[MaryOfBurgundy|Mary of Burgundy]] and her Habsburg marriage preserved much of the northern inheritance, allowing a Burgundian political and court tradition to survive beyond the state's collapse.

## Key Characteristics

- Composite and geographically discontinuous polity spanning French and imperial jurisdictions.
- Dynastic project built through marriage, inheritance, diplomacy, purchase, and war.
- Power base combining the Dijon duchy with the commercial wealth of Flanders and the Low Countries.
- Quasi-royal court using chivalry, art, ritual, and spectacle to project sovereignty.
- Strategic actor able to shift between England and France during the Hundred Years' War.
- Vulnerable to territorial gaps, military overreach, and dynastic succession failure.
- Failed kingdom whose northern inheritance and identity continued under the Habsburgs.

## Qualifications

The state was not simply the modern French wine region, but neither was it a unified nation-state in modern terms. Its borders, titles, jurisdictions, and urban loyalties varied, and the episode's claim that it might have survived under a more patient ruler or with a male heir is a counterfactual rather than an established outcome.
