# Hercule Poirot

## Overview

Hercule Poirot is [[AgathaChristie|Agatha Christie's]] Belgian detective, presented as a physically unheroic modern outsider whose fastidious observation and social distance expose hidden guilt inside respectable English settings.

## Current Profile

[[185-agatha-christie-glt9092045420]] emphasizes Poirot's 1920 debut as a Belgian refugee rather than a nostalgic English gentleman. His short stature, conspicuous foreignness, ordered habits, and geometrically modern home oppose the masculine adventure ideal associated with earlier detectives. The episode interprets that design as fitted to a post-First World War culture more suspicious of heroic violence.

Poirot's outsider position also supports [[SocialStereotypeMisdirection]]. English characters who dismiss or mock him for being foreign often reveal their own prejudice and become less reliable judges of the people around them. His work culminates not only in technical explanation but in [[DetectiveFictionMoralOrder]], where concealed action is named and judged.

## Key Characteristics

- Belgian refugee introduced in Christie's first published novel.
- Physically unheroic, fastidious, conspicuous, and committed to mental order.
- Associated with modern design rather than exclusively nostalgic rural English imagery.
- Uses outsider status to reveal English complacency and prejudice.
- Solves cases through interpretation of people and arranged evidence rather than physical force.
- Functions as an agent of explanation and moral judgment within the episode's reading.

## Evidence

- Modern outsider: [[185-agatha-christie-glt9092045420]] contrasts Poirot's refugee identity, appearance, and modern flat with the heroic masculine detective tradition.
- Social prejudice: [[185-agatha-christie-glt9092045420]] notes that English characters who sneer at foreigners often appear foolish or morally suspect.
- Judgment function: [[185-agatha-christie-glt9092045420]] connects Christie's detectives to the restoration of order after hidden wrongdoing is exposed.

## Qualifications

This profile represents one episode's historical and moral interpretation rather than a survey of every Poirot novel, adaptation, or shift in characterization. “Modern” and “postwar” describe the hosts' reading of the 1920 debut; they do not establish a complete account of Christie's intentions. The episode's defense against simple xenophobia does not imply that every national stereotype in the fiction is harmless.

## What Changed

- Established Poirot as a canonical entity rather than an unlinked character mention.
- Defined his refugee foreignness and physical unheroism as historically modern design choices.
- Connected his outsider position to social misdirection and moral judgment.
