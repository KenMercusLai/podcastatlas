# Washita Massacre

## Overview

The Washita Massacre was the Seventh Cavalry's 1868 dawn attack under [[GeorgeArmstrongCuster]] on [[BlackKettle]]'s Cheyenne village, presented by the source as a massacre whose official celebration restored Custer's military standing.

## Current Profile

The attack emerged from a winter campaign designed to strike villages when mobile fighters were least prepared to evade the Army. Custer divided his force into four groups, approached through snow, and attacked to “Garryowen” despite warning that a larger Native presence might be nearby.

Soldiers and Osage guides killed residents, including women and children; Custer reportedly ordered soldiers to stop shooting women and children while ordering the village's men killed. His troops burned the settlement and killed 875 ponies. When the force recognized a larger village nearby, it withdrew using captured women and children as leverage.

Major Elliott and seventeen others were left behind and later found dead. Custer claimed 103 deaths, while lower estimates and disputes over who was a combatant complicate the count. The Army treated the action as a decisive victory, but contemporary criticism and the episode's emphasis on surprise, noncombatant deaths, material destruction, and hostages support the massacre designation.

## Key Characteristics

- Winter timing converted village settlement into military vulnerability.
- Surprise and force division reflected Custer's confidence in offensive action.
- The target was a peace-associated village rather than a clearly isolated war party.
- Killing, pony slaughter, and burning attacked people and the material basis of community life.
- Captive women and children were used as protection during withdrawal.
- Official victory language coexisted with contemporary and later massacre criticism.
- Elliott's loss repeated Custer's failure to account adequately for missing subordinates.

## Qualifications

- The source reports competing death totals without independently resolving them.
- It does not establish the identity or combat status of every person killed.
- The relationship between Black Kettle's village and raids used to justify the campaign remains disputed.
- Reports of captive women's sexual exploitation are treated as plausible but not fully provable in every detail.
- The source is a popular-history episode rather than a complete comparison of Army, Cheyenne, Osage, archaeological, and contemporary press evidence.

## What Changed

- Established a source-bounded account distinguishing the Army's victory claim from the massacre interpretation.
