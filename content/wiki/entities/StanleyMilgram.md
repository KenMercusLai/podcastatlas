# Stanley Milgram / 斯坦利·米尔格拉姆

## Overview
Stanley Milgram / 斯坦利·米尔格拉姆 is represented here as a classic social psychologist whose obedience experiments provide a reference point for authority pressure and experimental ethics.

## Current Profile
[[123-renxing-shiyan-dangxin-ni-ziji-779479569|123.人性实验：当心你自己！]] places Milgram beside [[SolomonAsch]] and [[PhilipZimbardo]] in the familiar postwar experimental canon. It uses that canon as background for a broader argument that social situations, group identity, attention, and authority can shape conduct outside conscious self-understanding.

[[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-7b40091055d1394cc6a3435ffecc95c8-53960014-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-7b40091055d1394cc6a3435ffecc95c8-53960014|Did I Really Do That?]] gives Milgram's work a narrower applied role. [[SaulKassin]] compares the experiment's incremental authority pressure with interrogation manuals that begin from confident accusation and seek progressively incriminating admissions. The comparison is conceptual: it does not establish that laboratory obedience and police interrogation are identical.

## Key Characteristics
- Classic experimental figure in [[SocialPsychology]].
- Associated here with authority, obedience, escalating compliance, and shock-experiment debates.
- Used as both a substantive reference and an [[ExperimentalScienceEthics|experimental-ethics]] case.
- Provides a comparison point for situational pressure without determining every real-world outcome.

## Evidence
- Experimental canon - [[123-renxing-shiyan-dangxin-ni-ziji-779479569]] names Milgram with Asch and Zimbardo as familiar background for social psychology's evidence-seeking challenge to intuitive “human nature” claims.
- Interrogation comparison - [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-7b40091055d1394cc6a3435ffecc95c8-53960014-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-7b40091055d1394cc6a3435ffecc95c8-53960014]] uses the staged escalation and authority structure of Milgram's experiment to interpret confession-producing interrogation tactics.

## Qualifications
The bounded sources use Milgram as a reference rather than supplying his complete methods, replication history, archival debates, or ethical record. The reported 65 percent figure and its comparison with interrogation remain episode-scoped. Laboratory obedience should not be treated as a direct quantitative estimate of false-confession risk.
