## Overview

李广 / Li Guang is synthesized as the Western Han “Flying General” whose frontier reputation joins tactical composure, riding and archery, simplified personal command, and unusually strong soldier loyalty. The earliest current battlefield anecdote has him save roughly one hundred riders from several thousand [[Xiongnu|匈奴]] cavalry by turning their calm exposure into [[AmbiguityBasedDeterrentBluff|an apparent ambush signal]]. Later sources show the same composure in an encirclement, but also severe losses, missed opportunity, no marquisate, route failure, inquiry, and suicide. His shared-hardship leadership and accepted responsibility support the favorable reputation transmitted through [[SimaQian|司马迁]], while the killing of the Baling commandant from restored office prevents a one-dimensional heroic profile.

## Current Profile

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-243-bairen-xiatui-sanqian-dijun-liguang-ruhe-zuodao-de-lp6a-9rzznikouo5xbknz8ywaz1r|Hanji 243]] supplies the earliest current demonstration of Li Guang's judgment under immediate numerical inferiority. When roughly one hundred riders meet several thousand Xiongnu cavalry far from the Han camp, he judges that flight would reveal weakness and invite mounted pursuit. He orders the force to stay, move closer, and dismount. Because that behavior is anomalous for an isolated unit, the enemy reportedly suspects concealed support and withdraws at night. The episode makes discipline, control of visible behavior, and modeling of enemy uncertainty the mechanism; exact numbers and enemy reasoning remain source-scoped.

The command relationship behind that discipline recurs across the evidence. Hanji 243 and 325-2 say Li Guang shares rewards, ordinary food, water, and living conditions, waits for soldiers before drinking or eating, and leaves little wealth. The army's and public's grief is therefore read as an outcome of visible burden-sharing as well as martial fame. Hanji 325-1 reinforces that moral reputation through “桃李不言，下自成蹊,” while Hanji 380-1 and 377-1 show how the reputation shapes his grandson [[LiLingWesternHan|李陵]] without proving hereditary destiny.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-257-handai-dagangjing-jiujing-you-duogang-2-lnqd8g2sh79s1uywcbyn56n_vmmv|Hanji 257]] exposes the command-design tradeoff. Li Guang follows water and grass, minimizes formation control, camp construction, night signals, and paperwork, and relies on close observation; [[ChengBushiWesternHan|程不识]] maintains heavier marching, camp, watch, and administrative routines. Soldiers prefer Li Guang and are described as willing to die for him, but neither force is attacked in the reported deployment. [[SimaGuang|司马光]] therefore treats the method as [[ExceptionDependentMilitaryCommand|dependent on exceptional talent]] and unsafe to generalize to successors or peers.

An earlier Youbeiping encirclement in [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-308-bei-tangshi-chui-cheng-shen-de-ta-jing-shi-ge-luchi-lsjtzqsil36lxejij8gazn58qeeb|Hanji 308]] shows the battlefield capacity beneath that simplicity. After separation from [[ZhangQianWesternHan|张骞]], Li Guang sends [[LiGanWesternHan|李敢]] through the opposing formation to reduce fear, forms an outward-facing circular defense, conserves arrows for close range, personally targets opposing officers, and holds until relief. Severe losses and a court judgment of 功过相当 bound the result: tactical survival and morale control do not become a clean operational victory.

The final Mobei sequence moves from ambition to failure. Hanji 324-1 says the aging Li Guang repeatedly petitions [[HanWudi|汉武帝]], receives the front-general appointment under [[WeiQingHanGeneral|卫青]], and enters the campaign burdened by Wudi's private warning that he is unlucky. Hanji 324-2 says new intelligence changes the target, Li Guang asks to retain the direct vanguard, and Wei instead pairs him with [[ZhaoShiqiWesternHan|赵食其]] on the longer, water-poor eastern route. The episode attributes the refusal both to Wudi's warning and Wei's hope that [[GongsunAoWesternHan|公孙敖]] can recover rank, but does not establish their relative weight or prove intent to cause failure.

Hanji 325-2 completes the sequence: the eastern force lacks a guide, becomes lost, misses the main battle and rendezvous, and faces inquiry. Li Guang clears his officers, accepts responsibility, and dies rather than answer junior officials after a long career. The host suspects deliberate guide removal and political maneuvering, but the evidence does not prove either. Hanji 335 compresses the same ending and shows the later grievance: Li Gan blames and assaults Wei Qing, while Wei contains the incident before [[HuoQubing|霍去病]] kills Li Gan.

The reputation remains ethically divided. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-276-baling-heye-jiangjun-liguang-weihe-bei-xiaobing-qiling-lq813ahfpxsjjy7qoq90b4cnvyzx|Hanji 276]] says a Baling commandant enforces the night ban against the out-of-office Li Guang. Once restored as Youbeiping governor, Li Guang summons and kills him. The commandant's manner may have been rude and the episode calls him drunk, but enforcement is not shown to be unlawful. The strongest supported judgment is therefore [[PersonalRevengeThroughStatePower|private revenge through public power]], not heroic vindication.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han “Flying General” whose reputation rests on tactical composure, riding and archery, and morale control under numerical inferiority.
- Commander who turns anomalous calm and dismounting into an inferred-ambush signal when flight would invite destruction.
- Frugal, generous leader whose sharing of rewards, food, water, and living conditions supports strong soldier loyalty.
- Observation-led commander whose light routines reduce friction but depend heavily on personal perception and are unsafe to imitate as general doctrine.
- Long-serving but unenfeoffed officer whose final Mobei campaign ends in reassignment, missing guide, route failure, inquiry, accepted responsibility, and suicide.
- Father and grandfather whose reputation shapes Li Gan's grievance and Li Ling's frontier self-conception.
- Ethically mixed public figure whose care for soldiers coexists with lethal retaliation against a commandant from restored state office.

## Qualifications

These eleven compact podcast sources do not reconstruct Li Guang's full campaigns or the complete Shiji and Hanshu traditions. Hanji 243's punishment context, troop totals, distances, orders, enemy inference, “Flying General” chronology, battle total, mourning scale, and modern sincerity lesson remain source-scoped; its successful bluff does not prove a generally safe tactic. Hanji 257 reports no attack on either compared force, so transfer-risk conclusions remain normative rather than an observed test. Hanji 308's routes, totals, archery detail, casualties, task definition, and relief remain episode-attributed, and its title does not establish that Li Guang became lost in that earlier battle. Hanji 324-1 and 324-2 establish distrust, reassignment, and anger without proving a plan to make him fail. Hanji 325-2 does not prove deliberate guide removal or encoded accusation by Sima Qian. Hanji 276 does not independently establish intoxication, exact authority, apology, apparent forgiveness, or execution procedure. Hanji 377-1's heredity-and-fate opening is rhetoric rather than evidence of inherited destiny.

## What Changed

- Added the earlier small-force survival case in which Li Guang uses disciplined dismounting and ambiguity to induce fear of an unseen ambush.
- Connected the bluff's credibility to the existing evidence for shared hardship, soldier trust, and control under pressure.
- Distinguished one successful psychological maneuver from a transferable doctrine and preserved force sizes and enemy reasoning as source-scoped.
- Extended the positive reputation evidence without weakening the existing limits from severe losses, final route failure, and misuse of public power.
