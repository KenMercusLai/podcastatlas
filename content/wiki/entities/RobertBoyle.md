## Overview

Robert Boyle / 罗伯特·波义耳 appears as an experimental natural philosopher whose vacuum work, scientific network, and emphasis on measured and recorded experiment help mark the methodological separation of chemistry from alchemy.

## Current Profile

One source uses Boyle's dispute with [[ThomasHobbes|Thomas Hobbes]] to show that apparatus, witnessed demonstration, and public experiment were themselves contested forms of authority. A second places him in the [[RoyalSociety|Royal Society]] network around [[IsaacNewton|Isaac Newton]], [[JohnLocke|John Locke]], and other natural philosophers, making early-modern science collective rather than the work of isolated geniuses. The new source emphasizes method: avoid deciding the result in advance, measure quantities, record conditions and data, and make experiments repeatable. Boyle therefore represents a change in verification norms, not an instant moment when all alchemical ideas disappeared.

## Key Characteristics

- Uses apparatus and public experiment to make natural-philosophical claims testable by others.
- Belongs to a correspondence and institutional network rather than a solitary discovery story.
- Represents measurement, recorded conditions, and reproducibility as stronger verification norms.
- Helps explain chemistry's differentiation from alchemy without implying a clean overnight rupture.

## Qualifications

The sources are podcast summaries rather than a full study of Boyle's writings, theology, corpuscular theory, laboratory practice, or historical reception. They use him as a methodological landmark and should not be read as proving that he alone founded modern chemistry or fully abandoned every alchemical question. Public witnessing and replication are norms whose actual early-modern implementation varied.
