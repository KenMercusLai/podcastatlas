# Louis Fieser

## Overview

Louis Fieser was a Harvard chemist presented as a co-inventor of napalm whose wartime experimentation helped create an effective gelled incendiary and who later urged President Richard Nixon to pursue an international prohibition on napalm and similar weapons.

## Current Profile

[[may-the-best-firebomb-win-may-the-best-firebomb-win]] supplies the invention history. Fieser brought an inventive, hands-on style to [[HoytHottel]]'s incendiary competition and recruited [[EBHirschberg|E. B. Hirschberg]] to test gels, fuels, consistency, ignition, and dispersal. Their gasoline mixture using aluminum naphthenate and aluminum palmitate produced burning globules that adhered to surfaces and transferred heat longer than ordinary gasoline.

[[goodbye-farewell-and-amen-goodbye-farewell-and-amen]] places that work inside a wider story of scientific creation without public reckoning. After the wartime incendiary project, Fieser returned to Harvard, taught chemistry, and co-authored a major textbook with his wife. The episode does not describe that later work as acknowledged atonement.

His later appeal for prohibition distinguishes personal reconsideration from institutional memory. The source reports the appeal but does not establish when his judgment changed, how he understood responsibility, or whether the proposal affected policy.

## Key Characteristics

- Harvard chemist and napalm co-inventor in the episodes' account.
- Combined inventive laboratory practice with systematic comparison of gel consistency, adhesion, ignition, and dispersal.
- Worked with E. B. Hirschberg under Hoyt Hottel's competitive incendiary-research program.
- Returned to teaching and textbook authorship after the war.
- Worked within an institution that publicly celebrated many inventions while leaving napalm unmarked.
- Later urged an international agreement prohibiting napalm and similar weapons.
