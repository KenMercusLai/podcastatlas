# Khudadad Khan

## Overview

Khudadad Khan was a Punjabi Muslim machine gunner in the Duke of Connaught's Own Baluchis whose defense near Hollebeke during the [[FirstBattleOfYpres|First Battle of Ypres]] made him the first Muslim recipient of the Victoria Cross.

## Current Profile

[[597-the-first-world-war-the-massacre-of-the-innocents-part-4-glt9434623550]] presents Khan as the principal individual case for the Indian Army's role in the 1914 defense of Ypres. Born in Punjab in 1888, he served in a six-man machine-gun detachment near Hollebeke.

After the other members of the detachment were killed and Khan himself was wounded, he continued firing, disabled the gun before abandoning the position, and crawled back under German fire. [[GeorgeV|George V]] later awarded him the Victoria Cross.

The episode places this action inside a much larger imperial contribution, stating that about 1.5 million Indians, including roughly 400,000 Muslims, served Britain during the First World War. Khan's profile therefore connects an individual decoration to colonial manpower and to a Western Front story often narrated mainly through European armies.

## Key Characteristics

- Punjabi Muslim soldier in the Duke of Connaught's Own Baluchis.
- Machine gunner in the Indian Army contingent defending the Ypres sector.
- Continued fighting after being wounded and after the rest of his detachment was killed.
- Disabled his weapon before withdrawing from the position.
- First Muslim awarded the Victoria Cross.
- Individual case that makes the scale and diversity of Indian wartime service visible.

## Qualifications

This is a bounded battlefield profile based on one podcast summary, not a complete biography or regimental history. Exact unit wording, action sequence, casualty detail, award circumstances, service totals, and the category used to count Muslim participants remain source-scoped until checked against military records and specialist scholarship. One decorated action should not stand in for the varied experiences of Indian soldiers or for the coercive and political structure of British imperial service.
