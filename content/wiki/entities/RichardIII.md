# Richard III / 理查三世

## Overview
Richard III / 理查三世 is the English protector-turned-king whose seizure of Edward V, usurpation, and probable responsibility for the princes' deaths remain inseparable from later Tudor and Shakespearean memory.

## Current Profile
The sources establish a necessary source-critical boundary: [[TheDaughterOfTime|《时间的女儿》]] reopens accusations shaped by later winners, while [[RichardIIIPlay|Shakespeare's Richard III]] intensifies a Tudor-friendly villain image. Part 1 adds the pre-1483 man: slight and probably affected by scoliosis but militarily capable, serious, pious, administratively effective, associated with the North, and loyal to Edward IV after Clarence's downfall. Part 2 does not discard either context or caution, but reaches a firmer event-level judgment after Richard escalates from protector to usurper. His control of access, motive, inability to produce the boys, and failure to accuse Buckingham make responsibility overwhelmingly probable, though not conclusively proved.

## Key Characteristics
- His public image is a prime wiki case of literary and dynastic memory overpowering uncertain evidence.
- Responsible revision of his reputation depends on source dating, political incentive, material evidence, and rival explanations.
- Shakespeare's play is treated as a major transmitter of the hostile Richard image, not as neutral biography.
- Before 1483 he was a loyal and competent servant of Edward IV with a northern power base, military experience, and serious piety.
- His 1483 path is best understood as escalating improvisation rather than either a fully pre-planned coup or innocent protectorship.
- The current evidence set judges him probably responsible for the princes' deaths without converting political inference into forensic certainty.
- The 2012 Leicester remains discovery complicates caricature by adding material evidence, including wounds and spinal curvature.

- Princes evidence: [[69-xianliao-tuili-wenxue-lishixuezhe-ke-bu-jiu-shi-zhentan-ma-666244465]] centers the [[PrincesInTheTower]] accusation as the case where later narratives and political winners shape public certainty.
- Material evidence: [[69-xianliao-tuili-wenxue-lishixuezhe-ke-bu-jiu-shi-zhentan-ma-666244465]] mentions the Leicester parking-lot discovery as evidence that supports identification while complicating the exaggerated literary image.
- Shakespeare evidence: [[495-lu-da-peng-tan-sha-shi-bi-ya-xi-ju-yu-ying-guo-li-shi-de-hu-xiang-ying-xiang-lthqtli5t6jsfn38mjtitzpoq3oo]] says [[RichardIIIPlay]] darkens Richard III to support Tudor legitimacy and turn Henry VII's arrival into the restoration of order.

## Qualifications
The murder inference is political and circumstantial rather than forensic. The sources do not establish the date, method, agents, or remains of the deaths. Part 1's positive traits neither prove innocence nor make the later sequence inevitable; piety, competence, loyalty, and ruthlessness could coexist in fifteenth-century rule. Richard's possible role in Henry VI's death also remains uncertain. Tudor and Shakespearean hostility can distort a reputation without making him innocent.

## What Changed
- Shifted the current judgment from open accusation to probable responsibility while preserving the absence of conclusive proof.
- Added escalating improvisation as the mechanism connecting protectorship, usurpation, and likely murder.
- Added the pre-1483 profile of loyal service, northern authority, military competence, piety, and qualified ruthlessness.
- Preserved the distinction between event-level culpability and later Tudor-Shakespearean exaggeration.
