# Law School Admission Test (LSAT)

## Overview

The Law School Admission Test, or LSAT, is a standardized admissions assessment that [[the-tortoise-and-the-hare-the-tortoise-and-the-hare|The Tortoise and the Hare]] examines as both a carefully engineered comparison tool and a speeded gateway into legal education.

## Current Profile

The episode describes sections of roughly 20–25 questions completed in 35 minutes and reports that individual questions can take years to develop and pretest. Its designers remove items showing unexplained group differences and defend fixed timing as standardization and as a measure of efficient reading and analysis.

Gladwell's objection is construct validity: fixed limits measure difficult reasoning under uniform time pressure, while important legal work may reward slow, persistent analysis over months. More time or flexible allocation could reveal additional capable candidates, but it would also change score distributions and weaken a convenient ranking mechanism. The episode ultimately argues for recognizing both speed and deliberation rather than treating either as complete legal merit.

## Key Characteristics

- Uses fixed, short section limits to compare candidates under uniform conditions.
- Combines reasoning difficulty with reading and problem-solving speed.
- Relies on extensive item development and pretesting in the episode's account.
- Screens individual items for measurable group-performance disparities.
- Feeds a high-stakes admissions hierarchy in which score separation has institutional value.
- May underrepresent candidates whose strongest reasoning emerges with more time.

## Evidence

### Timing and test construction

- [[the-tortoise-and-the-hare-the-tortoise-and-the-hare]] reports 35-minute sections, multi-year question development, repeated testing, and removal of an item with unexplained sex-linked performance differences.

### Standardization rationale

- [[the-tortoise-and-the-hare-the-tortoise-and-the-hare]] attributes to test representatives the view that uniform timing supports comparability and tests efficiency relevant to law school.

### Validity challenge

- [[the-tortoise-and-the-hare-the-tortoise-and-the-hare]] contrasts timed performance with slow Supreme Court analysis and argues that additional time could change who crosses elite-school score thresholds.

## Qualifications

The profile reflects one episode's structured summary rather than current LSAC technical documentation, validity studies, accommodation policy, scoring specifications, or longitudinal outcome research. It does not establish that all slower candidates reason better, that more time would improve fairness on every dimension, or that speed is irrelevant to law. The reported timing and admissions practices are historical to the 2019 episode and should not be assumed to describe current administration without verification.

## What Changed

- Established the episode's account of LSAT timing, item design, and standardization rationale.
- Added the distinction between item-level bias review and test-level construct validity.
- Preserved speed as a real legal capability while rejecting it as the whole of legal merit.
