# Robert Peel

## Overview

Robert Peel was a British Conservative prime minister remembered across these sources through repeal of the Corn Laws, creation of the Metropolitan Police, and consent-based principles that define police effectiveness through public approval and voluntary cooperation.

## Current Profile

[[35-the-prime-ministers-world-cup-glt6124252189]] presents Peel as a politician whose public reputation benefits from clear, teachable institutional associations. Corn Law repeal and the Metropolitan Police give voters concise reasons to recognize him, helping him defeat the seeded [[RobertWalpole|Robert Walpole]] with 64%.

The hosts regard Walpole's foundational role and longevity as strong reasons to rank him higher, so Peel's victory becomes evidence that accessible schoolbook memory can outweigh diffuse constitutional importance. The result does not establish that Peel's premiership was more consequential overall.

[[taxonomy-of-the-modern-mystery-story]] develops the policing association rather than the premiership. It emphasizes principles attributed to Peel: police effectiveness depends on public approval, voluntary cooperation, limited reliance on force, and the idea that police and public are not separate populations. In the episode's argument, those principles explain why low trust can reduce witness cooperation and homicide clearance.

## Key Characteristics

- Conservative prime minister associated with repeal of the Corn Laws.
- Remembered as a founder of the Metropolitan Police.
- Benefits from clear institutional and schoolbook associations.
- Defeated the more constitutionally foundational Walpole in the audience poll.
- Associated with a consent-based account of policing that treats public cooperation as operational capacity.

## Qualifications

The sources do not provide a full account of Peel's policing reforms, Irish policy, party transformation, economic thought, governments, or the social consequences of Corn Law repeal. The schoolbook-recognition explanation is plausible but not demonstrated from individual voter evidence. The policing lecture does not reproduce the principles' drafting history or resolve scholarly questions about their later attribution, implementation, coercive context, or unequal experience.
