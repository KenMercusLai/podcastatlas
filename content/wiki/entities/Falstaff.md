# Sir John Falstaff

## Overview

Sir John Falstaff is Shakespeare's comic knight and Prince Hal companion, used by the episode as a skeptical counterweight to honour, glory, and the making of [[HenryVOfEngland|Henry V]].

## Current Profile

Falstaff is cowardly, dishonest, funny, and dramatically indispensable precisely because he punctures the martial values celebrated around kings and battles. His dismissal of honour as empty air makes bodily survival and common sense answer the elite language of glory. Prince Hal's eventual rejection of him turns a personal break into a public transformation toward kingship.

The character is a composite invention rather than a historical companion. His name partly recalls John Fastolf, while Shakespeare initially called him Oldcastle. The historical [[JohnOldcastle]] was a soldier and later Lollard rebel, not the coward portrayed on stage.

## Key Characteristics

- Comic companion within Shakespeare's Prince Hal narrative.
- Cowardly and dishonest figure whose wit creates moral force.
- Skeptic of honour and military glory.
- Counterweight against uncomplicated heroic kingship.
- Composite character distinct from both Oldcastle and Fastolf.
- Figure whose rejection marks Hal's public transition toward Henry V.

## Qualifications

The episode offers an interpretation of Falstaff's dramatic function, not a complete literary analysis of every play in which he appears. Parallels with Oldcastle and Fastolf concern naming and inspiration; they do not make the character a reliable portrait of either man.

## What Changed

- Established Falstaff as a moral counterweight to heroic honour and as the dramatic relationship Hal must reject to become Henry V.
