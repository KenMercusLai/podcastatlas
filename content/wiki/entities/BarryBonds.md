# Barry Bonds

## Overview

Barry Bonds is a Major League Baseball player used in the episode as the contrasting standard case for transformative performance enhancement and competitive coercion.

## Current Profile

[[the-standard-case-the-standard-case|The Standard Case]] presents Bonds as a player believed to have used performance-enhancing drugs late in his career, accompanied by dramatic physical and performance change. At age 37 he hit 73 home runs after seasons more commonly in the 30-to-40 range.

George Will argues that the scale of transformation pressured competitors to accept comparable health risks in order to keep pace. [[MalcolmGladwell]] therefore locates the moral problem not in medical technology alone but in how extensively it was used, what new capacity it enabled, and the coercive effect it could have on a competitive field.

## Key Characteristics

- Serves as the episode's standard case for materially transformative enhancement.
- Combines alleged drug use with unusually large late-career physical and performance change.
- Makes competitor risk pressure part of the ethical analysis.
- Shows why degree and effect can matter more than a binary technology-versus-no-technology rule.

## Evidence

### Transformation

- [[the-standard-case-the-standard-case]] contrasts Bonds's late-career body and 73-home-run season with his more typical earlier output.

### Competitive coercion

- [[the-standard-case-the-standard-case]] relays Will's claim that rivals could feel forced to accept similar health risks to compete.

## Qualifications

This profile represents the episode's ethical comparator, not an independent adjudication of Bonds's drug history. It relies on a structured summary rather than medical evidence, league records, legal findings, complete performance analysis, or Bonds's account. Changes in performance can have several causes, and the threshold at which enhancement becomes transformative remains contestable.

## What Changed

- Established Bonds as the taxonomy's transformative and competitively coercive standard case.
- Added scale, created capacity, health risk, and pressure on rivals as distinct moral considerations.
