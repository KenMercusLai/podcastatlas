# Will Creeley

## Overview
Will Creeley is the [[FoundationForIndividualRightsAndExpression|Foundation for Individual Rights and Expression]] legal director interviewed in [[metas-youth-safety-settlement-could-change-how-people-access-the-internet]] about Meta's youth-safety settlement and online speech rights.

## Current Profile
In this source, Creeley functions as a civil-liberties critic of settlement-driven platform regulation. He accepts that excessive screen time and social-media harms are real concerns, but argues that broad enforcement tools can make ordinary social-media access depend on identification and can chill unpopular or dissenting speech.

## Key Characteristics
- Creeley frames age separation on major platforms as likely identity verification for all users.
- He distinguishes adult-content age checks from identity checks for general-purpose social platforms.
- He treats minors and adults as both having speech-access interests online.
- He argues that platform curation has First Amendment protection when imposed changes would affect editorial judgment.
- He prefers parent, child, and user control over one-size-fits-all government or platform mandates.

## Evidence
- Identity-verification critique: [[metas-youth-safety-settlement-could-change-how-people-access-the-internet]] records Creeley's warning that separating children from adults may require everyone to show identification.
- Constitutional boundary: [[metas-youth-safety-settlement-could-change-how-people-access-the-internet]] has Creeley cite minors' speech rights, adults' anonymous-access interests, and the narrowness of [[FreeSpeechCoalitionVPaxton]].
- Settlement-pressure concern: [[metas-youth-safety-settlement-could-change-how-people-access-the-internet]] describes Creeley's view that a private settlement can produce changes a state law might not survive.

## Qualifications
The source gives Creeley's perspective and does not include a direct response from Meta, plaintiffs, parents, child-safety advocates, TikTok, or YouTube. His analysis is therefore a civil-liberties legal critique rather than a settled judicial finding about the settlement.
