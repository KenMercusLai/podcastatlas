## Overview

欧阳歙 / Ouyang Xi is currently synthesized as a high-status Eastern Han official whose fall makes [[DutianAccountabilityEasternHan|degree-field accountability]] concrete. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-717-13-ge-zi-yinfa-de-diwang-zhinu-liz3jsbvw3snfapzsrqw917fqv7v|Hanji 717]] presents him as a Shangshu-lineage scholar-official, former Runan taishou, and Situ who is jailed for inaccurate land measurement and large embezzlement, then dies in prison despite mass petitioning.

## Current Profile

Ouyang Xi's profile combines cultural prestige and administrative liability. The source says his family transmitted Shangshu learning for generations and had many disciples, giving him unusual social and scholarly capital. He also held major offices under the late Xin/Gengshi/Eastern Han transition before reaching Situ.

His fall is framed through earlier Runan service. The charge is not merely vague corruption: it ties inaccurate degree-field reporting to embezzlement of a very large sum. More than a thousand people petition for him, and Li Zhen offers to die in his place, but [[LiuXiu|Liu Xiu]] does not relent. The durable wiki claim is that Ouyang Xi becomes a visible high-status punishment signal inside Guangwu's fiscal-administrative cleanup.

## Key Characteristics

- Eastern Han Situ whose previous local service becomes the basis for a later accountability case.
- Shangshu-lineage official with many disciples and enough prestige to draw large-scale petitions.
- Former Runan taishou accused of inaccurate degree-field reporting and major embezzlement.
- High-status punishment target whom Liu Xiu refuses to spare despite emotional and scholarly pressure.
- Evidence point for the claim that fiscal enforcement could override classical reputation and office rank.

## Qualifications

The current page is source-bounded to Hanji 717. It does not yet reconstruct Ouyang Xi's full earlier career, the legal record behind the embezzlement amount, or whether petitioners disputed guilt or pleaded for mercy despite guilt. The host's harsh moral judgment is kept as source interpretation; the canonical claim is narrower, concerning office rank, scholarly prestige, degree-field falsification, and punishment.
