# Jonathan Keats

## Overview
Jonathan Keats is the artist and self-described experimental philosopher in [[salt-020260914-archive-keats-podcast]] and [[02026-heggli-keats-csank-podcast-v2]]. He turns philosophical questions into public situations, artifacts, and long-duration experiments.

## Current Profile
Keats's wiki role is to add an art-and-philosophy route into the [[LongNow]] branch. Where other Long Now sources emphasize institutional design, ecological memory, progress communication, or mathematical models, Keats builds objects that let people inhabit questions about thought, time, identity, cities, ecology, and future accountability. His bristlecone work now extends from a proposed living calendar to a publicly accessible municipal clock whose rate changes with tree growth, making [[Chronodiversity]] a civic experience rather than only a speculative idea.

## Key Characteristics
- Keats treats thought experiments as public situations whose conclusions should remain open.
- His work often uses intentionally absurd or speculative objects to reveal ordinary assumptions about time, labor, identity, and value.
- He moves from short conceptual gestures toward projects that operate across centuries or millennia.
- He relies on institutional hosts such as [[LongNow]] when they provide space for inquiry without prescribing outcomes.
- His deep-time work makes future viewers or living ecological processes part of the artwork's meaning.
- He uses variable environmental processes to question whether accuracy must mean uniform precision.

## Evidence
### Public Experiment
- [[salt-020260914-archive-keats-podcast]] describes Keats's 2000 gallery project, where he used a factory time clock and time cards to mark thoughts and made the punched card the purchasable trace.
- [[salt-020260914-archive-keats-podcast]] says he became dissatisfied with thought experiments used only as predetermined arguments and began calling himself an experimental philosopher.

### Speculative Objects
- [[salt-020260914-archive-keats-podcast]] links lead time ingots, a time-warp undershirt, neutron-star architecture, rotating cities, and epigenetic "cloning" to Keats's habit of making improbable proposals that expose hidden assumptions.

### Deep-Time Practice
- [[salt-020260914-archive-keats-podcast]] presents Keats's 100-year and 1,000-year cameras as ways to make people imagine future viewers looking back on present choices.
- [[salt-020260914-archive-keats-podcast]] introduces [[CenturiesOfTheBristlecone]] as a 5,000-year calendar proposal rooted in tree growth and climate exposure.
- [[02026-heggli-keats-csank-podcast-v2]] adds dated survey markers around a sapling and a municipal clock, engineered with Phil Abernathy and Brittany Nicole Cox, whose pendulum rate follows bristlecone growth.

### Chronodiversity and Public Responsibility
- [[02026-heggli-keats-csank-podcast-v2]] frames chronodiversity as part of biodiversity and presents tree growth, river flow, and river meanders as alternative temporal standards that expose ecological feedback.
- [[02026-heggli-keats-csank-podcast-v2]] distinguishes the Clock of the Long Now's precision from a biological clock's environmental accuracy and treats the two approaches as mutually illuminating.

## Qualifications
Keats's projects are not presented as conventional policy instruments. The later source adds engineering partners and a museum-access plan, but long-run operation, conservation effects, calibration, and governance remain unresolved. Their demonstrated value in the bounded sources is imagination, conversation, ritual, and public accountability rather than guaranteed technical or ecological effectiveness.

## What Changed
- The bristlecone proposal is now a more concrete calendar-and-clock system, with survey markers, named engineering collaborators, and a public museum partner.
- Keats's temporal argument now explicitly includes chronodiversity and a distinction between uniform precision and environmental accuracy.
