# Henry IV of England

## Overview

Henry IV was the Lancastrian king whose path from younger [[LordsAppellant|Lord Appellant]] and admired chivalric magnate to exiled inheritance claimant, deposer of [[RichardIIOfEngland|Richard II]], and survivor of rebellion created the disputed crown inherited by [[HenryVOfEngland|Henry V]].

## Current Profile

Before kingship Henry combined noble popularity, tournament and military prestige, travel, devotion, and proximity to the royal line. He joined the younger wing of the Lords Appellant and defeated Robert de Vere at Radcot Bridge, but Richard later used office and honor to separate Henry and Mowbray from the older Appellants. When Richard resumed revenge in 1397, their former membership made both men vulnerable. Their mutual accusations led to a planned trial by combat that Richard stopped before exiling Henry for ten years.

The immediate rupture came after [[JohnOfGaunt|John of Gaunt's]] death, when Richard prevented Henry's return and moved to seize the Lancastrian inheritance. Henry landed in 1399 publicly claiming his duchy. Richard's absence in Ireland, elite fear of arbitrary confiscation, and rapid loss of support let inheritance recovery expand into capture, deposition, and a partly elective kingship. Henry's hereditary claim was not clean: the Mortimer line complicated succession, and an argument against female-line transmission sat awkwardly beside English claims in France.

Henry supplemented dynastic weakness with property protection, public approval, English-language communication, election, coronation ritual, and a promise of better government. Richard's death after the failed Epiphany Rising nevertheless made regicide inseparable from the regime's origin. Financial weakness, tax expectations, Welsh revolt, Percy dependence, and foreign nonrecognition kept ordinary governing pressures tied to the title.

At [[BattleOfShrewsbury|Shrewsbury]], Henry's rapid march and battlefield survival prevented [[HenryPercyHotspur|Hotspur's]] coalition from maturing. Victory did not end the Welsh, Percy, or moral crises: [[OwainGlyndwr|Owain Glyndŵr]] remained active, Archbishop Scroop's execution stained the reign, and illness increased dependence on Henry's sons. French civil conflict offered leverage, but Henry died with the usurpation's moral burden unresolved and the operational succession secure.

## Key Characteristics

- Celebrated chivalric magnate and former Lord Appellant whose popularity preceded kingship.
- Exile and inheritance claimant whose return widened into deposition when Richard's coalition collapsed.
- Founder of a contested dynasty using election, property security, public consent, vernacular address, and ritual to supplement weak hereditary right.
- Rapid political and military survivor who defeated Hotspur before the rebel coalition consolidated.
- Manager of Welsh, Percy, fiscal, and foreign pressure through force, pardon, family government, and shifting alliances.
- Devout but morally compromised ruler whose regime remained burdened by Richard's death and Scroop's execution.

## Qualifications

The sources do not settle Henry's original intentions on landing, the constitutional weight of election and consent, the hereditary priority among competing lines, or responsibility for Richard's death. Language of public will should not be mistaken for modern democracy. The purpose of the Tripartite Indenture, Northumberland's intentions, Scroop's prosecution, Henry's diagnosis, providential interpretations of illness, and particular diplomatic choices remain open or source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Added the legal irony and ritual stakes surrounding deposition and accession.
- Distinguished Henry's initial inheritance claim from the crown-taking enabled by Richard's political collapse.
