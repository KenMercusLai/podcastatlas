# Henry IV of England

## Overview

Henry IV is the Lancastrian king whose disputed accession, survival of rebellion, family government, and opportunistic interventions in France shaped the inheritance of [[HenryVOfEngland|Henry V]].

## Current Profile

The sources present Henry's reign as a sustained struggle to make a disputed crown durable. In 1403, [[HenryPercyHotspur|Hotspur's]] rebellion, recruitment around Chester, and advance toward [[BattleOfShrewsbury|Shrewsbury]] threatened to join wider opposition. Henry answered with a forced march, attacked after negotiation failed, survived Hotspur's attempt to reach him, and converted victory into deterrence through the execution and display of rebel leaders.

Victory did not end the crisis. [[OwainGlyndwr|Owain Glyndŵr's]] revolt remained powerful, Northumberland required containment, and Henry's deceptive arrest and execution of Archbishop Scroop became a moral and political stain. Recurrent illness then reduced the king's capacity, encouraged providential interpretations among contemporaries, and made his unusually capable sons increasingly important to government.

Across the Channel, the reign was formally covered by an Anglo-French truce but strategically closer to a cold war. French authorities denied Henry's full royal legitimacy, supported English enemies in Scotland and Wales, and treated him as Henry of Lancaster, while English interests faced maritime pressure from [[Harfleur]]. French factional conflict nevertheless gave him leverage: he backed the Burgundian side in 1411 and the Armagnac side in 1412, extracting wealth and concessions without committing to a stable partner. He died with the usurpation's moral burden unresolved but the succession operationally secure.

## Key Characteristics

- Usurping founder of a Lancastrian regime burdened by contested legitimacy.
- Rapid political and military survivor who defeated Hotspur at Shrewsbury before the rebel coalition matured.
- Devout ruler whose execution of Archbishop Scroop sharpened the moral contradiction in his reputation.
- Manager of continuing Welsh, Percy, and foreign threats through force, pardon, rival power centers, and shifting alliances.
- Ailing king who increasingly depended on capable sons and family government.
- Father who left Henry V both an insecure title and an effective succession.

## Qualifications

The sources do not provide a full account of Henry's seizure of the throne or administration. The purpose of the Tripartite Indenture, Northumberland's intentions, Scroop's prosecution, and particular diplomatic choices remain open to interpretation. Contemporary claims that illness was divine punishment are evidence of reputation, not diagnosis or causation; modern identification of the disease from symptom reports remains uncertain. Henry's shifting French alignments should not be mistaken for ideological commitment.

## What Changed

- Extended the profile backward to Shrewsbury and the survival of Hotspur's rebellion.
- Added Glyndŵr, Northumberland, and Scroop as continuing tests of Lancastrian authority.
- Added illness and family government as the bridge from personal incapacity to stable succession.
- Qualified Henry's devotion with coercion, broken trust, execution, and punitive display.
