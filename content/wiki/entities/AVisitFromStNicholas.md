# A Visit from St. Nicholas

## Overview

“A Visit from St. Nicholas,” widely known as “'Twas the Night Before Christmas,” is an anonymously published 1823 poem conventionally attributed to [[ClementClarkeMoore]] and influential in the formation of the modern [[SantaClaus]].

## Current Profile

[[twas-the-night-before-christmas-twas-the-night-before-christmas]] treats the poem as both a literary-attribution problem and a cultural intervention. Its lively anapestic meter aided memorization, while its nocturnal visitor, prosperous household, gifts for children, and eight named reindeer supplied a durable American Christmas template.

The episode reconstructs the claim that [[HenryLivingstonJr]] wrote the poem but concludes that the evidence favors Moore. Family recollection and stylistic resemblance make the alternative intelligible; the lack of a surviving Livingston manuscript or public claim, together with Moore's relevant child-oriented and Christmas verse, makes it substantially weaker.

## Key Characteristics

- Published anonymously in the *Troy Sentinel* in 1823.
- Conventionally attributed to Clement Clarke Moore.
- Written in a memorable anapestic meter associated with light verse.
- Names eight reindeer and depicts a domestic nocturnal gift-giver.
- Helped redirect Christmas imagination toward children and the family home.
- Became the subject of a durable alternative claim for Henry Livingston Jr.

## Evidence

- Publication and cultural role: [[twas-the-night-before-christmas-twas-the-night-before-christmas]] places the anonymous 1823 printing beside the poem's reindeer, household scene, and broad memorability.
- Attribution: [[twas-the-night-before-christmas-twas-the-night-before-christmas]] compares inherited Livingston claims and stylistic arguments with Moore's relevant vocabulary, imagery, rhymes, meter, and child-oriented Christmas writing.
- Historical setting: [[twas-the-night-before-christmas-twas-the-night-before-christmas]] connects the poem's gift-giving visitor to elite New York efforts to domesticate older public Christmas customs.
