# Henry V of England

## Overview

Henry V is the English king whose Welsh apprenticeship, survival at [[BattleOfShrewsbury|Shrewsbury]], battlefield command at [[BattleOfAgincourt|Agincourt]], conquest of [[Normandy]], and [[TreatyOfTroyes|Treaty of Troyes]] created a formidable but unfinished claim to rule both [[England]] and [[France]].

## Current Profile

The sources present Henry as a highly capable but morally complicated soldier, diplomat, propagandist, and administrator whose formation began well before accession. He was thirteen when his father took the crown and received the titles Prince of Wales, Duke of Cornwall, and Earl of Chester two days after the coronation. Assignment to Richardian Cheshire and then the Welsh war exposed him early to politically hostile territory. Command against [[OwainGlyndwr|Owain Glyndŵr]] taught the young prince tactics, finance, logistics, stronghold recovery, and economic blockade. At Shrewsbury in 1403 he joined his father's army, survived an arrow driven deeply into his face, and returned to command after John Bradmore's exceptional surgery. This record sharply qualifies Shakespeare's image of an idle tavern-haunting Prince Hal.

Henry inherited from [[HenryIVOfEngland|Henry IV]] a contested dynasty and an Anglo-French relationship that remained hostile beneath formal truce. Within two years of accession, he used France's civil conflict, dynastic claims, marriage proposals, territorial demands, and unpaid ransom as both negotiating instruments and grounds for war. His religious conviction and the domestic value of victory made the invasion an opportunity, while the [[SouthamptonPlot]] showed the political danger of failure.

His 1415 invasion joined political theater to operational risk. Parliament, magnates, shipping, a force dominated by archers, and heavy artillery supported a campaign aimed first at [[Harfleur]] and potentially at [[Normandy]], but the prolonged siege cost time, health, and manpower. Rather than sail home with a damaged port as his only visible success, he chose a march toward Calais that French control of the Somme turned into a hungry inland detour.

Before Agincourt he held together an exhausted army through discipline, calm command, confession, masses, patriotic appeal, and confidence that divine justice favored his small force. He then used [[TerrainConstrainedCombinedArms|terrain, archers, stakes, formation, and initiative]] to force battle on terms that degraded the larger French army.

The victory also exposes the limits of heroic kingship. When Henry believed French forces might renew the attack and his baggage train was under threat, he ordered prisoners killed despite his soldiers' ransom interest and the order's conflict with chivalric and Christian norms. He nevertheless treated Agincourt as divine confirmation, used it to reinforce [[LancastrianLegitimacy]], and assembled taxation, naval security, imperial diplomacy, and Burgundian cooperation for a larger conquest.

His religious profile is not reducible to a single line from youthful reform interest to mature persecution. Lancastrian Wycliffite connections, possession of an English Bible, support for academic freedom, and friendship with [[JohnOldcastle]] complicate his later reputation for intense orthodoxy. They do not prove private Lollard belief, and Oldcastle's later rebellion created a direct conflict between personal history, heresy policy, and royal authority.

His 1420 settlement was more ambitious than holding Normandy: marriage to [[CatherineOfValois|Catherine of Valois]] made him regent and heir to [[CharlesVIOfFrance|Charles VI]]. Yet continued resistance and Henry's death in 1422 left this [[AngloFrenchDualMonarchy|dual-monarchy]] project to an infant.

## Key Characteristics

- Dynastic heir given major titles and exposure to unstable Cheshire and Wales from age thirteen.
- Precocious commander formed by Welsh war, administration, logistics, blockade, battlefield injury, and recovery.
- Ambitious religious claimant who combined diplomacy, dynastic claims, parliamentary finance, military preparation, and conquest discipline before invading France.
- Battlefield commander who sustained cohesion under exhaustion and used formation, archers, stakes, terrain, and initiative at Agincourt.
- Operational gambler whose march toward Calais and later fear-driven prisoner-killing order expose the risks and coercion inside his command.
- Regime builder and diplomat who converted military success into domestic legitimacy and used imperial and Burgundian relationships to isolate opponents.
- Conqueror and political communicator who established control over Normandy and Rouen while using English and French identities and advancing English-language administration.

## Qualifications

The sources do not resolve how sincere Henry's maximalist diplomacy was, whether he sought battle during the march, whether refusal to retreat was prudent, or whether he could have sustained the French settlement had he lived. Admiration for his competence is balanced by Harfleur's destruction and expulsions, a hanging used to enforce discipline, prisoner killing, battlefield mass death, looting, taxation, continuing resistance, religious persecution, and the possibility that seeking all France was strategic overreach. His friendship with Oldcastle and interest in reform do not establish Lollard adherence or a clear private conversion narrative. The tennis-balls story, exact force and fleet totals, and motives behind particular decisions remain disputed or reconstructed.

## What Changed

- Extended Henry's profile backward to Welsh command, administrative training, Shrewsbury, grave injury, and recovery.
- Replaced the simple wayward-youth frame with evidence of early military and governmental responsibility.
- Added the tension between earlier reformist contacts and later royal orthodoxy.
- Added Oldcastle as the personal relationship through which religious dissent became a test of kingship.
- Added princely titles and deliberate exposure to dangerous regions as the opening of Henry's apprenticeship.
