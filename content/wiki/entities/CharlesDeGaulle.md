# Charles de Gaulle

## Overview

Charles de Gaulle was the French president who initially underestimated May 1968, appeared close to losing authority as protest and strikes spread, then recovered through army reassurance, parliamentary dissolution, a radio address, counter-demonstration, and election.

## Current Profile

[[353-paris-1968-the-students-revolt-part-1-glt6124707164]] presents de Gaulle before and during the outbreak as the aging embodiment of France, confident that French youth were more disciplined than protesters abroad. He and his ministers first interpreted unrest partly as students avoiding exams, then oscillated between concession and repression while campus conflict became barricades, occupations, and nationwide strikes.

[[354-paris-1968-the-return-of-de-gaulle-part-2-glt6246374874]] places that misreading against his longer symbolic career: Verdun, Free French leadership, liberation, the Fifth Republic, and an almost monarchical embodiment of the nation. His unannounced trip to Baden-Baden tested army loyalty and restored his confidence, but caused panic among officials left in Paris. On returning, he accepted [[GeorgesPompidou|Georges Pompidou's]] proposal to dissolve the National Assembly, used radio to evoke his wartime broadcasts, framed the contest as order against communist intimidation, and benefited from a large Gaullist demonstration and landslide election result.

## Key Characteristics

- Embodied a personalized and highly presidential idea of French national authority.
- Drew legitimacy from wartime resistance, liberation, and the creation of the Fifth Republic.
- Expected French youth to remain disciplined and initially misread campus revolt as limited disorder or exam avoidance.
- Oscillated between concession and repression as police confrontation, occupations, and strikes widened.
- Treated army loyalty as the decisive backstop of state authority during the crisis.
- Recovered politically by shifting the contest from street mobilization to broadcast leadership and elections.
- Used historical memory and anti-communist framing to assemble a public constituency for order.

## Evidence

- Accumulated authority: [[354-paris-1968-the-return-of-de-gaulle-part-2-glt6246374874]] connects de Gaulle's presidential role to Verdun, Free France, the 1944 liberation, and the Fifth Republic.
- Early misreading: [[353-paris-1968-the-students-revolt-part-1-glt6124707164]] describes his confidence in French youth, dismissal of student motives, and uneven movement between conciliation and repression.
- Crisis and hesitation: [[354-paris-1968-the-return-of-de-gaulle-part-2-glt6246374874]] describes his failed 24 May intervention, sleeplessness, and despair on 28-29 May.
- Military backstop: [[354-paris-1968-the-return-of-de-gaulle-part-2-glt6246374874]] presents the Baden-Baden meeting with Jacques Massu as a test of army loyalty and a turning point in de Gaulle's confidence.
- Political recovery: [[354-paris-1968-the-return-of-de-gaulle-part-2-glt6246374874]] supports the sequence of return, dissolution, radio address, Gaullist march, and election landslide.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to two narrative episodes about May 1968 rather than a full assessment of de Gaulle's career, Algeria policy, constitutional legacy, foreign policy, or resignation in 1969. The sources reconstruct private emotion and counterfactual danger dramatically; the army's actual response to an order for force and the precise causal weight of policing, concessions, the Baden-Baden meeting, dissolution, and public desire for order cannot be established from them alone.

## What Changed

- Added de Gaulle's pre-crisis confidence, early dismissal, and oscillation between concession and repression.
- Reframed his later recovery as the reversal of an earlier failure to understand the student revolt.
