# Salem Witch Trials / 塞勒姆猎巫

## Overview

The Salem Witch Trials / 塞勒姆猎巫 were the lethal 1692 New England proceedings in which household symptoms and local accusations expanded through religious fear, public performance, legal procedure, coerced confession, and death sentences.

## Current Profile

The current synthesis treats Salem as a systemic crisis and an exceptional escalation. War, disease, poverty, political uncertainty, Puritan discipline, gendered suspicion, property conflict, family pressure, and local grudges made accusation available; [[SpectralEvidence]] and denunciation incentives then helped subjective experience become institutional proof. The Springfield comparison shows that shared witchcraft belief did not ordinarily require this outcome: courts could doubt a case, many accusations did not proceed, and many prosecutions failed.

## Key Characteristics

- Girls' abnormal symptoms become a public and religiously meaningful household crisis.
- Accusation expands from socially marginal women to respectable villagers, defenders, children, men, and previous accusers.
- [[SpectralEvidence]] allows claimed visions and courtroom bodily performances to count against the accused.
- Confession and naming others become possible survival strategies under [[CoercedDenunciation]].
- Outside authority eventually limits evidence and arrests rather than the community correcting itself spontaneously.
- Later apology, reputation restoration, and memorialization provide slow and incomplete repair.
- Comparative evidence from Springfield marks Salem as an aberrant trajectory, not the necessary result of Puritan belief.

## Evidence

### Social pressure and procedural escalation

- [[91-liewu-sailemu-1692-cong-yizheng-kaishi-714732018]] connects external crisis, village conflict, gendered suspicion, spectacle, spectral evidence, confession incentives, executions, and later memory.

### Comparative qualification

- [[331-american-witches-glt9152886733]] contrasts Salem with the Parsons proceedings, reports that most accusations did not produce conviction, and presents Salem as extraordinary.

## Qualifications

The two sources are podcast syntheses rather than a complete trial archive. Springfield is a useful control case but does not by itself explain every difference in chronology, court structure, evidence, or community dynamics. Modern analogies to public shaming can clarify accusation incentives while obscuring Salem's theology, criminal procedure, imprisonment, and lethal punishment.

## What Changed

- Reframed Salem as an exceptional institutional escalation within a wider field of failed or halted witchcraft cases.
- Added the distinction between belief in witchcraft and sufficient proof against a particular defendant.
- Preserved systemic pressure while narrowing claims of inevitability.
