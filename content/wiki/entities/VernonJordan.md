# Vernon Jordan

## Overview

Vernon Jordan was a civil-rights lawyer who began his career with [[DonaldHollowell|Donald L. Hollowell]] in 1960 and supplies the episode’s principal firsthand interpretation of Hollowell’s discipline, practical judgment, and leadership under segregation.

## Current Profile

The episode says Jordan joined Hollowell immediately after law school for $35 a week after segregation had limited Black graduates’ access to professional preparation. On his first day he helped obtain the release of jailed student demonstrators; later he traveled across Georgia with Hollowell, relying on local Black professionals and families for lodging and food where segregated facilities excluded them.

Jordan’s retrospective contribution is a theory of strategic survival. His high-school decision to perform with a band honoring segregationist senator Richard Russell, rather than risk the jobs of his principal and bandmaster, is presented as practical judgment under constrained power. “Get smart” means preserving people, capacity, and future options until action can be effective; it does not make the demand, the segregation, or the compromise just.

## Key Characteristics

- Young Black lawyer entering a profession constrained by Georgia segregation.
- Junior colleague who learned civil-rights practice through direct work and travel with Hollowell.
- Witness to the hospitality and distributed support through which local Black communities sustained movement lawyers.
- Interpreter of composure as preparation and strategic judgment rather than emotional absence.
- Advocate of constrained practicality when open refusal would predictably shift punishment onto vulnerable allies.

## Qualifications

The profile derives from one structured episode summary centered on Jordan’s memories of Hollowell. It does not provide a full biography of Jordan’s later civil-rights, organizational, political, business, or advisory work. The “get smart” formulation is context-bound: it cannot establish that accommodation is always prudent, that anger is politically useless, or that people facing injustice owe observers emotional restraint.
