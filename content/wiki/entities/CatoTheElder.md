# Cato the Elder / 老加图

## Overview

Cato the Elder / 老加图 is the austere Roman soldier-politician and rival of [[ScipioAfricanus]] who later turned Carthage's renewed prosperity into a sustained argument that [[Carthage]] must be destroyed.

## Current Profile

Cato first appears in the African campaign as a figure of contrast inside the Roman command. Serving with Scipio's expedition, he presents himself as austere, conservative, hostile to Greek glamour, and critical of the commander's spending and popularity with soldiers. His later triumph in Spain gives him independent prestige, and his attacks on Lucius Scipio's accounts and Scipio Africanus's conduct turn style, finance, and republican accountability into factional weapons.

After visiting Carthage in 152 BC, Cato interprets its grain, wealth, upgraded harbors, and dockyard capacity as evidence that defeat has not made the city safe. The fresh fig he reportedly displays in the Senate dramatizes geographic proximity, while his recurring destruction formula makes annihilation a standing answer to matters beyond Carthaginian policy itself.

Part 4 places Cato against [[Polybius]] and Scipio Nasica, who represent more sympathetic or preservationist positions. His campaign joins strategic fear to [[RomanEnemyMemory]]: material recovery becomes proof of possible renewed threat because Hannibalic devastation remains politically alive. Calling the policy genocidal may illuminate its annihilatory aim, but the episode treats the modern category as anachronistic and does not reduce Roman motives to Cato alone.

## Key Characteristics

- Austere conservative who converts military service and a Spanish triumph into political standing.
- Opponent of Scipionic expenditure, glamour, popularity, and financial opacity.
- Senatorial advocate of Carthage's destruction.
- Interprets commercial and infrastructural recovery as renewed military danger.
- Uses vivid repetition and the fresh-fig story to make distance and threat politically immediate.
- Represents the annihilation side of a real Roman elite debate.

## Qualifications

This page remains bounded to Cato's relationship with the Scipios and Carthage rather than his full offices, writings, censorship, or social program. The command role in Africa, Spanish campaign, financial accusations, exact wording and frequency of his destruction formula, fig performance, private motives, and relative weight of fear, strategy, faction, greed, and moral ideology remain source-scoped.
