## Overview

[[CaoPi|曹丕]] is Cao Cao's son and successor, shown here as a contingent heir, a pre-accession administrator, and the ruler who converts Cao-family control of the Han court into formal dynastic replacement.

## Current Profile

Cao Pi's succession is not presented as inevitable. Cao Chong's death removes a favored potential rival, and a later Hejian episode shows Cao Pi accepting advice to delegate limited force and refer a mass-punishment question upward. These sources give him both contingency and bounded pre-accession judgment.

Hanji 1150 completes the transition only in outline: after [[CaoCao|曹操]] dies, Cao Pi receives the abdication of [[EmperorXianOfHan|汉献帝]], establishes Wei, and makes Liu Xie Shanyang Gong. The settlement preserves predecessor status, household life, and succession rather than treating formal replacement as requiring immediate extermination.

Hanji 1149 adds the earlier comparison with [[CaoZhi|曹植]]. The episode presents Cao Pi as less dazzling but more controlled, politically rational, and capable of managing officials and appearances. This extends the profile backward into succession reliability while remaining a source-scoped contrast rather than proof that one farewell performance or one rival's mistake determined the outcome.

## Key Characteristics

- Son who inherits and formalizes Cao-family power.
- Contingent beneficiary of Cao Chong's early death.
- Administrator who delegates a limited revolt rather than seeking personal military credit.
- Decision-maker who refers an expansive captive-execution question to superior authority.
- Founding ruler whose predecessor settlement preserves Liu Xie's fief and recognized status.
- Succession contender presented as steadier and more politically delegable than Cao Zhi.

## Evidence

Succession contingency:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1111-caocao-zui-tong-de-yinian-erzi-yaozhe-qiu-lianyin-ai-dui-lr5tvu8-kti41ezapvq2vz2wfjh6|Hanji 1111]] reports Cao-family remarks treating Cao Chong's death as favorable to the surviving sons and possibly decisive for Cao Pi.

Pre-accession judgment:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1127-caocao-cuanwei-sanjian-ta-zhide-shi-shenme-livrh3obobkcfhj_ngnhe-kgv2ls|Hanji 1127]] says Cao Pi delegates the Hejian revolt and seeks Cao Cao's ruling rather than execute controlled surrenderers automatically.

Dynastic conversion and predecessor settlement:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-24-shangyang-yue-wo-za-lie-le-lt4yd6pwlezqia5yljg-gankyhut|Zhouji 24]] uses Cao Pi's receipt of abdication as the endpoint of a power-exit analogy.
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1150-caocao-weishenme-yao-sha-huaiyun-de-dong-guiren-ltsk2hxmdh9is39ejkypzqruoeiu|Hanji 1150]] adds the Shanyang title, Liu Xie's later life, posthumous honor, and continuation of the fief.

Succession reliability:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1149-jiemi-sanguo-dingji-moushi-xunyou-de-guanchang-shengcunshu-ljv1fwephe86-qczfy0o1n2yh7um|Hanji 1149]] contrasts Cao Pi's restraint and official-management capacity with Cao Zhi's literary brilliance and unstable self-command.

## Qualifications

These sources do not form a complete reign biography. The abdication is described inside a podcast synthesis rather than a detailed institutional record, and the degree of coercion, settlement terms, medical anecdotes, and comparative distinctiveness require narrower verification. Hanji 1111 supports a plausible succession counterfactual, not a formal Cao Chong designation. Hanji 1149's poet-versus-politician contrast and farewell anecdote are interpretive and do not isolate all causes of succession.

## What Changed

- Added the Cao Zhi comparison as an earlier succession-reliability layer.
- Distinguished steadier political presentation from a claim that one anecdote decided succession.
