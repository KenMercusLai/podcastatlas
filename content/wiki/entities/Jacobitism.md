# Jacobitism

## Overview

Jacobitism appears as the Stuart restoration movement whose 1745 military crisis reshaped British loyalism and whose defeat left a durable landscape of memory, repression, romance, and social change in Scotland.

## Current Profile

The anthem episode presents Jacobitism as a dynastic and religious legitimacy challenge rather than simple Scottish nationalism. [[CharlesEdwardStuart|Charles Edward Stuart]]’s landing and early victories produced panic because hereditary Stuart and Catholic-associated divine-right claims confronted Protestant succession, parliament, and the Hanoverian settlement. The crisis helped [[GodSaveTheKing|God Save the King]] become a mass loyalist anthem, even though the song may itself have moved from an older Stuart alignment into Hanoverian use.

The 1773 tour shows the movement’s afterlife. [[SamuelJohnson]] retained wistful Tory sympathy, while [[JamesBoswell]] cultivated a more theatrical Jacobite romance. Veterans, hiding places, forts, restricted tartan, [[FloraMacDonald]], and the prince’s escape route made 1745 emotionally present even when the travelers avoided Culloden battlefield. Jacobitism therefore became both political history and a lens on [[PostCullodenHighlandTransformation]].

## Key Characteristics

- Stuart restoration movement opposing the Hanoverian succession settlement.
- Dynastic and religious legitimacy conflict not reducible to Scotland versus England.
- 1745 military crisis that intensified loyalist symbolism and London anxiety.
- Defeated cause whose routes, survivors, dress, forts, and stories remained visible in 1773.
- Romantic memory field through which Johnson and Boswell interpreted friendship, place, and Highland change.

## Evidence

- Dynastic conflict and anthem breakthrough: [[678-britain-god-save-the-king-part-2-glt2603959218]] links the 1745 crisis to competing succession principles and the song’s theatrical spread.
- Political repurposing: [[678-britain-god-save-the-king-part-2-glt2603959218]] preserves the possibility that a Stuart-associated song became Hanoverian loyalism.
- Campaign and escape memory: [[652-londons-golden-age-the-ghosts-of-culloden-part-3-glt2422514848]] recounts the advance, defeat, escape, veterans, places, and Flora MacDonald encounter.
- Literary afterlife: [[652-londons-golden-age-the-ghosts-of-culloden-part-3-glt2422514848]] treats the 1773 itinerary partly as a Jacobite memory tour shaped by Johnson’s and Boswell’s sympathies.

## Qualifications

These sources do not settle the full coalition, theology, military history, regional alignment, or social program of Jacobitism. Anthem origins remain uncertain, the route-as-memory-tour interpretation is episode-attributed, and later romance can obscure internal Scottish division and the experiences of Highland communities themselves.

## What Changed

- Extended Jacobitism from the 1745 succession crisis into its 1773 literary and landscape afterlife.
- Added the distinction between failed restoration politics and durable romantic memory.
- Connected Jacobite memory to post-Culloden institutional and social transformation.

## Relationships

- [[CharlesEdwardStuart]] - claimant and campaign leader at the center of the 1745 rising.
- [[FloraMacDonald]] - escape participant who became a living carrier of Jacobite memory.
- [[GodSaveTheKing]] - anthem whose loyalist breakthrough and possible older allegiance run through the crisis.
- [[Scotland]] - geography, divided political field, and primary landscape of later memory.
- [[SamuelJohnson]] - traveler with qualified Tory and Stuart sympathy.
- [[JamesBoswell]] - traveler whose romantic Jacobitism animated the 1773 tour.
- [[PostCullodenHighlandTransformation]] - aftermath through which defeat acquired material and demographic consequences.
