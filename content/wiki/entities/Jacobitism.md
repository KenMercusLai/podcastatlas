# Jacobitism

## Overview

Jacobitism appears in this source as the Stuart restoration movement whose 1745 crisis helped make [[GodSaveTheKing|God Save the King]] a mass Hanoverian loyalist anthem.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Jacobitism as a dynastic and religious legitimacy challenge rather than simply Scottish nationalism. Bonnie Prince Charlie's 1745 landing and early victories made London panic because the conflict put hereditary Stuart claims and Catholic-associated divine-right legitimacy against Protestant succession, parliament, and the Hanoverian settlement.

Jacobitism matters to the anthem branch because the source keeps the song's older alignment uncertain. The hosts, following Percy Scholes's summary, say contemporary claims connected the words and music to James II, so the British anthem's later loyalist success may depend on a Jacobite-to-Hanoverian repurposing.

## Key Characteristics

- Stuart restoration movement opposing the Hanoverian succession settlement.
- 1745 crisis that made succession politics urgent in London and helped the anthem spread.
- Political conflict involving religion, heredity, parliamentary settlement, and monarchy rather than only national geography.
- Memory field often simplified into Scotland-versus-England in later retellings.
- Source-scoped origin context for [[GodSaveTheKing|God Save the King]] as a possible turncoat anthem.

## Evidence

- Dynastic conflict: [[678-britain-god-save-the-king-part-2-glt2603959218]] says the 1745 conflict opposed Stuart hereditary and divine-right claims to Hanoverian claims based on Protestantism and parliamentary settlement.
- Military crisis: [[678-britain-god-save-the-king-part-2-glt2603959218]] says Bonnie Prince Charlie landed in July 1745, took Edinburgh, won at Prestonpans, and helped produce panic in London.
- Anthem breakthrough: [[678-britain-god-save-the-king-part-2-glt2603959218]] says the song spread after the 28 September 1745 Drury Lane performance during the Jacobite crisis.
- Turncoat possibility: [[678-britain-god-save-the-king-part-2-glt2603959218]] says contemporary letters connected the song with James II, while Scholes described the anthem as a turncoat because it moved from Jacobite to Hanoverian use.
- Memory caution: [[678-britain-god-save-the-king-part-2-glt2603959218]] says Culloden is often remembered as a Scottish independence struggle, but the hosts frame it as a conflict between competing visions of Scotland.

## Qualifications
