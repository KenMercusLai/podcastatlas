# Charles VI of France

## Overview

Charles VI is the French king whose recurring mental crises, survival at the [[BalDesArdents]], weakened government, and divided kingdom made him the constitutional center of the [[TreatyOfTroyes|Treaty of Troyes]].

## Current Profile

A vivid 1393 court layer comes from the [[BalDesArdents]]. After an earlier mental collapse, a wedding entertainment intended to lift Charles's spirits placed him and five companions in pitch-covered wild-man costumes. A torch ignited the dancers; several died, while Charles survived because his aunt shielded him. [[154-the-most-disastrous-party-in-history-glt6506112996]] treats the catastrophe as making court danger and the king's instability publicly legible without by itself explaining the later political breakdown.

The Hundred Years' War sources depict Charles less as an active strategist than as the monarch around whom competing Armagnac, Burgundian, Dauphinist, and English claims operated. His recurring mental crises weakened his capacity to contain competition between [[LouisOfOrleans|Louis of Orléans]] and [[JohnTheFearless|John the Fearless]], while royal pardons and forced reconciliations failed to prevent civil war. After Paris became unsafe, he, Queen Isabeau, and [[CatherineOfValois|Catherine]] withdrew to Troyes. The 1420 treaty kept Charles as king while making [[HenryVOfEngland|Henry V]] regent and heir, preserving formal continuity while disinheriting Charles's surviving son.

## Key Characteristics

- French king whose recurring incapacity opened space for factional competition around the council and Dauphin.
- Survivor of a lethal court entertainment whose symbolism intensified anxiety about royal instability.
- Dynastic focus of rival Armagnac, Burgundian, Dauphinist, and English programs.
- Monarch retained by the Treaty of Troyes rather than immediately deposed.
- Father whose son Charles was displaced in favor of an English son-in-law.

## Evidence

- Court catastrophe: [[154-the-most-disastrous-party-in-history-glt6506112996]] recounts the flammable costumes, torch ignition, deaths, and Charles's survival at the Bal des Ardents.
- Incapacity and court competition: [[487-hundred-years-war-henry-v-s-invasion-of-france-part-1-glt7554933021]] connects Charles's recurring breakdowns to rivalry between Orléans and Burgundy.
- Failed containment: [[487-hundred-years-war-henry-v-s-invasion-of-france-part-1-glt7554933021]] describes Charles pardoning John after Orléans's murder in an unsuccessful effort to avoid civil war.
- Political weakness, flight, and settlement: [[490-hundred-years-war-england-triumphant-part-4-glt2692942762]] places factional violence around Charles's court, records the royal family's move to Troyes, and follows his retention as king under the treaty.
- Succession rupture: [[490-hundred-years-war-england-triumphant-part-4-glt2692942762]] says Henry became heir while the Dauphin was effectively disinherited.

## Qualifications

The sources center a dramatic court disaster, Henry V's strategy, and French factional collapse rather than a complete reign. Costume details, responsibility, casualties, and contemporary reaction to the Bal des Ardents remain source-scoped. Reported episodes of Charles believing himself glass or Saint George illustrate incapacity in the podcast but are not independently audited here, and the sources supply little analysis of lucid intervals, agency, treatment, or Charles's personal view of the Troyes settlement.

## What Changed

- Added the Bal des Ardents as an early spectacle that made court risk and Charles's instability publicly memorable.
- Qualified the event as a legitimacy amplifier rather than a sufficient cause of later civil and dynastic collapse.
