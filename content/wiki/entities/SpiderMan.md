# Spider-Man

## Overview

Spider-Man is the Marvel superhero whose Peter Parker identity moved a socially awkward teenager from the sidekick role to the center of heroic narrative, joining extraordinary power to guilt, adolescence, education, and everyday difficulty.

## Current Profile

The episode places Spider-Man within Marvel's Silver Age reinvention of the genre. Stan Lee, Steve Ditko, Jack Kirby, and their wider creative milieu built a more connected New York-centered world during the Space Race, when science could plausibly appear as both danger and liberation.

Peter Parker's importance lies less in effortless power than in divided obligations. High school, university, insecurity, mistakes, money, youth culture, and the consequences of action make superhero fantasy answerable to an adolescent social world.

The familiar responsibility principle captures that structure, although the episode notes that its original form is delivered by the narrator rather than spoken by Spider-Man. The distinction matters because later cultural memory often turns a narrative moral into a stable personal slogan.

## Key Characteristics

- Teenage protagonist elevated from the traditional sidekick position.
- Hero defined by guilt, responsibility, insecurity, and accident as well as power.
- Silver Age character embedded in New York, education, and youth culture.
- Product of a Marvel setting where science creates both danger and possibility.
- Anti-heroic or neurotic figure compared with more assured earlier heroes.

## Qualifications

This profile relies on one broad history rather than detailed publication analysis. Creator credit, the relation between 1960s politics and particular stories, the label “anti-heroic,” and the later wording and reception of the responsibility maxim remain source-scoped.
