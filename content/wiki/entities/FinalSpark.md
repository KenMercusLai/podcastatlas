# FinalSpark

## Overview
FinalSpark is the Swiss biocomputing startup discussed in [[ep-37-neurons-future-of-ai-processing]], where [[LenaCurtis]] describes its work connecting lab-grown human neurons to electrodes for information-processing research.

## Current Profile
The episode frames FinalSpark as early research infrastructure rather than a finished AI-hardware vendor. Its central asset is a remotely accessible neuro platform where researchers can stimulate living neural cultures, record electrical spikes, and analyze responses with code. The company's thesis is that biological processors may eventually complement silicon by reducing energy and cost for some AI workloads.

## Key Characteristics
- Founded in 2014 by [[FredJordan]] and [[MartinKutter]], according to the episode.
- Builds around living human neurons connected to electrodes rather than conventional logic gates.
- Offers remote access to neural cultures for universities, companies, and individuals.
- Treats [[BiologicalProcessorEnergyEfficiency]] as the central strategic motivation.
- Pursues internal R&D and patents while inviting external researchers to test ideas on the platform.

## Evidence
### Company origin and thesis
- [[ep-37-neurons-future-of-ai-processing]] says FinalSpark was founded in 2014 by Jordan and Kutter after they concluded that AI progress was strongly constrained by server, training, and usage costs.

### Platform model
- [[ep-37-neurons-future-of-ai-processing]] describes remote researchers writing Python experiments in a browser, sending stimulation to neurons in Switzerland, and reading neural activity back as digital data.

### Research maturity
- [[ep-37-neurons-future-of-ai-processing]] says FinalSpark has stored one bit of information in neurons and is still researching how neurons process information.

## Qualifications
- The source is a company-adjacent podcast interview, so cost, energy, and profit claims remain source-scoped.
- The episode does not establish that FinalSpark can run commercial generative-AI workloads today.
- Ethical and regulatory questions remain open because the field lacks specific legislation and public norms.
