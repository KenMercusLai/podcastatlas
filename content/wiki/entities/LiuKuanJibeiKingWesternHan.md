## Overview

济北王刘宽 / Liu Kuan is a Western Han prince used in the current source as a comparison for how political wrongdoing could reduce a royal burial's treatment and grave goods.

## Current Profile

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-311-wenxuejia-guomoruo-ruhe-niangjiu-kaogu-dabeiju-lpq9bozhrdxqn7tiotyx3j-epm9l|Hanji 311]] says Liu Kuan was close to [[LiuAn|淮南王刘安]], resented [[HanWudi|汉武帝]], and used a figurine in a curse ritual without directly joining Liu An's rebellion. After denunciation he dies by suicide; the emperor then reportedly cancels a prepared gold-thread jade suit and requires a simplified burial.

The episode says the later-discovered tomb contained few objects and identifies a jade face covering as its most valuable find. Its argumentative role is comparative: if political fault could produce a sparse royal burial for Liu Kuan, Liu Ji's own suspected disloyalty might qualify the expectation of a treasure-filled tomb. The analogy cannot establish Liu Ji's treatment.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han king of Jibei, distinct from the late-Han official [[LiuKuanLateHan|刘宽]].
- Presented as politically hostile to Han Wudi and associated with Liu An.
- Accused in the episode of a curse ritual rather than direct participation in rebellion.
- Reportedly denied a prepared gold-thread jade suit and buried under reduced rites.
- Later tomb described as sparse, with a jade face covering singled out.

## Qualifications

The current profile depends on a compact popular-history episode rather than court records or an excavation report. The exact charge, denunciation, suicide circumstances, prepared jade suit, funerary order, tomb identification, assemblage size, and value ranking remain source-scoped. Liu Kuan's treatment is an analogy for Liu Ji, not direct evidence of Liu Ji's burial.
