# Achaemenid Empire / Persian Empire

## Overview

The Achaemenid Empire / Persian Empire appears in the wiki as the imperial power whose expansion under [[CyrusTheGreat]], consolidation under [[DariusI]], suppression of the [[IonianRevolt]], and punitive expedition against Athens and Eretria lead to the [[BattleOfMarathon]].

## Current Profile

The new pre-Marathon source broadens Persia from the army at Marathon into a working empire. [[CyrusTheGreat]] conquers Lydia, [[Sardis]], and Babylon, bringing Ionian Greeks under Persian rule. Darius then inherits and stabilizes this order through revolt suppression, exemplary terror, tribute organization, Persepolis, and a universal claim that empire expresses truth and order against the lie.

Across the Marathon sources, Persia is not treated as a cartoon of despotism. It is a military, fiscal, ideological, and diplomatic system using satrapal centers, client tyrants, naval transport, cavalry, local intelligence, intimidation, religious honoring, and exile politics. The burning of Sardis turns Athenian involvement into an imperial affront; the destruction of [[Miletus]] and Eretria shows the punitive sequence before the fleet reaches Marathon.

At Marathon itself, the empire's commanders choose terrain that gives beach access, cavalry room, and political flexibility. The battle is a real Persian failure to take [[Athens]], but the source also warns that from an imperial viewpoint it may have looked less decisive than later Greek memory suggests.

## Key Characteristics

- The empire grows through conquest and incorporation, especially Cyrus's victories over Lydia, Sardis, and Babylon.
- Darius stabilizes the empire through administration, tribute, symbolic centers, and suppression of rebellion.
- Persian imperial authority is presented as ideological as well as military, tied to truth/order against the lie.
- The empire manages Ionian Greeks through client tyrants, satrapal authority, naval force, and exemplary punishment.
- Persian strategy at Marathon depends on terrain, cavalry, naval mobility, intimidation, and possible internal betrayal in Athens.
- The sources treat Persian defeat at Marathon as militarily real but not necessarily empire-defining from the Persian viewpoint.
- Greek-source dominance makes the empire's inner view harder to reconstruct than Greek memory.

## Evidence

- Imperial foundation and administration: [[668-greece-vs-persia-the-rise-of-the-first-superpower-part-1-glt8965800182]] connects Cyrus's conquests to Ionian subjection and Darius's tribute system, Persepolis, and administrative stabilization.
- Ideological order: [[668-greece-vs-persia-the-rise-of-the-first-superpower-part-1-glt8965800182]] says Darius casts imperial rule as truth and order against the lie.
- Ionian revolt and punishment: [[668-greece-vs-persia-the-rise-of-the-first-superpower-part-1-glt8965800182]] follows Aristagoras, Sardis, Miletus, Naxos, Delos, and Eretria as the escalation path toward Marathon.
- Revenge motive and punitive sequence: [[669-greece-vs-persia-the-battle-of-marathon-part-2-glt2696648260]] says Darius sought revenge for Athenian support of the Ionian revolt and describes Eretria's destruction before the fleet moves to Marathon.
- Strategic landing and political warfare: [[669-greece-vs-persia-the-battle-of-marathon-part-2-glt2696648260]] says Marathon offered beach, plain, and cavalry space, while Hippias and possible betrayal were part of the Persian threat.
- Imperial perspective: [[669-greece-vs-persia-the-battle-of-marathon-part-2-glt2696648260]] notes that Marathon may have seemed peripheral to Persia and that no Persian account comparable to Herodotus survives.

## Qualifications

The profile remains source-scoped to the two Marathon episodes. It does not synthesize the whole Achaemenid state, court, economy, religion, administration, or later Xerxes invasion. The Persian religious-ideological terms, Darius's inner motives, and Persian view of Marathon are all mediated through the episode's reconstruction and surviving Greek narrative asymmetry.

## What Changed

- Expanded the Persian Empire node from a Marathon expedition profile into the pre-Marathon imperial context: Cyrus's conquests, Darius's consolidation, ideology, Ionian revolt, and punitive sequence.
- Preserved the prior qualification that Persian inner perspective is partly inferred because comparable Persian narrative evidence is absent from the source frame.
