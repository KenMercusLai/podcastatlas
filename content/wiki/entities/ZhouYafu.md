## Overview

周亚夫 / Zhou Yafu appears in the current wiki as a Western Han minister-general whose military merit, principled judgment, loss of imperial trust, and prison death form a tension rather than a single personality verdict. He also anchors [[XuFuPhysiognomist|许负]]'s source-scoped prediction narrative and a personnel judgment about [[ZhaoYuWesternHan|赵禹]].

## Current Profile

The current profile joins camp command, campaign strategy, court policy, downfall, and a bounded personnel judgment. Xu Fu is said to predict that Zhou will inherit a marquis title, become a major military and political figure, and finally die of hunger. The sources fulfill those stages through his brother's loss of the title, Zhou's rise before and during the Seven States crisis, and imprisonment followed by refusal of food after his son's burial-armor purchase triggers a rebellion accusation.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-228-zhouyafu-jiang-wendi-ju-yu-yingwai-liuheng-de-biaoxian-lingren-yiwai-lg4dt0ojthk1rvvma4prssxyx6w9|Hanji 228 Xiliu]] supplies the earliest operational layer. During a reported Xiongnu emergency, Zhou's camp stays armed and quiet, refuses entry to the emperor's advance party and then [[LiuHeng|汉文帝]], waits for formal notification, enforces a ban on galloping, and uses armored military ritual. Liu Heng treats these restrictions as evidence of readiness rather than disloyalty, contrasts Xiliu with the easily entered Bashang and Jimen camps, and later appoints Zhou 中尉. The bounded lesson is [[WartimeCampCommandAuthority|credible internal camp authority]], not an unrestricted right to defy the sovereign.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-234-zhouyafu-zhanchang-dalian-hanjingdi-na-lai-de-danzi-li6iqhtuxgq5xwgbn_vb7lljgyxr|Hanji 234]] now supplies the military rise rather than leaving it as retrospective reputation. Zhou proposes that Liang fix the Wu-Chu main force while the central army cuts its food route, accepts [[ZhaoSheAdviserWesternHan|赵涉]]'s warning and takes a longer route around a reported ambush, refuses immediate relief despite pressure from [[LiuWuLiangKingWesternHan|刘武]] and a later order from [[HanJingdi|景帝]], and uses fortification and time against a hungry opponent. The episode also credits him with remaining still during a camp panic and reinforcing the northwest when a southeast attack appears to be a feint.

This campaign expands the meaning of Zhou's firmness. In war it can protect an agreed mechanism from political urgency, especially when he remains receptive to Zhao She's corrective advice and evidence. In court, later sources show the same directness operating under different authority and coalition conditions. The comparison supports domain-sensitive judgment, not a claim that one immutable personality both wins the war and causes his death.

As a superior, Zhou reportedly refuses to treat colleagues' praise of Zhao Yu's cleanliness and fairness as sufficient evidence of office fitness. He sees strict textual legality and harsh treatment of self and others as reasons not to entrust Zhao with important responsibility. This presents Zhou as separating probity from discretionary fitness, though the evidence does not reconstruct his broader administrative practice.

Hanji 244 now supplies the full source-scoped downfall chain. After illness and removal from the chancellorship, Jingdi serves Zhou meat without chopsticks; Zhou asks an attendant for them, reacts badly to the emperor's rebuke, and leaves quickly. Later, Zhou's son buys discarded armor and shields for burial and mistreats workers, a report reaches the court, and investigators convert the goods into a rebellion case. Zhou's burial explanation is answered with the claim that he intends to rebel underground; after coercive investigation, he refuses food and dies in prison.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-242-cong-chengxiang-dao-mianguan-zhouyafu-fanle-zhichang-daji-1-lu6ohybs3wq9p9z-rmzj-j5vjcrs|Hanji 242 part 1]] now supplies the pre-dismissal sequence rather than leaving it as a retrospective grievance list. Zhou opposes Liu Rong's removal, refuses a marquis title for Empress Wang's brother Wang Xin because he lacks state merit, and objects to rewarding six surrendered Xiongnu leaders because doing so could weaken the court's condemnation of disloyal subjects. Jingdi instead prioritizes succession control, family settlement, and defection incentives; Liu Wu also continues criticizing Zhou before Dowager Dou. After the final policy loss, Zhou claims illness and stays away from court before being removed as chancellor.

The joined account treats these disagreements as a cumulative loss of protection, not proof that the minister was simply wrong or that one refusal mechanically caused his death. Zhou's merit rule and loyalty analogy identify institutional risks, while Jingdi's use of titles for frontier inducement identifies a different state objective. The narrower judgment is that principled consistency becomes politically vulnerable when it repeatedly blocks the sovereign and senior family interests without a coalition or acceptable alternative; damaged trust, ritual signaling, family misconduct, ambiguous evidence, and hostile prosecutorial discretion later compound that vulnerability.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han marquis and minister-general whose current military profile runs from Xiliu camp command to the Seven States campaign.
- Xiliu commander whose entry, movement, and ritual rules remain operative even during an imperial inspection under frontier threat.
- Logistics commander who fixes the enemy against Liang, cuts food supply, fortifies, and delays battle until attrition changes the balance.
- Strategically firm but tactically receptive, adopting Zhao She's safer route and later preparing for a feint.
- Narratively linked to three pre-dismissal policy conflicts, removal from office, hostile prosecution, and prison self-starvation.
- Personnel judge who distinguishes clean reputation from fitness for complex office and distrusts harsh textual rigidity without flexibility.
- Figure whose directness succeeds under campaign authority but becomes politically exposed under court hierarchy; personality causation remains qualified.

## Qualifications

The prophecy material is preserved as episode narrative, not evidence that physiognomy predicts lives. Hanji 228 Xiliu's dates, threats, places, dialogue, ritual, appointment sequence, and contrast between three camps remain source-attributed; easy imperial entry is suggestive but not an independent combat-readiness audit, and the case does not prove that commanders may generally refuse lawful civilian authority. Hanji 234's route, ambush, speeches, command sequence, refusal order, troop movements, night panic, feint, dates, and modern leadership lessons remain source-attributed; it does not prove that commanders should generally disobey rulers or that Liang's losses were strategically negligible. Hanji 347-1 transcribes Zhou's name as “周雅夫”; this page normalizes it to 周亚夫 from context. That source supports a narrow personnel judgment about Zhao Yu, not a claim that Zhou always governed flexibly. Hanji 242 part 1's chronology, founding-compact wording, policy procedure, private motives, illness, dismissal grounds, surrendered leaders, and downstream effects remain source-attributed. Its Xiongnu dispute establishes a clash between loyalty signaling and defection incentives, not which policy was superior. Hanji 244's “psychological blacklist,” imperial test, pre-existing intent to kill, exact armor law, interrogation dialogue, torture, grievance list, and personality causation are likewise bounded. The son's misconduct opens a reporting channel but does not by itself establish Zhou's guilt, and the judicial reply makes the rebellion claim difficult to falsify.

## What Changed

- Added the Xiliu inspection as Zhou's earliest operational command case and a precursor to his later campaign reputation.
- Distinguished formal sovereign supremacy from the commander's immediate control of entry, movement, and ritual inside a prepared camp.
- Strengthened the domain-sensitive reading of Zhou's firmness: effective under bounded military delegation, politically exposed under later court hierarchy.
