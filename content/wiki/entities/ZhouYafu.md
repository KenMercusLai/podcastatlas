## Overview

周亚夫 / Zhou Yafu appears in the current wiki as a Western Han minister-general whose military merit, principled judgment, loss of imperial trust, and prison death form a tension rather than a single personality verdict. He also anchors [[XuFuPhysiognomist|许负]]'s source-scoped prediction narrative and a personnel judgment about [[ZhaoYuWesternHan|赵禹]].

## Current Profile

The current profile joins a narrated life arc to a bounded personnel judgment. Xu Fu is said to predict that Zhou will inherit a marquis title, become a major military and political figure, and finally die of hunger. Two sources fulfill those stages through his brother's loss of the title, Zhou's rise around the Seven States crisis, and imprisonment followed by refusal of food after his son's burial-armor purchase triggers a rebellion accusation.

As a superior, Zhou reportedly refuses to treat colleagues' praise of Zhao Yu's cleanliness and fairness as sufficient evidence of office fitness. He sees strict textual legality and harsh treatment of self and others as reasons not to entrust Zhao with important responsibility. This presents Zhou as separating probity from discretionary fitness, though the evidence does not reconstruct his broader administrative practice.

Hanji 244 now supplies the full source-scoped downfall chain. After illness and removal from the chancellorship, Jingdi serves Zhou meat without chopsticks; Zhou asks an attendant for them, reacts badly to the emperor's rebuke, and leaves quickly. Later, Zhou's son buys discarded armor and shields for burial and mistreats workers, a report reaches the court, and investigators convert the goods into a rebellion case. Zhou's burial explanation is answered with the claim that he intends to rebel underground; after coercive investigation, he refuses food and dies in prison.

The host connects this ending to accumulated disputes over succession, Liang relief, and an outer-relative enfeoffment, then treats Zhou's battlefield directness and inflexibility as the common thread. The page adopts the narrower judgment that principled consistency, damaged trust, ritual signaling, family misconduct, ambiguous evidence, and hostile prosecutorial discretion can interact. It does not treat personality as sufficient cause or the banquet as proof of a pre-decided execution.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han marquis and major military-political figure in the Xu Fu prediction narrative.
- Initially skeptical that either inheritance or death by hunger could fit his circumstances.
- Narratively linked to the Seven States crisis, accumulated court conflict, and prison self-starvation.
- Presented in Hanji 347-1 as able to distinguish clean reputation from fitness for complex office.
- Cautious about harsh, strictly textual legal judgment when it lacks flexibility.
- Poorly adapts battlefield directness to court hierarchy in the host's interpretation, while the causal weight of personality remains qualified.

## Qualifications

The prophecy material is preserved as episode narrative, not evidence that physiognomy predicts lives. Hanji 347-1 transcribes Zhou's name as “周雅夫”; this page normalizes it to 周亚夫 from context. That source supports a narrow personnel judgment about Zhao Yu, not a claim that Zhou always governed flexibly. Hanji 244's “psychological blacklist,” imperial test, pre-existing intent to kill, exact armor law, interrogation dialogue, torture, grievance list, and personality causation remain source-attributed. The son's misconduct opens a reporting channel but does not by itself establish Zhou's guilt, and the judicial reply makes the rebellion claim difficult to falsify.
