## Overview

杨康 / Yang Kang is a major character in [[LegendOfTheCondorHeroes|《射雕英雄传》]] used by the bounded source as a contrast to [[GuoJingJinYong|郭靖]]: intelligence, privilege, and prestigious instruction do not produce侠义 when learning is oriented toward status, shortcuts, and manipulation.

## Current Profile

The episode treats Yang Kang's path as multi-causal rather than innate evil or a simple failure of wealth. He grows up inside a princely household structured by rank, indulgence, concealed parentage, and politically complicated relationships. His mother, his teachers, and the court environment shape the conditions of development, but they do not eliminate his own repeated choices.

His learning style sharpens the contrast. Where Guo Jing accepts small increments, repeats difficult practice, and values instruction itself, Yang Kang is described as pursuing results, prestigious affiliation, and immediate advantage. His refusal of limited guidance when a formal master title is unavailable becomes the episode's clearest case of status displacing learning. Cleverness then serves evasion: deception and shortcuts appear to solve immediate problems while weakening responsibility and long-term formation.

## Key Characteristics

- Begins with greater material security, social rank, and apparent quickness than Guo Jing.
- Develops inside concealed family history, court hierarchy, indulgence, and complex adult loyalties.
- Values recognized affiliation and visible result more than incremental learning.
- Uses cleverness and deception to avoid responsibility or seek shortcuts.
- Receives martial instruction without equivalent success in character formation.
- Remains responsible for choices even when family, teachers, and environment help explain them.

## Qualifications

This profile is based on one conversational literary reading rather than a direct comparison of editions or a complete character study. The source's claims about parental relationships, court influence, a teacher's failures, Mei Chaofeng's psychological effect, motives, and learning preferences remain interpretations. Explaining conduct through development does not excuse harm, while contrasting Yang Kang with Guo Jing does not prove that hardship reliably creates virtue or privilege reliably causes corruption.
