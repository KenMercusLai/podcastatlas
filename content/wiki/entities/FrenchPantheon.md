## Overview
The French Panthéon is a former religious building turned national memorial whose admissions, removals, ceremonies, and biographical framings make it an institution of public honor and political memory.

## Current Profile
The source treats the Panthéon not as a neutral hall of settled greatness but as a changing interface between historical reputation and present statecraft. From Jean Moulin's 1964 admission through later presidential commemorations of Resistance figures and minorities, selection can consolidate a national story, recognize neglected lives, and lend inherited prestige to current leaders at the same time. [[MarcBloch|Marc Bloch]] and his wife Simone's admission therefore combines overdue recognition with disputes about family consent, identity framing, public familiarity, and instrumentalization.

## Key Characteristics
- Converts selected lives into officially authorized symbols of France.
- Retains the political afterlife of its movement from religious site to national memorial.
- Uses admission ceremonies and presidential speech to frame which aspects of a life represent the nation.
- Gives Resistance memory a recurring role in postwar legitimacy and unity narratives.
- Can broaden recognition while also exposing conflict over appropriation, identity, and elite self-representation.

## Qualifications
The source supplies selected twentieth- and twenty-first-century examples, not a complete institutional history or full list of admissions, removals, procedures, and public responses. Political use does not by itself invalidate the people honored, and overdue recognition does not eliminate disagreement over how a ceremony frames their lives.

## What Changed
- Established the Panthéon as both a memorial institution and an active mechanism of state-authored national memory.
