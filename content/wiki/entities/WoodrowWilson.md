# Woodrow Wilson

## Overview

Woodrow Wilson is represented through two distinct bounded roles: the U.S. president managing neutrality after the [[RMSLusitania|Lusitania]] sinking and the former [[PrincetonUniversity|Princeton]] president whose racism and segregation record became the subject of a campus commemoration dispute.

## Current Profile

In the First World War branch, Wilson is a restraint-minded neutral leader under rising public and diplomatic pressure. After 128 Americans died on Lusitania, he avoided immediate war but sent protest notes warning Germany that further attacks could create a war issue. His position split American politics and his Cabinet, contributing to William Jennings Bryan's resignation, while the sinking became part of the memory surrounding Germany's 1917 submarine escalation.

The domestic and institutional-memory branch makes that profile morally less narrow. Wilson's racism and role in segregating the federal civil service became grounds for removing his name from Princeton's public-policy school. He therefore appears not only as a wartime decision-maker but as an honored institutional ancestor whose achievements, harms, and namesake status became objects of later judgment.

## Key Characteristics

- U.S. president who tried to preserve neutrality after German submarine attacks killed American citizens.
- Diplomatic escalator who used protest notes while stopping short of war in 1915.
- Leader whose restraint drew interventionist criticism and Cabinet dissent.
- Former Princeton president embedded in the university's institutional identity and public honors.
- Racist officeholder whom the episode identifies with segregation of the federal civil service.
- Contested commemorative figure whose achievements were weighed against concrete racial harm.

## Qualifications

These sources do not provide a full Wilson biography, primary federal records, or a comprehensive assessment of his presidency, domestic program, war leadership, Fourteen Points, League of Nations, race politics, or postwar diplomacy. The Lusitania episode is bounded to a maritime crisis, while the Princeton episode is a structured summary of an argument about reform strategy. Its account of Wilson's racism and federal segregation is treated as source-grounded here but not independently reconstructed, and the page does not assume that achievements and harms can be reduced to one commensurable score.
