# James Stagg

## Overview

James Stagg was the RAF group captain and chief meteorological adviser whose forecast informed the timing of the Allied D-Day landings in June 1944.

## Current Profile

[[89-climate-weather-glt6521438447]] presents Stagg as the specialist who translated uncertain and changing weather information into military advice for Eisenhower. He recommended delaying the invasion from 5 June and then supported using the brief opportunity on 6 June, when the operation's demands for visibility, sea conditions, moonlight, and tide could be acceptably aligned.

The episode uses him as a constructive climate-history case. Weather did not simply impose an outcome: forecasting, communication, and command judgment converted environmental observation into [[DDay|D-Day]] risk management. The claim that a longer delay would have exposed the fleet to the 19 June storm remains counterfactual, but it clarifies why his advice carried unusually high stakes.

## Key Characteristics

- He served as the meteorological intermediary between specialist forecasts and the Allied command decision.
- He treated the forecast as probabilistic operational advice rather than certainty.
- His recommendation combined delay with willingness to identify a narrow launch window.
- His historical importance depends on both scientific judgment and Eisenhower's authority to act on it.

## Evidence

- Timing advice: [[89-climate-weather-glt6521438447]] says Stagg persuaded Eisenhower to postpone the 5 June launch and then recommended proceeding on 6 June.
- Operational constraints: [[89-climate-weather-glt6521438447]] describes the need to coordinate skies, seas, moonlight, and tide.
- Counterfactual stakes: [[89-climate-weather-glt6521438447]] argues that a two-week postponement could have put the invasion fleet into a major 19 June storm.

## Qualifications

This profile is based on one short popular-history segment rather than Stagg's papers or a full meteorological history of Operation Overlord. It does not reconstruct disagreements among forecast teams, the precise forecast record, or every command contribution. The avoided-storm argument is a plausible counterfactual presented by the episode, not a directly observable outcome.
