# Graham Allison

## Overview

Graham Allison is a Harvard foreign-policy scholar presented in the sources as a framework-builder on great-power competition and as a cautious analyst of military intervention, nuclear order, and alliance strategy.

## Current Profile

[[152-guanyu-2026-nian-de-si-ge-caixiang-nhx-c5xjwaovpaqjpua24diohsaz]] invokes Allison only briefly to frame how [[China]] and the [[UnitedStates]] can each mistake a self-centered worldview for a complete account of world order. [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-graham-allison-on-the-global-realignment-iran-china-israel-greenland-40373425]] supplies the direct profile: Allison praises demonstrated military capacity but repeatedly asks whether tactical destruction produces a stable political outcome, a defensible exit, or stronger alliances.

His method is historical and probabilistic rather than pacifist or deterministic. He treats the U.S.-China power transition as dangerous without forecasting inevitable war, regards near-term attack on [[Taiwan]] as unlikely absent provocation, supports nuclear nonproliferation while remaining uneasy about preventive war, and argues that Greenland's strategic utility does not require U.S. ownership.

## Key Characteristics

- Separates battlefield or intelligence performance from political settlement and war termination.
- Uses historical cases to expose hubris and unintended consequences without treating analogy as an automatic forecast.
- Frames U.S.-China competition as structurally dangerous but not mechanically destined for war.
- Treats alliance maintenance and permission-based access as strategic assets rather than obstacles to unilateral power.
- Regards postwar great-power peace, nuclear non-use, and limited proliferation as fragile institutional achievements.

## Qualifications

The first source uses Allison as an interpretive reference rather than examining his work. The second is a wide-ranging interview, not a systematic defense of his scholarship or a verification of its empirical claims. His Iran, Taiwan, Chinese technology, proliferation, public-opinion, and inequality judgments are source-dated assessments. His use of historical patterns does not establish that present cases will reproduce earlier outcomes.
