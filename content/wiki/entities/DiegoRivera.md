# Diego Rivera

## Overview

Diego Rivera was a Mexican muralist whom the episode places at the junction of post-revolutionary Indigenous-facing national art and the transformation of [[JoseGuadalupePosada|Posada]]'s satirical skeleton into [[LaCatrina]].

## Current Profile

Rivera matters here less as a complete biographical subject than as a cultural mediator. The episode presents his murals, alongside Frida Kahlo's art, as central to the international image of twentieth-century Mexico. In *Dream of a Sunday Afternoon in Alameda Central Park*, he reused Posada's elegant skeleton and helped stabilize La Catrina as a recognizable national figure.

## Key Characteristics

- Major twentieth-century Mexican muralist.
- Associated with post-revolutionary celebration of Indigenous subjects and rejection of European cultural hierarchy.
- Reworked Posada's calavera within a broad historical mural.
- Helped make [[LaCatrina]] a durable national image.
- Connects elite art, political history, and popular festival iconography.

## Evidence

- National-art context: [[264-mexico-day-of-the-dead-glt9022174156]] places Rivera and Frida Kahlo at the center of the outside world's image of Mexican art from the 1920s to the 1940s.
- Visual transformation: [[264-mexico-day-of-the-dead-glt9022174156]] traces the modern La Catrina figure through Rivera's *Dream of a Sunday Afternoon in Alameda Central Park*.
- Political genealogy: [[264-mexico-day-of-the-dead-glt9022174156]] connects the mural figure to Posada's earlier anti-elite satire.

## Qualifications

The source uses Rivera to explain one visual genealogy and does not provide a comprehensive account of his politics, commissions, artistic development, or relationship with the Mexican state. Indigenous celebration and rejection of European influence are episode-level summaries rather than exhaustive descriptions of his work.

## What Changed

- Created a focused profile of Rivera as mediator between Posada's satire and national festival imagery.
