# Gina Poe

## Overview
Gina Poe is presented in the bounded Huberman Lab sources as a sleep-neuroscience guest explaining sleep stages, memory consolidation, creativity, emotional-memory processing, and locus-coeruleus regulation.

## Current Profile
The current wiki evidence for Gina Poe comes from the full-length [[use-sleep-to-enhance-learning-memory-emotional-state-dr-gina-poe-scim4085406904]] interview and its shorter [[essentials-use-sleep-to-enhance-learning-memory-emotional-state-dr-gina-poe-scim7156610982|Essentials derivative]]. Across them, Poe distinguishes wakefulness, non-REM sleep, and REM sleep as different brain states with different rhythms and chemistry. Her main contribution is a staged sleep model: early-night slow-wave sleep supports restoration and memory transfer, later REM-rich cycles support creative association and emotional-memory processing, and pre-sleep arousal or alcohol can interfere with adaptive sleep.

The full interview broadens that model through [[LocusCoeruleusStateRegulation]]. Poe connects wake attention and stress, REM norepinephrine shutdown, trauma-memory updating, preliminary hormone-linked differences, and opioid-withdrawal sleep disturbance. She keeps these mechanisms provisional where the evidence is animal, retrospective, or incomplete and does not present them as medication, hormone, PTSD, or addiction-treatment protocols.

## Key Characteristics
- Explains sleep as active staged brain work rather than passive shutdown or quiet wakefulness.
- Links early-night slow-wave sleep to growth hormone, energy rebuilding, protein synthesis, cleanup, and memory transfer.
- Links stage-two spindles, P-waves, and later REM sleep to schema formation, insight, and creativity.
- Uses the locus coeruleus and norepinephrine to explain why REM may help separate traumatic memories from overwhelming physiological charge.
- Extends the locus-coeruleus model to opioid-withdrawal sleep disruption and relapse-like behavior in animal work.
- Keeps practical guidance bounded by context, including circadian regularity, alcohol effects, tracker limits, and pre-sleep arousal.
