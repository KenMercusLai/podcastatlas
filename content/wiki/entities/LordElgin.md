# Lord Elgin

## Overview

Lord Elgin was the British diplomat and collector whose removal of Parthenon sculpture became, for [[LordByron|Lord Byron / 拜伦]], a symbol of cultural plunder and British intrusion into Greece.

## Current Profile

[[441-lord-byron-scandal-sex-and-celebrity-part-2-glt1954408695]] presents a conflict rather than a settled verdict. Elgin's agent Giovanni Battista Lusieri moved from drawing and casting the Acropolis sculpture to removing it after reporting beauty and decay; the episode says Ottoman permission was obtained through a bribery-normalized administrative system and that no formal Greek or Ottoman objection was made at the time.

Byron answered with fierce poetic condemnation, while [[JohnCamHobhouse|John Cam Hobhouse]] argued that decay and local burning of marble for lime placed the works at risk. The disagreement makes Elgin a case where imperial power, legal permission, local voice, conservation, personal animus, and later cultural ownership cannot be collapsed into one uncomplicated account.

## Key Characteristics

- British diplomatic figure associated with removal of Parthenon sculpture from Ottoman-ruled Athens.
- Patron whose agent shifted from documentation and casting to physical removal.
- Target of Byron's anti-plunder poetry and personal mockery.
- Center of a preservation-versus-dispossession dispute already visible among contemporary travelers.
- Case where formal permission does not by itself settle cultural legitimacy or ownership.

## Evidence

- Removal process: [[441-lord-byron-scandal-sex-and-celebrity-part-2-glt1954408695]] describes Lusieri's work, Ottoman permission, bribery context, and the absence of recorded formal objection at the time.
- Condemnation: [[441-lord-byron-scandal-sex-and-celebrity-part-2-glt1954408695]] connects Byron's *The Curse of Minerva* and *Childe Harold's Pilgrimage* to a highly personal attack on Elgin.
- Preservation case: [[441-lord-byron-scandal-sex-and-celebrity-part-2-glt1954408695]] reports Hobhouse's concern about decay and marble burning for lime.

## Qualifications

The page records the episode's framing, not a final adjudication of the Parthenon sculptures dispute. Ottoman authorization, bribery, local silence, physical risk, removal damage, modern Greek claims, and the difference between legal authority and cultural consent require broader evidence. Byron's false syphilis insinuation about Elgin's nose is personal abuse, not evidence.
