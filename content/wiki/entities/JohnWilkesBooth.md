# John Wilkes Booth

## Overview

John Wilkes Booth is Abraham Lincoln's assassin, presented as a famous actor, Confederate sympathizer, and white supremacist whose Brutus fantasy joined political violence to theatrical self-dramatization.

## Current Profile

[[203-american-civil-war-aftermath-legacy-part-4-glt3990109453]] connects Booth's act directly to the racial politics of the war's end. Lincoln's limited endorsement of Black suffrage intensified Booth's hostility, and the assassination on Good Friday in 1865 removed the president days after Confederate surrender.

[[590-the-assassination-of-abraham-lincoln-death-at-the-theatre-part-1-glt2286421954]] supplies the biographical and ideological prehistory. Booth was not an obscure failed performer but a prominent, athletic Shakespearean actor from a theatrical family. He identified intensely with the Confederacy, defended slavery, focused his rage on Lincoln as Southern defeat deepened, and imagined political murder through the inherited role of Brutus confronting Caesar. Contemporary anti-Lincoln tyrant analogies gave that fantasy a wider rhetorical setting without removing Booth's responsibility.

His operational path also changed. An earlier scheme sought to abduct Lincoln and exchange him for Confederate prisoners, using John Surratt and Mary Surratt's network; after Richmond fell, Lee surrendered, and Lincoln publicly endorsed limited Black suffrage, Booth shifted toward killing him. He learned of the Ford's Theatre visit on April 14, obtained a horse, contacted George Atzerodt, David Herold, and Lewis Powell, and used his familiarity with the theatre to prepare.

[[591-the-assassination-of-abraham-lincoln-manhunt-for-the-killer-part-2-glt7618197031]] reconstructs Booth as an actor who used his knowledge of the play to time the shot, wounded Henry Rathbone, escaped with David Herold, and depended on a wider Confederate-sympathizer network before dying at the Garrett farm. His journal invokes Brutus, William Tell, lost national cause, and an inability to repent, revealing an effort to author the moral meaning of the crime while hiding from capture.

The source interprets that self-image as a catastrophic misreading. Booth imagined tyrannicide, yet the assassination helped make Lincoln a martyr and damaged the cause Booth claimed to serve. The political consequence still cannot be measured as a simple alternative history: Lincoln's greater skill mattered, but transforming Southern racial order would have required extraordinary sustained force and Northern commitment.

## Key Characteristics

- Prominent Shakespearean actor, Confederate sympathizer, and white supremacist identified as Lincoln's assassin.
- Opponent of Black political rights in the episode's account.
- Self-dramatizing tyrannicide who compared himself with Brutus and William Tell.
- Organizer whose unrealized kidnapping scheme became a multi-target murder conspiracy supported by a wider sympathizer network.
- Catalyst of a leadership rupture and indirect creator of Lincoln's martyr memory.

## Qualifications

This page is limited to three popular-history episode summaries rather than Booth's complete biography, journal corpus, trial record, or a documentary reconstruction of every helper. The exact shape and command links of the kidnapping scheme, the point at which murder became fixed, the causal weight of political rhetoric, the precise shout at Ford's Theatre, spur and fall sequence, journal wording, last words, motives, and degree of assistance remain disputed or episode-attributed. A wider support network does not prove authorization by Jefferson Davis or Confederate high command, and the effect of Lincoln's survival remains counterfactual.
