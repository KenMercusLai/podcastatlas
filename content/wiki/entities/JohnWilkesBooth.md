# John Wilkes Booth

## Overview

John Wilkes Booth is Abraham Lincoln's assassin, presented as a famous actor, Confederate sympathizer, and white supremacist whose Brutus fantasy joined political violence to theatrical self-dramatization.

## Current Profile

[[203-american-civil-war-aftermath-legacy-part-4-glt3990109453]] connects Booth's act directly to the racial politics of the war's end. Lincoln's limited endorsement of Black suffrage intensified Booth's hostility, and the assassination on Good Friday in 1865 removed the president days after Confederate surrender.

[[591-the-assassination-of-abraham-lincoln-manhunt-for-the-killer-part-2-glt7618197031]] reconstructs Booth as an actor who used his knowledge of the play to time the shot, wounded Henry Rathbone, escaped with David Herold, and depended on a wider Confederate-sympathizer network before dying at the Garrett farm. His journal invokes Brutus, William Tell, lost national cause, and an inability to repent, revealing an effort to author the moral meaning of the crime while hiding from capture.

The source interprets that self-image as a catastrophic misreading. Booth imagined tyrannicide, yet the assassination helped make Lincoln a martyr and damaged the cause Booth claimed to serve. The political consequence still cannot be measured as a simple alternative history: Lincoln's greater skill mattered, but transforming Southern racial order would have required extraordinary sustained force and Northern commitment.

## Key Characteristics

- Famous Shakespearean actor, Confederate sympathizer, and white supremacist identified as Lincoln's assassin.
- Opponent of Black political rights in the episode's account.
- Self-dramatizing tyrannicide who compared himself with Brutus and William Tell.
- Principal actor in a multi-target conspiracy and flight supported by a wider sympathizer network.
- Catalyst of a leadership rupture and indirect creator of Lincoln's martyr memory.

## Evidence

### Racial and political motive

- [[203-american-civil-war-aftermath-legacy-part-4-glt3990109453]] says Booth heard Lincoln endorse Black voting rights and regarded the president as a tyrant.
- [[591-the-assassination-of-abraham-lincoln-manhunt-for-the-killer-part-2-glt7618197031]] connects Booth's white supremacy and Confederate loyalty to a journal defense of the killing after the cause was lost.

### Performance, conspiracy, and escape

- [[591-the-assassination-of-abraham-lincoln-manhunt-for-the-killer-part-2-glt7618197031]] connects theatrical knowledge, close-range murder, the multi-target plot, a fractured leg, support contacts, the Potomac crossing, and the Garrett-farm standoff.

### Reconstruction rupture

- [[203-american-civil-war-aftermath-legacy-part-4-glt3990109453]] links the assassination to Johnson's succession and to the loss of Lincoln's greater political skill.

### Memory consequence

- [[203-american-civil-war-aftermath-legacy-part-4-glt3990109453]] argues that the killing preserved Lincoln as a martyr outside the compromises of later Reconstruction.
- [[591-the-assassination-of-abraham-lincoln-manhunt-for-the-killer-part-2-glt7618197031]] interprets Booth's Brutus analogy through the irony that killing Caesar made the victim a martyr and injured the assassin's own cause.

## Qualifications

This page is limited to two popular-history episode summaries rather than Booth's complete biography, journal corpus, trial record, or a documentary reconstruction of every helper. The precise shout at Ford's Theatre, spur and fall sequence, journal wording, last words, motives, and degree of assistance remain disputed or episode-attributed. A wider support network does not prove authorization by Jefferson Davis or Confederate high command, and the effect of Lincoln's survival remains counterfactual.

## What Changed

- Added Booth's theatrical method, Brutus self-image, flight, and dependence on the wider conspiracy network.
- Reframed his legacy around the unintended martyrdom and political backfire of the killing.
