# 《时尚受害者》 / Fashion Victims

## Overview

[[FashionVictimsBook|Fashion Victims]] is Alison Matthews David's history of the dangers embedded in clothing, cosmetics, dyes, accessories, and their production. Across two independent podcast discussions, it serves as a bridge between fashion history, public health, labor history, gender, material culture, and environmental accounting.

## Current Profile

The book treats attractive objects as evidence-bearing material systems. A ballet skirt, crinoline, green artificial flower, felt hat, cosmetic powder, shoe, belt, or pair of jeans can reveal how industrial innovation changes fire load, toxic exposure, movement, workplace conditions, and the distribution of risk.

Its central corrective is that “fashion victim” should not mean only a consumer who follows trends unwisely. Wearers can be harmed, but makers, performers, cleaners, children, animals, and downstream communities may encounter longer exposure and have less power to refuse it. Historical risk was often known; persistence therefore requires explanations involving profit, status, regulation, gender expectations, and worker organization as well as incomplete knowledge.

## Key Characteristics

- Uses garments and fashion materials to connect beauty and novelty to injury, disease, poisoning, fire, and environmental harm.
- Shifts attention between consumer experience and the often-heavier exposure borne by makers and performers.
- Treats mercury, arsenic, lead, solvents, radioactive metal, and flammable fabrics as parts of production systems rather than isolated curiosities.
- Links bodily restriction and discomfort to class display, gender norms, movement, and wearer agency.
- Extends its historical cases into modern global supply chains where safer consumer experiences can conceal worker harm.

## Evidence

### Material and bodily hazards

- [[16-shishang-shouhaizhe-jing-luse-huo-wei-zui-du-yanse-559889282]] ranges across infection, poisons, fire, machinery, restrictive dress, synthetic substitutes, and cleaning chemicals.
- [[158-killer-fashion-glt5960489800]] details flammable tulle and crinolines, hobble skirts, footwear, mercury felt, arsenical green, lead cosmetics, radioactive accessories, sandblasted denim, and toxic adhesives.

### Labor and regulatory distribution

- [[16-shishang-shouhaizhe-jing-luse-huo-wei-zui-du-yanse-559889282]] foregrounds hatters, flower makers, seamstresses, laundresses, dancers, children, and overseas workers as possible victims before purchase.
- [[158-killer-fashion-glt5960489800]] adds differences in national bans, public inquiries, union pressure, industrial resistance, and globalized garment work.

### Gender, status, and agency

- Both sources resist explaining dangerous dress only as female vanity; [[158-killer-fashion-glt5960489800]] further qualifies the account by noting the social value, pleasure, support, and boundary-setting some wearers found in fashionable clothing.

## Qualifications

The two source notes are public-history discussions rather than a complete toxicological, regulatory, or global labor history. Numerical claims about theatre-fire deaths, poison quantities, cosmetic contamination, and individual cases remain source-scoped. The sources also cover many places and centuries unevenly, so examples demonstrate recurring mechanisms without establishing uniform exposure or motive.

## What Changed

- Added independent English-language coverage that corroborates the book's main material-risk and labor arguments.
- Qualified a simple coercion account by preserving wearers' comfort, pleasure, status, support, and boundary-setting alongside danger.
- Added cross-national regulation and worker organization as explanations for why known hazards were controlled unevenly.
