# Nuremberg Laws

## Overview

The Nuremberg Laws were the 1935 measures through which [[NaziGermany|Nazi Germany]] formalized racial exclusion from citizenship and intimate life while making the swastika the national flag.

## Current Profile

The episode groups three laws passed at the 1935 party rally: the flag law, the Reich citizenship law, and the Law for the Protection of German Blood and German Honour. Together they fused party and state symbolism, restricted full citizenship to people of “German or kindred blood,” prohibited marriage and sexual relations between Jews and Germans, and restricted Jewish households from employing younger German women.

The laws answered a political problem without resolving the regime's intellectual one. They appeased activists and conservative demands for legal order by displacing some street disorder into enforceable state categories, yet officials still had to decide who counted as Jewish. Genealogy, religious records, courts, police, medicine, and denunciation therefore carried racial ideology into daily life.

## Key Characteristics

- They converted antisemitic exclusion into national statutory form.
- They fused Nazi party symbolism with the German state through the flag law.
- They removed full citizenship on racial grounds.
- They criminalized designated marriages and sexual relationships.
- They extended political control into ancestry, households, and private life.

## Qualifications

This profile follows one narrative episode and is not a complete legal history of every implementing decree or later amendment. The laws' temporary reduction of unauthorized street action was not moderation: legal regularity made persecution more systematic and intrusive.

## What Changed

- Established the laws as a transition from unstable local coercion to systematic national racial exclusion.
- Made their intrusion into citizenship, ancestry, sexuality, and household life explicit.
