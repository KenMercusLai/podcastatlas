## Overview

[[CaoZhi|曹植]] is Cao Cao's poet son, presented in Hanji 1149 as a serious but ultimately weakened succession contender whose literary brilliance does not compensate for drinking, rule-breaking, and unreliable self-command.

## Current Profile

The episode says [[CaoCao|曹操]] leaves Cao Zhi at Ye and assigns [[XingYongLateHan|刑颙]] as a strict household counselor, implying that Cao Zhi once received deliberate political formation. Cao Zhi instead prefers more congenial literary company, including [[LiuZhenLateHan|刘桢]], although Liu Zhen himself warns that ornate talent is not a substitute for Xing Yong's practical counsel.

The succession judgment turns on reliability. The reported Sima Gate violation and Cao Zhi's drinking make talent hard to trust with power, while [[CaoPi|曹丕]] is portrayed as more controlled, politically legible, and able to manage officials. Hanji 1160 repeats the breach after Cao Pi's selection and adds that the gate officer was executed, widening the episode from personal intemperance to collateral institutional harm. Its claim that Cao Zhi continued drinking after release remains a source-scoped interpretation of failed emotional recovery.

Hanji 1156 adds a factional layer through [[DingYiLateHan|丁仪]], described as a Cao Zhi adviser with a grievance against Cao Pi. The episode places the likely accusation of [[CuiYanLateHan|崔琰]], a public Cao Pi supporter, inside that contest. This establishes political alignment and a plausible interest structure, not Cao Zhi's direction of the accusation or knowledge of it.

Hanji 1159 sharpens the distinction between talent and delegation. Cao Zhi's improvised tribute before Cao Cao's campaign wins immediate admiration, but the episode argues that literary display does not answer the wider question of governing reliability or adviser quality. Its portrayal of Ding Yi and [[YangXiu|杨修]] as politically unhelpful belongs to the host's causal reconstruction and does not make Cao Zhi responsible for every action associated with his camp.

## Key Characteristics

- Exceptional literary ability and expressive temperament.
- Receives strict political mentorship but resists its style.
- Prefers congenial talent while underweighting corrective counsel.
- Loses political trust through drinking and boundary violations.
- Allows disappointment and intoxication to produce consequences for a subordinate gatekeeper.
- Illustrates the gap between brilliance and dependable governing capacity.
- Attracts advisers whose rivalry with Cao Pi makes the succession struggle a factional risk field.

## Qualifications

The sources do not establish that one gate incident, one farewell, or adviser alignment alone decided the succession, nor that poetic temperament and political capacity are inherently opposed. Hanji 1156 does not show Cao Zhi directing, approving, or knowing of the accusation against Cui Yan; it places a member of his camp in a plausible but indirectly reconstructed role. Hanji 1159's assessment of Ding Yi and Yang Xiu is partisan to its explanatory frame, and Hanji 1160's exact road privilege, legal penalty, arrest, release, gate officer's execution, and post-release drinking require verification against the underlying historical texts.

## What Changed

- Added Cao Zhi's literary farewell as evidence of real comparative strength rather than treating the contest as one-sided.
- Added adviser quality as a qualified part of the episode's succession explanation.
- Preserved the boundary against making one incident, performance, or factional association decisive.
