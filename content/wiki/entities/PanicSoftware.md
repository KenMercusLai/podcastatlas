# Panic

## Overview

Panic is a Portland-based software company and maker of the [[Playdate]] handheld console.

## Current Profile

The company appears in [[should-we-all-be-getting-tariff-refunds-right-now]] as an unusually traceable consumer-tariff case. After initially absorbing import duties and later adding them as an order-level charge, Panic used those records to return customer-paid amounts when the relevant duties were invalidated and refunded.

## Key Characteristics

- Small software company, described as having about 25 employees, that expanded into physical hardware.
- Direct seller of Playdate consoles manufactured in Malaysia.
- Initially absorbed tariff costs before exposing the charge separately at checkout.
- Preserved customer-level payment records that made exact repayment possible.
- Chose to return identifiable customer tariff funds despite the material cost to the business.

## Evidence

- **Hardware transition:** [[should-we-all-be-getting-tariff-refunds-right-now]] describes Playdate as Panic's first physical product and outlines its Malaysian manufacturing choice.
- **Tariff treatment:** [[should-we-all-be-getting-tariff-refunds-right-now]] says Panic first absorbed roughly $100,000, then displayed a 19% tariff line and explanation.
- **Customer repayment:** [[should-we-all-be-getting-tariff-refunds-right-now]] reports that the company wrote refund-processing code and automatically returned traceable charges.
- **Corporate judgment:** [[should-we-all-be-getting-tariff-refunds-right-now]] contrasts Panic's direct repayment with larger console companies' different responses.

## Qualifications

Panic's direct-sales model, small product range, and explicit charge make it a favorable case for customer-level refunds. The source does not show that its approach scales to multi-tier retail distribution or establishes a legal obligation for other importers.

## What Changed

- Established Panic as a concrete case of transparent tariff pass-through followed by matched consumer repayment.
- Connected its refund process to customer trust and corporate identity without treating those effects as measured marketing returns.
