# Suez Crisis / 苏伊士运河危机

## Overview

The Suez Crisis was the 1956 confrontation that followed [[GamalAbdelNasser|Gamal Abdel Nasser]]'s nationalisation of the Suez Canal Company and brought [[Egypt]], the [[UnitedKingdom]], [[France]], and [[Israel]] toward war under intense American and Cold War pressure.

## Current Profile

This first-part source explains the road to intervention rather than the invasion and withdrawal. It begins with the mismatch between Britain's continuing strategic reliance on the canal and its declining ability to sustain empire. Egypt regarded the British Canal Zone presence as incompatible with sovereignty; Britain regarded the route as vital to oil, shipping, imperial connection, and prestige even after agreeing to withdraw its troops.

The immediate crisis followed the withdrawal of Western and World Bank financing for the Aswan High Dam. Nasser nationalised the canal company, offered compensation, and kept shipping moving. Those facts weakened a narrow legal or operational case for force, but Britain and France enlarged the issue into regime change. France connected Nasser to the Algerian revolt, Israel had its own security conflict with Egypt, and British leaders feared that accepting nationalisation would accelerate the collapse of Western influence.

Diplomatic conferences, the Menzies mission, and UN talks did not eliminate disagreement, but an Egyptian open-navigation promise gave some British officials hope of settlement. Secret planning continued in parallel. The source ends with the French proposal that Israel attack through Sinai, allowing Britain and France to present intervention as protection of the canal.

## Key Characteristics

- Began with a legal nationalisation rather than an Egyptian closure or destruction of the canal.
- Joined anti-colonial sovereignty and development finance to European oil, shipping, and prestige concerns.
- Turned from a canal-management dispute into an objective of replacing Egypt's government.
- Combined distinct British, French, and Israeli motives rather than one unified strategic rationale.
- Unfolded within the Cold War, with the United States fearing that intervention would strengthen Soviet influence.
- Continued escalating despite effective Egyptian canal operation and partial diplomatic progress.

## Qualifications

The source stops before Eden's formal answer, the invasion, Eisenhower's coercion, withdrawal, and the crisis's long-term consequences. It is a narrative interpretation rather than a complete legal, Egyptian, French, Israeli, military, or diplomatic archive. Quantities, private conversations, operational plans, motives, and judgments about inevitability remain source-scoped.
