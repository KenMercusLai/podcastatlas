# Jesus

## Overview

Jesus is the first-century Jewish teacher and central figure of Christianity reconstructed historically across [[287-jesus-christ-the-mystery-part-1-glt4572965796]] and [[288-jesus-christ-the-history-part-2-glt4776258433]]. The combined evidence accepts his existence, association with [[JohnTheBaptist]], public ministry, Jerusalem conflict, and crucifixion while separating those judgments from disputed Gospel details and the supernatural meaning of resurrection claims.

## Current Profile

The current evidence set presents Jesus as a skilled-artisan Galilean whose memorable parables, healings, exorcisms, judgment preaching, and claims to unusual authority attracted followers. [[GalileanJudeanCulturalBoundary]] clarifies that Galilean formation did not make him external to Jewish life: he was Judean by descent, synagogue practice, and temple worship while growing up under a client king and at a distance from Jerusalem's priestly establishment and nearby Greco-Roman cities. His selection of twelve disciples evoked Israel's twelve tribes, while his Jerusalem entry and execution as king of the Judeans support—but do not conclusively prove—a conscious messianic claim.

His [[KingdomOfGodEschatology|Kingdom of God]] was not described as an armed state with soldiers, courts, or ordinary law. It could therefore challenge imperial, temple, and social authority without making Jesus a conventional military rebel or a modern political ideologue. The temple incident and Passover setting created a plausible path from symbolic confrontation to arrest and Roman crucifixion.

## Key Characteristics

- Historically anchored by existence, baptism or close association with John, and crucifixion.
- Formed as a Galilean within Jewish religious life and a sharpening boundary against Greco-Roman cultural identification.
- Remembered as a remarkable teacher using durable parables and compressed sayings.
- Preached judgment and the imminent Kingdom of God while claiming unusual authority within it.
- Used the twelve disciples, Jerusalem entry, and kingly language in ways the episode reads as messianic.
- Engaged socially marginal people without presenting a modern socialist or conventional revolutionary program.
- Became the object of rapid resurrection belief and exalted devotion after a humiliating public death.

## Evidence

- Historical anchors: [[288-jesus-christ-the-history-part-2-glt4776258433]] says skeptical scholars commonly accept Jesus's baptism by John and crucifixion and treats his teaching as evidence of a distinctive historical voice.
- External attestation and setting: [[287-jesus-christ-the-mystery-part-1-glt4572965796]] combines [[Josephus]], Roman writers, Gospel criticism, political geography, and archaeology to support existence and contextualize Jesus's Galilean formation.
- Authority and message: [[288-jesus-christ-the-history-part-2-glt4776258433]] connects the twelve, judgment, healings, exorcisms, messianic enactment, and Kingdom preaching.
- Jerusalem conflict: [[288-jesus-christ-the-history-part-2-glt4776258433]] treats the donkey entry and temple incident as actions that heightened concern among temple and Roman authorities.
- Death and afterlife: [[288-jesus-christ-the-history-part-2-glt4776258433]] joins crucifixion, early Last Supper tradition, followers' reported experiences, resurrection belief, and exalted titles.

## Qualifications

This is a source-bounded historical profile, not a theological ruling or a complete account of Jesus scholarship. The episodes dispute or qualify much of the Nativity reconstruction, Gospel authorship and transmission, ministry length, particular sayings, intentions, and Passion details. Josephus's Jesus passage is partly interpolated, and archaeological context cannot determine an individual's beliefs. Historical method can examine testimony, memory, political context, and the emergence of belief but cannot adjudicate the supernatural truth of resurrection.

## What Changed

- Added external Jewish and Roman attestation to the earlier life-and-teaching reconstruction.
- Reframed Galilean identity as a Jewish regional formation shaped by distance from Jerusalem and Greco-Roman civic culture.
- Preserved the distinction between execution on a political charge and leadership of a conventional armed revolt.
