# Philip VI of France

## Overview

Philip VI of France appears as the Valois claimant chosen after the direct [[CapetianDynasty|Capetian]] male line failed, and as the French king whose pressure over [[Gascony]] and confiscation of [[Aquitaine]] opens the [[HundredYearsWar|Hundred Years' War]] in this source.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Philip as the French establishment's answer to the 1328 succession crisis. [[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]] has a claim through [[IsabellaOfFrance|Isabella]], but the French reject a claim transmitted through a woman and choose Philip of Valois, even though the exclusion rule is described as not yet fully settled.

Philip then acts from the position of a much stronger kingdom. The source describes [[France]] as wealthier, more populous, administratively prestigious, and culturally central to chivalry. Philip demands homage, supports [[Scotland]] under the [[FrancoScottishAlliance|Franco-Scottish alliance]], and in May 1337 confiscates Aquitaine, which the hosts treat as the effective declaration of war.

The campaign evidence shows that material advantage did not guarantee operational control. Edward III's wool embargo destabilized French influence in [[Flanders]], [[BattleOfSluys|Sluys]] removed the immediate naval advantage, and the 1346 Normandy devastation forced Philip to respond. At [[BattleOfCrecy|Crécy]], he recognized the English position's strength but chose battle under reputational pressure; compromised Genoese deployment, cavalry disorder, and repeated attacks then produced defeat.

Philip also tried to activate the war's wider alliance system by calling for a Scottish invasion and later attempted to relieve [[Calais]]. David II was captured at Neville's Cross, and Philip withdrew without breaking the siege, leaving Calais to surrender.

## Key Characteristics

- Valois ruler chosen after the direct Capetian male line ends.
- French alternative to Edward III's female-line claim.
- Ruler of the materially stronger kingdom in the episode's Anglo-French asymmetry.
- Uses homage and forfeiture pressure around Gascony and Aquitaine.
- Bound into Scottish policy through the Franco-Scottish alliance.
- Ruler whose reputational need to answer devastation contributes to accepting battle at Crécy.
- Unable in this source to coordinate French maritime, battlefield, Scottish, and Calais relief efforts into a successful response.

## Evidence

- Succession choice: [[318-hundred-years-war-a-game-of-thrones-part-1-glt5584156008]] says Philip becomes king after Charles IV's widow gives birth to a daughter and Edward's claim is rejected.
- French power setting: [[318-hundred-years-war-a-game-of-thrones-part-1-glt5584156008]] frames Philip's kingdom as richer, more populous, and more prestigious than England.
- Homage pressure: [[318-hundred-years-war-a-game-of-thrones-part-1-glt5584156008]] says Philip demands homage from Edward for his French lands.
- Scottish obligation: [[318-hundred-years-war-a-game-of-thrones-part-1-glt5584156008]] says David II's flight to France and the alliance force Philip to support Scottish rights.
- War threshold: [[318-hundred-years-war-a-game-of-thrones-part-1-glt5584156008]] treats Philip's May 1337 confiscation of Aquitaine as effectively war.
- Flanders and Sluys: [[319-hundred-years-war-triumph-of-the-longbow-part-2-glt3100937638]] connects wool pressure, Flemish support, and French naval defeat to the weakening of Philip's opening position.
- Crécy decision: [[319-hundred-years-war-triumph-of-the-longbow-part-2-glt3100937638]] says Philip recognized the strong English position but fought because retreat carried unacceptable loss of face.
- Multi-front failure: [[319-hundred-years-war-triumph-of-the-longbow-part-2-glt3100937638]] links the failed Scottish invasion and unsuccessful relief of Calais to the wider English triumph of 1346-1347.

## Qualifications

The profile remains bounded to two popular-history episodes rather than a full reign. Force ratios, casualty figures, tactical sequencing, motives, and judgments of Philip's military skill remain source-scoped. The Calais siege chronology is internally inconsistent in the newer source.

## What Changed

- Extended Philip from the succession and Aquitaine crisis into the failed operational response at Sluys, Crécy, and Calais.
- Added reputational pressure and multi-front coordination failure without treating French material strength as battlefield destiny.
