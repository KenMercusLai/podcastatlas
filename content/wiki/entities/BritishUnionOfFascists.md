# British Union of Fascists

## Overview

The British Union of Fascists was [[OswaldMosley|Oswald Mosley]]'s Blackshirt organization, founded in 1932 around Italian fascist models, charismatic spectacle, corporatism, and organized coercion before adapting through antisemitic East End campaigning and ultra-appeasement.

## Current Profile

The BUF initially joined protectionism and interventionist economics to nationalist corporatism, abolition or transformation of parliamentary representation, black uniforms, veteran comradeship, and political theater. A defense force recruited in part from unemployed men enforced its meetings. The official launch did not yet foreground antisemitism, although anti-Semites entered the movement and helped prepare its later direction.

Elite curiosity, the January Club, and Lord Rothermere's press support briefly helped the BUF look respectable. That strategy collapsed after Blackshirts visibly beat hecklers at Olympia in June 1934. Rothermere withdrew support, the Night of the Long Knives sharpened public alarm about fascist violence, and reported membership fell dramatically by 1935.

The weakened movement then attacked big business and chain stores, appealed to small traders threatened by retail consolidation, and increasingly fused those complaints with antisemitism. In late 1935 and 1936 it competed with communists and other political groups through street meetings in poor East London neighborhoods.

The [[BattleOfCableStreet|Cable Street]] blockade stopped its planned march but did not immediately destroy the organization. The episode reports continued meetings, increased estimated London membership, and anti-Jewish violence in Stepney soon afterward. By the late 1930s the BUF presented itself as the anti-war alternative to both major parties, opposed war over Poland, and defended further appeasement of Hitler. Wartime detention of Mosley and hundreds of fascists under Regulation 18B then broke its momentum.

## Key Characteristics

- Uniformed, leader-centered movement modeled immediately on Mussolini's Italian fascism.
- Corporatist and anti-parliamentary organization that fused economic intervention with executive authority.
- Political spectacle backed by a defense force willing to beat and eject opponents.
- Movement whose elite and press respectability collapsed after publicly visible Olympia violence.
- Local organizer of small-trader resentment and explicit antisemitic scapegoating.
- Ultra-appeasement movement that used peace and anti-establishment rhetoric to seek late-1930s growth.
- Organization whose momentum ended under wartime emergency detention rather than at Cable Street alone.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to two episodes and does not provide a complete organizational history, membership series, financing account, regional comparison, or study of rank-and-file motives. The sources distinguish early official rhetoric from later campaigning: the 1932 launch did not center Jews, but anti-Semites were already joining and explicit antisemitism became central after decline. Membership, branch and crowd figures, establishment motives, deliberate orchestration of Olympia violence, and causal interpretations of rise and decline remain source-scoped. Peace advocacy alone is not fascism; here it belonged to an organization defined by authoritarianism, uniforms, coercion, antisemitism, and accommodation of Nazi aggression.

## What Changed

- Added the BUF's Italian model, corporatist program, uniform culture, defense force, and early attempt to turn spectacle into political identity.
- Added establishment curiosity and press backing as contingent sources of respectability.
- Identified Olympia, reinforced by the Night of the Long Knives, as the decisive public break before East End rebuilding.
- Qualified the movement's antisemitic chronology without minimizing the later centrality of anti-Jewish politics.
