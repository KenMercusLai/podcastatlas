# Antonin Scalia

## Overview

Antonin Scalia was a U.S. Supreme Court justice whose clerkship-selection remarks provide [[the-tortoise-and-the-hare-the-tortoise-and-the-hare|The Tortoise and the Hare]] with its central example of prestige used as a risk-reduction shortcut.

## Current Profile

At a 2009 American University event, Scalia reportedly said that he generally chose clerks from the hardest law schools to enter because he could not afford a hiring mistake. The episode interprets this as reliance on elite admission to certify the “best and brightest,” even if the schools themselves were not necessarily better teachers.

The rule becomes internally unstable through [[JeffreySutton]]. Scalia reportedly regarded Sutton as one of his finest clerks while acknowledging that Sutton's Ohio State background would ordinarily have kept him outside the initial pool. The episode therefore uses Scalia as both an articulate defender of the proxy and a source of evidence against applying it categorically.

## Key Characteristics

- Treated elite law-school admission as a practical screen against clerkship hiring mistakes.
- Distinguished selective admission from the quality of instruction a school provides.
- Operated in a role where each clerkship appointment carried unusually high perceived stakes.
- Praised Jeffrey Sutton despite Sutton's non-elite educational path.
- Embodies the tension between understandable risk management and self-reinforcing prestige exclusion.

## Evidence

### Prestige as risk control

- [[the-tortoise-and-the-hare-the-tortoise-and-the-hare]] reports Scalia's claim that he favored the hardest schools to enter because a mistaken clerk hire was too costly.

### Counterexample inside the rule

- [[the-tortoise-and-the-hare-the-tortoise-and-the-hare]] reports that Scalia named Sutton among his finest clerks while acknowledging that he would not initially have selected him under the normal filter.

### Institutional interpretation

- [[the-tortoise-and-the-hare-the-tortoise-and-the-hare]] uses the audience's approval and the clerkship's slow analytical work to argue that prestige assumptions can persist despite contrary evidence.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to one structured episode summary and does not attempt a general account of Scalia's jurisprudence, complete clerk-hiring record, or every selection criterion he used. A high-stakes employer can rationally seek low-cost signals; the source challenges the signal's categorical use rather than proving that selectivity contains no information.

## What Changed

- Established the episode-specific clerkship-selection rationale attributed to Scalia.
- Added Sutton as an acknowledged counterexample within Scalia's own hiring experience.
- Framed prestige screening as risk management with exclusion and validity costs.
