# Horst-Wessel-Lied

## Overview

The Horst-Wessel-Lied appears as the Nazi movement song that became a toxic national symbol under [[NaziGermany]].

## Current Profile

The anthem episode distinguishes the older [[GermanNationalAnthem]] from explicitly Nazi political music. Written by [[HorstWessel]] in 1929, the song became martyr propaganda after [[JosephGoebbels]] transformed Wessel's death into a movement story. Under the Nazis, public ceremony paired it with the national anthem, and after 1945 both tune and lyrics were banned.

The collapse episode adds the song's movement function before state power. Its hymn-like form, marches through communist districts, funeral performance, and quasi-Christian ritual converted a street fighter's death into sacred sacrifice, comradeship, and political belonging. The song therefore joined paramilitary recruitment to a wider effort to replace religious and civic sources of meaning with Nazi loyalty.

## Key Characteristics

- Nazi movement song written before the regime took power.
- Propaganda object built around martyr memory after Horst Wessel's death.
- Hymn-like ritual instrument joining street mobilization to pseudo-Christian martyrdom.
- Ceremonial counterpart to the German anthem during Nazi rule.
- Post-1945 banned symbol treated as politically toxic in later legal and public memory.

## Evidence

- Authorship and date: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] says Horst Wessel wrote the song in 1929.
- Martyr propaganda: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] says Joseph Goebbels transformed Wessel's death into Nazi propaganda.
- State ceremony: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] says the song became a national symbol under the Nazis and a 1934 edict required the Nazi salute when it was played.
- Postwar status: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] says both tune and lyrics were banned after 1945.
- Movement ritual: [[297-the-nazis-hitlers-triumph-part-3-glt2055084600]] connects the song to marches, funeral propaganda, Christian appropriation, and repeated martyr commemoration.

## Qualifications

This page is bounded to two episodes and does not reconstruct the song's full textual history, Wessel's complete biography, audience reception, or postwar German criminal law. The pseudo-Christian interpretation describes Nazi propaganda form and does not imply uniform belief or genuine doctrinal continuity.
