# Law of 22 Prairial

## Overview

The Law of 22 Prairial was a June 1794 measure that compressed Revolutionary Tribunal procedure, greatly broadened political offences, and made death the only guilty sentence.

## Current Profile

Introduced by [[GeorgesCouthon|Georges Couthon]] on 10 June and presented by the episode as largely written by [[MaximilienRobespierre|Robespierre]], the law removed defence counsel and ordinary evidentiary testimony. Jurors could convict through inner conviction, while the category “enemies of the people” absorbed criticism, false news, moral insult, official abuse, interference with food supply, and broadly hostile conduct. Because acquittal or death became the available outcomes, the law accelerated both executions and anticipatory fear among deputies.

## Key Characteristics

- It removed defence counsel for accused persons.
- It displaced testimony and ordinary evidence with jurors' inner conviction.
- It broadened enemy categories across speech, morality, office, supply, and conduct.
- It made death the only sentence after conviction.
- Its combination of vague liability and Robespierre's unnamed warnings made insiders unable to infer safety.

## Qualifications

- The episode's account of drafting responsibility is source-scoped and does not substitute for a full legislative history.
- A broad law does not prove that every listed category was enforced identically or that every later execution followed the same factual pattern.
- Fear around the law contributed to coalition formation but was not the only cause of Robespierre's fall.

## What Changed

- A canonical page now records the law's procedural design and its role in converting legal escalation into insider coalition pressure.
