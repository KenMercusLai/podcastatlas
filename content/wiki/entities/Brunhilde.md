# Brunhilde

## Overview

Brunhilde was a Visigothic princess who became queen of Austrasia, repeatedly governed as a Merovingian regent, and sustained a long dynastic and territorial struggle against [[Fredegund]] and her family.

## Current Profile

[[521-warlords-of-the-west-killer-queens-part-2-glt5666966864]] introduces Brunhilde through royal birth, marriage to Sigebert, and grief for her murdered sister Galswintha. She built alliances at the Austrasian court and pressed Sigebert to seek judgment against Chilperic. When Sigebert was assassinated during his apparent victory in 575, Brunhilde's young son Childebert was smuggled to safety and crowned while she was confined at Rouen.

Her failed marriage to Chilperic's son Merovich was one attempt to recover leverage. More durable authority came through dynastic maternity and regency: she negotiated the Treaty of Andelot with Guntram, later governed for her grandsons, and at about seventy again acted for underage heirs. These repeated returns make her political life longer than the personal feud alone.

Her final defeat depended on aristocratic defection as well as Clothar's force. Pippin and Bishop Arnulf of Metz surrendered Brunhilde and her great-grandchildren; Clothar then tried, tortured, publicly humiliated, and killed her. The episode treats the charge that she murdered ten kings as a victor's consolidation of blame, not a transparent finding.

## Key Characteristics

- Visigothic princess whose foreign royal status supplied lineage and diplomatic capital.
- Austrasian queen who converted kinship grievance into legal, religious, and military action.
- Alliance builder repeatedly restored to power through sons, grandsons, and great-grandchildren.
- Treaty-maker and regent across Austrasia and Burgundy.
- Rival of Fredegund whose struggle was conducted without the two queens meeting.
- Defeated ruler whose brutal execution enabled hostile retrospective blame.

## Qualifications

The source is more sympathetic to Brunhilde than to Fredegund but still depends on narrative reconstruction. Her role in Sigebert's war, encouragement of the Merovich marriage, responsibility for later family conflict, exact authority across regions, and the charges used at her trial require evidence beyond this episode. Political victimhood at the end of her life does not make every earlier action benign.

## What Changed

- Established Brunhilde as a repeated regent and treaty-making dynastic actor across several generations.
- Distinguished durable government from the narrower revenge narrative centered on Galswintha.
- Treated the final murder charges as victorious political framing rather than settled fact.
