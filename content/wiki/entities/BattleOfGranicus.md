# Battle of the Granicus

## Overview

The Battle of the Granicus was [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great's]] first major victory in Asia, breaking the initial western defense of the [[AchaemenidEmpire]] and converting a debt-strained invasion into a campaign able to draw on captured revenue.

## Current Profile

In spring 334 BCE, Persian regional forces met Alexander at the Granicus River. The riverbed, banks, missiles, cavalry concentration, and uncertain crossing sequence make the engagement difficult to reconstruct. Arrian has [[Parmenion]] advise delay and Alexander attack immediately; Diodorus instead describes a dawn crossing. The source preserves the conflict rather than choosing a harmonized account.

Alexander's conspicuous position at the head of the Companion cavalry made him a command signal and a target. He helped widen the Macedonian foothold but nearly died in fighting with Persian nobles before Cleitus the Black intervened. Once Persian leaders fell and their cavalry withdrew, the Macedonians surrounded the Greek mercenaries; most were reportedly killed and roughly two thousand survivors enslaved.

The victory created a leadership vacuum, opened cities and treasuries in western Asia Minor, and relieved the expedition's immediate fiscal crisis. Its strategic result therefore cannot be separated from its coercive aftermath or from the possibility that Alexander's death would have ended a campaign organized around his person.

## Key Characteristics

- River-crossing battle whose precise timing and tactical sequence differ across ancient accounts.
- Alexander personally leads the decisive cavalry effort and becomes the obvious Persian target.
- Persian command losses help turn localized success into wider cavalry withdrawal.
- Greek mercenaries suffer killing and enslavement after Persian cavalry leaves the field.
- Victory opens western Anatolian revenue and turns a precarious invasion into continued conquest.

## Qualifications

The profile rests on one structured episode summary comparing late ancient traditions. Crossing time, opening maneuvers, river geometry, troop placement, casualty totals, speeches, the identity and blows of Alexander's attackers, Cleitus's intervention, and mercenary numbers remain disputed or source-scoped. Fiscal relief does not show that the battle alone made later conquest inevitable.
