# Lord Dunmore's Proclamation

## Overview

Lord Dunmore's Proclamation was the November 1775 Virginia measure offering freedom to enslaved people who left rebel enslavers and joined the British side.

## Current Profile

The proclamation joined wartime manpower policy to an emancipation route and alarmed slaveholding white Virginians. The episode treats that alarm as important to Virginia's movement toward independence while refusing to turn Dunmore into a universal abolitionist: he later governed slaveholding Barbados without general emancipation. The document therefore reveals both Black agency and British strategic opportunism inside the Revolution's liberty-slavery contradiction.

## Key Characteristics

- Wartime offer of freedom tied to service for Britain.
- Directed at enslaved people held by rebels rather than universal emancipation.
- Major political shock to slaveholding Virginia and the Carolinas.
- Early British emancipation tactic before the Declaration of Independence.
- Evidence that liberty, allegiance, military need, and slavery were entangled.

## Evidence

- Terms and timing: [[348-the-boston-tea-party-part-2-glt5189620267]] dates the proclamation to November 1775 and describes its service-linked freedom offer.
- Political effect: [[348-the-boston-tea-party-part-2-glt5189620267]] cites the argument that it strongly moved white Virginians toward independence.
- Motive boundary: [[348-the-boston-tea-party-part-2-glt5189620267]] notes Dunmore's later slaveholding governorship in Barbados as evidence against reading him as a consistent abolitionist.
- Contemporary contradiction: [[348-the-boston-tea-party-part-2-glt5189620267]] pairs the measure with Samuel Johnson's attack on slaveholders demanding liberty.

## Qualifications

The source does not quantify escape, enlistment, survival, capture, or eventual freedom. The proclamation's effect on white Virginia is an important causal claim but does not establish preservation of slavery as the sole motive of the Revolution or erase other constitutional and imperial grievances.

## What Changed

- Created the proclamation as the early-war link among Black freedom-seeking, British strategy, and slaveholder radicalization.
