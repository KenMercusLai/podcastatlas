# Lester Glick

## Overview

Lester Glick was a Mennonite conscientious objector and [[MinnesotaStarvationExperiment]] volunteer whose severe illness and persistent hunger redirected him from medicine toward social-work education and lifelong service.

## Current Profile

At 23, while serving in a tuberculosis ward rather than fighting in World War II, Glick encountered an appeal to starve so that others might be better fed. The episode connects his decision to Matthew 25 and a Mennonite duty to feed, welcome, clothe, and visit people in need.

The experiment brought emaciation, weakness, hair loss, anger, withdrawal, food obsession, and a tuberculosis lesion. His children describe later weight cycling, continuous hunger, and keeping emergency candy close at hand. Tuberculosis closed his intended route to medical school, but graduate social-work training opened another: he founded programs at Goshen College, Syracuse University, and the University of Southern Mississippi and taught care as loving kindness rather than service administration alone.

His Wednesday rice meals, neighborhood vegetables, and shared cinnamon rolls show the ambiguity of the legacy. They converted remembered deprivation into empathy and generosity, but they also remained signs that the experiment continued to organize his relationship with food and family life.

## Key Characteristics

- Entered the experiment as a young Mennonite conscientious objector motivated by religious service.
- Documented severe bodily, psychological, and social deterioration in diaries.
- Lived with persistent hunger, weight cycling, food-security behavior, and family-described emotional scars.
- Redirected an interrupted medical vocation into social-work education.
- Treated hunger as a loss of physical, emotional, relational, and spiritual sustenance.
- Practiced generosity through teaching, family rituals, gardening, and food sharing.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to a structured podcast summary rather than Glick's complete diaries, memoir, medical record, institutional archives, or an independent biography. Family recollection is valuable evidence of lived aftermath but cannot by itself assign every later physical or emotional difficulty to the experiment. His later service does not retroactively remove the study's harms or settle its ethics.
