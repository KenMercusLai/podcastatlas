# First Battle of the Marne

## Overview

The First Battle of the Marne was the September 1914 Allied counterattack that stopped the German advance near Paris, preserved France and the Allied coalition from immediate defeat, and redirected the [[WesternFrontFirstWorldWar|Western Front]] toward the Aisne, entrenchment, and the race to the sea.

## Current Profile

[[596-the-first-world-war-the-miracle-on-the-marne-part-3-glt2229565266]] presents the battle as a reversal produced by interacting decisions and constraints. Germany's armies had advanced rapidly through Belgium and northern France but were exhausted, short of reliable transport, and increasingly disorganized. Alexander von Kluck's First Army turned east of Paris, exposing its flank and widening a gap with Karl von Bülow's Second Army.

[[JosephJoffre|Joseph Joffre]] used that opening to coordinate a counterattack beginning on 6 September. [[JosephGallieni|Joseph Gallieni]] committed the Paris garrison, while the British Expeditionary Force advanced into the gap after Britain's cabinet, Kitchener, and Joffre kept Sir John French in the Allied line. Fighting across the broad front remained precarious rather than tactically clean.

The German retreat emerged partly from fractured command information. Moltke's headquarters lacked a reliable operational picture, Richard Hentsch found Bülow pessimistic, and the Second Army's withdrawal made the First Army's position untenable. The retreat saved the German armies from destruction but ended the prospect of the opening campaign's rapid victory.

## Key Characteristics

- Allied counterattack launched when German forces were exhausted and overextended.
- Exposed German flank and army-to-army gap created by the inward turn east of Paris.
- Coalition operation joining French armies, the Paris garrison, and the British Expeditionary Force.
- Command contest shaped by Joffre's timing and German communications failure.
- Broad, uneven battle whose result came through endurance and retreat rather than one decisive tactical blow.
- Strategic defeat of Germany's opening rapid-victory campaign.
- Transition toward the Aisne stalemate, trench systems, and the northward race to the sea.

## Evidence

- Operational opening: [[596-the-first-world-war-the-miracle-on-the-marne-part-3-glt2229565266]] connects German marching exhaustion, transport breakdown, the First Army's turn, and French intelligence to the opportunity for counterattack.
- Allied coordination: [[596-the-first-world-war-the-miracle-on-the-marne-part-3-glt2229565266]] attributes the attack to Joffre's concentration, Gallieni's Paris force, and continued British participation.
- German retreat: [[596-the-first-world-war-the-miracle-on-the-marne-part-3-glt2229565266]] links Moltke's distance, poor communications, Hentsch's mission, Bülow's pessimism, and Kluck's compelled withdrawal.
- Strategic consequence: [[596-the-first-world-war-the-miracle-on-the-marne-part-3-glt2229565266]] follows the retreat to the Aisne, stronger defensive positions, trench digging, and attempts to outflank northward.
- Public memory: [[596-the-first-world-war-the-miracle-on-the-marne-part-3-glt2229565266]] contrasts the battle's multi-causal outcome with the later miracle and taxi legends.

## Qualifications

This profile rests on one narrative podcast summary rather than a complete operational history. Exact force movements, casualty totals, command authority, intelligence effects, quoted dialogue, and the causal weight assigned to individual commanders remain source-scoped. Calling the battle decisive does not mean it ended the war: it preserved the Allied position while helping create a longer industrial stalemate.
