# Monticello

## Overview
Monticello is [[ThomasJefferson|Thomas Jefferson]]'s Virginia plantation and architectural project, presented by the episode as both a monument to Enlightenment learning and a material system sustained by slavery.

## Current Profile
The current profile treats Monticello as the place where Jefferson's brilliance and contradiction become concrete. The episode describes it as a fusion of European neoclassicism, American style, scientific curiosity, and cultivated taste. It also stresses that the house was a plantation with about 150 enslaved African Americans and that Jefferson's debt, collateral, labor needs, and attachment to the property helped prevent him from acting on the liberty principles he articulated.

## Key Characteristics
- Jefferson's beloved architectural and intellectual project in Virginia.
- Plantation household whose functioning depended on enslaved labor.
- Site where [[SallyHemings|Sally Hemings]] and the Hemings family were embedded in domestic service and kinship entanglement.
- Symbol of the [[JeffersonianLibertyContradiction|Jeffersonian liberty contradiction]] because it joined Enlightenment design to slavery.
- Financial and emotional anchor that the episode treats as central to Jefferson's failure to emancipate most enslaved people.

## Evidence
- Architectural meaning: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] describes Monticello as Jefferson's architectural masterpiece and as a fusion of European neoclassicism with American style.
- Enslaved population: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] says roughly 150 African Americans were enslaved there.
- Hemings household: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] places Sally Hemings and her family inside Jefferson's household structure rather than outside the estate story.
- Debt and attachment: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] argues that Jefferson could not free enslaved workers without losing the labor and collateral that sustained Monticello.

## Qualifications
The page follows the episode's interpretive framing. Monticello is not reduced to architecture or to atrocity alone; it is the combined object through which the episode reads Jefferson's learning, taste, debt, household power, and moral failure.
