# Peasants’ Revolt of 1381

## Overview

The Peasants’ Revolt of 1381 was a wide English rising against servitude, taxation, legal and ecclesiastical power, corrupt officials, and economic privilege whose London climax joined radical demands to loyalty toward [[RichardIIOfEngland|Richard II]].

## Current Profile

The episode rejects a simple picture of peasants confronting nobles. Kentish and Essex rebels entered a divided London where townspeople, merchants, political rivals, prisoners, religious radicals, and opportunists widened the movement. They attacked prisons, Lambeth Palace, the Savoy, the Hospitallers, the Tower, monopolists, foreigners, and above all the documents that made obligations enforceable.

The program was similarly composite. Mile End demands included the end of serfdom, fixed rents, freedom from forced labor, removal of supposed traitors, and abolition of monopolies, tolls, and privileges. [[WatTyler|Tyler’s]] later Smithfield demands extended toward free hunting, the destruction of manorial jurisdiction, and a reduced church hierarchy. Yet this social and religious radicalism coexisted with [[RebelRoyalism|royalism]]: rebels asked the king to free them from intermediate lords rather than to abolish monarchy.

The London phase ended after [[WilliamWalworth]] struck Tyler and Richard claimed leadership of the armed crowd, drawing it toward Clerkenwell until loyal forces surrounded it. Suppression and executions followed elsewhere, and the king eventually revoked his concessions. The movement failed to achieve its stated institutional program, but poll taxes vanished for centuries and the rising became both a warning about popular limits and a contested symbol of later radical traditions.

## Key Characteristics

- Multi-regional rising driven by poll-tax pressure, servitude, lordship, official corruption, and economic restrictions.
- Royalist revolt that attacked ministers and intermediaries while affirming the young king’s legitimacy.
- Coalition combining rural grievance, religious radicalism, London rivalries, elite opportunism, and prison-breaking.
- Movement that targeted records and property as instruments of authority rather than treating destruction as simple looting.
- Violent uprising that included executions, xenophobic killing, sanctuary violations, and disputed acts of assistance.
- Immediate political failure with a narrower durable legacy in taxation and institutional memory.

## Qualifications

The episode covers the revolt’s London climax and later suppression rather than supplying a complete regional history. Rebel organization, insider assistance at London Bridge and the Tower, Richard’s sincerity, the intent behind Tyler’s death, and direct Wycliffite influence remain uncertain. Later readings of the revolt as progressive, Protestant, American, libertarian, or Thatcherite are retrospective interpretations and do not resolve its ideological mixture or violence.

## What Changed

- Established the revolt as a coalition whose royalism, social radicalism, commercial conflict, and xenophobic violence must be held together.
- Distinguished failure of the stated program from the durable political toxicity of poll taxation.
