# Robbie Ross

## Overview

Robbie Ross was a friend of [[OscarWilde|Oscar Wilde]] whom the episode presents as both a turning point in Wilde's sexual life and an adviser during the confrontation with the [[MarquessOfQueensberry|Marquess of Queensberry]].

## Current Profile

The episode says that Ross met Wilde in 1886 while preparing for Cambridge and was unusually comfortable with what would now be called a gay identity. It credits him with introducing Wilde to male same-sex experience, but treats that change as activating or clarifying themes already visible in Wilde's art rather than mechanically causing them.

Ross later joined [[AlfredDouglas|Lord Alfred Douglas]] in encouraging Wilde to prosecute Queensberry for libel. The proposed defense would frame Wilde and Douglas's relationship as elevated, Greek, Platonic, and Shakespearean, but the legal strategy could not contain the evidence gathered about other encounters.

## Key Characteristics

- Friend connected to a major change in Wilde's sexual life.
- Young man presented as unusually self-possessed about same-sex desire for the period.
- Adviser who encouraged Wilde's failed libel prosecution.
- Participant in an idealized defense of male same-sex love through classical and literary language.
- Figure whose influence matters without displacing Wilde's responsibility for his own decisions.

## Evidence

- Sexual turning point: [[341-the-trials-of-oscar-wilde-sex-and-scandal-part-1-glt1940599692]] places the 1886 meeting before Wilde's increasingly risky later conduct.
- Legal advice: [[341-the-trials-of-oscar-wilde-sex-and-scandal-part-1-glt1940599692]] identifies Ross and Douglas as supporters of prosecuting Queensberry for libel.
- Cultural language: [[341-the-trials-of-oscar-wilde-sex-and-scandal-part-1-glt1940599692]] connects the planned defense to Greek, Platonic, and Shakespearean models of male love.

## Qualifications

This profile rests on one podcast account, not Ross's papers or a full biography. His age, confidence, role in Wilde's sexual development, advice, motives, and later relationship with Wilde need primary-source corroboration; “introduced” should not be mistaken for sole causal responsibility.
