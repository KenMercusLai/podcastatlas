## Overview

宗均 / Zong Jun is the Eastern Han official whose Hanji 737 profile joins local pacification, personnel judgment, and court office. He first appears as Jiujiang taishou diagnosing tiger attacks and disorder through corrupt officials, burdens, and public disturbance, then as shangshu ling warning [[EmperorMingOfHan|汉明帝]] that literary polish and clean self-presentation are not enough for real governing capacity.

## Current Profile

Hanji 737 gives Zong Jun a compact but coherent administrative profile. In Jiujiang, he does not treat tiger attacks as an isolated animal-control problem. He attributes the crisis to cruel or corrupt officials and to people driven into disorder, then removes traps, cleans out bad officials, promotes loyal and capable officials, reduces taxes and burdens, and restores enough order that the tiger problem disappears in the episode's telling.

The same episode makes his lesson portable to personnel policy. After being appointed shangshu ling, Zong argues that writing-skilled officials can use polish to deceive and that clean officials may only keep themselves clean while lacking the ability to handle bandits or disorder. Mingdi accepts the warning. The page therefore treats Zong as a case for governing competence beyond reputation, not merely as a clean local official.

## Key Characteristics

- Eastern Han official whose current wiki profile is source-bounded to Jiujiang administration and shangshu ling advice.
- Local administrator who explains visible disorder through official conduct, taxation, and public grievance.
- Root-cause governor who pairs removal of harmful measures with personnel cleanup and burden reduction.
- Personnel critic who distinguishes literary skill, clean self-presentation, and actual governing ability.
- Court adviser whose warning is accepted by Mingdi as a correction to narrow official-evaluation preferences.

## Qualifications

The current page does not reconstruct Zong Jun's full biography, exact office chronology, or later career. The tiger-attack branch is preserved as the podcast's governance reading rather than as independent zoological proof. The key settled point is the source's personnel lesson: surface moral or literary signals do not equal administrative capacity under disorder.
