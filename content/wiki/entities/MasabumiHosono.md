# Masabumi Hosono

## Overview

Masabumi Hosono was a Japanese civil servant returning from railway study in Europe whose second-class [[RMSTitanic|Titanic]] passage and survival became a case of transnational mobility and shame.

## Current Profile

The pre-disaster episode places Hosono in Titanic's overlooked second-class and transnational professional world: he was a civil servant returning to Japan after studying Russian railways. The aftermath episode says he entered lifeboat 13 after a sailor called for someone to fill a spare place. Although he had resolved not to behave dishonorably, he was ostracized after returning to Japan and lost his job before later regaining it because of his specialized railway knowledge. That source uses Margaret Mel's interpretation to argue that this shame drew heavily on imported Western, especially Anglo-Saxon, expectations of masculine conduct rather than transparently expressing an older Japanese tradition.

## Key Characteristics

- Japanese civil servant with specialist railway knowledge.
- Second-class Titanic passenger returning from study of the Russian railway system.
- Survivor who entered lifeboat 13 when a spare place was called.
- Target of post-disaster ostracism and employment loss.
- Later reinstated because his technical knowledge remained valuable.
- Case showing the international circulation of gendered disaster morality.

- Voyage and survival: [[432-titanic-the-survivors-part-6-glt4613169046]] describes Hosono's railway study, second-class passage, personal resolution, and entry into lifeboat 13.
- Reputation and work: [[432-titanic-the-survivors-part-6-glt4613169046]] records ostracism, job loss, and later reinstatement.
- Interpretation: [[432-titanic-the-survivors-part-6-glt4613169046]] attributes to Margaret Mel the argument that Western masculine expectations shaped the shame imposed on Hosono.

## Qualifications

The interpretation of Hosono's shame is episode-attributed and does not establish one uniform Japanese response or a simple opposition between Western and Japanese traditions. His status as the only Japanese passenger, details of the railway study and lifeboat call, institutional decisions, and public reaction remain source-scoped.

- Added his pre-disaster position within second-class professional travel and Titanic's transnational passenger society.
