# Constantine the Great

## Overview

Constantine the Great enters the wiki as both a claimant produced by the [[Tetrarchy]]'s dynastic exclusions and the clean-shaven emperor whose later image advertised restored peace, stability, and civilian rule.

## Current Profile

The Diocletian episode places Constantine at the succession fault line of the Tetrarchy. Although present at the 305 abdication, he was not appointed Caesar; after joining his father [[ConstantiusChlorus|Constantius Chlorus]] in Britain, he claimed power when Constantius died at York. This is source-bounded context for his rise rather than a full account of the later civil wars or reign.

The beards episode places Constantine in a sequence of changing Roman visual codes. After [[Hadrian]] and third-century military emperors made beards signs of Greek culture and soldierly authority, Constantine's clean-shaven presentation advertised a different political order and also informed early Christian images of Christ as emperor of heaven.

## Key Characteristics

- Re-established a clean-shaven imperial model.
- Used presentation legible as civilian peace after military crisis.
- Dynastic claimant excluded from Diocletian's planned 305 succession.
- Son of Constantius Chlorus who claimed power after his father's death in Britain.
- Sits at the junction of Roman imperial and early Christian visual codes.

## Evidence

- Succession exclusion: [[260-croatia-the-man-who-saved-the-roman-empire-glt9631298578]] says Constantine attended the abdication but was omitted from the new Caesar appointments.
- Dynastic return: [[260-croatia-the-man-who-saved-the-roman-empire-glt9631298578]] connects his later claim to Constantius's death at York.
- Political presentation: [[491-historys-greatest-beards-from-egyptian-queens-to-medieval-conquerors-part-1-glt6058938613]] connects Constantine's smooth face with restored stability and civilian order.
- Christian visual afterlife: [[491-historys-greatest-beards-from-egyptian-queens-to-medieval-conquerors-part-1-glt6058938613]] notes that early clean-shaven images of Christ could draw on imperial representation.

## Qualifications

This remains a source-bounded profile, not a general account of Constantine's civil wars, reign, conversion, or Christian policy. The Diocletian episode compresses the route from York to imperial supremacy, while the symbolic reading of a smooth face does not prove a single personal motive or a direct causal line for every early image of Christ.
