# Aquitaine

## Overview

Aquitaine is the French duchy whose confiscation helps open the [[HundredYearsWar|Hundred Years' War]] and whose later enlargement under English sovereignty reveals the gap between treaty possession and durable allegiance.

## Current Profile

The episode treats Aquitaine as both inheritance residue and war threshold. Earlier English kings had once held much larger continental lands under the Angevin system, but by the early 14th century their surviving French position is narrowed to Gascony and Bordeaux.

French pressure on Aquitaine intensifies under [[EdwardIIOfEngland|Edward II]], when French forces invade in 1324 and overrun most of the duchy except Bordeaux. In May 1337, [[PhilipVIOfFrance|Philip VI]] declares the confiscation of the duchy, a move the hosts treat as effectively a declaration of war against [[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]].

After Poitiers and Edward's failed 1359-1360 conquest campaign, the [[TreatyOfBretigny|Treaty of Brétigny]] greatly enlarges Aquitaine and is intended to free it from French overlordship. [[EdwardTheBlackPrince|The Black Prince]] governs it through a magnificent but costly court in territory damaged by war and free companies.

Heavy taxation and the prince's Castilian debts weaken loyalty. Aquitanian subjects appeal to [[CharlesVOfFrance|Charles V]], and the prince rejects a French summons because he claims sovereign status. This unresolved appellate conflict gives France a route back into the duchy and helps turn Aquitaine from England's treaty prize into the center of its territorial collapse.

## Key Characteristics

- Larger duchy behind the early English-held Gascony remnant.
- Symbol of England's diminished but persistent continental position.
- Exposed to French investigation, invasion, homage demands, and forfeiture threats.
- Invaded by France in 1324 during Edward II's weakness.
- Confiscated by Philip VI in 1337.
- Greatly enlarged as a sovereign English possession under Brétigny.
- Destabilized by war damage, free companies, taxation, appeals, and renewed sovereignty conflict.
