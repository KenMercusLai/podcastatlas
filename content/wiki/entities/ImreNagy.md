# Imre Nagy

## Overview

Imre Nagy was a Hungarian communist reformer whose New Course made him the principal alternative to Rákosi and whose return as prime minister during the 1956 uprising placed a cautious party reformer behind a movement already demanding democracy and national independence.

## Current Profile

[[719-revolution-in-the-soviet-empire-the-hungarian-uprising-part-1-glt4398850202]] presents Nagy as an unlikely revolutionary symbol. Born to a poor rural family, he became a communist in Russian captivity during the First World War, served in the Red Army, worked underground, and specialized in agricultural policy in Moscow. The episode also reports that he probably informed for Stalin's secret police, keeping his later reform image inside a long history of loyalty to communism.

After Stalin's death, Moscow made Nagy prime minister while [[MatyasRakosi|Rákosi]] retained party leadership. Nagy's New Course reduced the heavy-industry drive, released prisoners, and allowed peasants to leave collectives, but the Kremlin's 1955 reversal removed him from office and the party. His later readmission and public visibility made him a rallying point for reformers.

On 23 October, Nagy disappointed demonstrators by addressing them as comrades and urging negotiation and dispersal. He returned as prime minister after shooting and armed resistance had begun, appealed for order and disarmament, and inherited a crisis whose popular program had already outrun controlled party reform.

## Key Characteristics

- Combined deep communist loyalty with opposition to Rákosi's Stalinist economic and coercive program.
- Advanced a limited New Course centered on economic rebalancing, prisoner release, and reduced collectivization pressure.
- Depended on changing Kremlin preferences because party leadership remained in Rákosi's hands.
- Became a public symbol of possible reform after removal and later rehabilitation.
- Responded cautiously when mass demands moved beyond intra-party correction.
- Acquired formal authority only after violence and state breakdown had begun.

## Qualifications

The source stops before Nagy's later decisions and fate and should not be treated as a complete political biography. His alleged secret-police informing, private intentions, degree of independence, reasons for hesitation, and ability to control events remain secondary-source interpretations. His limited reform should not be retrospectively equated with the full student and street program.

## What Changed

- Established Nagy's path from loyal communist and Moscow exile to constrained reform premier.
- Distinguished his New Course from the broader democratic and national program of October 1956.
- Left his later revolutionary leadership and fate open pending the continuation.
