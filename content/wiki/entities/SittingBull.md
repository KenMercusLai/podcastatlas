# Sitting Bull

## Overview

Sitting Bull was a [[LakotaPeople|Lakota]] leader whose return from exile, forced accommodation to reservation life, association with the [[GhostDance]], killing during an agency-police arrest, and commercialized afterlife make him a central symbol of resistance and dispossession.

## Current Profile

After Little Bighorn, Sitting Bull fled to Canada, returned, was held at Fort Randall, and reached Standing Rock in 1883. He found bison-based life replaced by railroad and settler expansion, ration dependence, divided reservations, schools, police, and [[JamesMcLaughlin]]'s paternal authority. Urban exhibitions and Buffalo Bill's Wild West show offered travel and income but also made him a spectacle for audiences primed to see Custer's enemy.

The sources place him between symbolic authority and declining local power. He showed interest in and presided over Ghost Dances but is not established as [[Wovoka]]'s prophet or commander of an uprising. Press and officials nevertheless made his fame evidence of leadership. His arrest became lethal through McLaughlin's control struggle, agency-police divisions, family pressure, older feuds, and crowd confrontation; afterward his body, cabin, horse story, and public image fed [[FrontierViolenceSpectacle]].

## Key Characteristics

- He remained a powerful symbol of Lakota independence while his immediate authority was constrained and contested.
- Reservation dependency and McLaughlin's governance made his dispossession personal, political, and spiritual.
- His public appearances mixed agency control, income, curiosity, self-presentation, and humiliation.
- He engaged with the Ghost Dance without being established as its prophet or military commander.
- His fame made him a convenient target for [[SecurityCrisisAmplification]].
- His death emerged from an intimate arrest crisis rather than a planned battlefield encounter.
- His image and material remains were commercialized before and after death.

## Qualifications

- Both sources are narrative syntheses rather than full documentary comparisons of disputed details.
- McLaughlin's negative character judgment and the sources' claims about Sitting Bull's motives remain perspective-bound.
- Sitting Bull's participation in performance should not be reduced to either free entrepreneurial choice or total passivity.
- The dancing-horse story and some details of the arrest remain uncertain.
- His symbolic role should not erase political diversity among Lakota people.

## What Changed

- Backfilled his return, reservation subordination, and conflict with McLaughlin before the fatal arrest.
- Added public performance as both livelihood and pre-death commercialization.
- Clarified how press and political incentives converted fame into unsupported uprising leadership.
- Deepened the distinction between Ghost Dance participation and command.

## Relationships

- [[LakotaPeople]] - people and political community within which he exercised and contested authority.
- [[GhostDance]] - movement with which he associated during the final reservation crisis.
- [[Wovoka]] - religious teacher whose message reached the Lakota without making Sitting Bull its commander.
- [[JamesMcLaughlin]] - Standing Rock agent and central institutional antagonist.
- [[CoerciveReservationAssimilation]] - system that reduced his autonomy and reordered Lakota authority.
- [[SecurityCrisisAmplification]] - process that cast him as the mastermind of an uprising.
- [[WoundedKneeMassacre]] - subsequent atrocity involving followers who fled after his death.
- [[FrontierViolenceSpectacle]] - commercialization of his image, performances, cabin, and death.
