# Pompeii

## Overview

Pompeii is the ancient city south of [[MountVesuvius]] known through its long pre-Roman and Roman development, its destruction in the [[EruptionOfVesuvius79|79 CE eruption]], and the changing archaeological reconstruction of its buildings, people, labor, food, inscriptions, and streets.

## Current Profile

Pompeii's destruction unfolded over time. Darkness and wind-driven pumice obstructed streets, overloaded roofs, and made both flight and shelter dangerous, yet the city initially escaped the surge that destroyed [[Herculaneum]]. A temporary easing near dawn created movement toward the gates before the eruption column's final collapse sent a lethal flow across the city.

The earlier episode widens this disaster profile into a [[PompeiiArchaeologicalPalimpsest|layered urban history]]. Pompeii had Oscan and Greek connections before Roman colonization, retained siege and rebuilding traces, and depended on commerce, food service, sanitation, house alteration, and enslaved labor. About one third remained unexcavated in that account, and the visible tourist site is shaped by conservation, access, excavation history, and public appetite. Bodies, graffiti, seasonal remains, and inscriptions can recover everyday lives while also generating stories that later analysis revises.

## Key Characteristics

- Layered city whose pre-Roman, colonial, siege, rebuilding, and destruction phases resist a frozen-time-capsule interpretation.
- Urban community continuing ordinary milling, harvest, wine production, commerce, and worship amid poorly understood warnings.
- City first endangered by darkness, pumice accumulation, blocked routes, and roof collapse.
- Site of a temporary escape window before a later fatal pyroclastic flow.
- Archaeological record preserving differentiated people, possessions, food, inscriptions, animals, buildings, and volcanic deposits.
- Managed heritage site where excavation, conservation, tourism, display, and new technology continually reshape interpretation.

## Evidence

- Pre-disaster continuity: [[355-roman-apocalypse-pompeii-79-ad-glt6664993493]] describes rebuilding, food preparation, harvest, wine making, and business before the eruption.
- Pumice phase: [[355-roman-apocalypse-pompeii-79-ad-glt6664993493]] links darkness, street obstruction, roof loading, and failed sea escape to the city's first crisis.
- Final movement and death: [[355-roman-apocalypse-pompeii-79-ad-glt6664993493]] describes survivors moving toward gates before the later flow.
- Material traces: [[355-roman-apocalypse-pompeii-79-ad-glt6664993493]] cites victims, a doctor's instruments, sacred objects, household possessions, shackles, and the dog cast.
- Long city and ordinary systems: [[16-pompeii-glt3349011345]] connects earlier settlement, Roman colonization, siege marks, bars, food, sanitation, graffiti, property, and enslaved labor.
- Interpretation and conservation: [[16-pompeii-glt3349011345]] describes an unexcavated third, rotating access, conservation-led excavation, cast reanalysis, and the cumulative inscriptional recovery of [[GaiusAlleiusNigidiusMaius]].

## Qualifications

The sources synthesize archaeology, Plinian testimony, and volcanology; not every named person's identity, relationship, movement, belief, or motive is directly recoverable. Victim totals, excavation extent, flow timing, exact depth, sea conditions, food distribution, religious presence, and individual finds remain source-scoped. The palimpsest model corrects the time-capsule cliché without denying the exceptional concentration of evidence at the destruction horizon.

## What Changed

- Expanded the city from a disaster sequence into a layered urban, labor, conservation, tourism, and interpretation profile.
- Added inscriptions, food service, sanitation, enslaved labor, and revisable body identification as evidence for everyday life.
