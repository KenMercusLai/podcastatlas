# Richard I of England

## Overview

Richard I appears as a celebrated warrior whose crusading and literary afterlife preserved heroic glamour despite prolonged absence from England, costly warfare, captivity, ransom, and weak practical service to his kingdom.

## Current Profile

[[123-world-cup-of-kings-and-queens-part-1-glt3062961015]] deliberately separates Richard the romantic warrior from Richard the working king. His role in the Third Crusade, rivalry with Philip Augustus, military reputation, and later Robin Hood setting made “the Lionheart” unusually durable in popular culture.

Against that glamour, the episode stresses that Richard spent only a small portion of his ten-year reign in England. Crusading, continental war, captivity under Leopold of Austria, and the ransom imposed on his subjects made his kingship expensive and remote. His reputation therefore demonstrates how heroic memory can survive a poor institutional verdict.

## Key Characteristics

- Charismatic warrior king with an exceptionally durable heroic image.
- Major participant in the Third Crusade and rival of Philip Augustus.
- Spent little of his reign physically present in England.
- Prioritized crusading and continental warfare over routine English government.
- Captivity and ransom imposed substantial costs on his subjects.
- Robin Hood-era memory preserves romance beyond the practical record emphasized by the source.

## Evidence

- Warrior reputation - [[123-world-cup-of-kings-and-queens-part-1-glt3062961015]] connects Richard's glamour to crusade, warfare, and the Lionheart image.
- Absence from England - [[123-world-cup-of-kings-and-queens-part-1-glt3062961015]] contrasts a ten-year reign with only a short period spent in the kingdom.
- Fiscal burden - [[123-world-cup-of-kings-and-queens-part-1-glt3062961015]] links war, capture by Leopold of Austria, and ransom to extraction from Richard's subjects.
- Cultural afterlife - [[123-world-cup-of-kings-and-queens-part-1-glt3062961015]] treats the Robin Hood setting as a major reason romantic reputation outlived practical criticism.

## Qualifications

This source offers a provocative comparative sketch rather than a full study of Richard's administration, Angevin lands, diplomacy, military conduct, or the Third Crusade. Time in England is not the only possible measure of medieval kingship, and the episode's “worst king” framing is an argumentative judgment. Cultural durability does not itself prove contemporary popularity or administrative failure.

## What Changed

- Established a first profile separating crusading charisma and cultural memory from presence, cost, and practical kingship.
