# George III

## Overview

George III appears as the British king whose symbolic hold on colonial loyalty survived deep constitutional conflict before he declared all thirteen colonies in open rebellion.

## Current Profile

Franklin's admiration for George III establishes how real colonial imperial identity could be before rupture. The Boston Tea Party episode adds the king's political hardening after the destruction of tea and his August 1775 declaration that all thirteen colonies were in open rebellion. The combined profile therefore moves from respected monarch to the royal pole of a collapsing allegiance, while preserving the fact that the Second Continental Congress still professed loyalty to him after fighting began.

## Key Characteristics

- British monarch associated with Franklin's pre-revolutionary loyalty.
- Figure Franklin admired before turning against British rule.
- Symbolic reference point for [[ColonialLoyaltyRupture|colonial loyalty rupture]].
- Monarch whose position hardened after the Boston Tea Party.
- Declared all thirteen colonies in open rebellion while some colonial leaders still claimed royal loyalty.

## Qualifications

This page is not a full George III biography or constitutional allocation of responsibility between crown and ministers. It records the king's function as both the object of former colonial loyalty and the monarch associated with the final political hardening of 1775.
