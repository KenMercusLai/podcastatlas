# Jay Van Bavel

## Overview
Jay Van Bavel is the social psychologist represented in a [[HiddenBrain]] episode through research and examples connecting [[GroupIdentity]] to preference, perception, reward, polarization, and cooperation.

## Current Profile
Van Bavel's central claim is that identity is not merely a declared affiliation added after perception. A salient group can change which products feel attractive, which evidence receives attention, how distance or threat is estimated, and whose success feels rewarding. Minimal-group studies matter because they show that loyalty does not require a deep prior conflict, while sports, politics, policing, and COVID-19 illustrate how higher-stakes identities can organize sincere but divergent interpretations.

His practical position is neither to abolish groups nor to assume that information alone dissolves them. Group boundaries are flexible: leaders can activate a wider identity, admired members can alter norms, representation can change institutional behavior, and cooperative success can turn outsiders into teammates. These interventions remain conditional on history, incentives, power, and credible shared purpose.

## Key Characteristics
- Treats group identity as a context-sensitive influence on attention, valuation, perception, and behavior.
- Uses minimal-group evidence to show how little categorical structure may be needed for favoritism.
- Separates sincere identity-shaped interpretation from conscious fabrication.
- Connects partisan identity and social-media feedback to out-group hostility and distorted risk judgment.
- Emphasizes the flexibility of group boundaries rather than treating tribalism as fixed destiny.
- Proposes shared goals, inclusive leadership, representation, and valued cross-group membership as routes toward cooperation.

## Qualifications
This profile is bounded to one structured episode summary rather than Van Bavel's full scholarship, book, datasets, or later views. The source does not supply methods, samples, effect sizes, replication histories, or rival explanations for the studies it mentions. Laboratory favoritism does not establish inevitable discrimination, brain response is not a moral diagnosis, and symbolic or interpersonal inclusion cannot substitute for institutional reform or material justice.
