# Athens / 雅典

## Overview

Athens / 雅典 is represented in the wiki through two crisis moments: the 490 BC [[BattleOfMarathon|Battle of Marathon]], where the city survives Persian invasion and possible tyrant restoration, and the 86 BC Mithridatic revolt, where the city is broken by [[LuciusCorneliusSulla]] during Rome's civil-war crisis.

## Current Profile

The Marathon source presents Athens as a city whose future is genuinely endangered. After supporting the Ionian revolt, Athens faces a punitive [[AchaemenidEmpire|Persian]] expedition backed by [[HippiasAthenianTyrant]], the exiled former tyrant. [[Miltiades]] leads the Athenians to block the road from Marathon to Athens, and the city's survival depends on both the battlefield victory and the exhausted army's rapid return before the Persian fleet can exploit possible betrayal.

That source makes Marathon central to Athens' later self-understanding but also keeps the memory qualified. The battle may have preserved [[AthenianDemocracy]] and the city's later cultural importance, yet the episode resists a simple freedom-versus-despotism frame because Athens later becomes an empire and the Persian perspective is only indirectly recoverable through Greek evidence.

The later Roman source presents Athens as formally outside direct Roman provincial rule but already inside Rome's sphere of influence. Pro-Roman oligarchs benefit from Roman favor, while broader Athenian resentment is stirred by [[MithridatesVI]]'s successes in Asia and his claim to punish Roman greed. Aristion's revolution massacres or expels plutocrats and brings in Mithridatic support, but Sulla's arrival traps the city. Athens starves, diplomatic appeals to ancient glory fail, and Roman troops sack the city in March 86 BC. Sulla spares the city itself out of respect for its dead, yet Piraeus is flattened, democracy effectively ends, pro-Roman businessmen return, and later humiliation includes the removal of temple columns and libraries.

## Key Characteristics

- Athens is presented at Marathon as a city whose survival, democracy, and later cultural influence are still contingent.
- The 490 BC threat is both external invasion and internal political fracture through Hippias and possible betrayal.
- Marathon becomes a civic foundation story, but the source qualifies later claims that it was simply freedom defeating despotism.
- In the Sullan source, Athens is culturally prestigious but politically weakened under Roman shadow.
- Internal class and collaboration resentment make Athens receptive to Mithridates's anti-Roman campaign.
- Sulla's siege breaks Athens through starvation, sack, and restoration of pro-Roman interests.
- The Roman source treats 86 BC as the real end of Athens as a meaningful independent political power.

## Evidence

- Marathon survival: [[669-greece-vs-persia-the-battle-of-marathon-part-2-glt2696648260]] says defeat could have meant Athens' destruction, enslavement, or exile, while Hippias and possible betrayal made the danger political as well as military.
- Road-blocking strategy: [[669-greece-vs-persia-the-battle-of-marathon-part-2-glt2696648260]] says Miltiades marched the Athenians to block the road from Marathon to Athens, then returned rapidly after victory to deter the Persian fleet at Phalerum.
- Democratic and memory meaning: [[669-greece-vs-persia-the-battle-of-marathon-part-2-glt2696648260]] connects Marathon to Athens' later democracy and cultural influence while qualifying the freedom-versus-despotism story through Persian perspective and later Athenian imperialism.
- Roman shadow: [[703-roman-civil-war-marching-on-the-senate-part-3-glt3125075511]] says Greece is not formally part of Rome's empire, but Athens is under Roman influence.
- Oligarchic resentment: [[703-roman-civil-war-marching-on-the-senate-part-3-glt3125075511]] says pro-Roman oligarchs benefit from Roman favor while the mass of Athenians resent wealthy collaborators.
- Revolt and alliance: [[703-roman-civil-war-marching-on-the-senate-part-3-glt3125075511]] says Aristion's revolution asks Mithridates for alliance and receives an expeditionary force.
- Siege and sack: [[703-roman-civil-war-marching-on-the-senate-part-3-glt3125075511]] describes starvation, Sulla's rejection of antiquarian appeals, and the March 86 BC sack.
- Political aftermath: [[703-roman-civil-war-marching-on-the-senate-part-3-glt3125075511]] says Piraeus is flattened, Athens loses remaining independence, democracy is effectively abandoned, and pro-Roman businessmen are restored.

## Qualifications

The current page remains source-scoped to two crisis episodes. It does not synthesize all classical Athens, the philosophical schools, Athenian empire, drama, architecture, or later Roman-era cultural life. The Marathon counterfactual and the 86 BC "end of independence" claim are preserved as the respective episodes' interpretations.
