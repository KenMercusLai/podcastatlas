# Albert Kesselring

## Overview

Albert Kesselring was the German field marshal directing the defense of Italy during the opening of the [[ItalianCampaign1943|Italian campaign of 1943]].

## Current Profile

The source presents Kesselring through [[JamesHolland]]'s revisionist criticism. His concentration around Salerno threatened the main Allied landing but left southeastern Italy weak enough for the British to enter Taranto, Bari, and Brindisi and for the Allies to secure Foggia. After Salerno, he withdrew to the Volturno but continued defending south of Rome.

Holland treats this as over-optimism rather than defensive genius, arguing that a withdrawal toward the Pisa-Rimini line would have made more strategic sense after ports and airfields were lost. The profile is deliberately bounded because the interview does not supply a sustained defense of Kesselring's choices.

## Key Characteristics

- Concentrated German strength against the Salerno landing.
- Failed to prevent the Allied acquisition of southeastern ports and Foggia.
- Continued a forward defense south of Rome after the beachhead survived.
- Is portrayed as optimistic about holding all of Italy.
- Was a Luftwaffe field marshal exercising ground command, a background the source says contributed to friction with other German commanders.

## Evidence

- Salerno concentration: [[399-the-savage-storm-world-war-ii-and-the-battle-for-italy-glt8130436942]] credits German pressure with bringing the landing close to crisis while also linking that concentration to weakness in the southeast.
- Withdrawal judgment: [[399-the-savage-storm-world-war-ii-and-the-battle-for-italy-glt8130436942]] says Kesselring retreated to the Volturno after Salerno but rejected the deeper line associated with Rommel's recommendation.
- Reputation challenge: [[399-the-savage-storm-world-war-ii-and-the-battle-for-italy-glt8130436942]] records Holland explicitly disputing a favorable view of Kesselring and calling his Italian strategy over-optimistic.

## Qualifications

The current profile reflects one historian's argument in one interview. The source does not present Kesselring's own operational case in depth, and its counterfactual judgment about a Pisa-Rimini withdrawal remains contested and source-scoped.
