# Algeria

## Overview

Algeria is a North African country represented in the wiki through two distinct branches: contemporary rivalry and diplomacy around [[WesternSahara]] and [[Ceuta]], and the colonial, family, and literary conflict shaping [[AlbertCamus]].

## Current Profile

Contemporary Algeria appears as [[Morocco]]'s long-running rival in the [[WesternSahara]] dispute. [[MichaelReid]] says [[PedroSanchez]]'s shift from backing a UN referendum toward Morocco's autonomy plan angered Algeria and created political backlash inside [[Spain]]. Spain's later attempt to restore Algerian relations may then have irritated Morocco, making the Ceuta crossing part of triangular diplomacy rather than border management alone.

The historical-literary branch turns on Camus's pied-noir background. Algeria is his birthplace, the setting of much of his writing, and the unresolved field in which anti-colonial justice, settler-family belonging, concern for his mother, French intellectual judgment, and the Algerian War collided. The episode does not supply a general history of colonization or independence; it shows how Algeria prevented Camus's moral and political identity from becoming internally tidy.

## Key Characteristics

- North African state represented in current diplomacy through rivalry with Morocco over Western Sahara.
- Actor affected by Spain's shift toward Morocco's autonomy plan and later attempt at rapprochement.
- Colonial and family homeland central to Albert Camus's biography and literary imagination.
- Setting where abstract justice and immediate settler-family loyalty became politically conflicting commitments.
- Country whose distinct diplomatic and colonial-history branches should not be collapsed into one explanation.

## Qualifications

Neither source provides a general political or social history of Algeria. The contemporary source is centered on Spanish and Moroccan policy, while the Camus episode approaches French colonial Algeria mainly through one settler writer's biography. Algerian anti-colonial actors, violence, independence politics, and postcolonial state formation remain largely outside the supplied evidence. The episode's description of Camus as neutral is therefore preserved as an attributed profile, not adopted as a sufficient history of the war.
