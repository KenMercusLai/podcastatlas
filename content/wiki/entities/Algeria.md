# Algeria

## Overview

Algeria is a North African country represented through violent settler-colonial decolonization, named testimony about French torture, the family and literary conflict shaping [[AlbertCamus]], and contemporary rivalry around [[WesternSahara]] and [[Ceuta]].

## Current Profile

Contemporary Algeria appears as [[Morocco]]'s long-running rival in the [[WesternSahara]] dispute. [[MichaelReid]] says [[PedroSanchez]]'s shift from backing a UN referendum toward Morocco's autonomy plan angered Algeria and created political backlash inside [[Spain]]. Spain's later attempt to restore Algerian relations may then have irritated Morocco, making the Ceuta crossing part of triangular diplomacy rather than border management alone.

The decolonization source gives that literary conflict a wider political frame. Algeria's large European settler population, legal integration into France, collective punishment, segregation, torture, and FLN attacks on European civilians made the independence war a cycle in which colonial and anti-colonial violence escalated together. [[FrantzFanon]] interpreted colonialism as structurally violent and linked Algerian liberation to a continental struggle. The Camus source then shows how the same war divided anti-colonial justice from pied-noir family belonging and French intellectual judgment.

Named testimony gives that structural history an individual scale. French reservist [[StanislasHutin|Stanislas Hutin]] encountered 14-year-old shepherd [[SaidBoutout|Saïd Boutout]] being electrically tortured, protested, preserved testimony and photographs, and later spoke in schools. The case makes French military abuse concrete while preserving the boundary between what Hutin saw, what fellow soldiers told him, and what broader histories must establish.

## Key Characteristics

- North African state whose rivalry with Morocco over Western Sahara shapes Spain's policy and later rapprochement attempts.
- Settler-colonial territory whose legal incorporation into France intensified resistance to independence.
- War setting in which state repression and FLN attacks on civilians escalated together.
- Setting of named French military torture testimony preserved by Stanislas Hutin.
- Political field central to Frantz Fanon's theory of colonial and revolutionary violence.
- Colonial and family homeland central to Albert Camus's biography and literary imagination.
- Setting where abstract justice and immediate settler-family loyalty became politically conflicting commitments.

## Qualifications

The sources still do not provide a general political or social history of Algeria. The decolonization episode is a brief comparative survey that compresses the FLN, French institutions, civilian experience, and independence outcome; the Camus episode approaches the war through one settler writer; the Hutin segment centers one witness and named victim; and the contemporary source centers Spanish-Moroccan diplomacy. The new testimony does not identify every perpetrator or command chain, and fellow-soldier reports remain distinct from what Hutin personally witnessed. Fanon's violence thesis and the description of Camus as neutral remain attributed interpretations rather than settled judgments.
