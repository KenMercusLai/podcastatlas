# Charles I of England

## Overview

Charles I of England is the executed monarch whose judges reversed the inherited logic of treason by accusing the king of levying war against the people, even though his death in January 1649 preceded agreement on what sovereignty would replace him and helped create a royalist martyr tradition.

## Current Profile

The episode uses Charles chiefly as the constitutional absence around which the Republic formed. The Civil War had concerned the form of monarchy and church government; an army-backed purge and accelerated trial converted that conflict into regicide without a prior settled republican plan.

His execution made restoration of the old order harder but did not produce agreement on sovereignty. The comparison with Cromwell also becomes morally and institutionally important: the republic's leader later dismissed MPs, expanded executive power, controlled worship, and taxed in ways the episode presents as exceeding Charles's own practices.

The treason episode makes the jurisdictional conflict explicit. The Rump Commons empowered a special court, claimed sovereignty on behalf of the people, and treated Charles's war-making as treason; Charles refused to recognize the tribunal or enter a plea. The surviving trial volumes were compiled after the event from clerks' notes, so they are politically important but disputed records of a proceeding whose verdict the source describes as largely predetermined.

The trial episode adds Charles's active self-presentation. His covered head, refusal to plead, invocation of the people's liberties and ancient constitution, scaffold composure, and religious final language asserted lawful kingship against the court's authority. These actions did not prevent execution, but they helped *Eikon Basilike*, blood relics, public shock, and European reaction turn defeat into a posthumous royalist victory whose memory coexisted with republican celebration.

## Key Characteristics

- Stuart monarch executed after civil war and an army-backed political rupture.
- Death preceded a clear decision about the future of monarchy.
- Became the negative constitutional reference point for republican legitimacy.
- Removal did not resolve the division between parliamentary and military authority.
- Refused to recognize or plead before the court that tried him.
- Became the decisive test of whether sovereignty lay in the monarch or a Commons claiming to represent the people.
- Used courtroom and scaffold conduct to defend royal authority and enable a durable martyr afterlife.

## Qualifications

This remains a source-bounded profile rather than a full account of Charles's reign, Civil War responsibility, theology, or all strands of his posthumous memory. Calling the trial political theatre does not eliminate the constitutional seriousness of its rival sovereignty claims. The legal basis, command responsibility, trial record, signer coercion, final words, *Eikon Basilike* authorship and reception, crowd mood, and possible alternatives to execution remain disputed or source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Added Charles's courtroom and scaffold self-presentation as a political strategy, not only a refusal of jurisdiction.
- Added the immediate royalist martyrdom and republican-memory contest created by his execution.
- Qualified the proceeding as legally improvised without reducing it to empty spectacle.
