# Dido / Elissa

## Overview

Dido, also called Elissa, is the legendary Tyrian princess whose flight from [[Tyre]] and foundation of [[Carthage]] encode the city's remembered eastern origin, and whose transformation by [[Virgil]] makes her a central tragic witness to Roman destiny.

## Current Profile

In the foundation narrative, Dido flees after her brother Pygmalion kills her wealthy husband, Acherbas. She deceives Pygmalion about the treasure, escapes with supporters and wealth, travels through Cyprus, and establishes Carthage in North Africa. The oxhide bargain, in which she cuts a hide into strips to enclose the Byrsa hill, makes resourcefulness, exile, dynastic violence, and Tyrian continuity parts of Carthage's origin story.

The older or parallel tradition makes Dido a model of fidelity: she kills herself rather than marry the local ruler Iarbas and betray her murdered husband's memory. The [[Aeneid]] gives that death a different cause. Dido welcomes [[Aeneas]], loves him, and dies after divine command sends him onward toward Rome; her curse then makes private abandonment a mythical prehistory of Hannibal and the Punic conflict. Virgil's change is politically useful but not emotionally simple, because Dido remains a competent, generous, and grievously harmed queen.

Both source notes treat the narratives as legend rather than secure biography. Phoenician names and the durable parent-city relationship may preserve older Tyrian elements, while possible founder imagery and ritual parallels add context without proving individual events. Dido's importance is therefore double: she personifies Carthage's eastern origin and shows how later literature can recode an inherited founder into a witness against the human cost of empire.

## Key Characteristics

- Legendary Tyrian princess also known as Elissa.
- Exile whose story begins with dynastic murder and threatened wealth.
- Cunning founder who preserves resources through deception, escape, and the oxhide bargain.
- Personification of Carthage's remembered kinship with Tyre.
- Loyal widow in one tradition and abandoned lover of Aeneas in Virgil's transformation.
- Tragic figure whose cultural and political importance exceeds what can be established biographically.

## Qualifications

Dido is not treated as a securely recoverable historical individual. Her chronology conflicts with Aeneas's literary setting, and the murder, deception, journey, Cypriot episode, oxhide bargain, Iarbas proposal, deaths, dialogue, motives, name etymology, coin identification, ritual parallels, and foundation dates remain legendary, contested, or source-scoped. The Cleopatra comparison is suggestive rather than an established model.

## What Changed

- Distinguished the loyal-widow founder tradition from Virgil's abandoned queen.
- Added the oxhide bargain and fidelity-based death as major elements of the older foundation memory.
- Reframed Dido as a sympathetic measure of Roman destiny's human cost without treating the Aeneid as biography.
