# Wars of the Roses / 玫瑰战争

## Overview

The Wars of the Roses / 玫瑰战争 is the English dynastic conflict whose disputed succession, weak kingship, magnate power, military violence, and factional politics run from [[RichardOfYork|Richard of York's]] challenge through the 1483 princes-in-the-Tower crisis and inform the political texture of *A Song of Ice and Fire*.

## Current Profile

The Wakefield episode opens the conflict's earlier Yorkist phase. English defeat in France returned experienced fighters and displaced ambitions to a polity weakened by Henry VI's incapacity. Richard's descent from Edward III, protectorship, and administrative reputation made his claim plausible, but the sacred standing of an anointed king limited acceptance. The compromise that preserved Henry while disinheriting his son did not resolve the succession; it sharpened Lancastrian resistance and ended with Richard and Edmund dead at the [[BattleOfWakefield]].

The fantasy source treats the conflict as Martin's strongest political analogy while rejecting direct character-to-prototype mapping. The princes sequence sharpens the later Yorkist cycle: Edward IV's usurpation, Woodville marriage, patronage redistribution, Warwick and Clarence's rebellion, French and Burgundian involvement, exile, restoration, and Lancastrian destruction produced apparent security without resolving factional dependence. Part 2 then shows how a clear child succession collapsed through contested custody, council recognition, coronation, sanctuary, force, and legitimacy claims.

## Key Characteristics

- Dynastic struggle in which hereditary title depended on faction, military power, council recognition, marriage, ceremony, and the continuing authority of an anointed incumbent.
- Conflict whose domestic alignments interacted with French and Burgundian rivalry and support.
- Political order in which weak kingship, magnate military networks, marriage, and patronage could provoke regime-threatening resistance.
- Political environment where protection of a child king could become control and replacement.
- Source for the strongest broad historical analogy behind *A Song of Ice and Fire*'s noble-family conflict.
- Conflict in which Wakefield's public humiliation of a dead claimant and the later disappearance of the princes made bodies and memory instruments of legitimacy contest.
- Cautionary case against reducing layered history or fantasy to one-to-one prototypes.

## Qualifications

These sources cover a literary analogy, one compressed opening-phase episode, and two concentrated late episodes, not the full chronology, regional experience, military history, or social history of the wars. The relative weight of French defeat, Henry VI's incapacity, hereditary argument, magnate ambition, Edward's marriage, Warwick's motives, and international sponsorship remains interpretive. Richard's reason for leaving Sandal Castle is unresolved, Shakespeare is evidence for later memory rather than the battle itself, the fantasy comparison is structural and layered, and the princes' fate remains probabilistic rather than conclusively proved.

## What Changed

- Extended the profile backward to Henry VI's incapacity, Richard of York's protectorship and claim, and the disputed succession compromise.
- Added Wakefield as a military and symbolic rupture without claiming to resolve Richard's unexplained sortie.
