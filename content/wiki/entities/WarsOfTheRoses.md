# Wars of the Roses / 玫瑰战争

## Overview

The Wars of the Roses / 玫瑰战争 is the English dynastic conflict whose factional succession politics inform both the 1483 princes-in-the-Tower crisis and the political texture of *A Song of Ice and Fire*.

## Current Profile

The fantasy source treats the conflict as Martin's strongest political analogy while rejecting direct character-to-prototype mapping. Part 1 sharpens the preceding Yorkist cycle: Edward IV's usurpation, Woodville marriage, patronage redistribution, Warwick and Clarence's rebellion, French and Burgundian involvement, exile, restoration, and Lancastrian destruction produced apparent security without resolving factional dependence. Part 2 then shows how a clear child succession collapsed through contested custody, council recognition, coronation, sanctuary, force, and legitimacy claims.

## Key Characteristics

- Dynastic struggle in which hereditary title depended on faction, military power, council recognition, marriage, and ceremony.
- Conflict whose domestic alignments interacted with French and Burgundian rivalry and support.
- Political order in which marriage and patronage could rebalance magnate power and provoke regime-threatening resistance.
- Political environment where protection of a child king could become control and replacement.
- Source for the strongest broad historical analogy behind *A Song of Ice and Fire*'s noble-family conflict.
- Conflict whose late phase converted the missing princes into both a legitimacy crisis and a lasting historical mystery.
- Cautionary case against reducing layered history or fantasy to one-to-one prototypes.

## Evidence

- Fantasy-source layering: [[31-bing-yu-huo-zhi-ge-suiran-cong-bu-zicheng-lishi-594509848]] connects Lancaster and York, Henry VI, Margaret of Anjou, Richard of York, Edward IV, Warwick, the princes, and the Tudor settlement to Martin's recombined political world.
- Yorkist fracture and restoration: [[138-the-princes-in-the-tower-part-1-glt6274021473]] follows the Woodville marriage, Warwick-Clarence revolt, international alignments, Barnet, Tewkesbury, Henry VI's death, and the formation of Edward V's household.
- 1483 succession collapse: [[139-the-princes-in-the-tower-part-2-glt4685691538]] traces Edward IV's death, competing affinity control, Edward V's seizure, Hastings's execution, delegitimation, Richard III's coronation, and the princes' disappearance.
- Late conflict and settlement pressure: [[139-the-princes-in-the-tower-part-2-glt4685691538]] follows rescue rebellion, Buckingham's defection, and the movement of opposition toward Henry Tudor once the princes were assumed dead.

## Qualifications

These sources cover a literary analogy and two concentrated late episodes, not the full chronology, regional experience, military history, or social history of the wars. The causal weight of Edward's marriage, Warwick's motives, and international sponsorship remains interpretive. The fantasy comparison is structural and layered, while the princes' fate remains probabilistic rather than conclusively proved.
