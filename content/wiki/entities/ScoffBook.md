# Scoff: A History of Food and Class in Britain

## Overview

*Scoff: A History of Food and Class in Britain* is a book by [[PenVogler]] introduced as the interpretive basis for a conversation about how food, taste, vocabulary, and culinary reputation encode British class history.

## Current Profile

The covered episode presents *Scoff* through examples rather than a chapter-by-chapter account. Its implied argument is that British cuisine cannot be assessed only as a collection of dishes: land access, agricultural improvement, industrialization, domestic equipment, religion, foreign fashion, empire, literature, and the status meanings of meal names all influence what people eat and how that food is judged.

The book's frame also makes reputation historically variable. Postwar austerity can explain a real period of culinary weakness without erasing medieval spices, eighteenth-century abundance, regional foods, preservation practices, or Britain's repeated absorption of imported tastes.

## Key Characteristics

- Uses food as evidence for British class formation and distinction.
- Connects culinary history to agricultural, industrial, religious, and imperial change.
- Treats ordinary vocabulary and meal timing as class-coded evidence.
- Challenges the idea that British food has one timeless quality or character.
- Uses concrete dishes and ingredients to make large social changes legible.

## Evidence

- Book framing: [[49-food-glorious-food-glt7001895639]] introduces Vogler as the author of *Scoff* and organizes the conversation around food and class.
- Historical range: [[49-food-glorious-food-glt7001895639]] moves from medieval spice and Reformation change through eighteenth-century beef, industrialization, postwar rationing, and modern reputation.
- Social evidence: [[49-food-glorious-food-glt7001895639]] uses French menus, dinner/tea/supper vocabulary, ovens, meal composition, and ingredient prestige to connect eating with hierarchy.
- Imported tastes: [[49-food-glorious-food-glt7001895639]] uses curry, turkey, tea, cocoa, coffee, and chocolate to show British cuisine forming through absorption.
