# Thebes

## Overview

Thebes was a major Greek city whose fourth-century military ascendancy influenced Macedonian reform, whose defeat at the [[BattleOfChaeronea]] helped establish Macedonian hegemony, and whose 335 BCE revolt against [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]] ended in massacre, enslavement, and near-total destruction.

## Current Profile

Before that destruction, Thebes had been a center of military innovation and interstate power. [[Epaminondas]]'s victory over Sparta at Leuctra used depth, left-wing concentration, and delayed engagement; [[PhilipIIMacedon|Philip II's]] hostage years in the city exposed him to this political-military environment. Macedon's later army was not a copy, but Theban practice formed part of its inheritance. Philip then defeated the Theban-Athenian coalition at Chaeronea in 338 BCE, destroyed the Sacred Band, and imposed a harsher settlement on Thebes than on Athens.

The later revolt began after a false report that Alexander had died in Illyria. Returning exiles killed Macedonian officers, displaced the imposed oligarchy, besieged the garrison in the Cadmea, and appealed to other Greek states in the language of freedom from Macedonian rule. Athens supplied money but did not commit an army, and the other prospective allies also delayed or abstained.

Alexander covered roughly 250 miles in thirteen days and arrived with overwhelming force before reliable news could prepare the city. He offered safety to those who abandoned the revolt while demanding its leaders; the Thebans rejected a settlement that would have restored Macedonian domination. Ancient accounts then disagree over whether Alexander ordered the first assault or Perdiccas attacked without authorization.

After the defenses failed, Macedonian troops and Greek allies killed, raped, looted, and enslaved inhabitants. A League of Corinth council dominated by Thebes's enemies approved destruction of the city, sale of captives, and a ban on sheltering refugees. The formal vote distributed responsibility, but Alexander could have stopped the outcome and used its shock to suppress resistance elsewhere in Greece.

## Key Characteristics

- Major Greek polity with a recent history of both resistance to and domination over other Greek communities.
- Military center associated with Epaminondas, Leuctra, and practices later adapted inside Philip's wider system.
- Revolted after false intelligence suggested Alexander had died and Macedonian authority was vulnerable.
- Framed resistance as freedom while facing a settlement that required renewed submission.
- Fought largely alone after hoped-for allies withheld decisive military support.
- Became an exemplary target whose destruction communicated the cost of revolt across Greece.
- Retained enough sympathetic memory that communities sheltered refugees and the city was restored after Alexander's death.

## Qualifications

The page is grounded in two structured popular-history summaries drawing on sparse, late, and conflicting ancient accounts. The exact Leuctra formation, Philip's personal relationship with Epaminondas, Chaeronea's tactics, force sizes, casualties, number enslaved, portents, negotiation language, responsibility for the 335 opening attack, degree of command control during the sack, and motives of Alexander and the council remain disputed, estimated, or source-scoped. Calling the revolt a freedom struggle does not erase Thebes's own earlier coercion of other Greek communities; calling it reckless does not make Macedonian rule voluntary.

## What Changed

- Extended the profile backward to Epaminondas, Leuctra, and qualified Theban influence on Macedonian reform.
- Added Chaeronea, the Sacred Band's destruction, and Philip's harsher settlement of Thebes.
- Recast the 335 revolt as a challenge to the order created after Chaeronea rather than an isolated uprising.
