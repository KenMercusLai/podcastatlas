# Thomas Malory

## Overview

Thomas Malory was the fifteenth-century author of [[LeMorteDarthur|Le Morte Darthur]], an influential English synthesis that turns [[KingArthur|King Arthur's]] last battle and [[Lancelot]]'s divided excellence into a tragedy of civil war, betrayal, repentance, and broken fellowship.

## Current Profile

[[713-king-arthur-the-once-future-king-part-1-glt2516166521]] presents the author as probably Sir Thomas Malory of Newbold Revel, while keeping that identification qualified. Malory identifies himself as a prisoner, and the candidate described by the episode was imprisoned under both Lancastrian and Yorkist governments and excluded from several general pardons issued by Edward IV.

Malory completed the work around 1469-1470 amid renewed [[WarsOfTheRoses|Wars of the Roses]] instability. His Mordred uses Parliament, the Tower of London, and artillery, while his final battle condemns fickle allegiance and depicts wounded survivors being murdered and robbed. These anachronisms make the fall of the Round Table a fifteenth-century political meditation rather than a transparent record of early Britain.

Malory's moral center is the divided Lancelot represented in [[714-king-arthur-lancelot-and-the-round-table-part-2-glt6459272632]]. Lancelot is more merciful and courteous than other leading knights, yet his affair with [[Guinevere]] enables exposure, rescue, accidental killing, vengeance, and the war that leaves Mordred free to seize England. Sir Urry's healing and Lancelot's final religious life preserve divine grace without denying causal responsibility.

## Key Characteristics

- Fifteenth-century English author associated, though not conclusively identified, with Sir Thomas Malory of Newbold Revel.
- Prisoner whose political and legal circumstances remain relevant but incompletely resolved.
- Compiler and transformer of earlier Arthurian traditions into a powerful English narrative synthesis.
- Writer who preserves supernatural ambiguity around Arthur's death, healing, and return.
- Political tragedian whose Camelot reflects civil war, institutional seizure, shifting loyalty, and post-battle violence.
- Moral tragedian who keeps Lancelot's courtesy, adultery, responsibility, repentance, and grace active together.

## Evidence

### Authorship and imprisonment

- [[713-king-arthur-the-once-future-king-part-1-glt2516166521]] reports Malory's self-identification as a prisoner and the prevailing association with the Newbold Revel knight, while noting repeated imprisonment and exclusions from pardon.

### Arthurian synthesis

- [[713-king-arthur-the-once-future-king-part-1-glt2516166521]] uses Malory's account of Mordred, Camlann, Excalibur, Avalon, and the return tradition as its narrative center.

### Fifteenth-century political texture

- [[713-king-arthur-the-once-future-king-part-1-glt2516166521]] points to Parliament, the Tower, artillery, unstable loyalty, and violence against survivors as evidence that Malory reframed inherited material through his own age.

### Lancelot's moral contradiction

- [[714-king-arthur-lancelot-and-the-round-table-part-2-glt6459272632]] contrasts Lancelot's mercy and knightly ideals with the affair, Gareth and Gaheris's deaths, Gawain's vengeance, and Camelot's strategic collapse.
- [[714-king-arthur-lancelot-and-the-round-table-part-2-glt6459272632]] uses Sir Urry's healing, Lancelot's tears, Guinevere's renunciation, priesthood, and peaceful death to frame grace as compatible with acknowledged sin.

## Qualifications

The profile is bounded to two popular-history episodes. The authorial identification is strong enough to present as the prevailing view but not as certainty. Attempted murder, theft, extortion, livestock stealing, escape, burglary, and rape appear as accusations in the episode's account, not as facts this wiki independently adjudicates; defenses and legal terminology also remain disputed. Reading Lancelot as Malory's wished-for self and Gawain as his guilty likeness is psychological interpretation, while Wars of the Roses context does not make every narrative choice a coded contemporary event.

## What Changed

- Added Lancelot's inseparable virtue, consequence, repentance, and grace as the work's moral center.
- Made the biographical-crime and authorial-psychology boundaries explicit.
