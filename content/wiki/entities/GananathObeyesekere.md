# Gananath Obeyesekere

## Overview

Gananath Obeyesekere is an anthropologist whose critique of the Cook-as-Lono thesis argues that literal Hawaiian god-identification may reproduce a colonial European image of credulous Indigenous people.

## Current Profile

In the supplied episode, Obeyesekere challenges [[MarshallSahlins|Marshall Sahlins]] by arguing that “Lono” could also designate a ruler or chiefly status and that Europeans projected divinity onto a politically and ritually exceptional welcome. His intervention shifts the question from whether the recorded ceremonies occurred to whether European categories correctly describe what those ceremonies meant.

The episode does not make this critique a complete solution. Cook's timing, route, symbols, and reception remain strikingly aligned with Makahiki, while modern distinctions among ruler, god, human, and divine may also be too rigid. Obeyesekere therefore supplies a guardrail against colonial projection rather than proof that ritual identification played no role.

## Key Characteristics

- Anthropologist associated with the colonial-projection critique of Cook's divinity narrative.
- Emphasizes possible chiefly or political meanings of the term rendered as Lono.
- Central participant in the contested [[CookLonoDebate]].
- Scholar presented through a popular-history summary rather than direct study of his full work.

## Evidence

- Projection critique: [[726-the-death-of-captain-cook-massacre-on-the-beach-part-2-glt2770007115]] attributes to Obeyesekere the argument that literal god-making reflects colonial assumptions and that Lono could function as a ruler's designation.
- Remaining ambiguity: [[726-the-death-of-captain-cook-massacre-on-the-beach-part-2-glt2770007115]] preserves Makahiki correspondences and category-translation problems that prevent the critique from settling one alternative meaning.

## Qualifications

This profile represents Obeyesekere only within one episode's account of a major anthropological dispute. It does not reconstruct his broader scholarship, all evidence behind the “practical rationality” argument, scholarly responses, or Hawaiian interpretations of the debate. Rejecting a simplistic divinity claim should not impose an equally rigid modern separation between ritual and politics.
