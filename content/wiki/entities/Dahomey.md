# Dahomey

## Overview

Dahomey is the West African kingdom whose women warriors, the [[Agojie]], anchor the episode's closing case and the [[TheWomanKing]] controversy.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Dahomey through the tension between military fascination and slavery controversy. Its women warriors begin largely as a king's palace bodyguard because no man was allowed to sleep overnight in the palace, then become a more formal military force under [[Ghezo]]. The source also keeps Dahomey's political economy morally visible: war captives could be kept as serfs or sold to Europeans, and the kingdom's violent demonstrations may have been both real practices and staged spectacle for outsiders.

## Key Characteristics

- West African kingdom whose women warriors are the source's best-known "Amazon" case.
- Palace-centered origin for the [[Agojie]] before formal battlefield role.
- Connected to war captives, serfdom, and sale to Europeans in the episode's account.
- Subject of European fascination, fear, and possibly staged violent spectacle.
- Modern memory filtered through [[TheWomanKing]] and debates about slavery.

## Evidence

- Palace origin: [[317-african-amazons-glt5973082485]] says Dahomey's women warriors began largely as the king's palace bodyguard because no man was allowed to sleep overnight in the palace.
- Formalization: [[317-african-amazons-glt5973082485]] says they became a more formal fighting unit under [[Ghezo]].
- Captives and slavery: [[317-african-amazons-glt5973082485]] says captives taken in war could be kept as serfs or sold to Europeans.
- European descriptions: [[317-african-amazons-glt5973082485]] says European accounts often described the women as braver, better trained, and more effective than male soldiers.
- Spectacle caution: [[317-african-amazons-glt5973082485]] says some violent demonstrations may have been staged to impress or intimidate European observers.
