## Overview

徐乐 / Xu Le is presented as a Western Han memorialist whose “土崩／瓦解” distinction separates bottom-up collapse of the social base from a narrower elite or regional rebellion. His warning to [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] is that harvest failure, poverty, unreduced taxes, and frontier burdens can make apparently weak actors more dangerous than powerful rebels whose cause lacks broad support.

## Current Profile

Xu Le uses [[ChenSheng|陈胜]] as the “土崩” case. Chen lacks noble status, territory, established reputation, wealth, or exceptional ability in the memorial's telling, yet poverty, resentment, official indifference, corruption, and failure to reform allow his uprising to cascade. Political force comes from a population already near the point of collective exit, not from the initiator's prior resources.

The Seven States supply the contrasting “瓦解” case. Their rulers possess territory, forces, and elite standing, but fail because the wider population remains comparatively settled and the dynasty's legitimacy has not broadly decayed. Xu Le applies the comparison to contemporary Guandong hardship and advises policy change before unrest becomes self-reinforcing. Wudi summons him after reading the memorial and appoints him as a palace gentleman alongside [[YanAnWesternHan|严安]] and [[ZhuFuYan|主父偃]].

## Key Characteristics

- Distinguishes regime danger by breadth of social support rather than the formal rank or army size of the first rebel.
- Treats popular hardship, resentment, official blindness, corruption, and policy immobility as interacting precursors to collapse.
- Uses Chen Sheng to show how low-resource initiation can become high-impact revolt when the social base is ready to respond.
- Uses the Seven States to show that elite military capacity can remain containable without broad popular alignment.
- Recommends changing policy before visible rebellion, making prevention depend on accurate feedback about livelihood pressure.

## Qualifications

The profile rests on a compact podcast summary rather than a full collation of Xu Le's memorial and later career. “土崩” and “瓦解” are analytical ideal types; actual rebellions can combine elite organization, regional military power, popular distress, opportunism, and coercion. The source does not independently establish the Seven States' numbers, popular attitudes, Guandong conditions, or the exact causal weight of taxes and war burdens. It also does not show whether appointment produced policy change.

## What Changed

- Established Xu Le's profile around the distinction between social-base collapse and elite rebellion.
- Added the Chen Sheng and Seven States comparison as the evidence mechanism behind that distinction.
- Preserved mixed-cause and policy-effect limits rather than turning the binary into a universal law.
