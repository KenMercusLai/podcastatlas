# Great Irish Famine

## Overview

The Great Irish Famine was the 1845-1849 subsistence and mortality catastrophe that transformed Ireland's population, diaspora, political memory, and relationship with British rule.

## Current Profile

The episode begins from extreme dietary dependence: about three million people obtained more than 90 percent of their calories from potatoes. Blight destroyed about half the 1845 crop, the entire 1846 crop failed, and normal harvest did not return until 1849. Around one million people died from starvation and illness and more than one million emigrated during the famine years.

The famine is treated as both crop failure and policy failure. Public works reached about 700,000 people, soup kitchens fed roughly three million daily, private and transatlantic charity was extensive, and authorities had abundant information about suffering. Yet the soup kitchens ended in 1847, providential and Malthusian ideas shaped policy, anti-Irish stereotypes mattered, and a contemporary Lord Lieutenant described the continued course as a policy of extermination. Irish food exports and the diversity of landlords and businesses complicate allocation of responsibility without erasing grave British governmental culpability.

The long aftermath joined population collapse to diaspora nationalism. Ireland's population continued falling for a century, millions left across the nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, and emigrant memory—especially in North America—made the famine a durable indictment of the Union.

## Key Characteristics

- Multi-year crop catastrophe intensified by extreme dependence on the potato.
- Mass-death event in which starvation and disease killed about one million people.
- Migration rupture that sent more than one million abroad during the famine years.
- Policy crisis shaped by relief withdrawal, ideology, information, prejudice, and disputed responsibility.
- Demographic break followed by roughly a century of continued population decline.
- Diaspora-memory event that deepened hostility toward British rule.

## Qualifications

All quantities are episode-level approximations rather than an independent demographic audit. The source explicitly rejects a story in which every landlord or Irish commercial actor behaved alike, and notes food exports by Irish farmers and businesses. Those qualifications do not establish that relief was adequate or remove the source's contemporary-standard culpability judgment. Regional variation, disease categories, market mechanisms, property law, workhouse policy, and full historiographical debate remain outside this note.

## What Changed

- Established the famine as a combined ecological, subsistence, policy, demographic, migration, and memory rupture.
- Preserved complexity about local actors without using it to dissolve governmental responsibility.
