# Battle of Copenhagen (1801)

## Overview

The Battle of Copenhagen was the 2 April 1801 British attack on Danish naval defenses during the Baltic crisis created by the [[LeagueOfArmedNeutrality|League of Armed Neutrality]].

## Current Profile

The strategic prelude joined Britain's growing isolation to conflict over neutral shipping and access to Baltic timber, hemp, and iron. After negotiations failed, the Admiralty ordered [[HydeParker|Hyde Parker]]'s fleet to force the strait, disable Danish naval power, and potentially continue toward Kronstadt. Parker hesitated before Copenhagen's shallow harbor, shoals, batteries, forts, warships, hulks, and floating gun platforms, while [[HoratioNelson]] argued that delay threatened the mission and accepted personal command of the assault.

The battle itself made strategy, hydrography, close-range fire, command discretion, and negotiation inseparable. Nelson shifted to the shallower-draft Elephant, scouted the shoals, consulted captains, and approached from the south through the King's Deep, but Agamemnon, Bellona, and Russell still grounded. Danish ships and floating batteries resisted fiercely before their capital, inflicting serious British losses even as their line was progressively disabled.

At the battle's command crisis, [[HydeParker]] ordered the action discontinued from a more distant position. Nelson refused to repeat the order and kept the close-action signal flying, while [[EdwardRiou]] obeyed with his frigates and was killed during withdrawal. Nelson then used a flag-of-truce note combining conciliation and threat to secure a ceasefire. Denmark's later temporary neutrality mattered, but Tsar Paul I's assassination and Alexander I's policy change also helped collapse the league, so the battle was an important lever rather than a self-sufficient cause.

## Key Characteristics

- Shoal-constrained naval attack dependent on pilots, reconnaissance, draft, wind, and precise approach routes.
- Preemptive British operation driven by neutral-shipping conflict, Baltic supplies, and fear of French maritime recovery.
- Close-range gunnery contest fought beside a defended capital and watched by civilians ashore.
- Command crisis in which different positions and ranks produced conflicting judgments about withdrawal.
- Tactical victory converted into political leverage through a coercive but face-saving truce proposal.
- British-centered heroic memory qualified by Danish resistance, casualties, and wider Russian political change.

## Qualifications

The bounded evidence consists of two consecutive British popular-history episodes. Their Danish perspective is limited, the blind-eye story may be mythic, and exact timings, losses, captures, ship positions, reported words, diplomatic motives, legal claims about neutral shipping, and counterfactual claims about delay or withdrawal require broader evidence. The league's collapse cannot be attributed to the battle alone.

## What Changed

- Extended the battle profile backward into failed negotiation, British war aims, Parker's hesitation, and Nelson's demand for speed.
- Joined tactical action to command conflict, negotiation, Baltic resources, and wider Russian political change.
