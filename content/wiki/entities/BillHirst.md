# Bill Hirst

## Overview
Bill Hirst is a psychologist presented in [[free-brian-williams-free-brian-williams|Free Brian Williams]] as a researcher on [[FlashbulbMemory]] and the long-term consistency of September 11 recollections.

## Current Profile
The episode attributes to Hirst, Liz Phelps, and collaborators a longitudinal study that surveyed 3,246 people shortly after September 11 and again after one, two, and ten years. In the episode's account, answers changed substantially while confidence remained extremely high, and some participants preferred their later memory to their own earlier written report.

Hirst explains retrieval as a moment when memory can incorporate later information and describes time-slice errors that place events from the same general period into the wrong sequence. He treats [[BrianWilliams]]'s account as compatible with ordinary memory failure, but compatibility with a mechanism is not proof that it caused a particular disputed recollection.

## Key Characteristics
- Supplies the episode's principal research account of vivid confidence persisting despite declining consistency.
- Explains retrieval as an opportunity for alteration and incorporation of later details.
- Uses time-slice error to distinguish a chronology mistake from wholesale invention.
- Treats memory science as a reason for calibrated investigation rather than automatic belief or automatic accusation of lying.

## Evidence
- Study design and pattern - [[free-brian-williams-free-brian-williams]] reports the September 11 sample, repeated questioning, consistency decline, and retained confidence.
- Retrieval mechanism - [[free-brian-williams-free-brian-williams]] attributes to Hirst the explanation that recalled memories can become vulnerable to alteration.
- Applied judgment - [[free-brian-williams-free-brian-williams]] reports Hirst's view that Williams's behavior could reflect a normal memory failure.

## Qualifications
The source is a structured summary of a public podcast, not the underlying papers, questionnaires, statistical analysis, or a comprehensive review of Hirst's scholarship. The reported average consistency decline and expert consensus remain episode-attributed. Applying population-level findings to Williams establishes plausibility, not individual causation or innocence.
