# Puritans

## Overview

The Puritans were English Protestants who sought to reform the Church of England and, through the [[GreatMigrationNewEngland|Great Migration]], built a durable religious and civic experiment in [[MassachusettsBayColony]]. “Puritan” began as a hostile label, and the episode distinguishes these reformers from the separatist Mayflower Pilgrims.

## Current Profile

The Puritans described here joined individual Bible reading, learned practical preaching, congregational self-government, family migration, local representation, written law, education, and communal discipline. They did not reject clothing, music, dancing, taverns, marital pleasure, or divorce in the categorical ways later stereotypes suggest.

Their participatory institutions were nevertheless bounded. Women lacked equal legal and political standing; dissenting religious groups could face expulsion or death; and Indigenous peoples faced land seizure, enslavement, cultural suppression, and war. The community therefore combined institutional innovation and mutual obligation with coercive conformity and settler-colonial violence.

## Key Characteristics

- Sought reform within the Church of England rather than initial separation from it.
- Migrated in family groups to establish a permanent godly commonwealth.
- Joined congregational autonomy to elected town and colonial institutions.
- Treated literacy and direct engagement with scripture as obligations for men and women.
- Used mediation and restoration alongside fines, whipping, disenfranchisement, banishment, and rare execution.
- Recognized marriage as civil rather than sacramental and permitted divorce.
- Restricted religious, legal, and political inclusion while dispossessing Indigenous peoples.

## Qualifications

The profile covers Massachusetts-centered Puritans during a defined seventeenth-century experiment and should not flatten differences across time, towns, theology, class, gender, or the Atlantic world. Its demographic and legal figures come from one structured episode summary without full citations.

## What Changed

- Added a profile separating English Protestant reform and Massachusetts institutions from later cultural stereotypes.
- Made internal participation and external exclusion parts of the same historical judgment.
