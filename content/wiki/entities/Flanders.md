# Flanders

## Overview

Flanders appears as the wealthy cloth-producing and urban region whose dependence on English wool made it a strategic hinge in the [[HundredYearsWar]] and whose commercial surplus supported the artistic world of [[JanVanEyck|Jan van Eyck]].

## Current Profile

Edward III's 1337 wool embargo placed pressure on Ghent, Bruges, Ypres, and other cloth centers. Jacob van Artevelde argued that continued production required English goodwill, but Flemish cities also owed loyalty to the French crown. Edward's claim to be king of France offered a political device for treating alliance with him as compatible with those oaths.

The region then supplied more than diplomatic access. Flemish forces intervened against the French rear at [[BattleOfSluys|Sluys]], helping convert commercial leverage into an operational alliance. The episode nevertheless shows the limits of this strategy: Flanders did not remove Edward's debts or guarantee immediate continental success.

The Van Eyck episode adds the cultural consequence of that commercial world. Ghent's scale and wealth, elite and bourgeois patronage, and a cloth economy built partly on English wool provide the material setting for detailed portraiture and the [[GhentAltarpiece]]. The region is therefore not only a strategic prize but also an artistic center where trade, court culture, devotion, and individual commemoration meet.

## Key Characteristics

- Wealthy urban cloth economy centered on cities including Ghent, Bruges, and Ypres.
- Dependent in the episode on English wool supply.
- Politically constrained by loyalty to the French crown.
- Pressured by Edward's wool embargo and courted through his French royal claim.
- Source of allied intervention at Sluys.
- Urban and commercial setting for Van Eyck's patronage and major works.
- Cloth wealth that the episode reads alongside the lamb and fleece symbolism of the Ghent Altarpiece.

## Evidence

- Economic dependence: [[319-hundred-years-war-triumph-of-the-longbow-part-2-glt3100937638]] links English wool to Flemish cloth production and urban pressure after the embargo.
- Oath workaround: [[319-hundred-years-war-triumph-of-the-longbow-part-2-glt3100937638]] presents Edward's French title as a means of reconciling alliance with existing loyalty.
- Military contribution: [[319-hundred-years-war-triumph-of-the-longbow-part-2-glt3100937638]] says Flemish allies attacked the French fleet from the rear at Sluys.
- Artistic economy: [[271-belgium-historys-greatest-artist-glt1331750756]] connects Ghent's size, cloth wealth, affluent patrons, and Burgundian culture to Van Eyck's art.
- Layered symbolism: [[271-belgium-historys-greatest-artist-glt1331750756]] reads the Mystic Lamb alongside sheep, English wool, and Flemish textile prosperity without displacing its Christian meaning.

## Qualifications

The war source follows an English strategic narrative and does not provide a full account of Flemish institutions, internal divisions, cloth supply alternatives, or the durability of Jacob van Artevelde's alignment. The art source uses broad wealth and population claims to contextualize selected works; its economic symbolism and patronage account are suggestive rather than a complete social history of Flemish art.
