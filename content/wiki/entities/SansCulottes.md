# Sans-culottes

## Overview

The sans-culottes were urban working-class radicals of the [[FrenchRevolution|French Revolution]], especially artisans, whose name and public image contrasted their trousers with elite knee-breeches.

## Current Profile

The episode presents sans-culotte identity as both material and ideological. Loose trousers, wooden clogs, the short carmagnole jacket, the bonnet rouge, and short unpowdered hair made social position and revolutionary commitment visible. Marseille volunteers appeared to fuse regional, working-class, and martial authenticity, while elite adoption of these styles showed that popular dress could become a prestigious political claim without dissolving class hierarchy.

## Key Characteristics

- The identity was associated with urban artisans and radical popular politics.
- Clothing made class difference and revolutionary belonging publicly legible.
- Greek and Roman liberty symbols overlapped with working-class style.
- The Marseille volunteers embodied the martial and regional version of the image.
- Elite imitation made the style politically influential but socially ambiguous.

## Evidence

### Dress and class identity

- [[507-the-french-revolution-the-marseillaise-song-of-war-part-5-glt5535769124]] identifies trousers, clogs, carmagnoles, red caps, and unpowdered hair as parts of sans-culotte public style.

### Revolutionary appropriation

- [[507-the-french-revolution-the-marseillaise-song-of-war-part-5-glt5535769124]] describes the Girondin ministry and Philippe Égalité adopting popular styles, showing their wider political prestige.

### Marseille and armed citizenship

- [[507-the-french-revolution-the-marseillaise-song-of-war-part-5-glt5535769124]] connects the carmagnole and Marseille fédérés to an image of working-class citizen-soldiers entering Paris.

## Qualifications

- Sans-culotte identity was not reducible to one uniform, occupation, organization, or political program.
- Elite performance of popular style did not make elite and working-class material conditions equivalent.
- The source concentrates on 1792 symbolism rather than a full social history of popular radicalism.

## What Changed

- Added the sans-culottes as a political identity produced through the fusion of class, dress, Roman symbolism, and armed citizenship.
