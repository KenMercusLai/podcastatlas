# Madame Roland

## Overview

Madame Roland was an influential Girondin salon organizer who shaped revolutionary action while publicly defending women's indirect, domestic political role.

## Current Profile

The sources use Roland to show both the reach and fragility of [[InformalFemalePoliticalInfluence]]. Her salon gathered Brissot and the emerging Girondin circle as war divided the Jacobins, and she unsuccessfully tried to persuade Robespierre to support the Girondin line. She later pressed her minister husband toward stronger revolutionary action and supported recruiting fédérés, yet argued that women should inspire politics without visibly participating. Her enemies then portrayed her as a corrupting hidden favorite, repeating the same misogynistic grammar she had applied to Marie Antoinette.

## Key Characteristics

- She organized a politically influential Girondin salon.
- She helped mediate the faction's war debate and failed to reconcile Brissot with Robespierre.
- She is portrayed as more forceful and dynamic than her husband.
- She supported recruiting fédérés and suspending the king.
- She defended female influence exercised privately rather than open female citizenship.
- Her opponents likened her to royal mistresses and blamed her as a hidden manipulator.

### Domestic-role position

- [[545-the-french-revolution-the-first-feminist-part-2-glt4335903757]] reports her view that women should inspire political action without appearing to participate.

### Misogynistic backlash

- [[545-the-french-revolution-the-first-feminist-part-2-glt4335903757]] shows Montagnard and sans-culotte enemies comparing her to Madame de Pompadour and Madame du Barry.

## Qualifications

- The episode supplies a focused political portrait rather than a complete biography or account of Girondin salons.
- Her reported judgment of Robespierre reflects factional conflict and does not independently establish his motives.
- Her indirect-politics position should not be generalized to all women or all Girondins.
- The source highlights the irony in her treatment of Marie Antoinette and her own later vilification without claiming the two women's political positions were identical.

## What Changed

- Extended the profile backward to her salon's role in the Girondin war circle and the failed effort to persuade Robespierre.
