# Franz Conrad von Hötzendorf

## Overview

Franz Conrad von Hötzendorf was Austria-Hungary's chief of the general staff, the leading military advocate of immediate war against [[Serbia]] after the Sarajevo assassination, and the commander whose failed Galician offensive and Przemyśl relief strategy accelerated Habsburg military breakdown.

## Current Profile

The episodes present Conrad as the clearest preventive-war voice in Vienna. Between 1906 and 1914 he repeatedly urged war not only against Serbia but also Montenegro, Russia, Romania, and even Italy. His conflict with [[FranzFerdinand]] is therefore substantive rather than a simple military-versus-civilian divide: the military heir repeatedly rejected war with Serbia because Russian intervention could destroy Austria-Hungary. After Sarajevo, Conrad pressed [[LeopoldBerchtold]] for war at once. His position converted the assassination into an opportunity to remove a perceived strategic threat, but it did not amount to a coherent plan for managing Russian intervention or general European conflict.

The [[Galicia]] and [[Przemysl|Przemyśl]] branch supplies the operational consequences missing from the earlier profile. Conrad attacked in the east despite Russian numerical strength, inadequate Austro-Hungarian rail capacity, supply weakness, and multilingual command friction. After defeat he ordered Przemyśl held, rejected evacuation before a second siege, and sent 175,000 men through the winter Carpathians in a failed relief attempt. The source treats prestige and imperial honor as important motives, while mass loss, exposure, disease, surrender, and reliance on German recapture made his decisions part of the empire's military and political collapse.

## Key Characteristics

- Longstanding and unusually recurrent advocate of preventive war against several neighboring states.
- Senior military participant in the first Austro-Hungarian deliberations after Sarajevo.
- Treated prompt armed punishment as necessary for imperial security and credibility.
- Focused on the Serbian threat more than the wider alliance consequences.
- Pursued offensives and fortress relief despite severe logistical, climatic, manpower, and command-cohesion constraints.
- Made Przemyśl's symbolic value part of operational judgment, contributing to a costly hold-and-relieve strategy.

## Qualifications

This profile remains selective. The source's count and range of preventive-war proposals are narrative summaries, while Franz Ferdinand's opposition does not mean every Austrian restraint came from him alone. Conrad was influential but not the sole author of policy, and structural railway, supply, linguistic, geographic, and coalition constraints were not his personal creation. The Przemyśl episode attributes prestige and imperial-honor motives without proving they alone determined each decision; force totals, loss figures, alternatives to holding the fortress, and responsibility across political and military institutions remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Extended Conrad's preventive-war advocacy across multiple proposed targets and the 1906-1914 period.
- Established Franz Ferdinand as a recurring internal opponent rather than merely a restraint removed at Sarajevo.
- Extended the profile into the failed Galician offensive, Przemyśl decisions, and Carpathian relief disaster.
- Added logistics, climate, command cohesion, prestige, and German dependence as constraints and consequences of his wartime command.
