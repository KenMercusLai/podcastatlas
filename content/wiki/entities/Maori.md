# Māori

## Overview

Māori are the Indigenous Polynesian people of New Zealand whose resistance, diplomacy, language relationship with [[Tupia]], and seafaring ancestry shape the episode's account of Cook's first encounters.

## Current Profile

The source presents Māori as independent political actors rather than passive objects of discovery. Groups withdraw, challenge, trade, perform armed collective displays, take objects, negotiate through Tupia, and suffer lethal British responses. Cook's admiration for their independence and humanity grows amid conflict, even as he threatens them with British firepower and claims their islands for the Crown.

The Tahitian-Māori language connection reveals a history of long-distance Polynesian settlement that preceded European mapping by centuries. At the same time, almost every Māori perspective in the episode passes through European journals and translation.

## Key Characteristics

- Indigenous Polynesian people with independent communities and political agency.
- Participants in first contact through refusal, armed display, trade, negotiation, appropriation, and resistance.
- Linguistically connected enough to Tahitian for Tupia to interpret.
- Heirs to long-distance navigation and settlement across the Pacific.
- Subjects of a record shaped by British violence, admiration, fear, and possession claims.

## Evidence

- Contact and resistance: [[381-captain-cook-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-2-glt6950185057]] recounts flight, haka-like armed display, object-taking, canoe encounters, and attempts at trade.
- Violence: [[381-captain-cook-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-2-glt6950185057]] records several Māori deaths during initial encounters and a failed British attempt to seize a canoe and prove friendly intent.
- Translation and ancestry: [[381-captain-cook-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-2-glt6950185057]] uses Tupia's communication with Māori to show linguistic kinship and support Cook's recognition of Polynesian voyaging range.
- Colonial claim: [[381-captain-cook-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-2-glt6950185057]] describes Cook's Queen Charlotte Sound possession ceremony with only one local elder witnessing it.

## Qualifications

This is not a general Māori history. It is bounded to one British popular-history account of 1769-70 encounters, relies mainly on European journals, and cannot recover the intentions, names, affiliations, exact words, or collective judgments of all communities involved.

## What Changed

- Created an agency-centered profile joining resistance, translation, Polynesian settlement, lethal encounter, and claim without consent.
