# Taliban

## Overview
The Taliban is an Afghan Islamist movement that emerged from the aftermath of the [[SovietAfghanWar|Soviet-Afghan War]] and civil conflict, later returned to national power, and now combines centralized hardline rule with lower visible violence, gender exclusion, ideological policing, poverty, and international isolation.

## Current Profile
[[87-afghanistan-part-1-glt7464087190]] rejects the idea that the Taliban is simply a survival from a timeless medieval Afghanistan. It locates the movement in the social damage of the Soviet war and postwar warlordism, arguing that Pashtun networks, religious legitimacy, anti-corruption claims, rural services, resistance to foreign forces, and anger at predatory elites helped it build support.

[[afghanistan-five-years-on-our-correspondent-visits-6a7ee547049c692a6b3ee0a9]] supplies the later governing profile. Five years after the return to power, [[HibatullahAkhundzada]] and hardliners centered in Kandahar are described as controlling the country and key revenue sources. Visible violence has fallen, but music bans, dress codes, vice-and-virtue policing, restrictions on women and girls, poverty, hunger, weak health care, and fear of open speech define the resulting order.

## Key Characteristics
- Movement formed through modern war, refugee, madrasa, and postwar political conditions rather than timeless continuity.
- Strongest historical social base among Pashtuns, without being reducible to all Pashtun communities.
- Uses anti-corruption, order, religious legitimacy, service provision, and resistance narratives.
- Built insurgent durability through rural presence and opponents' weak local legitimacy.
- Governing regime now centered on hardline authority from Kandahar.
- Combines lower visible violence with extensive ideological and gender control.

## Evidence
- Origins and appeal: [[87-afghanistan-part-1-glt7464087190]] connects Taliban formation to warlord abuses, Pashtun networks, rural services, anti-corruption rhetoric, and resistance to foreign occupation.
- Insurgent durability: [[87-afghanistan-part-1-glt7464087190]] argues that foreign-backed corruption and city-centered control strengthened the movement's claims.
- Current governing structure: [[afghanistan-five-years-on-our-correspondent-visits-6a7ee547049c692a6b3ee0a9]] reports hardline consolidation around Hibatullah Akhundzada and Kandahar.
- Social consequences: [[afghanistan-five-years-on-our-correspondent-visits-6a7ee547049c692a6b3ee0a9]] documents constrained public life, severe restrictions on women and girls, humanitarian stress, and limited educational workarounds.

## Qualifications
The historical source offers a compressed origin account and an attributed founding story, not a full organizational history. Anti-corruption claims and service provision explain part of the movement's appeal without validating its violence or rule. The current report is a time-bounded field snapshot, and neither source represents every Afghan region, faction, community, or period of Taliban governance.

## What Changed
- Added the Taliban's emergence from Soviet-war and postwar conditions.
- Distinguished social and insurgent appeal from endorsement of later coercive government.
- Connected rural durability and anti-corruption rhetoric to the legitimacy failures of foreign-backed elites.
