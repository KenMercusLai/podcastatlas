# Ku Klux Klan

## Overview

The Ku Klux Klan is a U.S. white-supremacist and nativist movement whose second incarnation became a mass social and political organization during the 1920s before scandal, exposure, backlash, and institutional weakness drove rapid decline.

## Current Profile

The bounded sources present the second Klan as a new 1915 organization rather than a continuous survival of the Reconstruction-era group. [[WilliamJosephSimmons]] combined first-Klan memory with American fraternal culture and symbols transmitted through [[ThomasDixonJr|Thomas Dixon Jr.]] and [[BirthOfANation|*The Birth of a Nation*]]. [[EdwardYoungClarke]] and [[ElizabethTyler]] then converted the small project into a territorial, commission-driven recruitment business.

At mass scale, the organization joined anti-Black racism, antisemitism, anti-Catholicism, nativism, Prohibition, sexual policing, and “100% Americanism” to Protestant respectability, picnics, fairs, newspapers, women's organizations, electoral machinery, and spectacle. Those ordinary-looking forms widened recruitment while armed raids, beatings, abduction, flogging, torture, murder, and intimidation remained close at hand.

Under [[HiramWesleyEvans]], the national organization sought respectability and mainstream political influence. [[DCStephenson]] built its strongest state machine in Indiana, but his predation against [[MadgeOberholtzer]], conviction, and disclosure of bribery exposed the contradiction between moral guardianship and leadership corruption. The episode distinguishes this 1920s mass formation from the smaller, intensely racist third Klan founded in 1946.

## Key Characteristics

- It defined authentic Americanness through exclusion of African Americans, Jews, Catholics, immigrants, and other constructed outsiders.
- It converted Lost Cause fiction, film imagery, and fraternal ritual into an invented but emotionally forceful claim of ancestral continuity.
- It blended spectacle and family sociability with secrecy, hierarchy, ritual, and vigilante force.
- Recruitment and revenue depended on commissions, territorial organizers, membership fees, official regalia, newspapers, events, and women's logistical labor.
- It converted membership networks into electoral endorsements, turnout, officeholding, and influence over public institutions.
- Its national unity was fragile because money, prestige, local variation, corruption, and leadership rivalry pulled the organization apart.
- Its resemblance to fascism is substantial but incomplete under a historically specific definition.

## Evidence

- Founding and invented tradition: [[656-the-ku-klux-klan-birth-of-a-nation-part-3-glt9883414135]] traces Simmons's 1915 relaunch, Stone Mountain cross-burning, and adoption of Dixon-Griffith imagery.
- Commercial recruitment and social form: [[656-the-ku-klux-klan-birth-of-a-nation-part-3-glt9883414135]] describes Klegels, fees, regalia, clergy, Masonic contacts, and local grievance marketing; [[657-the-ku-klux-klan-american-fascists-part-4-glt4147819855]] adds mass events, newspapers, family entertainment, and women's organizing.
- Violence and exclusion: [[656-the-ku-klux-klan-birth-of-a-nation-part-3-glt9883414135]] describes Mer Rouge, Goose Creek, and Texas terror; [[657-the-ku-klux-klan-american-fascists-part-4-glt4147819855]] adds Dallas beatings and Williamson County raids while stressing state variation.
- Political penetration: [[656-the-ku-klux-klan-birth-of-a-nation-part-3-glt9883414135]] traces Oregon and Texas breakthroughs; [[657-the-ku-klux-klan-american-fascists-part-4-glt4147819855]] follows Evans's national ambitions and Stephenson's Indiana machine.
- Collapse and afterlife: [[657-the-ku-klux-klan-american-fascists-part-4-glt4147819855]] links scandal, bribery, electoral backlash, violence, the Depression, and weak coalition capacity to decline before distinguishing the later third Klan.

## Qualifications

The page is grounded in two consecutive synthetic podcast episodes rather than a complete institutional history. Membership, crowd, officeholder, revenue, violence, and present-day estimates are source-attributed. The relative weight of ideology, entertainment, film, religion, class, gender, sales incentives, violence, policy success, scandal, and economic change remains debated, and local Klans differed. Calling the movement fascist depends on the definition used and should not erase its specifically U.S. histories of race, Protestant nativism, Reconstruction, and vigilantism.

## What Changed

- Added the 1915 founding and the distinction between remembered first-Klan lineage and a new organization.
- Added fiction, film, fraternalism, and commissioned sales as interacting recruitment infrastructure.
- Extended political and violence evidence backward from the mid-1920s peak to Oregon, Texas, Mer Rouge, and Goose Creek.
