## Overview

Lindisfarne is presented as the Northumbrian monastic and pilgrimage center where Cuthbert's burial and relic cult converted sacred reputation into regional religious influence and material wealth before Viking attack forced the community and relics to move.

## Current Profile

The episode locates [[StCuthbert|Cuthbert]] within the Irish- and Roman-connected Christianization of seventh-century Northumbria. After his death on Farne, his body was taken to Lindisfarne; the later report that it remained intact, together with healing claims for personal objects, made the monastery a destination for pilgrims from England and northern Europe.

Offerings made that cult materially rich. The episode therefore connects religious success to physical vulnerability: Lindisfarne's sacred prestige produced visible wealth, and the 793 sack conventionally marks the opening of the Viking Age in this narrative. In 875 the community moved Cuthbert's coffin through its dependent lands, allowing institutional identity and authority to survive beyond the original site.

## Key Characteristics

- Northumbrian monastery formed within overlapping Irish and Roman Christian networks.
- Burial and early cult center of St Cuthbert.
- International pilgrimage destination enriched by offerings and relic devotion.
- Sacred site whose visible wealth increased its exposure to Viking violence.
- Institutional community able to carry identity and authority through the movement of relics.

## Evidence

- Cult center - [[34-st-cuthberts-day-glt9977520388]] follows Cuthbert's burial, reported bodily preservation, healing objects, and pilgrim attraction.
- Wealth and vulnerability - [[34-st-cuthberts-day-glt9977520388]] explicitly connects pilgrim offerings with the monastery's spectacular wealth and the significance of the 793 raid.
- Mobile continuity - [[34-st-cuthberts-day-glt9977520388]] describes the 875 removal of Cuthbert's coffin through lands associated with the community.

## Qualifications

This bounded profile covers Lindisfarne through one Cuthbert-centered episode rather than a full institutional history. The preserved-body and healing claims belong to devotional tradition, while the simple causal link from wealth to Viking selection and the use of 793 as the Viking Age's beginning are narrative frames that require wider evidence. The later community's travels and settlement are only summarized.

## What Changed

- Established Lindisfarne as a cult institution joining pilgrimage, material wealth, vulnerability, and portable continuity.
- Distinguished the physical island from the mobile community and relic authority that survived departure.
