# The Canterbury Tales

## Overview

*The Canterbury Tales* is [[GeoffreyChaucer]]'s unfinished collection of stories framed by a pilgrimage from Southwark to Thomas Becket's shrine at Canterbury.

## Current Profile

The episode reads the work as a deliberately crowded social world. Its pilgrims include idealized representatives of fighting, prayer, and labor, but also merchants, professionals, compromised clergy, a drunken Miller, and the economically independent [[WifeOfBath]]. Their storytelling contest begins under rank hierarchy and then repeatedly disrupts it.

The work also joins historical pressure to literary play. Plague and death enter its tales; pilgrimage reverses isolation by gathering strangers; real and fictional people mix; and Chaucer mocks his own storytelling persona. These features make the work central to [[LiteratureAsSocialHistory]] without reducing its fictional characters to transparent demographic evidence.

## Key Characteristics

- Pilgrimage frame joining 30 travelers at the Tabard Inn for a storytelling contest.
- Multi-voiced representation of medieval ranks, occupations, ideals, and hypocrisies.
- Repeated disruption of formal hierarchy through interruption and tale order.
- Comic mixture of moral teaching, bawdy narrative, self-parody, and social observation.
- Persistent presence of plague memory, mortality, greed, violence, and religious corruption.
- Major contribution to [[VernacularLiteraryLegitimacy]] through ambitious English composition.

## Evidence

- **Social range:** [[416-the-canterbury-tales-part-4-glt1956280616]] presents the pilgrims as a cross-section of a mobile and changing society.
- **Hierarchy under pressure:** [[416-the-canterbury-tales-part-4-glt1956280616]] contrasts the Knight's precedence with the Miller's drunken interruption.
- **Historical pressure:** [[416-the-canterbury-tales-part-4-glt1956280616]] connects the Pardoner's tale and Saturn imagery to recurrent plague and death.
- **Fictional play:** [[416-the-canterbury-tales-part-4-glt1956280616]] identifies Harry Bailey as a historical innkeeper and describes Chaucer's comic self-interruption.

## Qualifications

The pilgrims are literary constructions, not a statistically representative survey of England. The episode's claims about plague season, social structure, character modernity, and direct correspondence between fictional figures and real economic groups remain interpretive and source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Established the work as a hierarchy-disrupting, plague-aware, multi-voiced social-history source with explicit fictional limits.
