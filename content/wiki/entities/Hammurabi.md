# Hammurabi

## Overview

Hammurabi is the early king of [[Babylon]] presented in [[181-the-birth-of-babylon-glt1570061216]] as both an expansive conqueror and a remembered lawgiver.

## Current Profile

The episode places Hammurabi in a kingship tradition that joined military success to divinely grounded justice, subject protection, and correct ritual. His conquests created an empire across much of southern Mesopotamia and into Syria, while his monumental written text preserved an unusually durable image of the ruler ordering marriage, inheritance, punishment, and social obligation under the gods.

The profile is qualified in two ways. The surviving text includes royal self-presentation and need not have functioned like a modern comprehensive statute book; even the label “code” is debated. Hammurabi's empire also largely unraveled after his death, making his reputation more durable than the political system he assembled.

## Key Characteristics

- Hammurabi expanded Babylonian rule across a large part of Mesopotamia and into Syria.
- He presented kingship as divinely ordered protection, justice, and ritual responsibility.
- His surviving monumental text made lawgiving central to his later reputation.
- The text combines rules and judgments with royal boasting and religious legitimation.
- His empire proved less durable than his remembered image as conqueror and lawgiver.

## Evidence

- Conquest and kingship: [[181-the-birth-of-babylon-glt1570061216]] describes Hammurabi's territorial expansion and self-presentation as a great king in the tradition of Sargon.
- Justice and text: [[181-the-birth-of-babylon-glt1570061216]] links his monument to marriage, inheritance, justice claims, and divine authority while noting debate over the modern “law code” label.
- Political fragility: [[181-the-birth-of-babylon-glt1570061216]] says much of the empire broke apart after his death as subordinated cities reasserted independence.
- Prestigious afterlife: [[181-the-birth-of-babylon-glt1570061216]] describes the later Elamite removal of the monument to Susa as plunder of an already valuable Babylonian past.

## Qualifications

This is an episode-bounded profile rather than a full reconstruction of Hammurabi's reign or jurisprudence. The source does not settle how the monument was applied in practice, how representative its cases were, or whether “code” is the best analytical category. Its account of empire, chronology, legal content, and Elamite appropriation remains public-history synthesis rather than independent primary-source adjudication.

## What Changed

- Created Hammurabi as both an imperial and lawgiving figure in Babylon's rise.
- Qualified the familiar modern “code” label and separated durable reputation from short-lived empire.
- Connected the later seizure of his monument to Babylon's accumulated cultural prestige.
