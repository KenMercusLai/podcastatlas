# Jean Shrimpton

## Overview

Jean Shrimpton is the British model whose work with David Bailey and 1965 Melbourne Derby Day appearance anchor the episodes' account of fashion imagery, celebrity, and changing respectability codes.

## Current Profile

Shrimpton appears as both a working model in a new image-making system and a symbolic carrier of the mid-1960s youth-fashion break. Her partnership with [[DavidBailey]] helped establish a willowy, girl-next-door alternative to curvier modeling ideals, while their 1962 Sunday Times colour-supplement image joined model, photographer, Mary Quant dress, and new print technology. Her short white shift dress in Melbourne, worn without the expected hat, gloves, or stockings, later generated press outrage and internationalized the image of rising hemlines.

## Key Characteristics

- British model associated with the global visibility of 1960s youth fashion.
- Worked with David Bailey in a model-photographer partnership central to new fashion imagery.
- Embodied a willowy, youthful, girl-next-door ideal rather than an older curvaceous one.
- Triggered a public-respectability scandal at Melbourne's 1965 Derby Day.
- Became associated with the miniskirt moment despite wearing a shift dress.
- Commanded international celebrity fees and illustrates how press attention can turn appearances into historical markers.

## Qualifications

This profile is limited to the two episodes' fashion-history narrative. It does not establish Shrimpton as the inventor or sole cause of shorter hemlines, independently verify the comparative fee claim, or cover her full modeling career.
