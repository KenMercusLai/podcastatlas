# Judas Iscariot

## Overview

Judas Iscariot is the New Testament disciple remembered for betraying [[Jesus]], represented across a comic reality-television analogy and a literary reading of the [[LastSupper]] and arrest.

## Current Profile

The more substantive bounded profile treats Judas as a narrative problem of agency. Jesus predicts betrayal, gives Judas bread, and the account then attributes his action to satanic entry; the later identifying kiss converts intimacy into the instrument of arrest. The combination raises rather than resolves the relation among prophecy, evil influence, foreknowledge, and free choice.

The comic source instead reduces Judas's canonical reputation and association with thirty pieces of silver to a reality-show “traitor” role. It pairs him with [[MarciaWilliams]] and eliminates them as untrustworthy before the public vote. That contrast is useful mainly as a warning: memorable archetype and imagined motive are not historical or theological analysis.

## Key Characteristics

- Disciple identified as Jesus's betrayer.
- Linked to both the meal's bread-giving scene and the arrest's identifying kiss.
- Narrative focus for the tension between foreknowledge and human agency.
- Familiar cultural archetype readily compressed into suspicion, money, and treachery.
- Historically underdetermined across both literary interpretation and comic reception.

## Evidence

- Meal and betrayal: [[24-dongxifang-de-fanju-zuihou-de-wancan-yu-lanting-yaji-lraekm-bgowhg1wxysfshcwoz5xp]] recounts Jesus's prediction, bread given to Judas, and the later kiss used to identify Jesus.
- Agency problem: [[24-dongxifang-de-fanju-zuihou-de-wancan-yu-lanting-yaji-lraekm-bgowhg1wxysfshcwoz5xp]] interprets satanic influence and prophecy as complicating simple voluntary blame.
- Betrayal archetype: [[206-historical-love-island-glt8073929093]] maps Judas's canonical reputation and money association onto a comic reality-show contestant.

## Qualifications

Neither source offers a source-critical reconstruction of Judas or reconciles the Gospel accounts. The free-will reading is a host interpretation, while the romantic pairing, cash-prize suspicion, and elimination are jokes. The payment tradition, Gospel differences, later theology, and antisemitic reception require context absent from both episodes.

## What Changed

- Replaced a purely comic archetype with a fuller literary profile centered on betrayal and agency while retaining the earlier source as reception evidence.
