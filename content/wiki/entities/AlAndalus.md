# al-Andalus / 安达卢斯

## Overview

al-Andalus / 安达卢斯 is the Islamic Iberian world created after the 711 conquest and connected to North Africa through the [[StraitOfGibraltar]], Berber military networks, trade, and shifting dynastic power.

## Current Profile

The sources break a simple Europe-versus-Africa or Christian-versus-Muslim geography. The first presents Tariq ibn Ziyad's mainly Berber crossing, Cordoban power, Taifa fragmentation, Christian kingdoms, and cross-religious alliances as an entangled two-shore history. The second adds a rise-and-collapse sequence from conquest and plunder through the Umayyad emirate and [[CordobaCaliphate|Cordoba Caliphate]] to civil war.

The region's scholarship, architecture, trade, and urban life coexist with conquest, slavery, taxation, hierarchy, religious repression, and ethnicized violence. [[HierarchicalPluralismAlAndalus]] therefore captures exchange without projecting modern equality backward, while [[CentralizedGoldenAgeFragility]] explains why political order mattered to Cordoba's prosperity and why fragmentation altered the balance with northern Christian kingdoms.

## Key Characteristics

- Connects Iberian Islamic history with North African military and political networks.
- Includes conquest, emirate, caliphate, civil war, Taifa fragmentation, and later Christian expansion.
- Sustained major urban, architectural, commercial, and intellectual centers.
- Enabled cross-religious exchange within a Muslim-dominant legal hierarchy.
- Shows that medieval alliances and conflicts did not always follow religious boundaries.

- Conquest and North Africa: [[496-yiminchao-beihou-de-xiuda-cong-gudian-shidai-manzu-duhai-yibiliya-shoufu-shidi-yundong-dao-putaoya-dahanghai-lu8pawnkiselmmrgubrey4pq-4fw]] and [[281-spain-the-caliphate-of-cordoba-glt2203638069]] make Tariq ibn Ziyad and Berber forces central to the 711 crossing.
- Civilization and hierarchy: [[496-yiminchao-beihou-de-xiuda-cong-gudian-shidai-manzu-duhai-yibiliya-shoufu-shidi-yundong-dao-putaoya-dahanghai-lu8pawnkiselmmrgubrey4pq-4fw]] describes Cordoba's learning and translation; [[281-spain-the-caliphate-of-cordoba-glt2203638069]] adds architecture, trade, urban life, jizya, slavery, and protected minority subordination.
- Political complexity: [[496-yiminchao-beihou-de-xiuda-cong-gudian-shidai-manzu-duhai-yibiliya-shoufu-shidi-yundong-dao-putaoya-dahanghai-lu8pawnkiselmmrgubrey4pq-4fw]] uses El Cid serving Muslim rulers to qualify rigid blocs; [[281-spain-the-caliphate-of-cordoba-glt2203638069]] emphasizes ethnic and factional conflict within Muslim rule.
- Fragmentation and contingency: [[281-spain-the-caliphate-of-cordoba-glt2203638069]] links Cordoba's civil war to altered Christian-Muslim power without treating the Reconquista as inevitable.

## Qualifications

This page remains bounded to two narrative episodes and does not cover the full regional or chronological diversity of Islamic Iberia. Demographic estimates, institutional uniformity, the balance of coercion and accommodation, and causal accounts of collapse require specialist corroboration.

## What Changed

- Added the conquest-to-collapse sequence and the institutional basis of Cordoba's prosperity.
- Distinguished real exchange from modern equality and religious hierarchy from total separation.
- Added internal ethnic and factional conflict to a field often simplified as Muslim versus Christian.
