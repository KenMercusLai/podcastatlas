# Aristagoras

## Overview

Aristagoras appears in the wiki as the Miletian ruler whose failed Naxos expedition and tactical embrace of democracy help launch the [[IonianRevolt]].

## Current Profile

The episode presents Aristagoras as [[Histias]]' nephew and son-in-law, ruling [[Miletus]] after Histias is pulled to Darius's court. Aristagoras tries to expand family power through a Persian-backed attack on Naxos, but the four-month siege fails and leaves him exposed to Persian punishment and local political pressure.

His democratic turn is treated as opportunistic but consequential. Facing likely dismissal, he gives up tyranny, urges Ionian cities to overthrow their tyrants, captures Ionian squadrons from the Persian fleet, and seeks mainland Greek help. That appeal pulls [[Athens]] and Eretria into the revolt and eventually makes them targets for [[DariusI]].

## Key Characteristics

- Aristagoras is portrayed as ambitious and politically vulnerable rather than purely ideological.
- The failed Naxos expedition is the pressure point that pushes him into revolt.
- His embrace of democracy is framed as tactical self-preservation.
- His appeal to Athens internationalizes the Ionian crisis.
- His actions connect local Miletian instability to the later Persian expedition to Marathon.

## Evidence

- Miletian succession: [[668-greece-vs-persia-the-rise-of-the-first-superpower-part-1-glt8965800182]] says Aristagoras replaces Histias as ruler of Miletus.
- Naxos failure: [[668-greece-vs-persia-the-rise-of-the-first-superpower-part-1-glt8965800182]] describes the failed Persian-backed Naxos expedition and Aristagoras's exposure.
- Democratic pivot: [[668-greece-vs-persia-the-rise-of-the-first-superpower-part-1-glt8965800182]] says he abdicates tyranny, backs democracy, and urges other Ionian cities to expel tyrants.
- Mainland appeal: [[668-greece-vs-persia-the-rise-of-the-first-superpower-part-1-glt8965800182]] says he travels to Athens in winter 499 BC to seek support.

## Qualifications

The profile preserves the episode's interpretation that Aristagoras's democratic conversion was tactical. It does not settle all source debates over his motives, the Naxos expedition, or Ionian democratic politics.
