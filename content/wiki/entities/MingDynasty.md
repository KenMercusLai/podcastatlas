## Overview

明朝 / Ming Dynasty enters this wiki as an institutionalized tribute-economy case, a domestic social-order case, a royal-household fiscal-burden case, and a late-Ming institutional-capacity case. The evidence spans foreign recognition, status regulation, tax-funded princely households, and [[RayHuang|黄仁宇]]'s interpretation of moralized rule, weak [[NumericalManagement|数目字管理]], and person-dependent reform.

## Current Profile

The current profile is not a general history of the Ming. It records outward-facing status economics, inward-facing status discipline, royal-household fiscal burden, and a qualified late-Ming governance interpretation.

In the foreign-relations branch, Ming tribute is presented as a formalized diplomatic and economic system. Tribute goods could receive official prices, envoys could be hosted at court expense, and foreign polities or merchants could profit from return gifts and above-market purchase terms. This makes Ming a mature case for [[TributeSystemEconomicIncentive|朝贡体系经济激励]] and for the fiscal burden inside prestige-seeking diplomacy.

Hanji 400-2 adds a domestic hierarchy branch. The episode says Zhu Yuanzhang's Ming order used strict household registration, inherited occupation, migration control, clothing rules, fabric distinctions, housing limits, vessel restrictions, and hairstyle categories to make status visible and enforceable. This ties Ming to [[HouseholdOccupationControl|户籍与职业管控]], [[HeavyAgricultureLightCommercePolicy|重农轻商政策]], and [[StatusCodedEverydayRegulation|身份编码式日常管制]].

Hanji 388-4 adds a royal-household branch through [[JingchengWangMing|敬城王]]. The episode contrasts wartime commoner loss with expanding noble and imperial-clan populations, then argues that Ming princely stipends were funded by ordinary taxes and could overwhelm provincial finance. This makes Ming a case for [[ImperialClanFiscalBurden|皇族宗藩财政负担]] as well as for tribute cost and everyday status coding.

The late-Ming institutional-capacity branch uses [[WanliFifteenYear|《万历十五年》]] to compare sharply different paths taken by [[WanliEmperor|万历帝]], [[ZhangJuzheng|张居正]], [[ShenShixing|申时行]], [[HaiRui|海瑞]], [[QiJiguang|戚继光]], and [[LiZhiMing|李贽]]. Withdrawal, coercive reform, accommodation, moral rigor, technical improvement, and intellectual dissent all fail in this interpretation to become durable institutional change. The dynasty therefore appears not only as a collection of policy burdens but also as a qualified case of [[MoralSubstitutionForInstitutions|道德替代制度]] and weak [[NumericalManagement|数目字管理]].

## Key Characteristics

- Dynasty used as a mature tribute-system example where prestige recognition carried fiscal cost.
- Court whose贡物 pricing,回赐, and reception practices could subsidize foreign recognition.
- Setting for fraud and conflict when tribute access became profitable.
- Domestic hierarchy case where household, occupation, and migration rules fixed people administratively.
- Social-order case where clothing, fabric, housing, vessels, footwear, and hairstyles made rank and occupation visible.
- Anti-merchant case where the episode presents commercial households as materially degraded relative to farming households.
- Royal-household fiscal and late-governance case where expanding obligations, moralized administration, weak standardization, and person-dependent reform pressure durable capacity.

## Evidence

- Institutionalized tribute economy: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-422-2-gudai-waiguoren-weisha-ai-dao-zhongguo-chaogong-yuanyin-papadalian-lr3erfvroy4b_ryu2sfsdgd-e4m1|Hanji 422-2]] says Ming tribute rules included official prices,回赐, hosted reception, and above-market returns for some贡物.
- Tribute access conflict: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-422-2-gudai-waiguoren-weisha-ai-dao-zhongguo-chaogong-yuanyin-papadalian-lr3erfvroy4b_ryu2sfsdgd-e4m1|Hanji 422-2]] cites fake envoys, the Ningbo tribute conflict, Tumu, and Altan Khan as tribute-economy stress cases.
- Household and occupation hierarchy: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-400-2-yidai-qiong-shidai-qiong-nandao-qiongren-jiu-gai-daidai-qiong-lluvrlqzbzx4bb_aatfmtyqibdym|Hanji 400-2]] says people were registered from birth, household records were periodically updated, adult work was inherited, and unauthorized migration or occupational change could be punished.
- Everyday status coding: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-400-2-yidai-qiong-shidai-qiong-nandao-qiongren-jiu-gai-daidai-qiong-lluvrlqzbzx4bb_aatfmtyqibdym|Hanji 400-2]] describes rules for dress, color, motif, fabric, hats, boots, houses, vessels, and hairstyles.
- Merchant degradation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-400-2-yidai-qiong-shidai-qiong-nandao-qiongren-jiu-gai-daidai-qiong-lluvrlqzbzx4bb_aatfmtyqibdym|Hanji 400-2]] says merchant households were barred from fabrics available to farming households.
- Imperial-clan population and stipends: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-388-4-jiemi-gudai-zuican-de-sheng-ta-ceng-si-le-90-baixing-luivvup1twdt6t6dgow9xnfwirbu|Hanji 388-4]] uses Jingcheng Wang's large household, a 1508 population-reporting memorial, and Shanxi stipend-versus-revenue figures to show princely kin as a fiscal burden.
- Late-Ming institutional capacity: [[vol-362-wanli-shiwunian-da-shibai-shidai-de-ren-hai-neng-nadao-da-jieguo-ma-gkwriw4o2at-a-vjhw-t-4dvr|Vol.362]] uses six contrasting lives to argue that personal authority, integrity, technical competence, and dissent did not become stable, repeatable governance.

## Qualifications

This remains a narrow source-bounded page. Hanji 422-2's price comparisons and military-causality interpretations, Hanji 400-2's account of Zhu Yuanzhang's rules, and Hanji 388-4's stipend figures remain source-scoped. Vol.362 derives from one structured podcast summary of Huang's thesis; its six-person selection, biographies, “great failure” conclusion, moral-versus-institutional framing, and causal claims require comparison with primary sources and other Ming scholarship.
