## Overview

明朝 / Ming Dynasty enters this wiki as an institutionalized tribute-economy case, a domestic social-order case, and a royal-household fiscal-burden case. Hanji 422-2 emphasizes foreign recognition, tribute pricing,回赐, and reception costs; Hanji 400-2 adds the domestic side through [[ZhuYuanzhang|朱元璋]]'s household, occupation, and everyday-status regulation; Hanji 388-4 adds the fiscal pressure of expanding tax-funded princely households.

## Current Profile

The current profile is not a general history of the Ming. It records two ways the dynasty appears in the wiki's source base: outward-facing status economics and inward-facing status discipline.

In the foreign-relations branch, Ming tribute is presented as a formalized diplomatic and economic system. Tribute goods could receive official prices, envoys could be hosted at court expense, and foreign polities or merchants could profit from return gifts and above-market purchase terms. This makes Ming a mature case for [[TributeSystemEconomicIncentive|朝贡体系经济激励]] and for the fiscal burden inside prestige-seeking diplomacy.

Hanji 400-2 adds a domestic hierarchy branch. The episode says Zhu Yuanzhang's Ming order used strict household registration, inherited occupation, migration control, clothing rules, fabric distinctions, housing limits, vessel restrictions, and hairstyle categories to make status visible and enforceable. This ties Ming to [[HouseholdOccupationControl|户籍与职业管控]], [[HeavyAgricultureLightCommercePolicy|重农轻商政策]], and [[StatusCodedEverydayRegulation|身份编码式日常管制]].

Hanji 388-4 adds a royal-household branch through [[JingchengWangMing|敬城王]]. The episode contrasts wartime commoner loss with expanding noble and imperial-clan populations, then argues that Ming princely stipends were funded by ordinary taxes and could overwhelm provincial finance. This makes Ming a case for [[ImperialClanFiscalBurden|皇族宗藩财政负担]] as well as for tribute cost and everyday status coding.

## Key Characteristics

- Dynasty used as a mature tribute-system example where prestige recognition carried fiscal cost.
- Court whose贡物 pricing,回赐, and reception practices could subsidize foreign recognition.
- Setting for fraud and conflict when tribute access became profitable.
- Domestic hierarchy case where household, occupation, and migration rules fixed people administratively.
- Social-order case where clothing, fabric, housing, vessels, footwear, and hairstyles made rank and occupation visible.
- Anti-merchant case where the episode presents commercial households as materially degraded relative to farming households.
- Royal-household fiscal case where expanding imperial kinship and tax-funded stipends pressure provincial revenue.

## Qualifications

This remains a narrow source-bounded page. Hanji 422-2's price comparisons and military-causality interpretations should remain attributed to that episode. Hanji 400-2's account of Zhu Yuanzhang's rules should likewise remain source-scoped for exact chronology, legal wording, and enforcement practice. Hanji 388-4's royal-stipend figures, Jingcheng Wang anecdote, and provincial fiscal comparison should remain source-scoped pending corroboration.
