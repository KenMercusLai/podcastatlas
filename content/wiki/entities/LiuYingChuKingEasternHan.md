## Overview

[[LiuYingChuKingEasternHan|刘英 / Liu Ying]] is the Eastern Han Chu king whose current profile now begins with a tolerated Huang-Lao and Buddhist devotion notice in Hanji 737 before the rebellion case opens in [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-739-zuoyong-tianxia-meinv-de-huangdi-qishi-ye-hui-gudu-lsoogpawume-fpmitbigklhzdbjl|Hanji 739]] and becomes the origin point for a Mingdi-era treason panic in [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-740-rang-ni-tihuguanding-de-zhichang-zhihui-zhichang-bibei-lih5b-ighkyoycawm5c-dbrnuiew|Hanji 740]]. The current profile holds together initial religious tolerance, later charges, [[EmperorMingOfHan|Mingdi]]'s family-limited punishment, Liu Ying's suicide, and the later prison case that expands through torture and implication.

## Current Profile

The current profile is not a full biography. Hanji 737 supplies the earliest layer: Liu Ying sends thirty bolts of silk to his kingdom chancellor, saying that he has many faults and wants to redeem them. Mingdi responds with a退赎诏, saying Liu Ying reveres Huang-Lao, worships Buddha, and has fasted for three months, so there is no suspicion requiring redemption. The court returns the silk and tells him to use it to support Buddhist disciples. In this layer, Liu Ying's religious practice is tolerated rather than criminalized.

Hanji 739 gives the case opening. Liu Ying and Fangshi produce gold-turtle and jade-crane objects with inscriptions, while Yan Guang reports that Liu Ying, Wang Ping, Yan Zhong, and others have fabricated prophecy texts and are plotting rebellion. Mingdi accepts the seriousness of the charge but does not execute Liu Ying immediately; he abolishes the Chu kingship, moves Liu Ying to Jing county in Danyang, grants household revenue for support, preserves Liu Ying's children's food rights, and lets Liu Ying's mother Xu Taihou remain in the Chu palace.

That initial family restraint fails to stop escalation. Hanji 739 records [[YuYanEasternHan|Yu Yan]]'s suicide after Mingdi rebukes him for disbelieving an earlier warning, then Liu Ying's own suicide after reaching Danyang. The same source already describes the case spreading through years of investigation, with deaths, exile, and imprisonment.

Hanji 740 gives the broader interpretive and corrective layer. Liu Ying matters there because his case reveals how royal suspicion, religious anxiety, and ruler anger can turn a single accusation into a broad court and local-elite purge. The episode's emphasis is less on battlefield rebellion than on the downstream legal and organizational damage after the ruler keeps searching for hidden accomplices.

Hanji 740 also makes Liu Ying a disambiguation case. The wiki already has [[LiuYing|刘盈 / Emperor Hui of Han]], so this page keeps the Eastern Han Chu king on a separate route. The Buddhist angle is important but source-scoped: it signals how private religious organization could alarm an imperial court, not that Buddhist practice itself is treated as proven treason.

## Key Characteristics

- Eastern Han royal kin whose accusation occurs under [[EmperorMingOfHan|汉明帝]].
- Initially tolerated Huang-Lao and Buddhist practitioner before the later treason case.
- Chu-king figure whose case begins with auspicious-object, prophecy, and rebellion accusations.
- Royal accused whom Mingdi initially punishes by deposition and relocation rather than execution.
- Political trigger rather than only a personal actor: his fall and suicide produce a wider prison case involving royal relatives, local elites, and officials.
- Source-scoped early Buddhist figure whose religious practice becomes part of the host's explanation of imperial suspicion.
- Disambiguated Liu Ying route kept separate from the Western Han emperor [[LiuYing|刘盈]].

## Qualifications

The page treats Liu Ying as the source set's case trigger, not as a fully reconstructed rebel organization. Hanji 737 shows tolerated Buddhist/Huang-Lao devotion; Hanji 739 records charges and punishment sequence but does not prove every later implication. Hanji 740's Buddhist-secret-society explanation remains interpretive and should not be generalized beyond the source without more evidence. Details about Wang Ping, Yan Zhong, Yan Guang, Xu Taihou, the objects, followers, exact charges, and the full legal sequence remain source-scoped.
