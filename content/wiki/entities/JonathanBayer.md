# Jonathan Bayer

## Overview

Jonathan Bayer is presented in [[running-hot|Running Hot]] as an EMS medical director in Berrien County, Michigan, who led a reduction in routine ambulance lights-and-sirens use.

## Current Profile

Bayer's central contribution is to frame warning-device use as a high-risk medical therapy rather than an automatic sign of emergency care. He preserves rapid response for conditions where minutes plausibly change survival while asking dispatchers and crews to avoid crash risk when a small delay is unlikely to alter the outcome.

The county's reform reportedly removed about half of previous lights-and-sirens responses. A comparison between initial ambulance classifications and hospital priorities found no increase in cases initially treated as lower priority that were later classified as priority one.

## Key Characteristics

- EMS medical director using outcome and safety evidence to revise dispatch practice.
- Treats lights and sirens as a selective intervention with benefits and harms.
- Retains urgency for cardiac arrest, choking, and respiratory arrest.
- Uses hospital reclassification as one check against dangerous under-triage.
- Communicates evidence publicly when residents interpret reduced siren use as slower care.

## Evidence

### Selective-response principle

- [[running-hot]] attributes to Bayer the high-risk-therapy analogy and the benefit-risk rule for warning-device use.

### Berrien County implementation

- [[running-hot]] reports that revised coding removed about half of lights-and-sirens responses while preserving priority-one treatment for narrow time-critical conditions.

### Outcome check and public explanation

- [[running-hot]] reports no increase in lower-priority patients later assigned priority one and says public complaints ended after he explained the evidence.

## Qualifications

This profile rests on one structured podcast summary rather than Bayer's biography, protocols, underlying studies, complete county data, or a comparative evaluation. Hospital priority is an incomplete proxy for patient harm, and one county's experience does not establish universal thresholds.
