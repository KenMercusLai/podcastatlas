# Revisionist History

## Overview

Revisionist History is [[MalcolmGladwell]]'s podcast, represented here by health-policy history, a live social-psychology conversation with [[AdamGrant]], and two connected treatments of policing and violence reduction.

## Current Profile

Across the bounded episodes, the show connects large institutional questions to personal stakes and closely observed scenes. A health-policy history moves from family cancer care through archival negotiation and counterfactual judgment to the political lineage of the [[AffordableCareAct]]. A live event uses autobiography, analogy, and friendly disagreement to test [[AdamGrant]]'s framework for relationship purpose and compatibility.

The Baltimore case adds investigative public policy: mayoral biography and criminological research lead into a weekly case-review room and one outreach worker's intervention. Across these forms, scale changes—from family to institution, conversation to framework, or city to network to relationship—are a recurring explanatory device.

The [[BrianLehrer]] conversation widens that branch from Baltimore practice to an argument about American violence as historically invented. It connects comparative police staffing, legitimacy and clearance, slavery-linked gun rights, frontier regulation, motive, and individualized prevention while preserving visible uncertainty around causation and historical interpretation.

## Key Characteristics

- Narrative history podcast connecting personal experience to institutional and policy change.
- Uses the 1974 Nixon-Kennedy negotiations as a lost-opportunity case.
- Frames later Massachusetts reform as part of the Affordable Care Act's political genealogy.
- Also publishes live intellectual conversations built around argument, personal examples, and research claims.
- Uses Gladwell's counterexamples and semantic reframing to pressure-test a guest's framework.
- Combines cross-city research with closely observed local scenes to explain a prevention mechanism.
- Uses an adversarial interview format to connect historical institutions with present policy tradeoffs.

## Qualifications

Only four episodes currently inform this page, so they do not establish the full range of the show. The health episode's “greatest lost chance” framing does not prove that the 1974 compromise could have passed Congress, the live event's research claims and personal stories are not independently verified here, and neither Baltimore's homicide sequence nor cross-national policing comparisons isolate the effect of the featured strategies.

## What Changed

- Expanded the show profile from policy narrative to include live, argumentative social-psychology conversation.
- Added investigative violence-reduction storytelling that moves between network research and relationship-level action.
- Extended the violence branch into historical gun institutions and contested police-capacity policy.
