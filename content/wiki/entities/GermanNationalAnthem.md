# German National Anthem / Das Lied der Deutschen

## Overview

The German national anthem is presented as *Das Lied der Deutschen*, a song whose official modern use centers the third verse rather than the first verse beginning "Deutschland über alles."

## Current Profile

The episode treats the anthem as a layered national symbol rather than a fixed ideological statement. Its melody comes from [[JosefHaydn]]'s 1797 Austrian imperial song, while [[AugustHeinrichHoffmannVonFallersleben]]'s 1841 text originally expressed liberal German unity above local state loyalties. Later use changed the song's public meaning: the [[WeimarRepublic]] made it official in 1922, [[NaziGermany]] emphasized the first verse and paired ceremony with the [[HorstWesselLied]], and [[WestGermany]] restored it only through a third-verse convention later carried into reunified [[Germany]].

## Key Characteristics

- Anthem whose current official public identity is anchored in the third verse's unity, rights, and freedom language.
- Musical object with an older Habsburg imperial melody reused for German national purposes.
- Text whose first verse moved from liberal unity-over-localism into a much darker post-Nazi interpretive field.
- Postwar symbol managed through [[SelectiveAnthemCanonization]] rather than full abandonment.
- Rivaled or challenged by [[AuferstandenAusRuinen]] and [[Kinderhymne]] during Germany's division and reunification debates.

## Evidence

- Current official focus: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] says modern Germany sings the third verse beginning "Einigkeit und Recht und Freiheit," not the first verse.
- Musical origin: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] traces the tune to [[JosefHaydn]]'s 1797 Austrian imperial anthem.
- Lyric origin: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] presents [[AugustHeinrichHoffmannVonFallersleben]] as a liberal nationalist writing for unity among German-speaking states in 1841.
- Regime passage: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] follows the song through [[WeimarRepublic]] adoption, Nazi emphasis on the first verse, West German restoration, and reunification.
- Alternatives and rivals: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] compares the song with [[HorstWesselLied]], [[AuferstandenAusRuinen]], and [[Kinderhymne]].

## Qualifications

The source does not treat every use of the first verse as identical with Nazi ideology. Its core caution is contextual: words that originally served liberal-national unity became harder to hear innocently after later militarist, Nazi, and postwar memories attached to them.
