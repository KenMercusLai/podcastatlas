# Le Morte Darthur

## Overview

*Le Morte Darthur* is [[ThomasMalory|Thomas Malory's]] fifteenth-century English Arthurian synthesis, whose account of [[Lancelot]] and [[Guinevere]], Mordred's betrayal, Camlann, Excalibur's return, Avalon, and the possibility of [[KingArthur|Arthur's]] return became especially influential after William Caxton printed it.

## Current Profile

[[713-king-arthur-the-once-future-king-part-1-glt2516166521]] treats the work as both inheritance and transformation. Its ending incorporates an early Camlann tradition and [[GeoffreyOfMonmouth|Geoffrey of Monmouth's]] developed story of Mordred and Avalon, but makes Arthur's mortality legible through the lost scabbard, the catastrophic last battle, Bedivere's delayed obedience, the mysterious barge, and competing reports of death or survival.

The work also speaks in the vocabulary of Malory's England. Mordred uses Parliament and the Tower of London, artillery appears, allegiances change, and wounded survivors are robbed and killed. Completed around 1469-1470 and later printed by Caxton, the synthesis turns the Round Table's destruction into a tragedy that could speak directly to readers living through the [[WarsOfTheRoses|Wars of the Roses]].

[[714-king-arthur-lancelot-and-the-round-table-part-2-glt6459272632]] extends that political tragedy through Lancelot. His affair gives enemies an opening; Guinevere's rescue kills Gareth and Gaheris; Gawain's vengeance draws Arthur into France; and Mordred seizes the resulting absence. The work does not reduce Lancelot to a villain: mercy, the healing of Sir Urry, tears, priesthood, and peaceful death preserve a theology of grace without cancelling consequence.

## Key Characteristics

- English synthesis of a long, multilingual Arthurian tradition rather than an origin point for the entire cycle.
- Influential narrative of Arthur's final conflict with Mordred and the destruction of the Round Table.
- Supernatural ending that withholds a definitive answer about Arthur's death or recovery.
- Work in which magical objects and prophecy coexist with Parliament, artillery, and recognizable fifteenth-century political violence.
- Printed text through which Caxton helped consolidate an Arthurian narrative for English readers.
- Tragedy in which exemplary knighthood and destructive adultery coexist in Lancelot.
- Penitential ending that preserves responsibility, forgiveness, renunciation, and grace.

## Qualifications

The episodes supply guided readings rather than a full account of manuscript versions, French and English sources, textual divisions, language, theology, or Caxton's editorial intervention. Modern references to a “unified” story should not erase the compilation's internal tensions or the wider multilingual tradition. Political-context and authorial-psychology readings do not make the work a one-to-one allegory or autobiography.

## What Changed

- Added Lancelot's divided excellence and the causal chain from exposed love to civil collapse.
- Added penitence and grace as part of the ending without weakening responsibility for harm.

## Relationships

- [[ThomasMalory]] - author and compiler who gave the work its English form.
- [[KingArthur]] - ruler whose fall and possible return organize the ending.
- [[Lancelot]] - knight whose virtues and adultery form the work's central moral contradiction.
- [[Guinevere]] - queen whose affair, rescue, and renunciation connect romance to tragedy.
- [[RoundTableFellowship]] - ideal whose destruction the work narrates through divided loyalties.
- [[GeoffreyOfMonmouth]] - earlier author whose Mordred, Camlann, and Avalon narrative feeds the later synthesis.
- [[ArthurianLegendAccretion]] - process through which the work inherits, reorganizes, and transmits earlier layers.
- [[WarsOfTheRoses]] - contemporary political setting that sharpens the work's civil-war tragedy.
