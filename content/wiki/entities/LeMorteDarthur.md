# Le Morte Darthur

## Overview

*Le Morte Darthur* is [[ThomasMalory|Thomas Malory's]] fifteenth-century English Arthurian synthesis, whose account of Mordred's betrayal, Camlann, Excalibur's return, Avalon, and the possibility of [[KingArthur|Arthur's]] return became especially influential after William Caxton printed it.

## Current Profile

[[713-king-arthur-the-once-future-king-part-1-glt2516166521]] treats the work as both inheritance and transformation. Its ending incorporates an early Camlann tradition and [[GeoffreyOfMonmouth|Geoffrey of Monmouth's]] developed story of Mordred and Avalon, but makes Arthur's mortality legible through the lost scabbard, the catastrophic last battle, Bedivere's delayed obedience, the mysterious barge, and competing reports of death or survival.

The work also speaks in the vocabulary of Malory's England. Mordred uses Parliament and the Tower of London, artillery appears, allegiances change, and wounded survivors are robbed and killed. Completed around 1469-1470 and later printed by Caxton, the synthesis turns the Round Table's destruction into a tragedy that could speak directly to readers living through the [[WarsOfTheRoses|Wars of the Roses]].

## Key Characteristics

- English synthesis of a long, multilingual Arthurian tradition rather than an origin point for the entire cycle.
- Influential narrative of Arthur's final conflict with Mordred and the destruction of the Round Table.
- Supernatural ending that withholds a definitive answer about Arthur's death or recovery.
- Work in which magical objects and prophecy coexist with Parliament, artillery, and recognizable fifteenth-century political violence.
- Printed text through which Caxton helped consolidate an Arthurian narrative for English readers.

## Qualifications

The episode supplies a guided reading of the ending rather than a full account of the work's manuscript versions, French and English sources, textual divisions, language, theology, or Caxton's editorial intervention. Modern references to a “unified” story should not erase the compilation's internal tensions or the wider multilingual tradition. Political-context readings do not make the work a one-to-one allegory of named Wars of the Roses figures.

## What Changed

- Established the work as a layered synthesis whose unresolved ending is central to Arthur's endurance.
- Distinguished inherited Arthurian material from Malory's fifteenth-century political vocabulary and Caxton's printed afterlife.
