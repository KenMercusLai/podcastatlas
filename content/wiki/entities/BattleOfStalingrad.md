# Battle of Stalingrad

## Overview

The Battle of Stalingrad was the Second World War struggle in which Soviet forces held a narrow urban bridgehead on the Volga, encircled the German Sixth Army through [[OperationUranus]], and forced its surrender after failed supply and relief efforts.

## Current Profile

[[215-stalingrad-and-the-red-army-part-2-glt4269997308]] presents the battle at two connected scales. Inside the city, Soviet troops adapted to German artillery and air power through [[SovietUrbanActiveDefense|small storm teams]], fortified buildings, close-range combat, and observation across the Volga corridor. [[PavlovsHouse|Pavlov's House]] represents this layer without standing for it alone.

At operational scale, Soviet commanders exploited the extended front and weaker Romanian-led flanks rather than trying to win only through frontal combat inside the city. Once the pincers met at Kalach, Hitler's hold order, the inadequate Luftwaffe airlift, the failed Winter Storm relief attempt, Soviet pressure, hunger, disease, cold, and psychological warfare reduced the pocket. The episode treats the eventual surrender as both a military outcome and the beginning of an enduring [[StalingradWarMemory|memory struggle]].

## Key Characteristics

- Joined house-to-house urban attrition to a wider steppe encirclement campaign.
- Required Soviet maintenance of supply, reinforcement, evacuation, observation, and command links across the Volga.
- Revealed effective Soviet adaptation in small-team fighting and combined-arms operations.
- Turned the Sixth Army's tactical presence into a logistical pocket after Operation Uranus.
- Was prolonged by Hitler's refusal to authorize withdrawal and by failed air and ground relief.
- Ended through progressive fragmentation, surrender, and clearance of remaining northern holdouts.
- Became a central symbol of the Soviet and Russian Great Patriotic War.

## Qualifications

This page reflects the second installment of a popular-history discussion rather than a comprehensive campaign history. It begins after the German advance and much of the urban battle are already underway, and it gives limited treatment to Soviet losses, civilian experience, Romanian and other Axis-allied perspectives, command disputes, or the full chronology of the Caucasus campaign. Numbers and causal weight remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Established the battle as an interaction between urban adaptation, operational encirclement, logistical collapse, and public memory.
