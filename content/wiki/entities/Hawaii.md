# Hawaii / 夏威夷

## Overview

Hawaii is a Polynesian island group presented across the sources as an Indigenous kingdom navigating European and American contact before becoming a plantation-labor, migration, banking, and U.S. territorial node.

## Current Profile

[[12-days-reconquest-of-spain-and-the-old-queen-of-hawaii-glt9407596415]] supplies the earlier political layer. Polynesian settlement preceded European contact by centuries; after Cook, weapons and disease intensified disruption, Kamehameha I unified the islands, and nineteenth-century rulers used monarchy, Christianity, travel, diplomacy, and foreign advisers to manage British and American pressure. [[KamehamehaIV]] and [[QueenEmmaOfHawaii|Queen Emma]] embodied a British-facing royal strategy through Anglican institutions, philanthropy, and Queen Victoria's court, but Emma lost the 1874 election to Kalakaua and the monarchy later fell amid American commercial pressure.

[[168-yin-yu-xin-yibu-riben-laogong-yimin-de-kuayang-huikuan-shi-lmgfmmgkawoa-6xyucnm1ljebzwu]] follows the plantation economy that helped reshape Hawaii before U.S. annexation. Sugar employers drew Japanese contract workers under difficult conditions; the Meiji state then tightened migration, savings, and remittance oversight. Migrants connected plantation labor to families in [[Japan]], consulates, postal transfers, [[YokohamaSpecieBank|Yokohama Specie Bank]], and later movement to the U.S. West Coast.

## Key Characteristics

- Indigenous Polynesian society unified as a kingdom under Kamehameha I after European contact.
- Monarchy that selectively adopted Christian, diplomatic, and royal institutions while negotiating foreign power.
- Strategic arena in which British cultural influence and American commercial influence overlapped and competed.
- Sugar-plantation economy dependent on imported contract labor, including large Japanese migration.
- Remittance and banking node connecting workers, families, consulates, employers, and the Japanese state.
- Bridge into later U.S. territorial, racial, labor, and migration politics.

## Evidence

- Monarchical adaptation and foreign pressure: [[12-days-reconquest-of-spain-and-the-old-queen-of-hawaii-glt9407596415]] connects unification, missionary influence, royal travel, Anglican institutions, Emma's 1874 candidacy, and later American commercial pressure.
- Royal social institutions: [[12-days-reconquest-of-spain-and-the-old-queen-of-hawaii-glt9407596415]] presents Queen Emma's hospital and educational work as a response to disease and public need.
- Contract-labor system: [[168-yin-yu-xin-yibu-riben-laogong-yimin-de-kuayang-huikuan-shi-lmgfmmgkawoa-6xyucnm1ljebzwu]] describes the poorly treated 1868 workers and large official migration to Hawaiian sugar plantations after 1885.
- Remittance infrastructure: [[168-yin-yu-xin-yibu-riben-laogong-yimin-de-kuayang-huikuan-shi-lmgfmmgkawoa-6xyucnm1ljebzwu]] connects compulsory savings, return-fare reserves, consular administration, postal channels, and Yokohama Specie Bank.

## Qualifications

The anniversary episode openly presents its Hawaii section as an introductory sketch and compresses centuries of Indigenous history, political conflict, disease, missionary activity, succession, and overthrow. Its British-American contrast and 1874 election framing should not turn Hawaiian rulers into proxies without independent aims. The labor-remittance episode begins later and focuses mainly on Japanese institutional records, so neither source supplies a complete Native Hawaiian social history or full account of annexation.

## What Changed

- Added the Indigenous monarchy, Queen Emma, Anglican, philanthropic, and foreign-pressure history preceding the labor-migration branch.
- Reframed plantation labor and remittance infrastructure as part of a longer transition from kingdom to U.S.-dominated political economy.
