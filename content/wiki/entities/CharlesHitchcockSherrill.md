# Charles Hitchcock Sherrill

## Overview

Charles Hitchcock Sherrill is presented as an American lawyer, diplomat, military officer, athlete, and [[InternationalOlympicCommittee|IOC]] member who helped secure United States participation in the 1936 Berlin Olympics.

## Current Profile

[[hitler-s-olympics-part-2-pangloss-polonius-prufrock-hitler-s-olympics-part-2-pangloss-polonius-prufrock|Hitler’s Olympics, Part 2]] grounds Sherrill's political judgment in a longer pattern of privilege and self-certainty. Yale sprinting success, a disputed claim to have invented the crouching start, law, diplomacy, presidential access, voluminous memoirs, and carefully assembled scrapbooks let him turn personal anecdotes into evidence of courage and importance. The episode's Polonius, Pangloss, Prufrock, and “Mr. B” comparisons describe a conventional insider whose optimism and attraction to whatever appeared successful weakened his skepticism toward authoritarian power.

That disposition produced a procedural victory with a moral blind spot. At the IOC's 1933 Vienna meeting, Sherrill helped obtain a written German assurance that Jewish athletes would not be excluded from Olympic selection. He celebrated the promise as a defense of American equality even though Nazi Germany was already attacking Jewish people and excluding them from professions and sports. By separating domestic persecution from team eligibility, he helped turn a regime-controlled promise into sufficient reason for Olympic planning to continue.

[[hitler-s-olympics-part-3-mustache-to-mustache-hitler-s-olympics-part-3-mustache-to-mustache|Hitler’s Olympics, Part 3]] carries that pattern forward through the elite social order that rewarded him. Wealth, Yale affiliation, public honors, and access to powerful men encouraged a code of optimism and mutual deference in which hierarchy looked legitimate and criticism from excluded groups looked disorderly.

That disposition shaped his Olympic diplomacy. Sherrill admired [[BenitoMussolini]], praised [[AdolfHitler]] after meeting him in August 1935, attended the Nuremberg Rally, and sought one visible Jewish athlete who could quiet American boycott pressure. [[HeleneMayer]]'s invitation let him claim success without challenging Nazi control over Jewish sport or the wider persecution formalized by the [[NurembergLaws|Nuremberg Laws]].

His final posture joined institutional closure to moral reversal. After American participation was decided, he called for peace, blamed Jewish critics for worsening antisemitism, and withdrew his claimed friendship when challenged. The episode therefore treats him less as a doctrinal Nazi than as a status-oriented accommodator whose prejudice and devotion to successful institutions made complicity respectable.

## Key Characteristics

- Elite American Olympic official who treated a written eligibility promise and preservation of the Berlin Games as diplomatic achievements.
- Patrician antisemite whose exclusionary assumptions differed from but accommodated Nazi persecution.
- Admirer of strongmen, ceremony, prestige, decorations, and personal recognition.
- Self-mythologizing optimist whose successful life reinforced confidence in his judgment and the existing social hierarchy.
- Advocate of token inclusion as an answer to systemic exclusion.
- Self-dramatizing intermediary who framed diplomacy as a contest among important men.
- Defender of institutional closure who recast further protest as a threat to peace.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to two interpretive podcast summaries. It does not independently verify Sherrill's speeches, books, memoir, scrapbooks, athletic claims, private motives, exact influence on Hitler, or causal responsibility for the American participation decision. Part 2's literary typology and Part 3's class analysis are explanatory interpretations rather than clinical or deterministic findings. The sources distinguish his genteel antisemitism from Nazi exterminatory ideology without treating that difference as exculpatory.

## What Changed

- Grounded Sherrill's later strongman accommodation in an earlier pattern of self-mythology, elite confidence, and weak skepticism toward success.
- Recast the 1933 written guarantee as the procedural precursor to the later token-athlete solution.
