# La Marseillaise

## Overview

La Marseillaise is the French revolutionary war song composed by [[RougetDeLisle|Rouget de Lisle]] in April 1792 and later adopted as the anthem of the French Republic.

## Current Profile

The song began as the "Song of the Army of the Rhine" after France declared war on Austria. Its route through soldiers, Jacobin networks, and armed Marseille volunteers transformed a Strasbourg composition into a Parisian emblem of citizenship, anti-tyranny, national defence, and emerging republicanism. Its power therefore lay not only in lyrics or melody but in [[CitizenSoldierSymbolicLegitimacy|who performed it]], while its blood imagery made liberation and revolutionary violence difficult to separate.

## Key Characteristics

- It was written for wartime patriotic mobilization rather than as a settled national anthem.
- Its lyrics call citizens to arms against tyranny without invoking monarchy.
- Marseille fédérés gave the song its enduring name and public political identity.
- Its emotional force joined liberation, danger, purification, and violence.
- It remained politically contestable even among revolutionaries before becoming official in 1795.

## Evidence

### Wartime origin and circulation

- [[507-the-french-revolution-the-marseillaise-song-of-war-part-5-glt5535769124]] follows the song from Rouget de Lisle's Strasbourg composition through Mireur's Marseille performance and printed distribution.

### Citizen performance and republican meaning

- [[507-the-french-revolution-the-marseillaise-song-of-war-part-5-glt5535769124]] says armed southern volunteers carried the song to Paris, where its citizen language and absence of kings made it implicitly republican.

### Contest and afterlife

- [[507-the-french-revolution-the-marseillaise-song-of-war-part-5-glt5535769124]] records Girondin parody, later anxiety about violent lyrics, 1795 official adoption, monarchical banning, and eventual national-anthem status.

## Qualifications

- Possible musical antecedents do not establish that Rouget de Lisle copied one identified composition.
- Its later status as a national anthem should not be projected unchanged onto its 1792 origin as a marching song.
- The source does not provide a complete institutional history of every ban, restoration, arrangement, or lyrical controversy.

## What Changed

- Added the anthem as a revolutionary object whose political meaning emerged through circulation and armed citizen performance.
