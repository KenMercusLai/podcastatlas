# 《自明本志令》 / Self-Clarification Edict

## Overview

《自明本志令》 / Self-Clarification Edict is [[CaoCao|曹操]]'s retrospective political self-account as presented in Hanji 1115. It narrates a rise from limited early ambition to northern consolidation, answers suspicion of imperial ambition, and draws a sharp boundary between yielding part of a fief and surrendering military command.

## Current Profile

The edict joins autobiography to political argument. Cao Cao recalls early office, enforcement against powerful people, withdrawal to study and hunt, return to service, and an initial ambition no greater than being remembered as a Han general. He then lists campaigns and settlements to explain how circumstances enlarged his power.

The central defense is not a simple denial of ambition. Cao Cao claims that his presence prevents additional kings and emperors, concedes that concentrated authority invites suspicion, and offers to return part of his fief. Yet he refuses to relinquish military command because doing so would endanger himself, his descendants, and the political order he says his force protects. Hanji 1115 therefore reads the text as candid self-defense, reputation management, and [[PowerRetentionSelfJustification|self-justified power retention]] at once.

## Key Characteristics

- Retrospective account that turns personal career history into a claim of political necessity.
- Presents modest initial ambition as a contrast to power accumulated through disorder and war.
- Answers suspicion of usurpation without denying the reality of concentrated authority.
- Offers partial material or symbolic concession while retaining military command.
- Merges personal and dynastic safety with a wider claim about preventing fragmentation.

## Qualifications

The current page follows the podcast's modern-language summary rather than a line-by-line edition of the underlying text. Title variants, textual transmission, exact wording, dates, and the relationship between stated motives and strategic rhetoric require direct textual scholarship. The edict is evidence for how Cao Cao publicly framed his position, not transparent access to every private motive.
