# Crazy Horse

## Overview

Crazy Horse was an Oglala [[LakotaPeople|Lakota]] war leader whose post-Little Bighorn resistance, surrender at Fort Robinson, isolation, attempted imprisonment, and killing make him a central figure in the episode's account of conquest becoming [[CoerciveReservationAssimilation|coercive confinement]].

## Current Profile

The episode begins after Little Bighorn, when tactical victory gave way to winter pursuit, hunger, scarce clothing, political division, and overwhelming U.S. capacity. Crazy Horse did not follow [[SittingBull]] to Canada. After defeat at Wolf Mountain and an unsuccessful vision quest, he returned to followers who had decided that continued resistance was untenable and surrendered at Fort Robinson on 6 May 1877.

Peace did not produce security. The episode says Crazy Horse expected an agency west of the Black Hills and a buffalo hunt, but those expectations would not be honored. He refused a Washington visit, faced rivalry from other leaders, and became the object of official fear after Frank Grouard translated a statement about fighting a northern enemy as a threat against every white man. When officers planned to send him to Chicago and then Florida, he resisted entry into a guardhouse and was fatally bayoneted. The narrative treats his death as the endpoint of military pressure, broken trust, factional rivalry, and possible interpretive betrayal, while keeping exact motives and wording uncertain.

## Key Characteristics

- He combined battlefield prestige with limited ability to sustain resistance against U.S. logistics, winter campaigning, and subsistence pressure.
- His refusal to invent guidance after an unsuccessful vision quest is presented as evidence of personal integrity.
- His surrender rested on constrained collective choice and expectations that the episode says were unlikely to be fulfilled.
- Rival leaders, interpreters, officers, and removal policy progressively narrowed his room to act after surrender.
- A disputed translation turned ambiguous military cooperation into evidence of a generalized threat.
- His resistance to imprisonment ended in a killing that the episode presents as tragic rather than inevitable.

## Qualifications

- This profile rests on one dramatic popular-history episode rather than a comparison of Lakota oral history, military records, biographies, and scholarship.
- The promised agency and hunt, Grouard's intent, named rivals' motives, Crazy Horse's visions and mental state, the precise struggle, and his last words remain source-scoped.
- Describing Native rivalry and assistance to U.S. forces must not obscure the unequal military and political system structuring those choices.
- The episode's symbolic reading of the dead eagle, dream, floor, and manner of death is interpretation rather than independent evidence.

## What Changed

- Established a profile joining post-Little Bighorn resistance to the coercive conditions of surrender.
- Distinguished formal surrender from safety, trust, or accepted reservation incorporation.
- Preserved mistranslation and betrayal as central but unresolved causes of the arrest crisis.
- Connected his death to the transition from open warfare into confinement and removal.

## Relationships

- [[LakotaPeople]] - people and political community within which he led, surrendered, and faced rival authority.
- [[SittingBull]] - fellow resistance leader who chose Canadian exile rather than Crazy Horse's southern surrender path.
- [[CoerciveReservationAssimilation]] - system that joined military exhaustion to dependency, factional governance, and threatened removal.
