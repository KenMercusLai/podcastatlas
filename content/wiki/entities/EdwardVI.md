# Edward VI

## Overview

Edward VI is the highly educated young Protestant king whose final succession plan attempts to preserve religious reform by excluding his half-sisters [[MaryI|Mary I]] and [[ElizabethI|Elizabeth I]] and placing [[LadyJaneGrey|Lady Jane Grey]] next in line.

## Current Profile

Edward's illness accelerates rather than fully explains the plan. His first draft favors male heirs of Jane and her sisters; after physicians conclude that he is likely to die, he revises the language to Jane and her heirs male. The change combines Protestant preservation with male-line preference, then crosses its own gender boundary because no male heir exists in time.

The device is the legal-documentary foundation of Jane's accession but not a self-executing settlement. Its confessional aim collides with the stronger public intelligibility of Mary's hereditary and statutory position, while the exclusion of Protestant Elizabeth shows that gender and prior illegitimacy matter alongside religion.

Edward's intellectual ability, independent-mindedness, and intense reforming commitment also qualify a simple theory that [[JohnDudleyDukeOfNorthumberland|Northumberland]] designed everything. Northumberland's family interest and implementation role remain substantial, but the improvised collapse suggests multiple authors, weak operational planning, and insufficient acceptance.

## Key Characteristics

- Edward is presented as an intellectually capable and committed reformer with real agency.
- He seeks to preserve Protestant succession beyond his own death.
- His draft changes from hypothetical male heirs to Jane herself as his death approaches.
- His plan excludes both Mary and Elizabeth rather than only the Catholic heir.
- The plan supplies Jane with a documentary claim but cannot by itself create political recognition.
- Its legality and success depend on adult councillors, judges, parliamentary authority, military control, and neutralizing rival claimants.

## Evidence

- Capacity and conviction: [[293-lady-jane-grey-the-nine-days-queen-part-1-glt2281550812]] rejects the weak-puppet image and emphasizes Edward's education, independence, and Protestant iconoclasm.
- Drafting and confessional design: [[293-lady-jane-grey-the-nine-days-queen-part-1-glt2281550812]] follows the change from male heirs to Jane herself and separates Catholic exclusion from gender and legitimacy arguments.
- Legal conflict: [[293-lady-jane-grey-the-nine-days-queen-part-1-glt2281550812]] notes that councillors, judges, and peers signed the device but Parliament did not enact it, unlike Henry VIII's succession settlement.
- Implementation failure: [[294-lady-jane-grey-the-axe-falls-part-2-glt7286066638]] shows that proclamation, force, and council backing collapse when Mary remains free and gathers support.

## Qualifications

The sources do not settle Edward's exact diagnosis, the division of initiative among Edward, Northumberland, judges, and councillors, or the legal efficacy of a royal device set against statute and Henry VIII's will. Recognizing Edward's agency does not make the plan solely his or remove the pressure that powerful adults could apply to a dying teenager.

- Added the device's two-stage drafting and its shift from male-line preference to Jane.
