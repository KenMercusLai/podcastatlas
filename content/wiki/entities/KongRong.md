## Overview

Kong Rong is a late-Han scholar-official whose current profile combines real moral courage and Confucian prestige with poor governing judgment, repeated public attacks on concentrated power, and a final prosecution in which political opposition is packaged through disloyalty and “unfilial” character accusations.

## Current Profile

Kong Rong first appears as a sixteen-year-old who shelters [[ZhangJianLateHan|张俭]] during the 169 CE party-prohibition danger and tries to bear the capital charge instead of [[KongBaoLateHan|孔褒]]. As an adult, he becomes a prestige actor: he helps legitimate [[LiuBei|刘备]] in the [[Xuzhou|徐州]] succession, honors [[ZhengXuan|郑玄]], recommends [[MiHengLateHan|祢衡]], carries imperial recognition to [[YuanShao|袁绍]], and confronts [[CaoCao|曹操]] over the weakly grounded prosecution of [[YangBiao|杨彪]].

The evidence also preserves serious limits. In [[BeihaiCommandery|北海郡]], Kong Rong does not convert lineage, literature, and moral reputation into grain, troops, strategic flexibility, or a safe advice channel; he kills [[ZuoChengzu|左承祖]], loses [[LiuYixun|刘义逊]], and is defeated by [[YuanTan|袁谭]]. Hanji 1089 then traces a public-speech escalation with [[CaoCao|曹操]] through the liquor-ban argument, campaign mockery, and the fabricated King Wu-Daji analogy aimed at [[CaoPi|曹丕]]'s taking of [[YuanXiLateHan|袁熙]]'s wife. Hanji 1090 completes the arc in 208 CE: after a capital-region enfeoffment proposal is read as threatening Cao Cao's base, [[LuCuiLateHan|路粹]] bundles old political, association, speech, and morality accusations into a death case. Kong Rong and [[KongRongChildren|his children]] are executed, while the “unfilial” label remains in tension with his earlier self-sacrifice and reported mourning conduct.

## Key Characteristics

- Early moral-risk taker who shelters Zhang Jian and claims responsibility under party-prohibition pressure.
- Confucian-prestige actor whose lineage, childhood reputation, literature, and old-Han standing carry weight in legitimacy disputes.
- Public advocate who supports Liu Bei, recommends Mi Heng, carries court recognition, and challenges Cao Cao over Yang Biao.
- Beihai governor whose prestige does not become durable military, administrative, or advisory capacity.
- Direct speaker whose satire targets policy, military conduct, household scandal, and rulerly legitimacy, repeatedly increasing political exposure.
- Politically prosecuted critic whose attributed private-morality statements are used alongside security and hierarchy charges.
- Household head whose execution expands to his young children, turning political defeat into family destruction.

## Qualifications

Kong Rong should not be flattened into childhood virtue, Beihai incompetence, reckless arrogance, or a pure free-speech martyr. Hanji 895 supports real early courage, while Hanji 1007 supports a real governance and advice-tolerance failure. Hanji 1089's awakening-versus-arrogance frame and its reconstruction of Cao Cao's anger are host interpretations; corrupt names, exact wording, family details, and private motives are not independently established. Hanji 1090's political-prosecution reading is likewise plausible inside its narrative but does not independently establish the exact proposal, charge text, sayings, dialogue, or legal procedure. The “unfilial” label and countervailing filial anecdotes remain a contested reputation field rather than a simple character verdict.

## What Changed

- Hanji 1089 adds a pre-prosecution escalation chain in which policy satire expands into attacks on Cao Cao's military and household legitimacy.
- The prestige-capacity judgment now includes failure to calibrate cultural authority and speech to concentrated coercive power, not only failure to govern Beihai.
- The page now treats the host's “arrogance” diagnosis as a qualified interpretation rather than allowing it to erase Kong Rong's earlier courage or the political construction of his final case.
