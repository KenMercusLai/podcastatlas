# Tower of London

## Overview

The Tower of London is a royal fortress presented here as the home of a medieval and early-modern menagerie whose animals embodied diplomatic reach, monarchical symbolism, public spectacle, and eventually the inadequacy of inherited captive-care arrangements.

## Current Profile

The episode begins the menagerie with Frederick II's gift of three lions to Henry III in 1235, then adds a Norwegian polar bear allowed to fish in the Thames and a French royal elephant. Permanent facilities and later public viewing made the collection a durable part of the Tower rather than a brief accumulation of gifts.

By the nineteenth century, the institution appeared shabby and unsuitable. The Duke of Wellington's removal of animals toward Regent's Park and the later closure of the menagerie mark a shift from royal possession toward specialized zoological institutions and stronger welfare concern. A reported ghost bear near the Crown Jewels preserves the collection in Tower folklore.

## Key Characteristics

- Royal site where diplomatic animal gifts became visible signs of prestige and international connection.
- Long-lived menagerie that developed from court possession toward public spectacle.
- Captive environment constrained by limited species knowledge and unsuitable urban facilities.
- Institutional case in the nineteenth-century separation of a fortress from zoological care.
- Folkloric setting where the vanished menagerie survives through a ghost-bear story.

## Qualifications

This profile covers the Tower only through one episode's menagerie theme, not its wider military, carceral, residential, ceremonial, or tourist history. Species identifications, court beliefs, dates, animal totals, welfare motives, and the ghost report remain source-scoped. Relocation does not by itself establish that later zoological captivity met modern welfare standards.
