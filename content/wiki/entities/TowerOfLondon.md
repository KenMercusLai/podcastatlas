# Tower of London

## Overview

The Tower of London is a royal fortress whose current evidence profile joins conventional royal lodging and coercive custody during the 1483 succession crisis to its long medieval and early-modern menagerie.

## Current Profile

The princes episode stresses that Edward V's initial lodging in the Tower was not inherently evidence of a murder plan: it was a recognized royal residence before coronation. Its meaning changed as the coronation was postponed, Richard of Shrewsbury joined his brother, servants were dismissed, access narrowed, and both boys disappeared under Richard III's control.

The menagerie episode begins a separate branch with Frederick II's gift of three lions to Henry III in 1235, then adds a Norwegian polar bear allowed to fish in the Thames and a French royal elephant. Permanent facilities and later public viewing made the collection a durable part of the Tower rather than a brief accumulation of gifts.

By the nineteenth century, the institution appeared shabby and unsuitable. The Duke of Wellington's removal of animals toward Regent's Park and the later closure of the menagerie mark a shift from royal possession toward specialized zoological institutions and stronger welfare concern. A reported ghost bear near the Crown Jewels preserves the collection in Tower folklore.

## Key Characteristics

- Royal site where diplomatic animal gifts became visible signs of prestige and international connection.
- Royal residence whose ordinary lodging function could become coercive when one faction monopolized access.
- Central location in the disappearance and probable murder of the princes in the Tower.
- Long-lived menagerie that developed from court possession toward public spectacle.
- Captive environment constrained by limited species knowledge and unsuitable urban facilities.
- Institutional case in the nineteenth-century separation of a fortress from zoological care.
- Folkloric setting where the vanished menagerie survives through a ghost-bear story.

## Evidence

- Royal display: [[213-london-moments-part-5-glt4986306476]] links the lions, polar bear, and elephant to royal donors and exotic-animal symbolism.
- Public institution and care limits: [[213-london-moments-part-5-glt4986306476]] follows permanent facilities, public viewing, deterioration, transfer, and closure.
- Folkloric afterlife: [[213-london-moments-part-5-glt4986306476]] recounts the 1816 apparition and the hosts' playful attempt to identify it.
- Royal lodging and controlled confinement: [[139-the-princes-in-the-tower-part-2-glt4685691538]] distinguishes Edward V's initially conventional residence from the later isolation and disappearance of both princes.

## Qualifications

This remains a partial profile rather than a full military, carceral, residential, ceremonial, or tourist history. The princes source does not establish the murder site, method, agents, or remains. Species identifications, court beliefs, dates, animal totals, welfare motives, and the ghost report remain source-scoped; relocation does not by itself establish that later zoological captivity met modern welfare standards.

## What Changed

- Expanded the profile from the menagerie to the Tower's dual role as royal lodging and controlled confinement in 1483.
- Distinguished ordinary pre-coronation residence from the later conditions that make the princes' disappearance politically suspicious.
