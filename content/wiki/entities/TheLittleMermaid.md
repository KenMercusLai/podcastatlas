# 《海的女儿》 / The Little Mermaid

## Overview

《海的女儿》 / *The Little Mermaid* is [[HansChristianAndersen|Hans Christian Andersen]]'s tale of a mermaid who gives up her voice and accepts bodily pain to enter the human world, pursue love, and seek an immortal soul. The wiki distinguishes this tragic literary work from the [[TheWaltDisneyCompany|Disney]] adaptation built around Ariel, Ursula, romantic fulfillment, and a violent good-versus-evil resolution.

## Current Profile

The original resists a romance-only reading. The prince also signifies land, churches, forests, unknown freedom, human life, and spiritual possibility. Andersen's mermaid is quiet, isolated, and fascinated by the surface world; the sea witch states the terrible cost, the prince and his bride are not villains, the sisters sacrifice for her, and she ultimately refuses revenge before dissolving into foam and entering a possible path toward salvation. [[55-antusheng-tonghua-hai-de-nver-he-bumie-de-linghun-639782306]]

The adaptation comparison also supports a qualified autobiographical reading. Andersen's pain, voicelessness, outsider longing, and rejection are connected to an unreturned attachment before composition. Disney preserves transformation and lost voice but converts the tragic structure into good versus evil: Ursula offers Ariel an exploitative golden contract, treats it as unbreakable, interferes with performance, and is killed by Eric after no institution provides a remedy. [[little-mermaid-part-1-the-golden-contract-little-mermaid-part-1-the-golden-contract|Little Mermaid Part 1: The Golden Contract]]

The two sources agree that Disney's cultural dominance can overwrite the original, but they emphasize different boundaries. The earlier discussion treats Andersen's sea witch as an explicit broker of a tragic exchange rather than a simple villain; the later episode treats Disney's Ursula as a deceptive and obstructive legal antagonist. Keeping the versions separate prevents an apparent character contradiction.

## Key Characteristics

- Fairy tale about transformation, outsider longing, bodily pain, lost voice, love, and spiritual aspiration.
- Tragedy in which the central figures are not organized into a simple hero-villain moral structure.
- Story whose prince and human world represent freedom and immortal-soul desire as well as romance.
- Qualified autobiographical work whose exclusion and unreturned love echo Andersen's reported experience.
- Source text substantially reorganized by Disney into romantic success, villainy, contractual exploitation, and violent rescue.
- Major case of adaptation memory replacing public knowledge of an original text.

## Qualifications

The source notes are podcast interpretations, not a complete textual, biographical, legal, or adaptation history. The autobiographical parallel does not prove a single authorial intention. Spiritual, romantic, queer, class, disability, gender, and adaptation readings can overlap without one exhausting the tale. The Disney contract critique is general rather than jurisdiction-specific, and its claim about children's moral learning is not supported here by direct causal evidence.
