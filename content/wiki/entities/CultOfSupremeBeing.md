# Cult of the Supreme Being

## Overview

The Cult of the Supreme Being was [[MaximilienRobespierre|Robespierre]]'s deist civic religion, intended to ground universal rights and revolutionary virtue in a transcendent moral order.

## Current Profile

The episode presents the cult not as an eccentric side project but as an attempted exit from permanent terror. Robespierre argued that an all-seeing Supreme Being guaranteed justice, inscribed morality in the human heart, and could make citizens regulate themselves. Its great festival on 8 June 1794, designed by [[JacquesLouisDavid|Jacques-Louis David]], staged this theory through mass choirs, allegorical destruction of atheism, procession, and a mountain on the Champ de Mars. Public enthusiasm coexisted with accusations that Robespierre was making himself priest, master, or god of the Revolution.

## Key Characteristics

- It joined deism to universal morality, natural rights, justice, and civic virtue.
- It offered divine observation as a hoped-for substitute for indefinite state surveillance and coercion.
- It opposed atheism without simply restoring established Christianity.
- Its festival used spectacle, music, allegory, landscape, and political office to embody belief.
- Its inclusive ambition intensified fears of Robespierre's moral and personal supremacy.

## Evidence

### Moral doctrine

- [[711-the-terror-killing-god-part-5-glt6491220072]] connects the Supreme Being to sacred natural rights, eternal justice, continual observation, and Robespierre's hope that virtue could become voluntary.

### Festival and political reception

- [[711-the-terror-killing-god-part-5-glt6491220072]] describes the Tuileries and Champ de Mars ceremonies, David's design, the destruction of atheism, mass participation, popular enjoyment, and hostile reactions among deputies.

## Qualifications

- The cult retained Christian and especially Protestant features while attempting to displace Christianity; it was neither orthodox Christianity nor simple secularism.
- Festival popularity does not establish durable assent to its theology or prove that it could replace coercive government.
- Claims about attendance, private belief, Robespierre's emotional state, and opponents' motives remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- A canonical page now records the cult as a political theory, mass ritual, and failed strategy for internalizing revolutionary virtue.
