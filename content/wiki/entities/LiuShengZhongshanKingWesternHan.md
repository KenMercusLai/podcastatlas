## Overview

中山王刘胜 / Liu Sheng is presented as a long-reigning Western Han vassal king who publicly complains about official pressure on royal kin, whose received reputation for wine and sex is later politically reinterpreted, and whose excavated burial shows the extraordinary material scale and jade-suit beliefs of elite Han death culture.

## Current Profile

Hanji 252 adds an active political role before the later reputation. At a royal banquet, Liu Sheng reportedly weeps and argues that court officials use factional pressure to estrange [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] from Liu royal kin. Wudi then improves princely treatment and reduces constraints. The scene makes Liu Sheng a successful royal complainant in the episode's account, although it does not establish how broad, durable, or administratively specific the policy change was.

Hanji 343-1's most useful contribution is not a settled claim about Liu Sheng's inner motives but a contrast between reputation and political effect. The host argues that a pleasure-centered, noncompetitive public image may have made Liu Sheng appear harmless to Wudi, allowing him to avoid the danger that ambition could bring to a vassal king. That reading fits [[SelfDeprecatingPoliticalSurvival|自污式政治自保]], but remains an attributed hypothesis because the source provides no direct evidence that Liu Sheng deliberately constructed the image.

The biographical frame is similarly bounded by the episode: Liu Sheng is said to have been enfeoffed at twelve, ruled for forty-one years, died at fifty-three, and had more than 120 children according to the *Hanshu*. These details establish the profile supplied by the episode, not an independently verified full biography.

Liu Sheng's archaeological afterlife adds a different dimension to the profile. The 1968 excavation of tombs associated with Liu Sheng and his wife is said to have yielded more than ten thousand objects, including the Changxin Palace Lamp, a coiled-dragon bronze vessel, and gold-thread jade suits. Those finds are used as evidence that Western Han royal thick burial could concentrate exceptional wealth and that jade clothing combined rank display with a belief in bodily or vital continuity after death. ## Key Characteristics

- Western Han vassal king associated in the episode with a long and politically stable tenure.
- Royal complainant who reportedly links official pressure to estrangement inside the Liu house and obtains more favorable treatment.
- Historically characterized as immersed in wine and sex rather than active in central power competition.
- Interpreted by the host as possibly using visible indulgence and nonambition to reduce imperial suspicion.
- Reported to have been enfeoffed at twelve, reigned for forty-one years, and died at fifty-three.
- Credited by the episode's reading of the *Hanshu* with more than 120 children.
- Associated in the second episode with a richly furnished excavated tomb and a gold-thread jade suit.

## Qualifications

The current profile is grounded in three short, structured podcast summaries. They do not reproduce the relevant primary passages, define the officials accused by Liu Sheng, or measure the duration and scope of Wudi's response. They also do not establish that the later negative characterization originated in the *Hanshu* in precisely this form or demonstrate deliberate strategic intent. Long survival and visible indulgence are compatible with the host's explanation, but they do not by themselves prove it; pleasure, privilege, later moral framing, and political signaling could overlap. The archaeological episode is not an excavation report, so its object totals, “national treasure” classification, spouse-tomb wording, and account of jade-suit rank and belief remain source-scoped. Excavation establishes burial objects, not the efficacy of bodily preservation or resurrection claims.
