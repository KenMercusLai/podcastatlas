## Overview

The [[HiroshimaAtomicBombing|Hiroshima atomic bombing]] appears as both a nuclear-weapons memory case and the turning point in [[JRobertOppenheimer|J. Robert Oppenheimer]]'s postwar moral and political life. The sources join sudden bodily destruction to continuing disputes about military necessity, political decision-making, scientific responsibility, and later nuclear-risk policy.

## Current Profile

Hanji 394-3 presents Hiroshima through the B-29 mission, the “Little Boy” bomb, intense light and heat, immediate casualties, and the host's claim that Japanese authorities initially obscured what had happened. [[344-oppenheimer-the-witch-hunt-part-2-glt1594914557]] adds the American decision and Los Alamos aftermath: Washington rather than Oppenheimer made the use decision, while invasion casualties, war termination, Soviet signaling, and Japanese surrender prospects remain contested explanations.

Together the sources make Hiroshima a meeting point of victim memory and distributed responsibility. The bombing anchors later arguments about Japan's nuclear-risk obligations, Oppenheimer's mixed pride and burden, and scientists' duties after creating weapons whose political use they do not directly control.

## Key Characteristics

- Nuclear-weapons memory case used to illustrate immediate destructive force.
- Japan wartime suffering node placed beside Nagasaki, Fukushima, and Chernobyl.
- Source-scoped example of official narrative control after catastrophic nuclear violence.
- Decision-history case whose invasion, surrender, signaling, and necessity arguments remain unresolved.
- Moral-memory reference connecting nuclear harm to scientific and political responsibility.

## Qualifications

Neither source settles casualty, temperature, yield, official-communication, military-necessity, Soviet-signaling, or surrender-timing claims. The page is not a complete account of the Pacific War, radiation medicine, survivor testimony, Japanese decision-making, or the U.S. documentary record.
