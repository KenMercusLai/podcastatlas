# Deodoro da Fonseca

## Overview

Deodoro da Fonseca appears across the sources as the initially hesitant marshal drawn into the 1889 coup against [[DomPedroII]], then as the republican leader who retained the old national-anthem tune.

## Current Profile

The biographical episode places Deodoro inside a confused officer and republican conspiracy rather than a mass uprising. His initial hesitation makes the coup look contingent and elite-led, while Pedro II's refusal to organize resistance allowed the transfer to proceed rapidly. The anthem episode then uses Deodoro as the republic's symbolic decision point: after a replacement contest, he preferred [[FranciscoManuelDaSilva]]'s older tune, showing how a new regime could reuse inherited national emotion.

## Key Characteristics

- Military leader associated with Brazil's transition from empire to republic.
- Initially hesitated before joining the officer-led coup against [[DomPedroII]].
- Represents the army's role in a transition the source distinguishes from broad popular revolution.
- Presided over a new-anthem contest in the episode's account.
- Preferred the older anthem tune, helping preserve it as the national melody.
- Makes [[RegimeSymbolContinuity]] visible at the republic's founding moment.

## Evidence

- Republican transition: [[681-brazil-the-emperors-anthem-part-5-glt5596929385]] says public opinion turned against Pedro II and Brazil became a republic under Marshal Deodoro da Fonseca.
- Anthem decision: [[681-brazil-the-emperors-anthem-part-5-glt5596929385]] says the new regime held a contest for a new anthem, but Deodoro preferred the old one.
- Coup role: [[268-brazil-the-last-emperor-glt5634696575]] says republican conspirators and officers drew a hesitant Deodoro into a confused coup without mass popular mobilization.

## Qualifications

The page is source-scoped to Deodoro's coup and anthem roles. It does not reconstruct his full military career, motives, presidency, or the wider republican movement, and the newer episode's elite-coup framing is not an independent adjudication of popular opinion.

## What Changed

- Reframed Deodoro's transition role as initially hesitant and officer-led rather than the expression of an established mass republican mandate.
- Connected the coup's contingent politics to the later symbolic continuity of the anthem.
