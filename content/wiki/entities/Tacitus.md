# Tacitus

## Overview

Tacitus was the Roman historian and son-in-law of [[GnaeusJuliusAgricola|Agricola]] whose works are principal sources for the conquest, revolt, and northern campaigns of [[RomanBritain]], while remaining literary interpretations of empire.

## Current Profile

Episode 500 uses Tacitus for Togidubnus, Caratacus, and the assault on Anglesey. The hosts argue that his dramatic description of the Menai defenders may draw on eyewitness testimony available through Agricola, who served under [[SuetoniusPaulinus]], but proximity does not make the scene transparent. Funeral-clad women, firebrands, prayers, sacred groves, and human-sacrifice claims also organize Roman fear of Britain into a conquest narrative.

Episode 502 reads the Agricola as eulogy, political literature, and historical evidence at once. Tacitus was unusually close to his subject through family and may have had British service experience, but the speeches he gives to Agricola and the Caledonian leader Calgacus are compositions rather than transcripts. The Calgacus speech therefore provides indirect evidence about Roman reflection on conquest, not direct access to Caledonian words.

Tacitus condemns robbery, corruption, luxury, servility, and the moral softening that can accompany Roman urban habits. Yet the episode emphasizes [[AncientImperialCritiqueLimits]]: his admiration for supposedly austere frontier peoples and his hostility to imperial poison do not entail regret for mass killing, enslavement, or conquest. His criticism is powerful but historically specific.

## Key Characteristics

- Agricola's son-in-law and laudatory biographer.
- Essential but interested literary source for the Roman advance into Caledonia.
- Important narrative source for Caratacus, British client rule, Anglesey, and the conquest's religious imagery.
- Authorial creator of speeches used to stage competing moral views of conquest.
- Critic of corruption, luxury, servility, and cultural softening under empire.
- Roman moralist whose critique does not map cleanly onto modern humanitarian anti-imperialism.
- Later writer whose treatments of frontier peoples acquired consequential imperial and ideological afterlives.

## Evidence

- Early-conquest evidence: [[500-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-the-empire-strikes-back-part-2-glt3137178317]] uses Tacitus for Togidubnus, Caratacus, and Paulinus's Anglesey campaign.
- Eyewitness possibility and literary risk: [[500-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-the-empire-strikes-back-part-2-glt3137178317]] links Agricola to Paulinus while treating the Druidic scene and sacrifice claims cautiously.
- Familial and evidentiary position: [[502-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-4-glt1970351222]] identifies Tacitus as Agricola's son-in-law and possible British veteran.
- Literary construction: [[502-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-4-glt1970351222]] treats Calgacus's famous denunciation as Tacitus's composition rather than a verbatim speech.
- Imperial ambivalence: [[502-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-4-glt1970351222]] reads the Agricola as praise for an ideal governor and as a darker portrait of empire as poison.
- Moral focus: [[502-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-4-glt1970351222]] stresses Tacitus's opposition to luxury and moral softening more than to massacre or enslavement.
- Source criticism: [[502-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-4-glt1970351222]] contrasts his Domitian-jealousy explanation with the strategic pressures of the Danubian crisis.

## Qualifications

The current profile is limited to these episodes' use of Tacitus for Britain and does not cover his complete career or corpus. His proximity to Agricola could provide information while also sharpening praise and hostility toward Domitian. Roman descriptions of Druids, sacrifice, barbarism, and speeches could justify conquest or stage moral conflict; archaeology does not validate every scene, number, motive, or sequence.

## What Changed

- Extended Tacitus from Agricola's northern campaigns back to Caratacus, client rule, and Anglesey.
- Added the possibility of Agricola-linked eyewitness information without treating the narrative as transparent.
- Added Druidic and sacrificial imagery as an interested conquest frame.

## Relationships

- [[GnaeusJuliusAgricola|Agricola]] - father-in-law, biographical subject, and model governor.
- [[RomanBritain]] - provincial setting for the Agricola narrative.
- [[SuetoniusPaulinus]] - commander whose Anglesey campaign Tacitus narrates.
- [[RomanConquestOfBritain]] - historical process for which his works are indispensable but interested evidence.
- [[RomanEmpire]] - political order Tacitus serves, criticizes, and morally interprets.
- [[AncientImperialCritiqueLimits]] - concept defining the scope of his critique.
- [[RomanProvincialConsolidation]] - practices his account presents as both administration and cultural subjection.
