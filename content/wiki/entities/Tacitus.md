# Tacitus

## Overview

Tacitus was the Roman historian and son-in-law of [[GnaeusJuliusAgricola|Agricola]] whose biography of the governor is both a principal source for northern Roman campaigning and a morally ambivalent interpretation of empire.

## Current Profile

Episode 502 reads the Agricola as eulogy, political literature, and historical evidence at once. Tacitus was unusually close to his subject through family and may have had British service experience, but the speeches he gives to Agricola and the Caledonian leader Calgacus are compositions rather than transcripts. The Calgacus speech therefore provides indirect evidence about Roman reflection on conquest, not direct access to Caledonian words.

Tacitus condemns robbery, corruption, luxury, servility, and the moral softening that can accompany Roman urban habits. Yet the episode emphasizes [[AncientImperialCritiqueLimits]]: his admiration for supposedly austere frontier peoples and his hostility to imperial poison do not entail regret for mass killing, enslavement, or conquest. His criticism is powerful but historically specific.

## Key Characteristics

- Agricola's son-in-law and laudatory biographer.
- Essential but interested literary source for the Roman advance into Caledonia.
- Authorial creator of speeches used to stage competing moral views of conquest.
- Critic of corruption, luxury, servility, and cultural softening under empire.
- Roman moralist whose critique does not map cleanly onto modern humanitarian anti-imperialism.
- Later writer whose treatments of frontier peoples acquired consequential imperial and ideological afterlives.

## Evidence

- Familial and evidentiary position: [[502-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-4-glt1970351222]] identifies Tacitus as Agricola's son-in-law and possible British veteran.
- Literary construction: [[502-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-4-glt1970351222]] treats Calgacus's famous denunciation as Tacitus's composition rather than a verbatim speech.
- Imperial ambivalence: [[502-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-4-glt1970351222]] reads the Agricola as praise for an ideal governor and as a darker portrait of empire as poison.
- Moral focus: [[502-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-4-glt1970351222]] stresses Tacitus's opposition to luxury and moral softening more than to massacre or enslavement.
- Source criticism: [[502-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-to-the-ends-of-the-earth-part-4-glt1970351222]] contrasts his Domitian-jealousy explanation with the strategic pressures of the Danubian crisis.

## Qualifications

The current profile is limited to the episode's use of Agricola and does not cover Tacitus's complete career or corpus. His proximity to Agricola could provide information while also sharpening praise and hostility toward Domitian. Archaeological evidence supports extensive northern campaigning but does not validate every speech, number, motive, or sequence in the literary account.

## What Changed

- Created Tacitus's page as both evidence source and literary interpreter.
- Separated the Calgacus speech from direct Caledonian testimony.
- Added the historical boundary between Roman moral criticism and modern anti-imperialism.
