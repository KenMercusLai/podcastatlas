# Wife of Bath

## Overview

The Wife of Bath is a wealthy clothmaker and pilgrim in [[TheCanterburyTales]] whose long prologue and tale center marriage, sex, abuse, female speech, and sovereignty.

## Current Profile

The episode treats her as more than a comic invention. Five marriages, repeated long-distance pilgrimage, commercial wealth, vivid clothing, and aggressive interpretation of authority make her a concentrated image of new social energies in late-medieval England.

Her tale moves from a knight's rape to a question about what women desire, answering with sovereignty in marriage. Her prologue complicates that lesson through stories of strategy, desire, and domestic violence. The source therefore links her voice to real possibilities created by cloth production, widowhood, inheritance, and post-plague labor scarcity while preserving the distinction between literary character and social record.

## Key Characteristics

- Economically independent clothmaker represented through conspicuous dress and mobility.
- Five-times-married pilgrim seeking another husband rather than embodying submissive widowhood.
- Forceful first-person speaker whose prologue is longer than her tale.
- Critic of arguments used to subordinate women.
- Narrator of a tale that makes female sovereignty the condition of marital transformation.
- Literary figure through whom work, wealth, abuse, sexuality, and consent intersect.

## Evidence

- **Economic and geographic mobility:** [[416-the-canterbury-tales-part-4-glt1956280616]] links her cloth wealth to pilgrimages to Rome, Santiago, and Jerusalem.
- **Voice and marriage:** [[416-the-canterbury-tales-part-4-glt1956280616]] emphasizes the length and argumentative force of her prologue.
- **Sovereignty theme:** [[416-the-canterbury-tales-part-4-glt1956280616]] summarizes her tale's movement from male violence toward a wife's power to choose.
- **Historical setting:** [[416-the-canterbury-tales-part-4-glt1956280616]] connects the character to post-plague work, widowhood, property, and remarriage possibilities.

## Qualifications

The Wife of Bath is not direct testimony from an ordinary medieval woman; she is a male author's complex fictional voice. The episode's claims about women's post-plague autonomy, the representativeness of her wealth and mobility, and her status as the "first ordinary woman" in English literature remain interpretive or attributed to Marion Turner.

## What Changed

- Established the Wife of Bath as a qualified literary lens on women's work, wealth, mobility, violence, and marital authority.
