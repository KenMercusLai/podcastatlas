# Ulysses S. Grant

## Overview

Ulysses S. Grant is the Union commander whose Vicksburg victory divided the Confederacy and who later accepted Robert E. Lee's surrender before, as president, using federal power against first-Klan terror.

## Current Profile

Grant's bounded profile joins military victory, restrained surrender, and contested federal protection. The July 4, 1863 [[SiegeOfVicksburg|Vicksburg]] surrender gave the Union control of the Mississippi and paired with [[BattleOfGettysburg|Gettysburg]] to narrow Confederate possibilities. At Appomattox, allowing officers to retain sidearms and men to take horses supported demobilization, while leaving open whether honorable treatment without deeper reckoning eased later Confederate self-presentation.

As president, Grant supported the Fifteenth Amendment and signed the Enforcement Acts. After earlier federal intervention proved too limited, he declared lawlessness in nine South Carolina counties in October 1871 and used troops and emergency authority against Klan conspiracies. The York County campaign shows that his administration could suppress organized terror when presidential authorization, [[AmosTAkerman|Amos T. Akerman]]'s legal direction, and [[LewisMerrill|Lewis Merrill]]'s investigation aligned, but it did not secure Reconstruction permanently.

## Key Characteristics

- Campaign commander whose Vicksburg victory secured the Mississippi and divided Confederate territory.
- Union commander who accepted Confederate surrender under restrained and practical terms.
- President who supported the Fifteenth Amendment and congressional Reconstruction.
- Executive who used federal law, troops, arrests, and suspension authority against first-Klan violence.
- Leader whose peace rhetoric existed beside willingness to apply coercive national power.
- Figure whose local enforcement success did not overcome the later national rollback of Reconstruction.

## Qualifications

This remains a selective military-and-Reconstruction profile, not a full account of Grant's campaigns, administration, corruption controversies, economic policy, Native policy, later civil-rights record, or memoirs. The suggestion that Appomattox leniency facilitated Lost Cause memory is interpretive rather than a simple causal chain. York County demonstrates real federal capacity but does not make Grant's wider Reconstruction policy uniformly effective or erase delayed protection, incomplete prosecution, court restriction, and political retreat.

## What Changed

- Added the 1871 South Carolina crackdown as evidence that coordinated federal power could suppress the first Klan locally.
- Qualified operational success through delayed protection, incomplete punishment, and Reconstruction's later rollback.
