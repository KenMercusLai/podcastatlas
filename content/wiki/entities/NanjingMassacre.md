# Nanjing Massacre / 南京大屠杀

## Overview

The Nanjing Massacre is the episode's name for the mass murder, rape, plunder, and terror committed by Japanese forces after they occupied Nanjing in December 1937.

## Current Profile

[[236-china-and-world-war-ii-part-2-glt5290070743]] treats Nanjing as one of the most horrific war crimes of the modern era and as an early demonstration of the wider brutality that would mark the Second World War. The violence lasted roughly six weeks, while civilians who remained after the Chinese military withdrawal did not anticipate its scale.

The episode discusses military brutalization, hierarchy and class inside the Japanese army, resentment after unexpectedly hard Chinese resistance, and permission from superiors as possible contributors. These explanations do not displace responsibility from the perpetrators or establish a single sufficient cause.

## Key Characteristics

- It followed the Japanese occupation of Nanjing in December 1937.
- It involved extensive murder, sexual violence, plunder, and terror against civilians and prisoners.
- The episode places its duration at roughly six weeks.
- It treats the atrocity as Japanese military responsibility while discussing several possible enabling conditions.
- Exact casualty totals remain uncertain and should not be collapsed into one settled number.

## Evidence

- Violence and duration: [[236-china-and-world-war-ii-part-2-glt5290070743]] describes roughly six weeks of mass killing, rape, plunder, and extreme brutality from mid-December 1937 into January 1938.
- Responsibility and explanation: [[236-china-and-world-war-ii-part-2-glt5290070743]] places responsibility on the Japanese army while considering brutalization, hierarchy, frustration, and command license.
- Historical significance: [[236-china-and-world-war-ii-part-2-glt5290070743]] presents Nanjing as both a central Chinese wartime trauma and a foreshadowing of wider global-war violence.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to one narrative podcast episode. The episode gives a range from tens of thousands into six figures but stresses uncertainty; the wiki does not adopt a precise casualty total from this source alone. Its comparative references to other armies are explanatory context, not equivalence or mitigation.
