# Robert Walpole

## Overview

Robert Walpole was Britain's long-serving early eighteenth-century first minister, conventionally treated as its first prime minister, whose 1742 fall supplies the episode's opening model of parliamentary authority becoming unrecoverable.

## Current Profile

[[193-how-prime-ministers-fall-glt7608869237]] presents Walpole as politically durable, earthy, corrupt by later standards, and initially successful at keeping Britain out of costly war. The War of Jenkins' Ear and the failed Cartagena campaign damaged that advantage. After an inquiry motion survived by only three votes and he then lost a Commons division connected to the Chippenham election by 235 to 236, Walpole resigned roughly ten days later. The episode treats those votes less as isolated causes than as public evidence that a long premiership had exhausted its support.

## Key Characteristics

- Conventionally identified as Britain's first prime minister.
- Governed from 1721 to 1742 and built unusual political longevity.
- Benefited from an earlier reputation for avoiding foreign war.
- Lost authority as the War of Jenkins' Ear and the Cartagena failure raised the cost of his leadership.
- Resigned after narrow parliamentary reverses exposed the fragility of his remaining support.

## Evidence

- Tenure and governing style: [[193-how-prime-ministers-fall-glt7608869237]] characterizes Walpole as long-serving, earthy, politically corrupt, and initially associated with avoiding war.
- War and parliamentary erosion: [[193-how-prime-ministers-fall-glt7608869237]] links the Cartagena failure to an inquiry vote survived by three and a later 235-236 defeat over the Chippenham election.
- Exit mechanism: [[193-how-prime-ministers-fall-glt7608869237]] says he resigned around ten days after the one-vote loss, making his case the episode's template for exposed terminal weakness.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to one conversational survey of prime ministerial downfalls, not a full account of Walpole's patronage system, domestic policy, Hanoverian politics, war strategy, or historical reputation. The episode's use of modern prime-ministerial language and corruption labels compresses an eighteenth-century political order into a comparison with later parliamentary leadership.
