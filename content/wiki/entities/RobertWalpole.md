# Robert Walpole

## Overview

Robert Walpole was Britain's long-serving early eighteenth-century first minister, conventionally treated as its first prime minister, whose cabinet leadership helped make parliamentary support central to practical government.

## Current Profile

[[16-dongxifang-de-zaixiang-lpor4-tu5o02vkbwd58e2tgl3rp|Weird History 16]] places Walpole in the formation of British prime-ministerial government after the Glorious Revolution, the Bill of Rights, and George I's accession. It says Walpole led cabinet business from 1721, selected ministers, organized parliamentary support, and advanced collective cabinet responsibility even while “prime minister” retained pejorative associations and was not yet a fully accepted formal title. His case therefore distinguishes an office's practical emergence from later naming and formalization.

[[193-how-prime-ministers-fall-glt7608869237]] presents the terminal side of that long authority. Walpole is politically durable, earthy, corrupt by later standards, and initially successful at keeping Britain out of costly war. The War of Jenkins' Ear and the failed Cartagena campaign damage that advantage. After an inquiry motion survives by only three votes and he loses a Commons division connected to the Chippenham election by 235 to 236, Walpole resigns roughly ten days later. The votes matter less as isolated causes than as public evidence that the governing support built around him has become unrecoverable.

## Key Characteristics

- Conventionally identified as Britain's first prime minister.
- Led cabinet business from 1721 to 1742 and built unusual political longevity before the title was fully formalized.
- Connected minister selection, cabinet solidarity, and parliamentary support to practical government leadership.
- Benefited from an earlier reputation for avoiding foreign war.
- Lost authority as the War of Jenkins' Ear and the Cartagena failure raised the cost of his leadership.
- Resigned after narrow parliamentary reverses exposed the fragility of his remaining support.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to two popular-history discussions, not a full account of Walpole's patronage system, domestic policy, Hanoverian politics, war strategy, or historical reputation. Both sources use later prime-ministerial language for an office still emerging in practice; the formation source's causal claims about George I, ministerial selection, and collective responsibility, and the downfall source's corruption labels and exact vote sequence require specialist verification.
