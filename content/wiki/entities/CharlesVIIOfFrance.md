# Charles VII of France

## Overview

Charles VII appears as the surviving Dauphin who escaped Burgundian Paris, survived disinheritance, used [[JoanOfArc|Joan of Arc]]'s campaign to reach anointing at Reims, and ultimately recovered enough of France to rehabilitate her.

## Current Profile

After the deaths of his elder brothers, Charles became Dauphin amid the [[ArmagnacBurgundianCivilWar]]. He escaped south to Bourges when Burgundian forces seized Paris, leaving him politically weak but still able to represent an alternative French legitimacy.

The Montereau killing made reconciliation with Burgundy far harder and helped push [[PhilipTheGood|Philip the Good]] toward England. The treaty excluded Charles, but his forces continued fighting, ensuring that [[HenryVOfEngland|Henry V]] never converted the settlement into uncontested rule.

By early 1429, the [[SiegeOfOrleans|Siege of Orléans]] threatened Charles's ability to hold the Loire and the south. He nevertheless treated Joan cautiously: court figures evaluated her, women examined her claimed virginity, and theologians at Poitiers tested her moral and spiritual standing before recommending that she be allowed to accompany soldiers. The source presents this as both religious scrutiny and a calculated response by a claimant with little strategic room, not simple credulity.

Joan's success at Orléans temporarily changed Charles's resource and legitimacy problem. Her reputation helped attract roughly 5,000 men despite his empty treasury, while the Loire victories and surrender of Troyes opened a risky road through hostile territory. Charles entered Reims on 16 July 1429 and was anointed the next day, turning a disputed claimant still widely called the Dauphin into a consecrated king before Christendom.

The Paris assault reveals Charles's different risk calculus. He did not fully support Joan and Alençon's attack, and after its failure her place at court became less secure. When Joan later campaigned independently and was captured, Charles neither ransomed her nor made an immediate public defense. The source treats political danger, fiscal limits, diplomacy, and possible relief at an uncontrolled symbol's removal as compatible interpretations, but does not establish his inner motive.

Charles initially remained silent after Joan's execution because association with a convicted heretic threatened his crown. His improving position changed that calculation: Burgundy reconciled with him in 1435, he entered Paris in 1436, began recovering Normandy in 1449, and ordered inquiries after taking Rouen in 1450. The eventual annulment of Joan's conviction validated her memory and protected the legitimacy of his coronation, showing rehabilitation as both corrective judgment and royal statecraft.

## Key Characteristics

- Survivor of Burgundian seizure and massacre in Paris.
- Political rival based south of the Burgundian-English zone.
- Blamed for John the Fearless's killing at Montereau.
- Disinherited claimant whose continued resistance qualified English triumph.
- Cautious patron who used court and clerical testing before authorizing Joan's entry into the Orléans campaign.
- Resource-constrained king whose caution diverged from Joan's campaign urgency after Reims.
- King whose recovery of territory allowed him to reopen Joan's case and consolidate the legitimacy her mission had supported.

## Qualifications

Responsibility and intent at Montereau remain uncertain even though political blame is clear. The sources do not reveal the sign Joan gave Charles or isolate religious belief from strategic desperation in his decision to authorize her. They also do not establish why he withheld full support at Paris, failed to ransom Joan, or remained silent after her capture; financial, diplomatic, strategic, personal, and legitimacy considerations may overlap. Nor was his later inquiry necessarily either purely self-interested or purely disinterested: correcting Joan's conviction and defending his anointed legitimacy were compatible purposes. The compressed chronology does not substitute for a full account of his reign.
