# Charles VII of France

## Overview

Charles VII appears as the surviving Dauphin who escaped Burgundian Paris, was blamed for [[JohnTheFearless|John the Fearless's]] murder, survived disinheritance under the [[TreatyOfTroyes|Treaty of Troyes]], and ultimately recovered enough of France to rehabilitate [[JoanOfArc|Joan of Arc]].

## Current Profile

After the deaths of his elder brothers, Charles became Dauphin amid the [[ArmagnacBurgundianCivilWar]]. He escaped south to Bourges when Burgundian forces seized Paris, leaving him politically weak but still able to represent an alternative French legitimacy.

The Montereau killing made reconciliation with Burgundy far harder and helped push [[PhilipTheGood|Philip the Good]] toward England. The treaty excluded Charles, but his forces continued fighting, ensuring that [[HenryVOfEngland|Henry V]] never converted the settlement into uncontested rule. Joan's role in relieving Orléans and enabling the Reims coronation then gave Charles a sacred and political legitimacy that her English-backed prosecution attempted to destroy.

Charles initially remained silent after Joan's execution because association with a convicted heretic threatened his crown. His improving position changed that calculation: Burgundy reconciled with him in 1435, he entered Paris in 1436, began recovering Normandy in 1449, and ordered inquiries after taking Rouen in 1450. The eventual annulment of Joan's conviction validated her memory and protected the legitimacy of his coronation, showing rehabilitation as both corrective judgment and royal statecraft.

## Key Characteristics

- Younger prince elevated after two elder dauphins died.
- Survivor of Burgundian seizure and massacre in Paris.
- Political rival based south of the Burgundian-English zone.
- Blamed for John the Fearless's killing at Montereau.
- Disinherited claimant whose continued resistance qualified English triumph.
- King whose recovery of territory allowed him to reopen Joan's case and consolidate the legitimacy her mission had supported.

## Qualifications

Responsibility and intent at Montereau remain uncertain even though political blame is clear. The sources do not establish that Charles's inquiry was either purely self-interested or purely disinterested: correcting Joan's conviction and defending his anointed legitimacy were compatible purposes. The compressed recovery chronology does not substitute for a full account of his reign or the war's final campaigns.

## What Changed

- Extended Charles from excluded claimant into a recovering king able to reverse the political meaning of Joan's conviction.
- Added his initial silence and later rehabilitation campaign as linked legitimacy choices under changing conditions.
