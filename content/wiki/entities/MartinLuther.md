# Martin Luther

## Overview

Martin Luther was a German reformer whose theology, vernacular force, and use of print helped turn a challenge to church authority into the Protestant Reformation.

## Current Profile

The current source enters after Luther's first revolutionary phase. By 1524-1525, [[AndreasKarlstadt]] and [[ThomasMuntzer|Thomas Müntzer]] claimed to extend scriptural reform beyond his limits, while Luther defended sacramental boundaries, princely authority, and social order. His response to the [[GermanPeasantsWar]] makes him both an enabling source of anti-authoritarian language and an opponent of the revolt it helped articulate.

The episode's judgment is deliberately divided. Luther's courage, religious originality, charisma, language, and printing strategy made him historically decisive, yet his call to kill rebellious peasants and his later antisemitic program remain grave parts of the same legacy.

## Key Characteristics

- Grounded Christian freedom in divine grace and scriptural authority rather than papal mediation.
- Used polemic, vernacular language, and print with unusual reach and force.
- Rejected radical deductions that Karlstadt, Müntzer, and rebel peasants drew from reform principles.
- Distinguished spiritual freedom from secular obedience through the [[TwoKingdomsDoctrine]].
- Relied on princely protection and treated disorder as a threat to religious reform.
- Combined personal courage and intellectual power with abusive, sometimes exterminatory rhetoric.

## Evidence

- Reform and interpretive conflict: [[437-luther-a-world-torn-apart-part-5-glt3328267667]] shows former allies using Christian freedom and Scripture to challenge serfdom, images, sacraments, property, and rulers.
- Political order: [[437-luther-a-world-torn-apart-part-5-glt3328267667]] follows Luther's pressure against Karlstadt, appeal to Saxon princes, two-kingdoms argument, and attack on the peasants.
- Historical agency: [[437-luther-a-world-torn-apart-part-5-glt3328267667]] attributes Luther's impact to the combination of experienced grace, compelling language, charisma, and print rather than to ideas alone.
- Moral legacy: [[437-luther-a-world-torn-apart-part-5-glt3328267667]] juxtaposes his achievements with anti-peasant violence and the coercive antisemitism of *On the Jews and Their Lies*.

## Qualifications

This profile is based on a series finale rather than a complete biography or theological study. The episode distinguishes Luther's theological antisemitism from Nazi racial antisemitism without minimizing the pamphlet or its later Nazi use. Claims that his Reformation led to private religion, secularism, atheism, or individualism remain broad, source-scoped genealogies.

## What Changed

- Established Luther as a new canonical entity through the 1524-1525 radical split.
- Added the tension between enabling reform language and rejecting political revolt.
- Preserved violent anti-peasant and antisemitic writing inside the current profile rather than treating it as an aside.

## Relationships

- [[ThomasMuntzer]] - former follower who radicalized apocalyptic and political implications Luther rejected.
- [[AndreasKarlstadt]] - Wittenberg colleague and rival pushed into censorship, exile, and constrained reconciliation.
- [[GermanPeasantsWar]] - revolt in which Luther's language was used by rebels before he urged princely suppression.
- [[SolaScripturaInterpretivePluralism]] - principle that empowered readers while weakening Luther's control over conclusions.
- [[TwoKingdomsDoctrine]] - framework separating Christian freedom from authorization to overthrow secular rule.
- [[RevolutionaryOutflanking]] - pattern describing Luther's movement from insurgent reformer to target of more radical allies.
- [[ReformationLegacyAmbivalence]] - moral-historical frame for evaluating his achievement and harm together.
