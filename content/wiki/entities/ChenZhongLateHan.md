## Overview

陈忠 / Chen Zhong now enters the wiki as an An-era court official whose advice alternates between failed domestic correction and successful frontier retention. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-798-donghan-shiqi-de-baixing-you-duo-ku-loolveumdtv4eo-omubfi5z2p2r5|Hanji 798]] shows him in 121 CE warning [[EmperorAnOfHan|汉安帝]] about disaster-era palace-emissary burden and a Three Excellencies/Shangshu authority mismatch, but An ignores both memorials. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-799-donghan-mingchen-yangzhen-ruhe-jujue-zou-houmen-lmufb7lvb-kzsvjtn6iu-zrpiptf|Hanji 799]] then shows Chen persuading An not to abandon the Western Regions and later recommending [[ZhouXieLateHan|周燮]] and [[FengLiangLateHan|冯良]].

## Current Profile

Hanji 798 gives Chen Zhong's earliest current role. He begins from public hardship: floods, seawater intrusion, locusts, rice shortfall, Qiang rebellion, poverty, and an empty treasury. Against that background he criticizes the court for sending palace-linked messengers and [[BoRongLateHan|伯荣]] between Ganling and the capital, because the missions make local officials repair roads and stations, provide supplies and gifts, and conscript vulnerable people.

The first warning is not only about Bo Rong as an individual. Chen Zhong argues that palace attendants, servants, and women envoys can blur the location of imperial authority when local society treats their preferences as the emperor's intention. If An does not correct the over-reception of Bo Rong, Chen warns, the burden will look ruler-endorsed. An does not accept the memorial.

Chen's second Hanji 798 memorial turns to office structure. He argues that the Three Excellencies retain high title but lack real control because Shangshu offices handle confidential affairs, personnel selection, punishments, rewards, and legal decisions. Yet disasters still bring blame to the Three Excellencies. This makes Chen the current source's diagnostic voice for [[ThreeExcellenciesBlameWithoutAuthority|三公无权背责]] before the later Yang Zhen crisis.

In Hanji 799's Western Regions debate, Chen Zhong argues that long-submitted states are actively probing Han intentions because they dislike Northern Xiongnu control and still look toward Han. If the court abandons Shanshan and the other states, they will attach to Northern Xiongnu; Xiongnu wealth and courage will grow, and pressure could connect toward Qiang regions, making [[HexiFourCommanderiesLateHan|河西四郡]] more dangerous.

Chen Zhong therefore recommends retaining the frontier rather than closing the passes. He urges a Dunhuang command structure and strengthened Hexi defenses, and Emperor An accepts his advice by appointing [[BanYongLateHan|班勇]] as Western Regions chief clerk with five hundred troops at Liuzhong.

The same Hanji 799 source later shows Chen Zhong as a recommender of high-reputation talent. He praises Zhou Xie of Runan and Feng Liang of Nanyang for learning and upright conduct, leading Emperor An to summon them with formal gifts. Their refusal makes Chen Zhong's recommendation successful in identifying prestige but unsuccessful in converting that prestige into service.

## Key Characteristics

- Court remonstrant who diagnoses palace-proximity missions as local labor and supply burden during public crisis.
- Institutional critic who identifies Three Excellencies blame after practical authority has moved into Shangshu channels.
- Advice-giver whose domestic correction fails under Emperor An in 121 CE.
- Frontier policy evaluator who rejects Western Regions abandonment as strategically self-defeating.
- Reads small-state alignment as a live signal of Han prestige and Xiongnu pressure.
- Recommender of respected scholars whose refusal exposes court-confidence limits.

## Qualifications

This page is bounded to Hanji 798 and Hanji 799. It does not settle Chen Zhong's full office history, family background, later career, or whether his domestic memorials and frontier advice were part of a continuous reform program. Hanji 798 and Hanji 799 also caution against flattening him into either a failed or successful adviser: the same ruler ignores his institutional correction and later accepts his frontier-retention case.
