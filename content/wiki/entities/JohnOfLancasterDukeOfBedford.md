# John of Lancaster, Duke of Bedford

## Overview

John of Lancaster, Duke of Bedford, was the leading English regent in France whose weakening position after Orléans and Reims made [[JoanOfArc|Joan of Arc]] both a military-symbolic threat and a target for ecclesiastical condemnation.

## Current Profile

After Henry V and Charles VI died in 1422, Bedford became the principal regent defending the infant [[HenryVIOfEngland|Henry VI's]] inherited claim in France. English territorial strength, Burgundian alliance, and victory at Verneuil gave that regency a formidable position, but the 1428-1429 attempt to force the Loire at Orléans made further expansion depend on a dispersed siege and strained resources.

The later episode presents Bedford confronting a compound crisis in 1429-1430: English captains had been captured, Champagne had weakened, [[CharlesVIIOfFrance|Charles VII]] had been anointed at Reims, and Joan's reputation supplied a compelling explanation for collapsing English confidence. Bedford described her influence through witchcraft, a claim the hosts treat as potentially sincere as well as politically useful.

After Burgundian forces captured Joan, Bedford sought to turn her captivity into a reversal of Charles's sacred advantage. [[PierreCauchon|Pierre Cauchon]] supplied an ecclesiastical route: the English would buy Joan, and a church tribunal would prosecute her under jurisdiction claimed from her capture in the bishopric of Beauvais.

## Key Characteristics

- Lancastrian regent managing English rule in France.
- Guardian of the infant dual monarchy at its apparent military and dynastic high point.
- Political leader facing military, diplomatic, and legitimacy losses after Orléans and Reims.
- Opponent who interpreted Joan's influence through witchcraft and demonic power.
- Sponsor of a strategy to convert Joan's capture into an ecclesiastical judgment against Charles VII's cause.

- Occult explanation: [[634-joan-of-arc-heroine-in-chains-part-3-glt6235222836]] attributes to Bedford a belief that Joan's witchcraft had damaged English rule.
- Trial strategy: [[634-joan-of-arc-heroine-in-chains-part-3-glt6235222836]] connects Bedford's political need to Cauchon's proposal for purchase and church prosecution.

## Qualifications

The sources cover Bedford chiefly through the 1422 succession and Joan's 1429-1430 campaign rather than providing a complete regency biography. They do not isolate his decisions from English councils, commanders, Burgundian partners, finance, or the wider dual-monarchy administration. They also cannot determine how literally he understood witchcraft, whether that explanation displaced recognition of English structural weakness, or how responsibility for the later trial was divided among Bedford, Cauchon, other English officials, and Burgundian intermediaries.

## What Changed

- Extended Bedford's profile back to the infant succession, Verneuil, and the attempt to turn English-Burgundian strength into control of the Loire.
