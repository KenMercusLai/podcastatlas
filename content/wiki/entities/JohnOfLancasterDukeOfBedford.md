# John of Lancaster, Duke of Bedford

## Overview

John of Lancaster, Duke of Bedford, was the leading English regent in France whose weakening position after Orléans and Reims made [[JoanOfArc|Joan of Arc]] both a military-symbolic threat and a target for ecclesiastical condemnation.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Bedford confronting a compound crisis in 1429-1430: English captains had been captured, Champagne had weakened, [[CharlesVIIOfFrance|Charles VII]] had been anointed at Reims, and Joan's reputation supplied a compelling explanation for collapsing English confidence. Bedford described her influence through witchcraft, a claim the hosts treat as potentially sincere as well as politically useful.

After Burgundian forces captured Joan, Bedford sought to turn her captivity into a reversal of Charles's sacred advantage. [[PierreCauchon|Pierre Cauchon]] supplied an ecclesiastical route: the English would buy Joan, and a church tribunal would prosecute her under jurisdiction claimed from her capture in the bishopric of Beauvais.

## Key Characteristics

- Lancastrian regent managing English rule in France.
- Political leader facing military, diplomatic, and legitimacy losses after Orléans and Reims.
- Opponent who interpreted Joan's influence through witchcraft and demonic power.
- Sponsor of a strategy to convert Joan's capture into an ecclesiastical judgment against Charles VII's cause.

## Evidence

- Strategic crisis: [[634-joan-of-arc-heroine-in-chains-part-3-glt6235222836]] links Bedford's position to lost battles, captured commanders, weakened Champagne, and Charles's coronation.
- Occult explanation: [[634-joan-of-arc-heroine-in-chains-part-3-glt6235222836]] attributes to Bedford a belief that Joan's witchcraft had damaged English rule.
- Trial strategy: [[634-joan-of-arc-heroine-in-chains-part-3-glt6235222836]] connects Bedford's political need to Cauchon's proposal for purchase and church prosecution.

## Qualifications

This source covers Bedford only at the end of Joan's 1429-1430 campaign and does not provide a complete regency biography. It cannot determine how literally he understood witchcraft, whether that explanation displaced recognition of English structural weakness, or how responsibility for the later trial was divided among Bedford, Cauchon, other English officials, and Burgundian intermediaries.
