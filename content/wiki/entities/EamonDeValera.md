# Éamon de Valera

## Overview

Éamon de Valera was an Irish republican political leader represented here from his post-Rising survival and Sinn Fein presidency through revolutionary diplomacy, treaty opposition, civil-war marginalization, and later constitutional transformation of the Irish Free State.

## Current Profile

The earliest episode places de Valera among the surviving Rising leaders and makes his 1917 presidency of [[SinnFein|Sinn Fein]] part of the movement's shift into a republican mass party. The settlement sought international recognition for an independent republic and then a referendum through which the Irish people would choose their form of government. His later arrest under the alleged German Plot made imprisonment part of the party's 1918 election setting.

The War of Independence opening presents de Valera escaping Lincoln jail, returning as president of [[DailEireann|Dail Eireann]], and spending much of 1919-1920 in the [[UnitedStates]]. His mission attracted large crowds and raised substantial bond funding for the republic but failed to win decisive recognition from either major American party. His absence also left more operating space for [[MichaelCollinsIrishRevolutionary|Michael Collins]] and [[ArthurGriffith|Arthur Griffith]] inside Ireland.

The treaty episode then presents de Valera as the republican leader who opened talks with [[DavidLloydGeorge|David Lloyd George]] but controversially stayed out of the decisive London delegation. He proposed external association: an Irish republic linked to, but not inside, the British imperial system. He called the delegates plenipotentiaries while requiring major questions to return to Dublin and later told them not to sign without further consultation, creating a disputed gap between formal authority and political instruction. The episode offers several possible explanations for his absence but does not establish one.

The civil-war episode presents him as politically central to rejecting the Anglo-Irish Treaty but less influential over the anti-treaty military campaign once armed leaders took control. His later “Document No. 2” retained external association without recognizing the British monarch as head of state or swearing allegiance. After military defeat, he entered parliament under the disputed oath in 1927, won power in 1932, removed the oath, and secured the Treaty Ports in 1938, shifting part of the republican program from insurrection to constitutional statecraft.

## Key Characteristics

- He became Sinn Fein president during its 1917 republican settlement and mass electoral expansion.
- He represented the revolutionary republic abroad, converting public attention into funds without securing formal US recognition.
- His American absence increased his international visibility while allowing other leaders greater operational prominence at home.
- He proposed external association as an alternative to the treaty's crown relationship and imperial membership.
- He delegated the London negotiations under instructions whose tension between plenipotentiary status and required consultation remained consequential.
- He aligned with the anti-treaty cause but was increasingly sidelined by military leaders during the civil war.
- His procedural acceptance of the oath enabled parliamentary return; in government he then removed it and recovered the Treaty Ports through institutions created by the treaty settlement.

## Qualifications

This is a four-source, bounded profile of one political sequence, not a full biography or assessment of de Valera's long rule. His Rising survival, the precise internal bargain of 1917, and the political effects of imprisonment are compressed. The American fundraising total, the political meaning of large crowds, and the claim that his absence enabled Collins's rise remain episode-attributed; money raised did not equal diplomatic recognition. The episodes' accounts of his influence, later attitude to the oath, relationship with Collins, and marginalization by militarists also remain attributed to their speakers. His absence from London is variously explained as pride, tactical positioning, fear of compromise, or reluctance to face Lloyd George; none is established here. The sources do not settle the feasibility of external association, the delegates' legal authority, responsibility for the war, or counterfactual claims about partition, church influence, economics, and party competition.
