# Battle of Chaeronea

## Overview

The Battle of Chaeronea was the 338 BCE victory in which [[PhilipIIMacedon|Philip II of Macedon]] and the young [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]] defeated an alliance led by Athens and [[Thebes]], ending the principal organized military resistance to Macedonian supremacy in Greece.

## Current Profile

Philip's long expansion made the battle the culmination of state-building rather than an isolated tactical surprise. Professional training, a deep sarissa phalanx, Companion cavalry, specialist infantry, siege capacity, and centralized command confronted citizen forces assembled by poleis whose political coordination was less continuous. The contrast does not make defeat automatic: Athens and Thebes fielded a serious coalition, and the Theban Sacred Band fought to destruction.

The exact battlefield sequence remains uncertain. A later tactical tradition has Philip's wing execute an orderly withdrawal that draws the Athenians out of alignment while Alexander attacks the Theban side. [[dchha71-mania-for-subjugation-dchha71-mania-for-subjugation]] presents this as plausible but underdocumented, not as a securely recoverable maneuver.

The durable consequence was political. Philip treated Athens relatively leniently, dealt more harshly with Thebes, and used victory to establish the [[LeagueOfCorinth]]. The result converted battlefield supremacy into a Macedonian-led Greek peace and Persian war project, while leaving coercion beneath the language of common purpose.

## Key Characteristics

- Culminating battle in Philip II's subordination of the principal southern Greek powers.
- Joined Macedonian professional combined arms and centralized command to a high-stakes coalition battle.
- Gave the young Alexander an important early command role without allowing his contribution to be reconstructed securely.
- Destroyed the Theban Sacred Band and imposed heavy losses and captivity on the defeated coalition.
- Produced differentiated settlements, with relative leniency toward Athens and sharper punishment for Thebes.
- Enabled the League of Corinth and Philip's leadership of the projected Persian expedition.

- Strategic consequence: [[116-alexander-the-great-part-1-glt3321594885]] connects Philip's army reforms and Chaeronea to Macedonian leadership of the Greek invasion coalition.
- Military and political setting: [[dchha71-mania-for-subjugation-dchha71-mania-for-subjugation]] connects Philip's professional army, Demosthenes's resistance, the allied defeat, and the subsequent settlement.
- Tactical uncertainty: [[dchha71-mania-for-subjugation-dchha71-mania-for-subjugation]] preserves the organized-retreat account while emphasizing the shortage of evidence for the exact sequence.
- Coercive order: [[116-alexander-the-great-part-1-glt3321594885]] and [[dchha71-mania-for-subjugation-dchha71-mania-for-subjugation]] treat the battle as the military precondition for the League of Corinth and the Persian campaign.

## Qualifications

The bounded sources are popular-history summaries, not a full comparison of the sparse ancient evidence. Force sizes, casualties, Alexander's precise role, Philip's alleged feigned retreat, the Sacred Band's final sequence, and the degree to which later victory narratives rationalize events remain uncertain. Greek political fragmentation and militia institutions are relevant conditions, not proof that every polis lacked competence or that Macedonian victory was inevitable.
