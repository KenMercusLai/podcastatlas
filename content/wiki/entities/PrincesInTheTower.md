# Princes in the Tower / 塔中王子案

## Overview

The Princes in the Tower / 塔中王子案 is the disappearance and probable murder of [[EdwardV]] and [[RichardOfShrewsbury]] after [[RichardIII]] took custody, displaced their claims, and became king in 1483.

## Current Profile

The complete evidence set now distinguishes three layers. Josephine Tey's [[TheDaughterOfTime|《时间的女儿》]] makes the case a model for challenging winner-shaped sources and inherited villain stories. The fantasy source shows its afterlife as a flexible disappearance motif rather than a solved historical code. The dedicated history episode reconstructs the political sequence and reaches the strongest current judgment: the boys were probably murdered on Richard's orders because he controlled access, benefited from their removal, failed to produce them, and did not use Buckingham as a convenient culprit after the rebellion.

That conclusion remains probabilistic. Stories about Brackenbury and James Tyrrell, Buckingham's possible motive, Lambert Simnel and Perkin Warbeck, the 1674 Tower bones, the Windsor bodies, and modern survival theories do not securely establish who acted, when the boys died, or which remains are theirs.

## Key Characteristics

- Historical disappearance involving two child heirs whose continued existence threatened a new regime.
- Strongest current explanation is murder authorized by Richard III, but the case lacks conclusive forensic proof.
- Demonstrates why source criticism can revise propaganda without requiring exoneration.
- Generated politically useful pretenders, confession stories, bone identifications, and survival theories across later centuries.
- Became a durable literary and fantasy motif through Josephine Tey, Shakespearean memory, and *A Song of Ice and Fire* comparisons.

## Evidence

- Revision and source criticism: [[69-xianliao-tuili-wenxue-lishixuezhe-ke-bu-jiu-shi-zhentan-ma-666244465]] uses [[TheDaughterOfTime]] to test motives, source timing, Tudor legitimacy, and later literary reputation rather than accepting inherited guilt as self-proving.
- Political sequence and strongest inference: [[139-the-princes-in-the-tower-part-2-glt4685691538]] follows custody, coronation delay, Hastings's execution, joint delegitimation, disappearance, rebellion, and Richard's conduct to judge his responsibility overwhelmingly probable.
- Alternatives and physical uncertainty: [[139-the-princes-in-the-tower-part-2-glt4685691538]] examines Buckingham, Tyrrell, pretenders, Tower and Windsor bones, and the Devon survival theory without adopting them as stronger explanations.
- Fictional afterlife: [[31-bing-yu-huo-zhi-ge-suiran-cong-bu-zicheng-lishi-594509848]] compares the princes' disappearance with the apparent loss and escape of the two youngest Stark children while rejecting one-to-one historical mapping.

## Qualifications

Political opportunity and motive support attribution but do not identify the killers, method, date, burial place, or remains. The prior revisionist source is not evidence of innocence, and the later episode's confidence is not a substitute for missing forensic proof. The *A Song of Ice and Fire* parallel concerns source layering, not historical identity.
