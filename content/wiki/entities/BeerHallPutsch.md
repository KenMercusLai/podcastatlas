# Beer Hall Putsch

## Overview

The Beer Hall Putsch was [[AdolfHitler]]'s failed November 1923 attempt to force Bavarian authorities and [[ErichLudendorff]] into a nationalist seizure of power modeled partly on [[BenitoMussolini]]'s March on Rome.

## Current Profile

The episode presents the putsch as operationally chaotic but historically catalytic. Hitler and Nazi stormtroopers entered a Munich beer hall, tried to compel Bavaria's right-wing triumvirate to join a march on Berlin, and failed to secure barracks, police sites, or durable official support. A march of roughly 2,000 supporters ended in police gunfire, deaths on both sides, Hitler's flight, and his arrest.

The failure did not end Hitler's career because the trial and prison term changed its political meaning. A sympathetic court let him perform patriotism before a national audience; the dead became movement martyrs; and incarceration supported the writing of [[MeinKampf|*Mein Kampf*]]. The central strategic consequence was a turn away from immediate coup-making toward the anti-democratic use of elections, constitutional procedure, and a leader cult.

## Key Characteristics

- Failed nationalist coup launched in Munich in November 1923.
- Attempt to coerce Bavarian officials into backing a march on Berlin.
- Event influenced by Mussolini's apparent success in Italy.
- Armed march defeated by police, with fatalities among marchers and police.
- Failure converted through trial publicity, prison, martyr narrative, and strategic adaptation.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to one narrative-history episode. The putsch's incompetence did not make its consequences trivial, but its later importance should not be read backward as proof that Hitler's recovery or eventual rule was inevitable. Participant motives, exact dialogue, tactical details, casualty framing, and counterfactual outcomes remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Created the event as the strategic hinge between direct coup failure and later legal-electoral subversion.
