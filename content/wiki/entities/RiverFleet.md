# River Fleet

## Overview

The River Fleet is a largely buried London river whose valley, street names, drainage role, and remembered pollution make hidden infrastructure legible in the modern city.

## Current Profile

The walking route crosses the Fleet after descending from Smithfield. Although the river was progressively covered from the seventeenth through nineteenth centuries, its slope helps explain the district's geography and the meat market's ability to move animal waste downhill.

The Fleet therefore links environmental and cultural memory. Fleet Street preserves its name, Jonathan Swift's description preserves an image of refuse and butchers' waste, and the walk uses topography to recover a watercourse no longer visible at street level.

## Key Characteristics

- Buried river still detectable through valley form, slope, and place names.
- Watercourse progressively covered during London's growth.
- Drainage route associated with Smithfield's animal waste and urban pollution.
- Example of infrastructure that remains historically explanatory after disappearing from view.
- Literary and linguistic memory carrier through Swift and Fleet Street.

## Evidence

- Burial and naming: [[212-haunted-london-part-4-glt8938796735]] says the Fleet gave Fleet Street its name and was gradually covered from the seventeenth to nineteenth centuries.
- Market relationship: [[212-haunted-london-part-4-glt8938796735]] links the river's valley to waste moving downhill from [[Smithfield]].
- Literary memory: [[212-haunted-london-part-4-glt8938796735]] cites Jonathan Swift's account of mud, dead animals, butchers' waste, and refuse in the river.
- Route legibility: [[212-haunted-london-part-4-glt8938796735]] uses the physical descent and crossing point to interpret the hidden watercourse.

## Qualifications

This profile is limited to one walking-tour account. It does not establish the Fleet's complete hydrology, sewer engineering, tributaries, covering chronology, water quality, or responsibility for pollution; those details and Swift's descriptive precision remain source-scoped.
