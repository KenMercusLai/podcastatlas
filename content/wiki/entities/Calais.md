# Calais

## Overview

Calais is the fortified Channel port that Edward III besieged after Crécy and converted into a garrisoned English bridgehead in northern [[France]].

## Current Profile

The episode treats Calais as the attempt to turn [[BattleOfCrecy|Crécy's]] battlefield success into lasting access. Marshes, walls, and Jean de Vienne's defense made the town difficult to take, while the siege required large-scale shipping, seamen, food, administration, and persistence. [[PhilipVIOfFrance|Philip VI]] failed to relieve it, and the town surrendered in August 1347.

The surrender story joins mercy to coercion. Six leading citizens appeared in submission and were spared after Queen Philippa intervened, but [[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]] expelled the wider population, introduced English settlers and trade privileges, and maintained a large garrison. Calais therefore becomes both a logistical achievement and an imposed colonial foothold.

## Key Characteristics

- Fortified port protected by walls and marshland.
- Strategic English foothold following Crécy.
- Capture dependent on maritime supply and administrative capacity.
- Surrender remembered through the threatened execution and sparing of six citizens.
- Population expelled and replaced with English settlers under trade privileges and garrison rule.
- Durable gain that survives after most other early English conquests are reversed.

## Evidence

- Strategic objective: [[319-hundred-years-war-triumph-of-the-longbow-part-2-glt3100937638]] says Edward sought a permanent northern foothold after Crécy.
- Siege system: [[319-hundred-years-war-triumph-of-the-longbow-part-2-glt3100937638]] emphasizes ships, seamen, supplies, administration, defenses, and Philip's failed relief.
- Surrender and settlement: [[319-hundred-years-war-triumph-of-the-longbow-part-2-glt3100937638]] records the six citizens' submission, Philippa's intervention, expulsion, resettlement, privileges, and garrison.

## Qualifications

The source gives inconsistent siege dates, first extending the operation to September 1347 and later placing surrender on 4 August 1347. Shipping and manpower totals, the reported royal exchange, and the moral framing of mercy remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Created Calais as the logistical and coercive bridge from Crécy victory to durable English territorial access.
