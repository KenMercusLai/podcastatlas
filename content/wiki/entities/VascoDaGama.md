# Vasco da Gama

## Overview

Vasco da Gama was the Portuguese commander who led the 1497-1499 voyage from Lisbon around Africa to Calicut, opening a direct Portuguese sea route to India.

## Current Profile

The source presents da Gama as a severe commander chosen by [[ManuelIOfPortugal|Manuel I]] for endurance and force rather than scholarly refinement. His expedition joined intense Catholic ritual to difficult seamanship, severe [[OceanicVoyageAttrition|attrition]], and a 23-day Indian Ocean crossing from East Africa to the Malabar Coast.

Arrival did not produce immediate commercial dominance. Portuguese gifts looked unimpressive in Calicut and established Muslim trading communities already linked the region to wider markets. On later voyages, da Gama answered that weak bargaining position with spectacular violence against pilgrims, fishermen, and coastal families, making him central to the episode's rejection of exploration as an innocent category.

## Key Characteristics

- Hard, disciplined commander selected to complete the route to India.
- Leader of a voyage framed by Catholic ritual and royal-commercial ambition.
- Navigator whose fleet reached Calicut after roughly 12,000 miles and 309 days.
- Survivor of an expedition that lost about two-thirds of its crew before returning.
- Imperial actor associated in the source with deliberate terror against noncombatants.

## Evidence

- Departure and purpose: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] connects da Gama's July 1497 departure to Manuel's spice, Christian, and anti-Muslim ambitions.
- Navigation and arrival: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] follows the fleet through Mozambique, Mombasa, and Malindi to Calicut in 1498.
- Attrition and return: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] says the expedition returned in September 1499 after losing roughly two-thirds of its crew.
- Organized violence: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] describes da Gama's later attack on the pilgrim ship Miri and killings of fishermen.

## Qualifications

The profile derives from a narrative episode rather than a full biography. Voyage numbers, descriptions of character, names and details of victims, and the account of specific atrocities require specialist corroboration. Indian and East African perspectives are present mainly through Portuguese encounters.

## What Changed

- Established da Gama as both a route-making navigator and a practitioner of terror within Portuguese expansion.
