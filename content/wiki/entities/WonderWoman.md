# Wonder Woman

## Overview

Wonder Woman is the Amazon superhero introduced in 1941 by psychologist William Moulton Marston, represented here as a powerful female hero whose creation joins feminism, psychological theory, classical costume, and the creator's unconventional private concerns.

## Current Profile

Princess Diana of Themyscira appears to invite a direct ancient-myth reading, yet the episode emphasizes modern authorship. Marston presented the character as propaganda for a new type of powerful woman and embedded his theories of power, submission, and truth inside the stories.

Recurring bondage imagery is therefore treated as historically meaningful rather than incidental decoration. It connects public gender argument to Marston's psychology and domestic life, while also warning against reducing a long-lived character entirely to one creator's biography.

Wonder Woman demonstrates how superhero fiction can borrow mythic names and settings while doing distinctly twentieth-century cultural work. The mythological surface and modern ideological project coexist; neither alone explains the character's history.

## Key Characteristics

- Amazon princess combining classical imagery with a modern comic-book form.
- Female superhero explicitly framed by her creator as an argument for powerful women.
- Character shaped by psychological ideas about truth, power, and submission.
- Early stories marked by recurring bondage imagery.
- Case showing that mythic costume does not establish direct cultural descent from ancient myth.

## Qualifications

The episode offers a compressed creator-centered account. Marston's motives, the precise meaning of repeated imagery, later writers' revisions, reader reception, and the character's wider feminist significance require broader textual and archival evidence.
