# Diego de Almagro

## Overview

Diego de Almagro was Pizarro's early expedition partner and later rival, disadvantaged in the venture's 1529 royal authorization before disputes over ransom, jurisdiction, and Cusco ended in his defeat and execution in 1538.

## Current Profile

[[644-the-fall-of-the-incas-empire-of-gold-part-1-glt4676536558]] places Almagro at the venture's beginning. An older, illegitimate, illiterate, and experienced conquistador, he partnered with [[FranciscoPizarro]] and Hernando de Luque in 1524. He lost an eye during the failed first expedition, remained in Panama to recruit while Pizarro explored, and was initially a close companion rather than an inevitable enemy.

The partnership's institutional imbalance emerged when Pizarro obtained the 1529 royal authorization. Pizarro received governorship, captain-generalship, and salary, while Almagro received only command of Tumbes. He again remained in Panama to recruit during the 1530 departure, making the later dispute over treasure and territory continuous with unequal authority and risk allocation established before Cajamarca.

[[646-the-fall-of-the-incas-death-to-the-emperor-part-3-glt8131543734]] introduces Almagro at Cajamarca with roughly 150 Spaniards, horses, and royal officials. His arrival shows [[Atahualpa]] that the captors are not an isolated raiding party. Almagro demands an equal division of the ransom but receives only a smaller allocation, and his faction supports killing Atahualpa so the force can move toward Cusco. The episode therefore places unequal spoils and the captive emperor inside the rivalry before later territorial war.

[[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] supplies Almagro's return to the center of the conquest. Sent south toward Chile amid worsening rivalry with [[FranciscoPizarro|Pizarro]], he leads an expedition marked by hardship and brutal forced porterage, finds no expected wealth, and comes back in April 1537. He tries to turn [[SiegeOfCusco1536|the siege]] into an alliance with [[MancoInca]], but his refusal to execute four Spanish scouts fails Manco's loyalty test; after defeating Manco's attack, he takes Cusco and imprisons [[HernandoPizarro]] and [[GonzaloPizarro]].

His earlier role in [[647-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-king-in-the-north-part-4-glt4740382633]] places that southern turn inside a widening conquistador competition. Almagro joins the march to Cusco and then moves toward Quito as Sebastián de Benalcázar and [[PedroDeAlvarado|Pedro de Alvarado]] race for the northern capital. Rather than fight Alvarado, he buys his ships and equipment for 100,000 gold pieces, absorbing a rival expedition into the Peruvian conquest before going south.

[[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] presents Almagro as initially strong but strategically exposed after that seizure. He controls Cusco and holds the Pizarro brothers, yet releases or loses both advantages, abandons arbitration, and marches toward a coast whose supply and communication networks favor Francisco Pizarro.

Illness leaves Rodrigo Orgóñez in command at Las Salinas. Defeat makes Almagro a prisoner in the same tower where he had confined Hernando; after roughly ten weeks, Hernando refuses an appeal to Charles V and orders his garroting and beheading. The execution creates a revenge constituency around Almagro's son and extends factional war beyond Almagro's death.

## Key Characteristics

- Persistent early partner who lost an eye during failed exploration and repeatedly raised reinforcements from Panama.
- Junior royal beneficiary whose unequal 1529 office preceded later disputes over ransom and territory.
- Reinforcement leader whose Cajamarca arrival exposed Atahualpa's misreading, followed by an unequal ransom share and support for execution.
- Conquistador claimant to Cusco within the New Toledo-New Castile jurisdictional dispute.
- Leader of a failed Chile expedition whose coerced porterage and fruitless outcome preceded an unsuccessful alliance offer to Manco.
- Negotiator who bought out Alvarado's competing Ecuador expedition.
- Initially advantaged by control of Cusco and Pizarro prisoners but weakened by releases, failed arbitration, illness, and coastal isolation.

## Qualifications

The page uses five episodes' compressed causal account. It does not settle Almagro's age, character, first-expedition details, exact numbers, ransom allocation, his weight in the decision to kill Atahualpa, the Alvarado buyout, the legal merits of the territorial dispute, treatment and mortality on the Chile expedition, the sincerity of his offer to Manco, the arbitrator's independence, or full responsibility for the feud. Describing tactical errors does not imply that his execution was lawful or stabilizing.
