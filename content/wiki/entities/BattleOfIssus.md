# Battle of Issus

## Overview

The Battle of Issus was the 333 BC confrontation in which [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]] defeated [[DariusIII|Darius III]] on a narrow coastal plain, turning constrained frontage, formation discipline, local breakthrough, and a spreading rout into a victory with major political consequences.

## Current Profile

The armies enter the battle after maneuver places Darius behind Alexander's former position and across his supply route. Whether deliberate or accidental, the move also brings the larger Persian force into a corridor bounded by mountains and sea, limiting its ability to use numbers and cavalry. Alexander expands his formation from a narrow approach, repeatedly halting to preserve alignment, then leads the attack on the Macedonian right.

Victory is not a single clean breakthrough. Persian troops opposite Alexander collapse, but the Macedonian phalanx loses cohesion crossing the river and Greek mercenaries exploit the gaps, killing officers and threatening the center. A flanking response helps stabilize it. Alexander then drives toward Darius; the king leaves by horse, and the resulting flight spreads through the Persian army. Narrow exits and pursuit turn defeat into mass killing.

## Key Characteristics

- Constricted terrain reduces Persian frontage, cavalry use, and numerical advantage.
- Deliberate pauses and formation alignment matter alongside Alexander's aggressive leadership.
- The Macedonian right breaks through while the phalanx center nearly fails.
- Darius's departure becomes a system-wide morale and command shock.
- Pursuit and bottlenecks produce much of the casualty asymmetry after organized resistance ends.
- The captured camp and royal family convert battlefield success into wealth, prestige, and political leverage.

## Qualifications

The episode compares incompatible later accounts. Force and casualty totals, Darius's intentions, the exact deployments, Alexander's initial troop position, the royal-chariot fighting, reasons for Darius's departure, and the treatment of the royal women cannot be reconstructed with full confidence.
