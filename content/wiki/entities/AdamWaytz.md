## Overview

Adam Waytz is the psychologist who guides [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639|When Doing Right Feels Wrong]] through the conflict between loyalty and justice, the contextual activation of moral values, and the organizational reframing of whistleblowing as loyalty to a legitimate mission.

## Current Profile

Waytz's account treats loyalty and justice as genuine moral values rather than labeling one moral and the other immoral. This explains why disclosure can look like courageous harm prevention to one observer and unforgivable betrayal to another, and why a person can believe a report is necessary while still experiencing [[MoralStress]].

The episode connects his interpretation to a reported experiment in which fairness cues increased reporting of poor work and loyalty cues reduced it. Waytz also uses a high-school lie about alcohol to illustrate distress after acting against an important intuition, and [[MoralLicensing]] to describe how a good identity can excuse less ethical conduct.

His practical recommendation is institutional: leaders should direct loyalty toward mission, society, and responsible citizenship so that criticism of misconduct is not automatically treated as personal betrayal. This supports [[WhistleblowingAsMissionLoyalty]] without claiming that every disclosure is accurate or justified.

## Key Characteristics

- Interprets whistleblowing as a conflict among moral commitments rather than a simple morality-versus-selfishness contest.
- Emphasizes that situational cues and social context can change which value becomes salient.
- Uses personal experience to explain distress after violating a moral intuition.
- Warns that a virtuous self-image or mission can license misconduct and silence.
- Recommends defining organizational loyalty around mission and responsible correction rather than personal allegiance.

## Qualifications

This profile is limited to one structured episode summary rather than Waytz's publications, full research record, transcript, or independent biography. The experiment, cultural claims, terminology, personal anecdote, and organizational recommendations remain source-scoped. The episode does not establish that value primes determine behavior in severe, dangerous, or legally complex whistleblowing cases.
