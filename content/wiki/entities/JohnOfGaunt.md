# John of Gaunt

## Overview

John of Gaunt was Edward III's son, England's dominant magnate, and the father of [[HenryIVOfEngland|Henry Bolingbroke]], whose wealth and Lancastrian inheritance became decisive in [[RichardIIOfEngland|Richard II's]] fall.

## Current Profile

The 1381 revolt shows the hostile side of Gaunt’s political weight. Rebels destroyed the Savoy Palace so thoroughly that it could not realistically be rebuilt and executed figures associated with him, including financier Richard Lyons and his physician. Yet the source also records a Rochester rebel helping Gaunt’s son [[HenryIVOfEngland|Henry Bolingbroke]] escape the Tower, underscoring the coalition’s inconsistent targeting.

The later sources present Gaunt as powerful enough to attract suspicion but consistently loyal to Richard. His departure for Castile in 1386 removed a stabilizing figure during the French invasion scare and the Wonderful Parliament crisis; his return later helped Richard recover a workable political balance after the [[LordsAppellant|Lords Appellant]] period.

Gaunt's death in 1399 transformed that stabilizing role into a succession crisis. Richard prevented the exiled Henry from returning and moved to absorb the Lancastrian estate. Henry's return as an inheritance claimant then attracted nobles who feared that arbitrary confiscation could endanger their own property, helping turn a family estate dispute into deposition and a new dynasty.

## Key Characteristics

- Son of Edward III and the wealthiest, most powerful magnate in late-fourteenth-century England.
- Symbol of magnate wealth and political privilege whose Savoy Palace became a major rebel target in 1381.
- Loyal but politically intimidating uncle whose absence exposed Richard's weak coalition management.
- Lancastrian patriarch whose return helped stabilize Richard's government after the Appellant crisis.
- Father of Henry Bolingbroke and source of the inheritance seized by Richard in 1399.
- Posthumous hinge between aristocratic property security and the founding crisis of Lancastrian rule.

## Evidence

- Dynastic and inheritance role: [[485-henry-iv-the-usurper-king-part-1-glt8868267122]] connects Gaunt's death, Henry's exile, the Lancastrian estate, noble property fears, and Henry's return.
- Revolt-era hostility: [[414-the-peasants-revolt-londons-burning-part-2-glt6726049231]] connects the destruction of the Savoy and attacks on Gaunt’s associates to the rising’s targeted violence and London rivalries.
- Political weight and loyalty: [[415-the-murder-of-richard-ii-part-3-glt6435684837]] describes Gaunt as England's richest and most powerful subject while treating him as loyal to Richard.
- Absence and stabilization: [[415-the-murder-of-richard-ii-part-3-glt6435684837]] links Gaunt's Castilian expedition to the 1386 political vacuum and his return to Richard's restored authority.
- Succession rupture: [[415-the-murder-of-richard-ii-part-3-glt6435684837]] identifies Gaunt's death and Richard's move against the inheritance as the immediate opening for Henry's return.

## Qualifications

These sources use Gaunt mainly to explain the revolt and Richard II’s and Henry IV’s political trajectories, not to provide a complete biography or assess his Castilian policy. Suspicion of his ambition is part of the political setting and is not treated as proof that he planned to seize Richard’s throne. The motives of those attacking his property and associates were mixed, and protection of Bolingbroke warns against treating the rebels as uniformly anti-Lancastrian.

## What Changed

- Added the Savoy’s destruction and attacks on Gaunt-linked figures as evidence of his hostile popular symbolism in 1381.
- Qualified that hostility with a rebel’s reported rescue of Bolingbroke.
