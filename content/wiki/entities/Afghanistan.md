# Afghanistan

## Overview
Afghanistan is represented across the wiki as an [[AfghanistanImperialCrossroads|imperial and cultural crossroads]], a state formed through regional coalition and colonial border-making, a repeated site of foreign-backed intervention, a country under consolidated [[Taliban]] rule, and the lived setting of women's writing about war, patriarchal control, education, and survival.

## Current Profile
The current evidence resists a single "graveyard of empires" story. [[87-afghanistan-part-1-glt7464087190]] follows long Persian incorporation, Hellenistic and Buddhist exchange, Islamic and Mongol conquest, Mughal expansion from Kabul, and the founding of the [[DurraniEmpire|Durrani Empire]] by [[AhmadShahDurrani|Ahmad Shah Durrani]]. Mountains complicated uniform rule, but cities and routes also connected the region to Persia, Central Asia, India, and China.

Foreign intervention forms a second layer rather than the whole history. [[88-the-first-anglo-afghan-war-glt5156371854]] traces the [[FirstAngloAfghanWar|First Anglo-Afghan War]] from [[GreatGame|Great Game]] anxiety and the replacement of [[DostMuhammad|Dost Muhammad]] with [[ShahShuja|Shah Shuja]] through occupation collapse and the 1842 retreat. The earlier survey extends that pattern through the [[DurandLine|Durand Line]], the [[SovietAfghanWar|Soviet-Afghan War]], and the post-2001 intervention, repeatedly connecting outside sponsorship to legitimacy, corruption, urban-rural distance, and incomplete control of the countryside.

The modern political profile comes from [[afghanistan-five-years-on-our-correspondent-visits-6a7ee547049c692a6b3ee0a9]]. Visible violence and street insecurity have fallen under the Taliban, but [[TalibanHardlineRule]], gender exclusion, poverty, hunger, unemployment, fear of speech, aid cuts, sanctions, and forced refugee return define the cost of that order.

[[156-yige-afuhan-nvren-de-laixin-zhishang-de-guang-he-chuban-beihou-de-gushi-922362198]] keeps the national frame grounded in lived experience. Afghan women's first-person writing shows how regime change, war, guardianship, dress, movement, music, education, courts, family survival, and publishing support operate at household scale without reducing women to passive victims.

## Key Characteristics
- Historically central crossroads incorporated into and productive of major imperial formations.
- State-formation history joining Durrani coalition-building to later borders that do not contain every transborder community.
- Political arena where foreign-backed rule repeatedly raises autonomy, legitimacy, corruption, and territorial-control problems.
- Present-day state under centralized Taliban hardline authority with lower visible violence but severe rights restrictions.
- Humanitarian setting shaped by poverty, aid contraction, sanctions, frozen reserves, weak health care, and refugee return.
- Literary and lived setting in which Afghan women document coercion, agency, teaching, family obligation, and survival.

## Evidence
- Crossroads and state formation: [[87-afghanistan-part-1-glt7464087190]] connects geography, cities, successive imperial systems, and Ahmad Shah Durrani's coalition to the emergence of an Afghan polity.
- Imperial intervention and legitimacy: [[88-the-first-anglo-afghan-war-glt5156371854]] connects the 1839-1842 invasion, occupation, retreat, and restoration of Dost Muhammad to the risks of imposed rule.
- Borders and modern war: [[87-afghanistan-part-1-glt7464087190]] links the Durand Line, communist factionalism, Soviet occupation, externally supported insurgency, civil war, Taliban formation, and post-2001 failure.
- Taliban state and humanitarian conditions: [[afghanistan-five-years-on-our-correspondent-visits-6a7ee547049c692a6b3ee0a9]] reports from Kabul, Herat, and Kandahar on security, restriction, poverty, education, aid, and external engagement.
- Women's lived experience and literary testimony: [[156-yige-afuhan-nvren-de-laixin-zhishang-de-guang-he-chuban-beihou-de-gushi-922362198]] presents Afghan women's stories and the material work of publishing them.

## Qualifications
The four sources cover different genres, moments, and scales: two narrative histories, a five-years-on reported snapshot, and a literature-and-publishing discussion. Neither a cross-period intervention analogy nor Taliban-era reporting should be treated as a complete national history. The long survey compresses more than two millennia and includes disputed or episode-attributed claims; the other sources broaden the record but do not exhaust civilian, regional, ethnic, class, religious, or gender perspectives.

## What Changed
- Extended historical centrality into a full crossroads account spanning Persian rule, Hellenistic and Buddhist exchange, Mughal expansion, and Durrani state formation.
- Added the Durand Line and Soviet-Afghan War as foundations for later transborder politics, insurgency, and Taliban emergence.
- Broadened the intervention synthesis from the First Anglo-Afghan War to Soviet and post-2001 cases while preserving the limits of analogy.
