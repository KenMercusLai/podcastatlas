# Afghanistan

## Overview
Afghanistan is represented across the wiki as a historically central imperial and cultural space, a site of failed foreign intervention, a country under consolidated [[Taliban]] rule, and the lived setting of women's writing about war, patriarchal control, education, and survival.

## Current Profile
The current evidence resists a single "graveyard of empires" story. [[88-the-first-anglo-afghan-war-glt5156371854]] traces the [[FirstAngloAfghanWar|First Anglo-Afghan War]] from [[GreatGame|Great Game]] anxiety and the replacement of [[DostMuhammad|Dost Muhammad]] with [[ShahShuja|Shah Shuja]] through occupation collapse and the 1842 retreat. It also stresses Afghanistan's earlier role within Kushan, Mughal, Durrani, Kabul, and Herat-centered histories.

The modern political profile comes from [[afghanistan-five-years-on-our-correspondent-visits-6a7ee547049c692a6b3ee0a9]]. Visible violence and street insecurity have fallen under the Taliban, but [[TalibanHardlineRule]], gender exclusion, poverty, hunger, unemployment, fear of speech, aid cuts, sanctions, and forced refugee return define the cost of that order.

[[156-yige-afuhan-nvren-de-laixin-zhishang-de-guang-he-chuban-beihou-de-gushi-922362198]] keeps the national frame grounded in lived experience. Afghan women's first-person writing shows how regime change, war, guardianship, dress, movement, music, education, courts, family survival, and publishing support operate at household scale without reducing women to passive victims.

## Key Characteristics
- Historically central region and state formation, not only a peripheral battlefield for outside powers.
- Political arena where foreign-backed rule repeatedly raises autonomy and legitimacy problems.
- Present-day state under centralized Taliban hardline authority with lower visible violence but severe rights restrictions.
- Humanitarian setting shaped by poverty, aid contraction, sanctions, frozen reserves, weak health care, and refugee return.
- Literary and lived setting in which Afghan women document coercion, agency, teaching, family obligation, and survival.
- Case where courtesy, consultation, corruption perceptions, and historical memory interact with institutions and force.

## Evidence
- Imperial intervention and historical centrality: [[88-the-first-anglo-afghan-war-glt5156371854]] connects earlier Afghan state and cultural history to the 1839-1842 invasion, occupation, retreat, and restoration of Dost Muhammad.
- Taliban state and humanitarian conditions: [[afghanistan-five-years-on-our-correspondent-visits-6a7ee547049c692a6b3ee0a9]] reports from Kabul, Herat, and Kandahar on security, restriction, poverty, education, aid, and external engagement.
- Women's lived experience and literary testimony: [[156-yige-afuhan-nvren-de-laixin-zhishang-de-guang-he-chuban-beihou-de-gushi-922362198]] presents Afghan women's stories and the material work of publishing them.
- Legitimacy across periods: [[88-the-first-anglo-afghan-war-glt5156371854]] compares Shah Shuja, Karzai, and Ghani while explicitly rejecting the inevitability of the 2021 Taliban victory.

## Qualifications
The three sources cover different genres, moments, and scales: a narrative history, a five-years-on reported snapshot, and a literature-and-publishing discussion. Neither the 1842-2021 analogy nor the Taliban-era reporting should be treated as a complete national history. The historical episode privileges elite and tribal politics, while the literary source and modern reporting broaden but do not exhaust civilian, regional, ethnic, class, or gender perspectives.

## What Changed
- Reframed Afghanistan as historically central rather than only a timeless graveyard for invaders.
- Added the First Anglo-Afghan War as a case of threat inflation, foreign-backed rule, occupation failure, and disaster memory.
- Connected nineteenth-century legitimacy debates to qualified modern comparisons involving Karzai, Ghani, and the Taliban.
