# John Weitz

## Overview
John Weitz, born Hans Werner Weitz, was a German Jewish refugee, United States Army and OSS operative, novelist, and historian whose conflicting accounts of a postwar Munich raid structure [[a-polite-word-for-liar-a-polite-word-for-liar|A Polite Word for Liar]].

## Current Profile
The episode presents Weitz as a Berlin-born refugee who fled Nazi Germany, entered American military intelligence, operated undercover in occupied Europe, participated in Dachau's liberation, and worked against the Nazi Werewolf organization after the war. His sons describe persistent sleep problems, rigid habits, drinking, volatility, and expectation of catastrophe, while his later writing repeatedly examined privileged Germans' accommodation of Hitler.

Weitz initially remembered the Munich raid as an armed operation with two Germans killed and six captured, contradicting [[LarryAdler]]'s account of no shooting and two older women. In a later account to historian Perry Biddiscombe, Weitz described no resistance, arrests, and the arrival of distressed mothers and grandmothers. The episode interprets this convergence as a possible choice of friendship over memory fundamentalism, but the record cannot determine whether it reflects correction, accommodation, changed access, or another reconstruction.

## Key Characteristics
- German Jewish refugee whose war service returned him covertly to occupied Europe.
- OSS operative associated in the episode with Dachau's liberation, prisoner impersonation, and postwar anti-Werewolf work.
- Carried lasting behavioral and emotional effects that his family attributed to wartime experience.
- Responded to sensitive memory through restraint and operational secrecy rather than Adler's expansive storytelling.
- Gave materially different versions of the Munich operation across time.

## Qualifications
The source is a structured episode summary rather than Weitz's operational file, interview transcript, complete family testimony, clinical assessment, or comparison of all published raid accounts. Family observations do not establish a diagnosis, and continued secrecy can reflect duty, trauma, temperament, or several motives. The later version cannot by itself prove friendship-driven accommodation or identify the historically correct account.
