# 五斗米道 / Wudoumi Dao

## Overview
五斗米道 / Wudoumi Dao is the late-Han religious movement the wiki now tracks through three related uses: Zhang Xiu's Ba-region healing/revolt branch, [[ZhangLuFivePecks|张鲁]]'s role in Liu Yan's Hanzhong route politics, and Zhang Lu's later Hanzhong religious-administrative order. Its significance is not only doctrine, but the capacity of religious practice to organize people, food, offices, trust, and local rule.

## Current Profile
Hanji 929 backfills an early Five-Pecks-style branch through [[ZhangXiuFivePecks|张修]] in [[BaCommanderyLateHan|巴郡]]. Zhang Xiu's healing practice resembles [[ZhangJiaoLateHan|张角]]'s religious mobilization, asks patients' families for five pecks of rice, and then turns into an autumn revolt whose followers are called "rice rebels."

Hanji 959 links Wudoumi Dao to Liu Yan's Hanzhong strategy through Zhang Lu's family tradition and his mother's access to [[LiuYanLateHan|刘焉]]. Hanji 995 then uses Wudoumi Dao as a comparator for late-Han religion as organization, while Hanji 1055 adds the most detailed governance layer: Zhang Lu uses confession, prayer, healing claims, local "jijiu" officers, lenient punishment sequencing, and reception of refugees to stabilize Hanzhong under nominal court accommodation.

## Key Characteristics
- Combines healing claims, ritual confession, and rice contribution into organized religious practice.
- Can become a revolt label when followers mobilize against late-Han authority.
- Serves Liu Yan's Hanzhong pass-control strategy through Zhang Lu's appointment.
- Becomes a local administrative structure in Zhang Lu's Hanzhong regime.
- Functions as a bounded comparator for religious organization without being equated with every violent religious-political case.

## Qualifications
The movement should not be flattened into a single moral category. Hanji 929 gives a revolt branch, Hanji 959 gives route-control utility, Hanji 995 gives a comparator for religious mobilization, and Hanji 1055 gives administrative stability under Zhang Lu. The page records reported healing practices as belief-practice and social organization, not as verified cures.
