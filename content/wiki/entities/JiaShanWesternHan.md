## Overview

贾山 / Jia Shan is a Western Han remonstrant whose current profile joins a warning about sovereign control of money to a broader theory of why imperial power can silence advisers, exhaust popular capacity, and convert recruited critics into recreational companions.

## Current Profile

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-211-wendi-liuheng-qingjian-jiashan-xian-zhiluan-ce-loc6cjf-qgkz5jgjckeur4h3wo7qp|Hanji 211]] presents Jia Shan addressing [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] after an eclipse-triggered call for criticism. His first claim concerns the speech environment: an emperor's authority is already frightening, so a formal invitation to speak does not ensure candor. If the ruler dislikes correction and adds intimidation or punishment, even exceptional wisdom and courage cannot make advisers speak effectively.

Jia Shan then uses a Zhou-Qin comparison to connect feedback with political economy. Zhou's many rulers are said to leave resources and labor among the people, whereas Qin Shihuang concentrates the realm's support on one ruler, exhausts popular capacity, criminalizes criticism, and becomes unable to understand the danger around him. The episode treats restrained desire, preserved popular capacity, and usable criticism as connected conditions of political survival rather than separate virtues.

The memorial ends with a role-boundary criticism. Liu Heng has summoned worthy and outspoken candidates but reportedly takes them hunting repeatedly. Jia Shan argues that ordinary companions may share feasts and recreation, while major ministers and upright advisers should meet the ruler in court to deliberate public affairs. The episode says Liu Heng praises and accepts this advice.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-216-hanchao-diyi-shoufu-jingran-shi-wendi-de-nanchong-ls2ebkz64ax0oywszpjrndfn3-k4|Hanji 216]] preserves a later monetary-sovereignty argument. Money may have little material value, but it purchases wealth and status; letting ordinary people make it therefore shares an authority Jia Shan presents as properly imperial. He asks Liu Heng to end private minting, but this warning is rejected. The two sources show neither uniform success nor simple ruler opposition: the same remonstrant is accepted on adviser use and rejected on coinage.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han court remonstrant who treats candid speech as an institutional problem shaped by the ruler's power, not merely the adviser's courage.
- Uses the Zhou-Qin contrast to connect restrained ruler consumption, preserved popular capacity, and access to criticism.
- Defends a boundary between imperial recreation and the deliberative role of senior ministers and recruited advisers.
- Treats money's exchange power, rather than its material substance alone, as politically decisive.
- Connects minting authority with sovereign control over wealth and rank.
- Adviser whose counsel receives different outcomes from Liu Heng: reported acceptance on court conduct and rejection on private minting.

## Qualifications

The profile rests on two short popular-history notes rather than Jia Shan's complete biography, the full memorial texts, or specialist work on early-Han politics and coinage. Hanji 211's kinship description, Zhou state count, Qin causal argument, wording, hunting frequency, and extent of adoption require textual comparison; its Zhou-Qin contrast is normative political diagnosis rather than a controlled explanation of dynastic outcomes. Hanji 216's constitutional meaning of “shared authority,” exact minting proposal, and reason Liu Heng rejected it likewise remain source-scoped. The sources support two distinct arguments by one remonstrant, not a complete economic or constitutional theory.

## What Changed

- Expanded Jia Shan from a one-issue monetary remonstrant into a thinker about authority, fear, popular capacity, and usable criticism.
- Distinguished the accepted adviser-role correction in Hanji 211 from the rejected minting warning in Hanji 216.
- Added the court-versus-recreation boundary without treating the episode's Zhou-Qin comparison as a complete causal history.
