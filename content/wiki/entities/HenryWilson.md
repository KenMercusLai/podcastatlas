# Henry Wilson

## Overview

Sir Henry Wilson was an Irish-born British field marshal, former Chief of the Imperial General Staff, and Ulster Unionist MP whose assassination on 22 June 1922 intensified the crisis around the Four Courts and the opening of the Irish Civil War.

## Current Profile

Wilson joined an Irish unionist upbringing to a senior imperial military career. He served in Burma and the Boer War, helped prepare the British Expeditionary Force, became Chief of the Imperial General Staff, and was promoted to field marshal in 1919. His involvement against imposing Home Rule in Ulster during the Curragh crisis blurred the boundary between professional soldier and political partisan, while his later demand for greater military force against the IRA put him at odds with [[DavidLloydGeorge|David Lloyd George]]'s turn toward negotiation.

After leaving military office, Wilson entered Parliament unopposed as an Ulster Unionist and became a symbol for opponents of the Anglo-Irish settlement. His advisory role to the Northern Irish government and hard public language made him a hated figure among nationalists amid sectarian violence. The episode nevertheless reports private qualifications: he opposed the Black and Tans as undisciplined forces and objected to sectarian policing, though not from a general rejection of coercion. IRA members Reggie Dunne and Joseph O'Sullivan killed him outside his London home; Britain's response helped convert his death into pressure on the emerging Irish state.

## Key Characteristics

- Irish-born unionist whose family identity was tied to Protestant settlement and the British connection.
- Senior imperial soldier with service in Burma, the Boer War, First World War planning, and the postwar army leadership.
- Politically active officer whose role in the Home Rule crisis damaged relations with Herbert Asquith.
- Advocate of treating the Irish War of Independence as a military problem rather than negotiating with the IRA.
- Ulster Unionist MP and rallying point for Conservative opponents of the Anglo-Irish settlement.
- Public symbol of Northern coercion whose private objections to indiscipline and sectarianism complicate, but do not reverse, the profile.
- Assassination victim whose death became an escalation point in the Four Courts crisis.

## Qualifications

This is a single-episode profile centered on Wilson's Irish politics and assassination, not a complete military biography. The account distinguishes public rhetoric from more qualified private views, but his preference for regular military discipline should not be mistaken for humanitarian opposition to force. The episode does not establish that Wilson personally directed Northern sectarian violence, that the Four Courts garrison ordered his death, or that his assassination alone caused the civil war. The claim that Collins authorized the killing remains a contested interpretation.
