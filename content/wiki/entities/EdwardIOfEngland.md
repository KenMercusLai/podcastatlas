# Edward I of England

## Overview

Edward I appears as a formidable medieval king whose military, legal, and administrative capacities cannot be separated from conquest, coercion, anti-Jewish persecution, and a hostile historical memory outside England.

## Current Profile

[[123-world-cup-of-kings-and-queens-part-1-glt3062961015]] presents Edward as physically imposing, militarily experienced, and effective at projecting royal authority. His defeat of Simon de Montfort and association with the Model Parliament support a traditional image of strong medieval government.

The same account makes effectiveness morally and nationally unstable. Edward's conquest of Wales, wars in Scotland, brutality after Evesham, and expulsion of England's Jewish population make the title “Hammer of the Scots” evidence of coercion rather than uncomplicated greatness. His heavy tournament loss to [[ElizabethI|Elizabeth I]] illustrates the gap between capability in office and modern affection.

Arthurian ritual adds symbolic statecraft to this coercive profile. In 1278 Edward and Eleanor of Castile presided over the reburial of remains claimed as Arthur and Guinevere at Glastonbury. During the struggle against Llywelyn ap Gruffudd, the ceremony let Edward present himself as heir to a ruler of united Britain while using a displayed corpse to challenge traditions in which Arthur remained alive and might return against English power. [[713-king-arthur-the-once-future-king-part-1-glt2516166521]]

## Key Characteristics

- Militarily capable ruler presented as a model of forceful medieval kingship.
- Defeated Simon de Montfort and restored royal authority after baronial conflict.
- Associated with administrative development and the Model Parliament.
- Conquered Wales and pursued domination in Scotland.
- Appropriated Arthurian burial and all-Britain kingship to support English rule and contain a rival return tradition.
- Combined state capacity with exemplary violence, persecution, and expulsion.
- Modern reputation changes sharply with national position and moral criteria.

## Evidence

- Royal and military capacity - [[123-world-cup-of-kings-and-queens-part-1-glt3062961015]] emphasizes Edward's stature, campaigning experience, victory over de Montfort, and administrative effectiveness.
- Institutional legacy - [[123-world-cup-of-kings-and-queens-part-1-glt3062961015]] connects him to the Model Parliament and a strong traditional image of medieval government.
- Conquest and coercion - [[123-world-cup-of-kings-and-queens-part-1-glt3062961015]] foregrounds Wales, Scotland, post-Evesham brutality, anti-Jewish policy, and expulsion.
- Comparative reputation - [[123-world-cup-of-kings-and-queens-part-1-glt3062961015]] uses his first-round defeat to show that effectiveness does not guarantee modern popularity.
- Arthurian appropriation - [[713-king-arthur-the-once-future-king-part-1-glt2516166521]] connects the 1278 Glastonbury reburial to Edward's conquest of Wales, Britain-wide inheritance claim, and opposition to return prophecy.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to two conversational surveys and is not a full account of Edward's reign, legislation, finances, wars, or the experiences of conquered and persecuted communities. The Model Parliament label, Glastonbury remains, motives assigned to the reburial, and audience effects remain source-scoped. Arthurian symbolism clarifies the political language of conquest; it does not authenticate the grave or reduce the conquest of Wales to ceremony.
