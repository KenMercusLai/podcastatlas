# Civil Constitution of the Clergy

## Overview

The Civil Constitution of the Clergy was the July 1790 revolutionary reorganization that made priests and bishops elected officeholders and required clergy to choose between loyalty to the nation and obedience to papal and ecclesiastical authority.

## Current Profile

The source treats the Civil Constitution and its oath as a major turning point from institutional reform toward [[RevolutionaryChurchStateRupture|religious-political rupture]]. It followed abolition of tithes, seizure of Church land, and dissolution of most monastic life. Roughly 54 percent of priests reportedly took the oath and 46 percent refused, but strong regional variation made religious allegiance a geographic and communal fault line that contributed to counterrevolution.

## Key Characteristics

- Reorganized diocesan and clerical authority under national law.
- Made priests and bishops elected rather than governed solely through older Church structures.
- Required a loyalty oath that forced a choice between nation and papal obedience.
- Divided clergy and lay communities along regional as well as ideological lines.
- Converted fiscal and administrative reform into a broader legitimacy crisis.

## Qualifications

- National percentages conceal local and regional variation.
- Refusal of the oath did not give every priest or lay Catholic the same political motive.
- The source emphasizes fracture but does not provide a complete legal or administrative history of the measure.

## What Changed

- Created the profile around the oath as the mechanism that translated institutional reform into divided legitimacy.
