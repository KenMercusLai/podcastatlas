# Elizabeth Tung

## Overview

Elizabeth Tung is the physician-researcher who connects Chicago's trauma-center geography to racial inequality, hospital reimbursement, and the moral strain placed on clinicians and institutions serving high-need populations.

## Current Profile

The episode attributes to Tung research showing that majority-Black census tracts were disproportionately located in trauma deserts, including an approximately sevenfold South Side disparity. It places that geography within the period from 1991 to 2018 when the South Side had no level-one trauma center.

Tung argues that payer mix can make high-need gunshot care financially unattractive because uninsured patients and Medicaid reimbursement bring hospitals less revenue than privately insured patients. She presents nationalized healthcare as the simplest way to align trauma-center planning with need and links the current incentive conflict to moral injury and demoralization among healthcare workers.

## Key Characteristics

- Studies the racial and geographic distribution of access to trauma care.
- Connects facility location to financing incentives rather than treating geography as accidental.
- Frames payer mix as a structural constraint on institutions serving the sickest populations.
- Links distorted incentives to clinician moral injury and demoralization.
- Advocates uniform coverage as a way to plan trauma capacity around need rather than ability to pay.

## Evidence

### Trauma-desert geography

- [[guns-part-5-the-footnote]] reports Tung's finding that majority-Black Chicago census tracts, especially on the South Side, were disproportionately distant from level-one trauma care.
- [[doctors-guns-and-money]] independently previews the same trauma-desert pattern and the episode-reported sevenfold South Side disparity.

### Financing and workforce effects

- [[guns-part-5-the-footnote]] attributes to Tung the payer-mix explanation, nationalized-healthcare proposal, and moral-injury framing.
- [[doctors-guns-and-money]] corroborates the narrower claim that Medicaid and uninsured care can make high-need neighborhoods financially unattractive locations for trauma centers.

## Qualifications

The sources do not provide Tung's paper, methods, geographic definitions, time-series controls, reimbursement data, comparative financing analysis, or direct policy design; the new source is also a short promotional preview. The sevenfold disparity, causal role of payer mix, nationalized-care judgment, and moral-injury account remain episode-scoped rather than a complete evaluation of Chicago or U.S. trauma-system planning.
