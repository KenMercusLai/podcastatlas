# David Shlapak

## Overview

David Shlapak is the [[RAND]] researcher in [[a-serious-game-a-serious-game|A Serious Game]] who uses strategic simulation and controlled randomness to push military participants beyond outcomes dictated by prior logic and experience.

## Current Profile

Shlapak appears through two major cases. In repeated games of a Russian attack on Baltic NATO states, changing participants did not eliminate an outcome in which Russia prevailed; the lesson was to examine an uncomfortable possibility, not to predict a certain war. In a North Korean collapse scenario, a seemingly bounded nuclear-security mission expanded into years of site control, records and scientist recovery, refugee congestion, chemical protection, manpower demand, casualties, and pressure surrounding possible nuclear use.

His introduction of dice makes the method explicit. Randomness is not treated as an oracle; it interrupts participants' tendency to make a synthetic crisis reproduce what they already expect.

## Key Characteristics

- Uses artificial but realistic situations to expose unconsidered possibilities.
- Introduces chance to disrupt habitual reasoning and deterministic play.
- Repeats games and changes participants before treating a surprising result as analytically important.
- Connects strategic objectives to manpower, logistics, civilians, contamination, and time.
- Distinguishes a scenario worth examining from an outcome that should be forecast as certain.

## Evidence

### Randomized disruption of expectations

- [[a-serious-game-a-serious-game|A Serious Game]] describes Shlapak introducing dice and finding that experienced officers welcomed being pushed away from outcomes determined only by their prior reasoning.

### Baltic and Korean scenarios

- [[a-serious-game-a-serious-game|A Serious Game]] attributes to Shlapak the repeated Baltic simulations and describes RAND's North Korean collapse exercise as revealing the mission's physical, human, logistical, and temporal scale.

## Qualifications

The source does not provide the Baltic or Korean game materials, adjudication rules, player roster, sensitivity analysis, or after-action reports. Repetition inside one design cannot establish a real-world result, and randomization can widen considered outcomes without proving that assigned probabilities are calibrated. The profile captures Shlapak's role in this episode rather than a complete account of his career.
