# Teodros II

## Overview

Teodros II is presented by [[705-queen-victorias-revenge-the-mad-emperor-of-abyssinia-part-1-glt8608697296]] and [[706-queen-victorias-revenge-storming-the-citadel-part-2-glt3285474568]] as the [[Abyssinia|Abyssinian]] emperor whose modernizing ambitions, religious monarchy, anger at British neglect and hostage-taking lead toward the [[AbyssinianExpedition1868|Abyssinian Expedition of 1868]], and whose suicide at [[Magdala]] ends both his reign and the campaign.

## Current Profile

The first episode follows Teodros from Kassa, a successful warlord in a fragmented Abyssinia, into the messianic imperial name Teodros II in 1855. He is portrayed as energetic, tactful, charismatic and reform-minded at first, but also proud, violent, religiously intense and increasingly suspicious. His admiration for Britain and desire for a Christian alliance turn into hostility when [[QueenVictoria]]'s answer does not arrive and when missionaries appear to insult his lineage and religious authority, and his response is to hold envoys, missionaries and technicians as leverage.

The second episode carries him from campaign to collapse. He returns to Magdala on 27 March 1868 in a storm with roughly 50,000 people and the heavy guns he had refused to abandon, including the 70-ton mortar Sebastopol, tells his courtiers of a prophecy that a European king would meet an Ethiopian king, greets [[HormuzdRassam]] with "today we must all be English", and then turns on him with the fear of being despised because he was black and the accusation that Rassam had plotted against their families. He dresses for war in gold-threaded silk and white pantaloons in a way Rassam compared to a harlequin, releases seventy Abyssinian captives, and tells his army to enrich themselves with the spoils of "these white slaves" while warning that their bellies would be filled with bullets first.

His end is violent and self-dramatizing. After drinking heavily he has about 300 prisoners thrown over the cliff on the Thursday before Easter, then prays aloud confessing that he was drunk when he gave the order. At the Battle of Magdala he watches from the heights as his charge is destroyed by continuous rifle fire, sends Rassam to seek terms, refuses to ask [[RobertNapier|Napier]] for terms on the ground that Napier had been sent by a woman, and after a failed suicide attempt tries to buy peace with 1,000 cows and 500 sheep, which Napier refused. When his men refuse to follow him down the mountain again, he releases them from their allegiance, shoots himself with the silver duelling pistol Queen Victoria had given him, and is buried in the Coptic church on the Magdala hilltop the next day.

In Ethiopia he is remembered very differently. The source describes him as a cult figure whose image appears on t-shirts, badges and the walls of bars in Addis Ababa, a patriotic hero who died fighting European imperialism and a symbol of Ethiopian independence; Emperor Theodros Square in central Addis Ababa holds a replica of Sebastopol while the real gun lies half-buried at Magdala, never fired and showing no sign of ever having worked.

## Key Characteristics

- He combines reunification ambition with a reform programme: crushing warlords, roads, royal justice reform, anti-slavery action and military modernization.
- He sees diplomacy through a Christian-monarchical lens, especially under pressure from Muslim powers and European rivalry.
- His relationship with Britain is highly symbolic: recognition, reply and respectful attention matter alongside weapons and technicians.
- His rule becomes increasingly coercive, and hostage-taking turns envoys, missionaries and technicians into leverage over Britain.
- His endgame is theatrical and prophetic rather than strategic: heavy guns, a European-king prophecy, ornate dress, contradictory speeches and gifts of livestock.
- Violence escalates against his own subjects, culminating in the massacre of about 300 prisoners and his own prayer admitting that he had been drunk.
- He dies by his own hand with Queen Victoria's gift pistol and is remembered in Ethiopia as an anti-imperial hero rather than as a tyrant.

## Evidence

- Modernizer and reunifier: [[705-queen-victorias-revenge-the-mad-emperor-of-abyssinia-part-1-glt8608697296]] says he wants to crush warlords, build roads, reform royal justice, fight slavery, modernize dress and improve military capability.
- Religious and diplomatic worldview: [[705-queen-victorias-revenge-the-mad-emperor-of-abyssinia-part-1-glt8608697296]] says he asks [[QueenVictoria]] for Christian friendship and struggles to understand why Britain and France allied with the [[OttomanEmpire]] against Russia in the Crimean War.
- Coercive turn: [[705-queen-victorias-revenge-the-mad-emperor-of-abyssinia-part-1-glt8608697296]] follows his punitive mutilations, artillery obsession, Stern's beating, [[CharlesDuncanCameron|Cameron]]'s imprisonment, [[HormuzdRassam]]'s arrest, and the demand for technicians and guns.
- Sebastopol and prophecy: [[706-queen-victorias-revenge-storming-the-citadel-part-2-glt3285474568]] says he dragged his favourite guns back to Magdala, including the 70-ton mortar Sebastopol cast by Swiss and German missionaries, and told courtiers about a prophecy that a European king would meet an Ethiopian king.
- Theatrical self-presentation: [[706-queen-victorias-revenge-storming-the-citadel-part-2-glt3285474568]] describes the gold-threaded silk robe and white pantaloons that Rassam thought made him look like a harlequin, the release of seventy Abyssinian captives, and his speech urging his army to enrich themselves from the spoils.
- Escalating violence: [[706-queen-victorias-revenge-storming-the-citadel-part-2-glt3285474568]] records the massacre of about 300 prisoners on the Thursday before Easter and his prayer confessing to God that drink had been taken.
- Collapse of negotiation: [[706-queen-victorias-revenge-storming-the-citadel-part-2-glt3285474568]] says he refused Rassam's advice to seek terms because Napier had been sent by a woman, sent an incoherent reply, made a failed suicide attempt, and later sent 1,000 cows and 500 sheep asking whether it was peace now.
- Death and burial: [[706-queen-victorias-revenge-storming-the-citadel-part-2-glt3285474568]] says he killed himself on Easter Monday with the silver duelling pistol given to him by Queen Victoria, that Dr Blanc's post-mortem found death gave him an air of calm grandeur, and that Rassam organised a Coptic funeral on the hilltop.
- Ethiopian memory: [[706-queen-victorias-revenge-storming-the-citadel-part-2-glt3285474568]] says he is now a cult figure in Addis Ababa and a symbol of Ethiopian independence, with a replica of Sebastopol in the square named after him.

## Qualifications

The sources repeatedly warn against caricature and are themselves one-sided. Teodros is shown through European and missionary records as modernizer, Christian ruler, admirer of Britain, violent autocrat, possible sufferer of mental collapse and hostage-taker. His mental state, the wording of the prophecy, the size of the prisoner massacre and the battle casualties are all reported rather than verified, and the Ethiopian perspective appears only through his later memory and a single reference to [[MenelikII]]. The wiki should treat his motives and his illness as source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Extended the profile from prewar hostage politics into the campaign endgame: the return to Magdala with Sebastopol, the prophecy, the prisoner massacre, the Battle of Magdala, the failed suicide and death with Queen Victoria's gift pistol.
- Added his posthumous reversal into an Ethiopian anti-imperial symbol, together with the physical residue of the story at Magdala and in Addis Ababa.
