# Plagues Upon the Earth

## Overview

*Plagues Upon the Earth* is [[KyleHarper]]'s global history of disease, presented in the source as the intellectual basis for a discussion spanning the Columbian Exchange, Atlantic slavery, urban public health, cholera, influenza, COVID-19, and globalization.

## Current Profile

The episode portrays the book as an ecological and multi-causal history. Pathogens matter through interactions among human bodies, animal reservoirs, vectors, crops, water, settlement, transport, empire, technology, and public institutions rather than as isolated biological invaders.

Its normative position is neither anti-modern nor complacent. Human connectivity creates biological opportunity for pathogens, but medicine, sanitation, technology, and globalization also produce large gains; the practical task is to understand and govern the risks attached to those gains.

## Key Characteristics

- Global history connecting disease to ecology, political economy, empire, and technology.
- Multi-causal account that keeps war, famine, labor systems, violence, and institutions beside pathogens.
- Uses ancient DNA and other biological evidence alongside historical interpretation.
- Treats connectivity as simultaneously beneficial and epidemiologically risky.
- Presented as ultimately hopeful about long-run public-health progress.

## Evidence

- Historical range - [[147-disease-the-new-world-and-modern-pandemics-glt1023018457]] moves from pre-Columbian tuberculosis and sixteenth-century epidemics through slavery, cholera, influenza, and COVID-19.
- Ecological method - [[147-disease-the-new-world-and-modern-pandemics-glt1023018457]] connects vectors, crops, water, settlement, transport, and population scale to disease opportunity.
- Progress boundary - [[147-disease-the-new-world-and-modern-pandemics-glt1023018457]] rejects reversing globalization while arguing that societies must manage its biological exposure.

## Qualifications

This profile derives from one short interview about the book, not a complete reading of it. Population estimates, pathogen origins, mortality totals, regional disease gradients, and causal comparisons remain the episode's compressed presentation unless independently supported elsewhere.
