# Plagues Upon the Earth

## Overview

*Plagues Upon the Earth* is [[KyleHarper]]'s global history of disease, presented in the sources as the intellectual basis for discussions spanning human prehistory, agriculture, cities, the Black Death, the Columbian Exchange, Atlantic slavery, urban public health, cholera, influenza, COVID-19, and globalization.

## Current Profile

The episode portrays the book as an ecological and multi-causal history. Pathogens matter through interactions among human bodies, animal reservoirs, vectors, crops, water, settlement, transport, empire, technology, and public institutions rather than as isolated biological invaders.

Its normative position is neither anti-modern nor complacent. Human connectivity creates biological opportunity for pathogens, but medicine, sanitation, technology, and globalization also produce large gains; the practical task is to understand and govern the risks attached to those gains.

The book's deepest contrast is between the long historical dominance of infectious mortality and the recent [[MortalityTransition|mortality transition]]. Human ecological flexibility expanded the disease pool, sedentary agriculture concentrated waste and animal contact, and cities often required rural migration to offset excess deaths; modern control changed these conditions without making pathogen evolution cease.

## Key Characteristics

- Global history connecting disease to ecology, political economy, empire, and technology.
- Multi-causal account that keeps war, famine, labor systems, violence, and institutions beside pathogens.
- Uses ancient DNA and other biological evidence alongside historical interpretation.
- Treats connectivity as simultaneously beneficial and epidemiologically risky.
- Presented as ultimately hopeful about long-run public-health progress.
- Extends disease history from hunter-gatherer ecologies and agriculture to modern changes in how humans die.

## Evidence

- Historical range - [[147-disease-the-new-world-and-modern-pandemics-glt1023018457]] moves from pre-Columbian tuberculosis and sixteenth-century epidemics through slavery, cholera, influenza, and COVID-19.
- Ecological method - [[147-disease-the-new-world-and-modern-pandemics-glt1023018457]] connects vectors, crops, water, settlement, transport, and population scale to disease opportunity.
- Progress boundary - [[147-disease-the-new-world-and-modern-pandemics-glt1023018457]] rejects reversing globalization while arguing that societies must manage its biological exposure.
- Deep-history range - [[146-disease-vs-the-rise-of-civilisation-glt4238916512]] connects migration, fire, agriculture, waste, cities, and plague to changing human pathogen ecologies.
- Mortality structure - [[146-disease-vs-the-rise-of-civilisation-glt4238916512]] frames the modern displacement of infectious death as an exceptional transformation with demographic and social consequences.

## Qualifications

This profile derives from two short interviews about the book, not a complete reading of it. Hunter-gatherer comparisons, pathogen ages, malaria origins, urban demographic claims, population estimates, mortality totals, regional disease gradients, plague geography, and causal comparisons remain the episodes' compressed presentation unless independently supported elsewhere.

## What Changed

- Created a book profile centered on ecological disease history and the dual effects of connectivity.
- Extended the profile into prehistory, agricultural settlement, the Black Death, and the mortality transition.
