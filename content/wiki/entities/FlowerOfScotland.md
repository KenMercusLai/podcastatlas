# Flower of Scotland

## Overview

Flower of Scotland appears in the wiki as the Scottish sporting anthem that gives [[Scotland]] a more specific public identity than [[GodSaveTheKing|God Save the King]] in football and rugby settings.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Flower of Scotland as a modern song that feels old because it draws on medieval victory memory. Composed in 1966 by Roy Williamson of The Corries, it celebrates Robert Bruce's victory over Edward II at Bannockburn in 1314. Its sporting power comes from converting that memory into stadium emotion, especially when [[Scotland]] plays [[England]].

## Key Characteristics

- Modern Scottish song composed in 1966 but built around medieval victory memory.
- Sporting anthem used by Scottish rugby and football instead of [[GodSaveTheKing|God Save the King]].
- Bannockburn-centered lyric whose force is strongest in Scotland-England sporting contexts.
- Example of how historical memory becomes active through team ceremony rather than only through state ceremony.
- Contrast case for [[England]]'s continued use of the British anthem.

## Evidence

- Composition and authorship: [[678-britain-god-save-the-king-part-2-glt2603959218]] says Roy Williamson of The Corries composed the song in 1966.
- Historical subject: [[678-britain-god-save-the-king-part-2-glt2603959218]] says the song celebrates Robert Bruce's victory over Edward II at Bannockburn in 1314.
- Sporting adoption: [[678-britain-god-save-the-king-part-2-glt2603959218]] says Scottish rugby and football eventually adopted the song instead of [[GodSaveTheKing|God Save the King]], with the Scottish FA adopting it for the 1998 World Cup.
- Rivalry effect: [[678-britain-god-save-the-king-part-2-glt2603959218]] says the song works especially well when [[Scotland]] plays [[England]] because it refers to defeating Edward's army.
