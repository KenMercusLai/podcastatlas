# Jan Hus

## Overview

Jan Hus was a Bohemian religious reformer whose scriptural emphasis, criticism of clergy and papal primacy, and execution at the Council of Constance made him a decisive precedent for the danger later faced by [[MartinLuther]].

## Current Profile

The episode presents Hus as an earlier critic influenced by John Wycliffe rather than as a fully formed Protestant before Protestantism. He elevated biblical authority, attacked clerical corruption, challenged papal primacy, and argued that laypeople should receive both bread and wine at mass.

Summoned to the Council of Constance in 1414 under safe conduct, Hus was arrested, tried, and burned after refusing to recant; the episode says his ashes were cast into the Rhine. His fate supplied [[JohannEck]] with a powerful precedent at Leipzig: connecting Luther to Hus turned a dispute about reform into a test of whether Luther would defend propositions already condemned as heresy.

## Key Characteristics

- Bohemian reformer influenced by John Wycliffe's criticism of church authority.
- Treated the Bible as a higher authority than corrupt clergy or unqualified papal claims.
- Criticized clerical corruption and defended communion in both kinds for laypeople.
- Refused recantation at the Council of Constance and was executed as a heretic.
- Became a precedent through which later reformers could be classified and endangered.

## Evidence

- Reform program: [[433-luther-the-man-who-changed-the-world-part-1-glt7298373279]] links Hus to Wycliffe, biblical authority, criticism of clergy, a challenge to papal primacy, and communion in bread and wine for laypeople.
- Trial and execution: [[433-luther-the-man-who-changed-the-world-part-1-glt7298373279]] describes his summons, arrest despite safe conduct, refusal to recant, burning, and disposal of his ashes.
- Reformation precedent: [[433-luther-the-man-who-changed-the-world-part-1-glt7298373279]] uses Hus to show why religious criticism could become heresy and why the later survival of Luther's challenge requires explanation.

## Qualifications

The source offers a compressed retrospective account rather than a complete study of Hus, Wycliffe, the Council of Constance, Hussite belief, or Bohemian politics. Exact reported motives, legal protections, dialogue, doctrinal distinctions, and the disposal of Hus's ashes remain episode-attributed. Calling Hus a Reformation precursor should not erase his fifteenth-century setting or imply that his program and Luther's were identical.

## What Changed

- Established Hus as an independent historical actor rather than only a label applied to Luther at Leipzig.
- Clarified how his execution made the boundary between internal reform and condemned heresy concrete.

## Relationships

- [[MartinLuther]] - later reformer whose opponents used the Hussite precedent to expose the implications of his authority claims.
- [[JohannEck]] - Leipzig debater who pressed Luther to defend propositions associated with Hus.
