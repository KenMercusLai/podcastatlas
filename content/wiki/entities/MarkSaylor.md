# Mark Saylor

## Overview

Mark Saylor was a California Highway Patrol officer whose 2009 death with three family members in a runaway loaner Lexus became the catalytic case in the [[Toyota]] sudden-acceleration scandal.

## Current Profile

[[blame-game-blame-game|Blame Game]] presents Saylor as an experienced, non-reckless driver caught in a catastrophic event. An improperly secured oversized floor mat reportedly could trap the accelerator in the loaner vehicle. The episode then makes a more controversial inference: panic, unfamiliar pedal geometry, and [[PedalMisapplicationUnderStress|pedal misapplication]] may explain why the functioning brakes were apparently not used to stop the car.

The source expressly separates this proposed mistake from negligence. Saylor's role in the wiki is therefore not that of a culpable driver, but of the person whose tragedy demonstrates how sincere perception, intended action, and bodily action can diverge under extreme stress.

## Key Characteristics

- California Highway Patrol officer driving an unfamiliar loaner Lexus.
- Killed with his wife, daughter, and brother-in-law in the 2009 crash.
- Driver in the case that catalyzed nationwide fear, recalls, hearings, and litigation.
- Presented as competent and non-reckless rather than negligent.
- Central but inferential example of pedal misapplication under panic.

## Evidence

### Crash and public consequence

- [[blame-game-blame-game]] reports the fatal crash, the emergency call, and the subsequent escalation of sudden-acceleration concern around Toyota and Lexus vehicles.

### Human-factors interpretation

- [[blame-game-blame-game]] combines the displaced-mat finding, controlled brake tests, event-recorder patterns, and Dick Schmidt's research to propose a sequence of trapped throttle followed by mistaken pedal application.

## Qualifications

The supplied source is a structured summary rather than the crash report, event-recorder record, vehicle inspection, 911 transcript, or testimony from the occupants. The pedal-misapplication sequence is Gladwell's inference, not direct evidence of Saylor's final foot movements. Calling the event non-negligent reflects the episode's judgment and should not be treated as an independent legal finding.
