# Stonehenge

## Overview

Stonehenge is a prehistoric monument on Salisbury Plain in southern Britain whose surviving earthworks, Welsh bluestones, local sarsens, burials, and solar axis record repeated reconstruction over roughly a millennium.

## Current Profile

[[199-stonehenge-glt3612348466]] presents Stonehenge as a changing place rather than a single completed design. Its earliest visible phase may have placed bluestones from Wales in a precise circle within earthworks; the site then became a major cremation cemetery. Around 2500 BCE, builders moved the bluestones inward and raised the famous sarsen circle and trilithon horseshoe before burial at the monument ceased.

The monument belonged to a wider [[StonehengeRitualLandscape|ritual landscape]] that included [[DurringtonWalls|Durrington Walls]], feasting, seasonal gathering, exchange, and possible builder accommodation. [[114-stonehenge-ancient-ritual-and-the-origins-of-paganism-glt2588049137]] reinforces the central confidence boundary: archaeology now answers much about when, how, and by whom Stonehenge was built while leaving its purpose and theology unresolved. Its midsummer-sunrise and midwinter-sunset axis is materially grounded, while claims about sacrifice, scientific computation, painted stones, music, restricted access, ancestor veneration, and the exact political organization of construction vary sharply in evidential strength.

## Key Characteristics

- A long-lived monument whose arrangement and use changed across many generations.
- A material combination of distant Welsh bluestones and much larger sarsens sourced closer to Salisbury Plain.
- A burial place, including a substantial cremation cemetery, before the major sarsen transformation.
- A geometrically planned and solstice-aligned monument requiring large-scale transport, dressing, and construction.
- A focal point within a broader landscape of gathering, feasting, ritual, mobility, and exchange.
- An archaeological case where strong material findings coexist with unresolved questions of belief, authority, and lived experience.

## Evidence

- Sequence and reconstruction: [[199-stonehenge-glt3612348466]] distinguishes the bluestone and cremation phase from the later sarsen circle and trilithon horseshoe.
- Material networks: [[199-stonehenge-glt3612348466]] reports geological provenance in Wales for bluestones and West Woods on the Marlborough Downs for one large sarsen upright.
- Burial and mobility: [[199-stonehenge-glt3612348466]] describes extensive cremation burial and isotope evidence that some buried people were not local to central southern England.
- Landscape and gathering: [[199-stonehenge-glt3612348466]] connects the monument to Durrington Walls, pig feasting, seasonal assembly, and long-distance exchange.
- Alignment boundary: [[199-stonehenge-glt3612348466]] accepts the solstice axis while rejecting direct evidence for human sacrifice or a modern scientific computer.
- Knowledge boundary: [[114-stonehenge-ancient-ritual-and-the-origins-of-paganism-glt2588049137]] separates improved knowledge of chronology, builders, and technique from continuing ignorance about purpose.

## Qualifications

The first bluestone arrangement and exact transport routes remain disputed. Material survival cannot recover the full sensory setting or decide whether stones were painted, decorated, sounded, hung with objects, restricted to selected people, or understood as ancestors. The comparison to Westminster Abbey and proposal that sarsens protected ancestor-associated bluestones are useful interpretations, not demonstrated functions. Ancient DNA shows population change after major construction but does not by itself establish genocide or explain the monument's decline. Calling the landscape sacred or spiritual is plausible from its scale and treatment, but neither source recovers a specific creed or ritual script.

## What Changed

- Established Stonehenge as a changing, multi-phase monument rather than a single-purpose structure.
- Separated secure material and chronological findings from more tentative claims about ancestors, authority, access, and ritual experience.
- Sharpened the distinction between growing knowledge of construction and persistent uncertainty about religious purpose.
