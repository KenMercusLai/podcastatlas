# Richard II of England

## Overview

Richard II was the English king whose sacred self-presentation, conflicts with magnates and Parliament, retaliation against the [[LordsAppellant|Lords Appellant]], and seizure of the Lancastrian inheritance culminated in deposition by [[HenryIVOfEngland|Henry Bolingbroke]] and a lasting dynastic legitimacy crisis.

## Current Profile

Richard's experience of the 1381 revolt forms the episode's psychological opening: disorder may have left him fearful of political helplessness while also showing that rebels could revere the king and hate his ministers. Without the military achievements or aristocratic ease associated with Edward III and the Black Prince, he emphasized anointing, lawful succession, Edward the Confessor, Westminster, style, ceremony, and religious imagery. [[SacralKingshipPoliticalControl]] was therefore both belief and political method.

His early government relied on favorites including Michael de la Pole, Simon Burley, and Robert de Vere. Failed war leadership, a French invasion scare, fiscal demands, and [[JohnOfGaunt|John of Gaunt's]] absence enabled the Wonderful Parliament to dismiss de la Pole and impose a governing council. The [[LordsAppellant]] then defeated de Vere at Radcot Bridge, forced Richard's surrender, and used the Merciless Parliament to destroy his circle. The sacred force of anointed monarchy helped keep deposition from becoming the immediate solution.

Richard recovered authority in 1389 through patience, his opponents' declining prestige, and selective rewards to Mowbray and Bolingbroke. He pursued a long French truce, intervened personally in Ireland, intensified royal imagery, and sought freedom from parliamentary finance. Yet large loans, intimidation, spending, confiscation, armed followers, and the 1397 revenge against Gloucester, Arundel, and Warwick made the restored monarchy less reconcilable.

The decisive rupture came after Richard exiled Bolingbroke and, following Gaunt's death, blocked his return and moved against the Lancastrian inheritance. What began as a royal attempt to neutralize a rival and enlarge the crown instead alarmed nobles about property and succession. Henry returned claiming only his duchy, gained support while Richard was in Ireland, and forced surrender at Flint Castle. Parliament approved deposition, Henry was crowned, and Richard died after a failed restoration rising.

Richard's removal solved an immediate governing crisis while transferring it into [[LancastrianLegitimacy|Lancastrian legitimacy]]. A weak hereditary case, the deposition of an anointed king, and suspicion of regicide let later opposition reactivate Richard's memory, while Shakespeare made the ceremonial and bodily fragility of kingship culturally durable.

## Key Characteristics

- Anointed hereditary king who made sacred dignity and visual distinction central to political authority.
- Ruler without comparable military glory whose favorites and difficult magnate relations weakened coalition support.
- Patient political survivor who recovered authority after the Appellant ascendancy.
- Financially ambitious monarch seeking independence from parliamentary taxation through loans, grants, confiscations, and loyal force.
- Vengeful ruler whose destruction of former opponents made reconciliation and submission increasingly unsafe.
- Deposed monarch whose imprisonment and death became a lasting liability for Henry IV and the Lancastrian dynasty.

## Qualifications

The sources provide narrative and interpretation rather than a complete administrative history. Richard's psychology after 1381, the coherence of an absolutist program, the motives of favorites and magnates, Henry's original intentions, Gloucester's death, hereditary priority among Lancaster and Mortimer, and the manner and responsibility for Richard's death remain disputed or source-scoped. The episode's Charles I analogy identifies a pattern of finance and personal rule but does not erase the different medieval institutions.
