## Overview

河间献王刘德 / Liu De is presented as a learned Western Han vassal king whose intelligence and scholarship support the posthumous title “献,” but whose cultural standing is also interpreted by the episode as a source of danger under [[HanWudi|汉武帝]].

## Current Profile

The current profile rests on one podcast installment and therefore separates the episode's narrative from settled causation. It portrays Liu De as Han Wudi's elder half-brother, a strong reader of Confucian texts, and an effective respondent on classical questions. His learning explains the favorable posthumous title in the host's account, while the same learning and teaching activity allegedly made him politically legible as more than a passive vassal king.

The pivotal scene is Han Wudi's remark that Tang rose from seventy *li* and King Wen from one hundred. In ordinary Confucian argument, the examples support rule through benevolence despite a small territorial base. The host instead reads the emperor's use of them as an insinuation that Liu De might harbor a path from limited territory to the realm. The source then connects Liu De's return to his fief, abandonment of study and teaching, heavy drinking, and dejected death to this warning. Because the supplied note also says the [[ZizhiTongjian|《资治通鉴》]] does not give his true cause of death, that chain remains an attributed interpretation.

## Key Characteristics

- Western Han royal prince and half-brother of Han Wudi in the episode's family frame.
- Learned vassal king associated with Confucian study, teaching, and fluent answers at court.
- Recipient of the posthumous title “献,” which the host links to intelligence and learning.
- Interpreted as politically vulnerable because cultural authority could attract people and trigger central suspicion.
- Said to have withdrawn from study and teaching and turned to pleasure after Han Wudi's warning.

## Qualifications

The page does not independently establish Liu De's full biography, textual collection, historical relationship to the [[Shijing|《诗经》]], or cause of death. The title promises a Shijing connection, but the supplied installment does not explain it. Han Wudi's intent, tone, suspicion, Liu De's inner response, and the causal relation between the encounter, withdrawal, drinking, and death remain source-scoped. The episode's broad contrast between Hebei cultural strength and the capital region also needs evidence beyond this note.
