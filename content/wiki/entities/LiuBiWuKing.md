## Overview

刘濞 / Liu Bi enters the current wiki as [[LiuBang|刘邦]]'s nephew and king of [[WuKingdomEarlyHan|early Han Wu]], then reappears as a bereaved and estranged vassal before becoming the leading ruler in the [[RebellionOfTheSevenStatesWesternHan|Rebellion of the Seven States]]. The four sources turn an early same-surname security appointment into a long grievance-and-autonomy crisis, followed by strategic narrowing and the loss of allied protection after defeat.

## Current Profile

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-189-liubang-siqian-zuihou-yibo-lllydj2xyk4cr7b9d2qx0hvfw-dz|Hanji 189]] supplies Liu Bi's institutional beginning. After [[YingBu|英布]] is defeated, Liu Bang reorganizes the former Jing field and appoints his nephew over three commanderies and fifty-three counties. The grant belongs to [[SameSurnameKingEnfeoffment|same-surname enfeoffment]]: exposed territory is entrusted to royal kin after a dangerous vassal branch falls.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-231-hanjingdi-jiuhou-jing-zaojiu-wulong-chuanwei-shijian-1-lluz1oxfqrazahrdas5sl5ybouwj|Hanji 231 part 1]] supplies the first current rupture. During Emperor Wen's reign, the future Jingdi reportedly kills Liu Bi's heir [[LiuXianWuCrownPrinceWesternHan|刘贤]] in a drunken board-game quarrel. The court sends the coffin to Wu; Liu Bi sends it back to Chang'an, then avoids required attendance for years. When the court detains Wu envoys and Liu Bi becomes frightened enough to consider revolt, a later envoy explains the fear. [[LiuHeng|Emperor Wen]] releases the detainees, gives Liu Bi age-honoring gifts, and excuses attendance, temporarily calming him. The sequence makes personal loss, public humiliation, nonattendance, and coercive scrutiny an escalation chain, not a sufficient single cause of rebellion.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-234-zhouyafu-zhanchang-dalian-hanjingdi-na-lai-de-danzi-li6iqhtuxgq5xwgbn_vb7lljgyxr|Hanji 234]] supplies the later reversal. After the court kills Chao Cuo and sends Yuan Ang and Liu Tong to seek withdrawal, Liu Bi detains the mission, reportedly calls himself an “eastern emperor,” and ignores Mei Cheng's warning that continuing the campaign will expose his political claim, rear, and food route. The episode says he tries to recruit Yuan Ang, then orders him killed after refusal.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-235-liubi-zhisi-jielu-zhichang-shengcun-qian-guize-lp1wxe_buqe_zrilsfwsfzqoa2ef|Hanji 235]] supplies the final narrowing and collapse. Liu Bi rejects [[TianLuboWesternHan|田禄伯]]'s proposed second front after an heir raises the risk of delegated betrayal, then lets senior commanders dispose of an unnamed young general's Luoyang plan through age and status rather than a supplied operational rebuttal. The contrast with [[ZhouQiuWesternHan|周丘]] is sharp: a retainer Liu Bi has disregarded can use one tally and local ties to mobilize quickly, but even that force loses its larger purpose when Liu Bi flees.

The same episode ends Liu Bi's arc. After the Wu-Chu force collapses, he crosses the Yangtze with remnants and seeks refuge from a ruler rendered as “东海王落望.” The court's payment changes that ruler's incentives; an invitation to review troops creates the access for Liu Bi's killing. The joined profile is not that Liu-family enfeoffment mechanically causes rebellion or that either rejected plan would have won. It shows how a large royal base can support resistance, while distrust, status-filtered advice, logistical defeat, and alliance repricing can progressively remove its options and protection.

## Key Characteristics

- Liu Bang's nephew and son of [[LiuXiDaiKing|刘喜]], appointed over a large Wu kingdom after Ying Bu's defeat and reorganization of the Jing field.
- Bereaved father whose coffin dispute, nonattendance, and fear after envoy detention form an early estrangement from the court.
- Same-surname royal appointment that later becomes a leading source of armed resistance to the center.
- Rebel ruler who rejects a withdrawal mission and reportedly claims an eastern imperial title.
- Coalition leader warned that continued advance could expose his political legitimacy, rear, and supply line.
- Decision-maker who rejects one proposal through defection fear and another after senior commanders dismiss its young advocate.
- Defeated leader whose remaining ally reprices the relationship and kills him after accepting a court payment.

## Evidence

Royal appointment and territorial base:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-189-liubang-siqian-zuihou-yibo-lllydj2xyk4cr7b9d2qx0hvfw-dz|Hanji 189]] says Liu Bang makes Liu Bi king of Wu over three commanderies and fifty-three counties after Ying Bu's defeat.

Early grievance and temporary de-escalation:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-231-hanjingdi-jiuhou-jing-zaojiu-wulong-chuanwei-shijian-1-lluz1oxfqrazahrdas5sl5ybouwj|Hanji 231 part 1]] links Liu Xian's death, the returned coffin, Liu Bi's long nonattendance, detained envoys, fear, and early revolt thought before Emperor Wen releases and exempts him.

Open break with the court:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-234-zhouyafu-zhanchang-dalian-hanjingdi-na-lai-de-danzi-li6iqhtuxgq5xwgbn_vb7lljgyxr|Hanji 234]] says Liu Bi detains Liu Tong, rejects withdrawal after Chao Cuo's death, and presents himself as an eastern emperor.

Warning and treatment of Yuan Ang:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-234-zhouyafu-zhanchang-dalian-hanjingdi-na-lai-de-danzi-li6iqhtuxgq5xwgbn_vb7lljgyxr|Hanji 234]] attributes to Mei Cheng a warning about legitimacy and logistical exposure and says Liu Bi confines Yuan Ang after the envoy refuses to defect.

Rejected options and underestimated capability:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-235-liubi-zhisi-jielu-zhichang-shengcun-qian-guize-lp1wxe_buqe_zrilsfwsfzqoa2ef|Hanji 235]] gives Tian Lubo's second-front proposal, the unnamed young general's rapid Luoyang plan, their rejection, and Zhou Qiu's tally-backed Xiapi mobilization.

Flight and killing:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-235-liubi-zhisi-jielu-zhichang-shengcun-qian-guize-lp1wxe_buqe_zrilsfwsfzqoa2ef|Hanji 235]] says Liu Bi flees south, gathers remnants, trusts the ruler called “东海王落望,” and is killed during a staged troop-review meeting after a court payment.

## Qualifications

These four popular-history notes cover Liu Bi's appointment, early estrangement, and late rebellion phases, not his full reign or the complete causes of the Seven States crisis. Hanji 231's quarrel, killing, coffin exchange, attendance rule, detention sequence, envoy speech, gifts, age exemption, private grief, and revolt thought remain source-scoped. Liu Xian's death does not replace territorial reduction, princely autonomy, military capacity, or coalition politics as causal factors. The size and administration of Wu, long-term preparations, Chao Cuo's role, Mei Cheng's letter, “eastern emperor” wording, treatment of later envoys, proposed routes, army totals, rejected plans, Zhou Qiu's mobilization, Liu Bi's flight, payment, killer's identity, and assassination scene remain source-scoped. Hanji 235's “2月30日,” 刘驹／刘居 variation, “桓” abbreviation, and “东海王落望” wording are treated as transcription or identity uncertainties rather than silently normalized facts.

## What Changed

- Backfilled Liu Bi's estrangement through his heir's death, the coffin dispute, prolonged nonattendance, detained envoys, and fear-driven thoughts of revolt.
- Added Emperor Wen's release, gifts, and attendance exemption as temporary de-escalation rather than proof that the structural conflict was solved.
- Added the decision phase in which mistrust and status-filtered advice narrow Liu Bi's strategic alternatives.
- Completed the current arc through defeat, southward flight, alliance repricing, and assassination while preserving the killer's uncertain identity.
- Preserved Liu Xian's death as one grievance rather than a single-cause theory of the Seven States rebellion.
