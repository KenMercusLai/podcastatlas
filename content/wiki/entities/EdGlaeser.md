# Ed Glaeser

## Overview
Ed Glaeser is the economist in two [[FreakonomicsRadio]] sources who interprets U.S. housing unaffordability and weak construction scale primarily through land-use constraints, local scarcity incentives, and the failure to build enough homes in high-demand places.

## Current Profile
Glaeser argues that rising prices combined with falling construction point toward supply constraints rather than land exhaustion or inequality alone. His [[ZoningTax|zoning-tax]] frame treats the gap between selling price and production cost as evidence that land-use restrictions and political opposition have manufactured scarcity. [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-76efcd195d1b26a4faab0a64b31c1972-52966951-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-76efcd195d1b26a4faab0a64b31c1972-52966951]] extends that diagnosis from price to production: small permitted sites and small projects inhibit repetition, mass production, and manufacturing-style scale.

His policy emphasis is institutional: reduce local veto power or make approval financially attractive to local governments, pursue density changes large enough to alter development incentives, and connect federal transport money or state targets to housing production. For modular construction, he proposes large factories supplying standardized components to small local installers, combined with education and a safety net for displaced workers. He still reserves structural-safety rules and housing support for vulnerable households, calls for cost-benefit scrutiny rather than blanket deregulation, and rejects the idea that construction alone explains fertility decisions.

## Key Characteristics
- Diagnoses housing scarcity through the joint behavior of prices, production, construction cost, and regulation.
- Treats overlapping rules and local opposition as more important than any single zoning provision.
- Distinguishes national housing supply from the locally concentrated effects of wealthy or institutional buyers.
- Favors strong production incentives and meaningful density increases over nominal permission changes.
- Treats a home primarily as a place to live rather than a speculative investment.
- Connects land-use reform to project scale, standardization, and construction productivity.
- Supports technological transition alongside education and worker protection.

## Qualifications
The supplied sources are episode summaries rather than Glaeser's papers or underlying datasets. Missing-unit estimates, establishment and parcel counts, city comparisons, construction-cost gaps, upzoning or modular buildout, policy effects, worker displacement, and causal interpretations remain episode-attributed. Safety and sanitation rules provide benefits, so his critique does not imply that every restriction is wasteful.

## What Changed
- Extended the supply diagnosis from housing prices and approvals to construction scale, modular production, and workforce transition.
