## Overview

贾复 / Jia Fu is synthesized as an Eastern Han founding general whose current profile now runs from active Jianwu 2 campaign command into post-unification withdrawal and late death-memory. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-686-liuxiu-fashi-zhi-ai-yinlihua-weihe-li-guoshi-wei-hou-lnqlqgq54srb1zzzgemfwuwujhvc|Hanji 686]] adds the early layer: Jia Fu volunteers against Yan Cen, wins further southern operations, then becomes a military-prestige pressure point when [[KouXunEasternHan|寇恂]] executes one of his lawbreaking subordinates. Hanji 715 presents him as a Jiaodong marquis who understands [[LiuXiu|光武帝刘秀]]'s postwar civilian turn and steps away from military authority; Hanji 730 remembers him at his 55 CE death as a quiet, wounded, high-merit commander.

## Current Profile

Hanji 686 supplies the active-campaign and law-conflict layer. When Liu Xiu asks who will take on Yan Cen and the Wan front, Jia Fu volunteers for the Yan Cen assignment. The source then credits Jia Fu's campaign with taking cities and inducing figures such as Yin Zun and Bao Si to surrender, followed by southern captures at Shaoling and Xinxī.

The same source complicates Jia Fu's high-merit profile. After one of his subordinates commits murder in [[YingchuanCommandery|颍川]], Kou Xun arrests and publicly executes the offender. Jia Fu treats the execution as personal humiliation and threatens to kill Kou Xun when passing through Yingchuan. The conflict ends only after Kou Xun defuses the encounter and Liu Xiu personally mediates, making Jia Fu part of a founding-order test: military merit cannot become a blanket shield against local law or commander discipline.

Hanji 715 supplies the settlement layer. After unification, Liu Xiu rewards Jia Fu as Jiaodong marquis, and the episode says Jia Fu and [[DengYuEasternHan|邓禹]] perceive that Liu Xiu wants to set aside weapons and govern through ritual and civilian instruction. Jia Fu therefore gives up military authority and withdraws into honored status.

Hanji 730 presents Jia Fu as the Jiaodong Ganghou whose death falls amid the late Guangwu notices of 54-55 CE. The episode emphasizes his undefeated fighting record and twelve wounds, then says Liu Xiu reduced his exposure to dangerous distant campaigns because Jia Fu fought too fiercely. Its interpretive use is about recognition and merit: when generals discuss achievements, Jia Fu keeps his head down and does not advertise himself, while Liu Xiu says he knows Jia Fu's merit.

## Key Characteristics

- Eastern Han founding commander active in the Jianwu 2 consolidation campaigns.
- High-merit general whose subordinate's crime tests whether military prestige can override local legal authority.
- Later Jiaodong marquis who surrenders military authority without being disgraced.
- Representative of [[HonorsWithoutOfficeSettlement|honors without office]] through retained title, honor, and limited participation.
- Martial figure whose undefeated record and many wounds embody high-risk battlefield service.
- Quiet merit case: he does not boast in achievement discussions, while Liu Xiu claims to know his contribution.

## Qualifications

This page remains bounded to three source notes rather than a complete campaign biography. Hanji 686 supplies active operations and the Jia Fu-Kou Xun conflict, but exact campaign geography, subordinate identity, and full rank chronology remain source-scoped. Hanji 715 gives Jia Fu's post-unification withdrawal and non-return-to-office layer, while Hanji 730 gives death memory, battlefield risk, and quiet merit. The current synthesis treats Jia Fu's anger at Kou Xun as a command-and-status conflict, not as a settled moral judgment on his full career.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 686 as Jia Fu's earliest active-campaign layer and the Jia Fu-Kou Xun legal-order conflict.
- Reframed his profile from postwar honored withdrawal alone into an arc from battlefield merit through discipline conflict to later withdrawal.
- Preserved Hanji 715 as the post-unification command-withdrawal settlement.
- Preserved Hanji 730 as death-memory, battlefield-risk, and quiet-merit evidence.
