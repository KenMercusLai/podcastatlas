# Metropolitan Museum of Art

## Overview

The Metropolitan Museum of Art is a New York art museum used in [[dragon-psychology-101-dragon-psychology-101|Dragon Psychology 101]] as the central case of an institution that can be financially strained while holding an immense collection it does not treat as an ordinary financial asset.

## Current Profile

The episode reports that the Met began mandatory admission for visitors from outside New York State in 2018 after a roughly $40 million deficit, staff reductions, canceled exhibitions, and leadership turmoil. At the same time, it held roughly two million objects and resisted selling collection works to address operating pressure.

The profile is not simply about wealth. Philippe de Montebello's long directorship and the reported acquisition of more than 84,000 works represent accumulation as institutional success, while a 1991 account of inaccessible stored rugs shows that ownership does not guarantee display, research access, or practical use. The Met's opposition to capitalizing collections then gives this separation an accounting form through [[MuseumCollectionNoncapitalization]].

## Key Characteristics

- Large art museum holding roughly two million objects in the episode's account.
- Institution whose collection is excluded from ordinary balance-sheet asset valuation.
- Case of continued acquisition alongside storage and access constraints.
- Example used to question whether permanent retention always advances public mission.
- Public-trust institution whose duties cannot be reduced to ordinary corporate asset liquidation.

- [[dragon-psychology-101-dragon-psychology-101]] describes C. Douglas Dillon's opposition to proposed capitalization and the exclusion of collection value from the museum's 2019 statement of financial position.

## Qualifications

The bounded source gives an argumentative account of the museum's finances, collection, and accounting position. It does not supply the Met's complete budget, collection policy, donor restrictions, legal duties, storage program, public-benefit analysis, deaccessioning rules, or response to the episode. Financial distress does not establish that selling art was legally available or institutionally preferable.
