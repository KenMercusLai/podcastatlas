# Aeneid

## Overview

The *Aeneid* is [[Virgil]]'s Roman epic of [[Aeneas]], read in this episode through its transformation of [[Dido]] and its simultaneous affirmation and moral complication of imperial destiny.

## Current Profile

The poem sends the Trojan Aeneas toward the future Rome and interrupts that journey at a [[Carthage]] still being built. Dido welcomes the refugees, hears the account of Troy, and becomes Aeneas's lover. Divine command then requires him to leave; Dido's suicide and curse connect erotic loss to the future hostility between Carthage and Rome.

The episode places this plot inside Augustan politics but resists treating the work as flat imperial propaganda. Carthage initially resembles an exemplary city and Dido appears as an effective, generous ruler. Aeneas must obey destiny, yet the poem makes the cost of that obedience emotionally and historically legible. Its afterlife, including Purcell's *Dido and Aeneas*, shows how the tragic queen can outgrow the national story that required her defeat.

## Key Characteristics

- Roman foundation epic organized around Aeneas's divinely required journey.
- Literary transformation of an older Dido or Elissa foundation tradition.
- Retrospective explanation of Roman-Carthaginian hostility through Dido's curse.
- Augustan work that celebrates Rome while making sacrifice and loss visible.
- Durable source for later artistic treatments of Dido's lament and abandonment.

## Evidence

- Plot and political frame: [[262-tunisia-dido-of-carthage-glt5520362156]] connects Aeneas's journey, Carthage, Dido's death, Hannibal, and Augustan Rome.
- Earlier tradition: [[262-tunisia-dido-of-carthage-glt5520362156]] contrasts the poem with versions in which Elissa never meets Aeneas and dies rather than marry Iarbas.
- Interpretive complexity: [[262-tunisia-dido-of-carthage-glt5520362156]] emphasizes Dido's competence and suffering as evidence against a simple propaganda reading.
- Cultural afterlife: [[262-tunisia-dido-of-carthage-glt5520362156]] closes with Purcell's operatic treatment and an adapted lament.

## Qualifications

The episode focuses narrowly on Dido and does not summarize the full epic. Claims about Virgil's political intention, Cleopatra as a model, audience reception, historical Carthage, and the precise relationship between earlier traditions and the surviving poem remain interpretive or source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Established the poem as a political transformation of inherited foundation legend rather than a historical account of Dido.
- Made simultaneous imperial affirmation and attention to imperial cost the central interpretive tension.

## Relationships

- [[Virgil]] - author who reshapes inherited material for an Augustan epic.
- [[Aeneas]] - Trojan protagonist whose duty organizes the poem's Roman future.
- [[Dido]] - tragic queen whose suffering complicates that future.
- [[Carthage]] - rival city placed imaginatively at the beginning of Rome's destiny.
- [[ImperialDestinyHumanCost]] - moral structure through which commanded history and individual loss coexist.
