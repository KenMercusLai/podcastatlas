# Richard Middleton

## Overview

Richard “Dick” Middleton was a Birmingham K-9 officer photographed holding the leash of his German shepherd Leo as the dog lunged toward [[WalterGadsden]] on May 3, 1963.

## Current Profile

The famous photograph made Middleton appear to be an officer intentionally using a police dog against a young Black civil-rights marcher. The episode disputes both parts of that reading: Gadsden said he was a bystander, while Middleton's colleague Bobby Hayes and Middleton's widow said Middleton was pulling Leo back after Gadsden entered the police-controlled area.

Middleton did not publicly defend himself, according to his widow, because he expected the photograph to outweigh his words. His profile therefore remains morally and evidentially bounded: the episode offers significant reason not to infer a deliberate command from the single frame, but does not independently reconstruct every action or remove the encounter from Birmingham's coercive policing and segregation.

## Key Characteristics

- Birmingham police K-9 officer partnered with the German shepherd Leo.
- Appears in Bill Hudson's iconic May 3, 1963 photograph with Walter Gadsden.
- Presented by colleague and family testimony as restraining rather than releasing the dog.
- Became a public symbol of racist police violence regardless of disputed individual intent.
- Reportedly remained silent because he believed the image would defeat any verbal defense.

## Evidence

### Encounter and restraint claim

- [[the-foot-soldier-of-birmingham-the-foot-soldier-of-birmingham]] reports Hayes's barricade account, Gadsden's recollection, the widow's interpretation, and the claim that surrounding photographs show Middleton pulling the leash away.

### Public afterlife

- [[the-foot-soldier-of-birmingham-the-foot-soldier-of-birmingham]] connects the photograph to international hate mail, lasting family harm, and Middleton's enlargement into the threatening officer of the [[FootSoldierMonument]].

## Qualifications

The restraint interpretation depends partly on a colleague, Middleton's widow, and reported surrounding images not reproduced in the supplied source. Rejecting certainty about deliberate release does not establish that every police action was appropriate, deny that Gadsden was bitten, or weaken the wider historical record of racist policing during the Birmingham campaign.

## What Changed

- Created a focused profile distinguishing what the photograph visibly captures from the intention often inferred from it.
- Kept individual-intent uncertainty inside the broader institutional setting of segregation and coercive policing.
