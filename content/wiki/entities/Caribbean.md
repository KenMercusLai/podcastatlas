# Caribbean

## Overview

The Caribbean is represented in the wiki through digital-infrastructure geopolitics, small-island economic vulnerability and resource governance, and postwar migration to Britain.

## Current Profile

The regional profile joins three distinct scales. Planned [[UnitedStates]] funding for [[UnderseaDataCables]] treats the Caribbean as a connectivity and [[TrustedNetworkGeopolitics]] concern. [[Barbuda]] and [[Jamaica]] show how disaster exposure, tourism dependence, property institutions, sand scarcity, and limited enforcement shape small open economies. The Windrush branch adds Caribbean people as mobile British subjects whose wartime service, ambition, return plans, family formation, religion, and British-born identities shaped [[PostwarCaribbeanBritishSettlement]].

## Key Characteristics

- Small open economies are highly exposed to hurricanes, oil prices, global rates, debt, tourism cycles, and outside infrastructure decisions.
- Regional cable routes can be commercially marginal yet strategically important for resilience and geopolitical trust.
- Land and beach-resource disputes join local institutions, tourism, investment, disaster recovery, and enforcement capacity.
- Postwar migration to Britain grew from imperial citizenship and mobility, not from one uniform plan of permanent settlement.
- The region's British story should preserve [[MigrantHistoricalAgency]] and differences among islands, families, generations, and later migration waves.

## Evidence

- Connectivity and geopolitics: [[tech-20260820-tech-pod-128-tech-20260820-tech-pod-128]] describes proposed U.S. funding to replace cables where commercial incentives may undersupply trusted routes.
- Economic and resource vulnerability: [[sand-heists-and-property-rights-in-the-caribbean-summer-school]] uses Barbuda land tenure and Jamaican sand theft to connect external shocks, tourism, property rules, and limited enforcement.
- Migration and settlement: [[401-windrush-the-story-of-black-britain-glt4826407019]] connects Caribbean British-subject mobility with wartime service, intended return, family formation, racism, church life, and later national memory.

## Qualifications

These three sources cover selected infrastructure, island-economy, and British-migration questions rather than the Caribbean's full political, cultural, linguistic, or national diversity. Regional generalization must not erase differences among islands or treat Windrush passengers as representative of every Caribbean person. Migration totals, church attendance, demographic comparisons, trade judgments, and infrastructure-risk figures remain source-scoped.
