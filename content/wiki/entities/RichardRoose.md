# Richard Roose

## Overview

Richard Roose was a cook connected to Bishop John Fisher's household whose poisoned-porridge case became an example of [[HenryVIII|Henry VIII]] creating a new high-treason offence and spectacular punishment after the act.

## Current Profile

The source says Fisher did not eat the porridge, but members of his household and poor recipients of its alms did, with fatal consequences. Roose was initially treated as a murderer. Henry's legislation then made poisoning high treason and prescribed boiling to death, converting a specific feared attack into a public statement about royal authority and foreign-coded danger.

Roose's case therefore matters less here as a settled account of individual motive than as evidence of retroactivity, political amplification, and [[ExemplaryTreasonPunishment]]. Euan Roger's decision that he would save Roose reflects uncertainty about whether the cook was caught in wider politics, not a finding that no poisoning occurred.

## Key Characteristics

- Cook associated with Bishop John Fisher's household.
- Accused in a poisoned-porridge incident that harmed household members and alms recipients.
- Initially treated as a murderer before poisoning was legislatively made high treason.
- Executed by public boiling at Smithfield.
- Used in the episode to expose retroactivity and uncertainty about political responsibility.

## Evidence

- Incident and victims: [[248-medieval-treason-part-1-glt9532907553]] describes the porridge, Fisher's escape, and harm to others.
- Legal transformation: [[248-medieval-treason-part-1-glt9532907553]] says legislation reclassified poisoning as high treason after the event.
- Punishment and interpretation: [[248-medieval-treason-part-1-glt9532907553]] connects boiling at Smithfield to Henry's fear of poisoning and Roger's qualified sympathy for Roose.

## Qualifications

The episode does not independently settle Roose's instructions, motive, knowledge, evidentiary record, or relationship to wider political actors. Its “save” judgment is an interpretive closing exercise, not an acquittal. The legal-retroactivity account should be checked against specialist legal history before being generalized.
