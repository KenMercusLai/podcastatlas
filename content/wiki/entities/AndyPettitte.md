# Andy Pettitte

## Overview

Andy Pettitte is a Major League Baseball pitcher whose admitted use of human growth hormone during injury rehabilitation anchors a case about restoration, enhancement, apology, and moral classification.

## Current Profile

[[the-standard-case-the-standard-case|The Standard Case]] presents Pettitte's public sequence as denial, exposure in the Mitchell Report, apology, explanation, and rejection of that explanation as self-serving. Trainer Brian McNamee reportedly said that he injected Pettitte two to four times while the pitcher rehabilitated an elbow injury in 2002.

The episode treats Pettitte's insistence that he sought recovery rather than advantage as a morally relevant claim rather than an automatic excuse. His use was limited to one disabled-list period and his later performance was described as close to his earlier baseline, so [[MalcolmGladwell]], Jonah Keri, and George Will place the case nearer [[TommyJohn]]'s restorative surgery than [[BarryBonds]]'s transformative drug use, conditional on Pettitte's account being accurate.

## Key Characteristics

- Admitted limited HGH use during rehabilitation from a specific elbow injury.
- Framed the intervention as a return-to-work measure rather than an attempt to exceed prior ability.
- Became a test of whether explanation inside an apology is clarification or self-exculpation.
- Supplies an intermediate case between accepted restorative medicine and competitively transformative enhancement.

## Qualifications

This profile derives from one structured podcast summary rather than medical files, testimony, league rules, the Mitchell Report, performance analysis, or Pettitte's complete statements. Restorative intent does not independently settle legality, rule compliance, safety, access, or competitive fairness, and the accuracy of the reported use pattern remains a premise of the episode's judgment.

## What Changed

- Established Pettitte as the episode's disputed middle case between restoration and transformation.
- Preserved purpose, duration, performance effect, and competitive harm as separate dimensions of judgment.
