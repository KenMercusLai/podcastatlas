## Overview

Camilla Townsend is a historian and long-term student of Nahuatl whose work reconstructs Mexica history through Indigenous-language evidence alongside archaeology and linguistics.

## Current Profile

In this episode, Townsend challenges conquest traditions that make Mexica action depend on superstition: she finds no evidence that the Mexica believed Cortes was a god or initially thought the world was ending. Her alternative account treats them as migrants, political integrators, imperial aggressors, urban residents, poets, religious thinkers, wartime opponents, and colonial survivors.

Her method centers alphabetic Nahuatl records written after conquest by Indigenous scholars who adapted Roman letters to preserve histories, prayers, stories, poems, and ritual language. She also insists on a double boundary around human sacrifice: denial erases real violence, while fixation turns one practice into an explanation of an entire society.

## Key Characteristics

- Historian using Nahuatl-language writing to recover Indigenous perspectives on Mexica history.
- Critic of the Spaniards-as-gods and conquest-as-apocalypse narratives.
- Interpreter of sacrifice through both devotional obligation and imperial coercion.
- Advocate for describing cultural achievement and political violence within the same historical account.

## Evidence

- Myth correction: [[51-aztecs-glt9590586130]] records her rejection of divine-identification and immediate-apocalypse explanations for the conquest.
- Moral and political synthesis: [[51-aztecs-glt9590586130]] joins Tenochtitlan's beauty and social organization to captive sacrifice, imperial intimidation, and regional oppression.
- Colonial continuity: [[51-aztecs-glt9590586130]] presents Indigenous writing, local leadership, legal differentiation, and survival as part of the post-conquest story.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to one podcast interview and describes Townsend's positions as presented there rather than her complete scholarship. The episode's claims about migration, sacrifice, demography, technology, colonial law, and Malinche require specialist corroboration beyond this source.
