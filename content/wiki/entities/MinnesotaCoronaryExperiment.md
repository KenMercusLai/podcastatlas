# Minnesota Coronary Experiment

## Overview

The Minnesota Coronary Experiment was a large controlled institutional feeding trial conducted from 1968 to 1973 to test whether replacing saturated animal fats with vegetable oils lowered cholesterol and mortality.

## Current Profile

Led by [[IvanFrantz]], the experiment used visually similar meals across six mental hospitals and one nursing home, with vegetable oil and margarine in the intervention diet and animal fats and butter in the comparison diet. More than 9,000 people participated, although exposure duration and usable follow-up varied.

The intervention lowered serum cholesterol but did not demonstrate lower coronary or all-cause mortality. Decades later, [[ChristopherRamsden]] used recovered records to report that greater cholesterol reduction was associated with higher mortality in an older subgroup exposed for more than a year. The trial therefore supplies an important but bounded challenge to treating a surrogate change as proof of clinical benefit.

## Key Characteristics

- Large institutional diet trial with unusually tight control over food provision.
- Double-blind comparison of saturated animal fats with linoleic-acid-rich vegetable oil and margarine.
- Produced a clear cholesterol change without demonstrated survival benefit.
- Received delayed and limited publication before later recovery and reanalysis.
- Depends on careful interpretation of duration, adherence, subgroup selection, and historical context.

## Qualifications

The supplied summary does not contain the protocol, attrition table, full endpoints, effect estimates, raw data, ethical framework for institutional participation, or later technical critiques. The older, longer-exposed subgroup should not be treated as the whole randomized sample, and the trial does not settle every oil, fat, dose, population, or cardiovascular endpoint.

## What Changed

- Created a trial profile separating design strength, surrogate response, clinical outcome, and reanalysis limits.
