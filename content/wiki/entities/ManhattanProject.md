# Manhattan Project

## Overview

The Manhattan Project was the U.S.-led wartime atomic-bomb program represented through [[JRobertOppenheimer|J. Robert Oppenheimer]]'s scientific leadership, [[LeslieGroves]]'s military direction, Los Alamos's rapid growth, the [[TrinityNuclearTest|Trinity test]], and the moral and political aftermath of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

## Current Profile

Part 1 reconstructs the project's central rationale and laboratory. Fear that [[NaziGermany|Nazi Germany]] might obtain a bomb first drove [[WartimeScientificMobilization|wartime scientific mobilization]]; Groves selected the politically vulnerable but unusually integrative Oppenheimer; and Los Alamos expanded from roughly 100 people to about 6,000 by mid-1945. The result was institutional rather than individual genius, combining theory, experiment, engineering, administration, military authority, and secrecy.

The project's rationale shifted before success. Germany's defeat removed the original enemy from the immediate use case, but the organization continued toward Trinity and a weapon for the war against Japan. Part 2 then distinguishes roles: scientists built and tested the weapon, while political leaders in Washington decided whether and how it would be used.

That boundary does not eliminate scientific responsibility. The Los Alamos response to Hiroshima ranged from celebration to sickness and guilt, and Oppenheimer's postwar career shows how technical achievement can create public authority, moral burden, and conflict over secrecy, [[AtomicKnowledgeInternationalControl|international control]], and the next generation of weapons.

## Key Characteristics

- Wartime scientific-state program initially justified by the feared race against a Nazi bomb.
- Rapidly built laboratory system combining military command with interdisciplinary scientific leadership.
- Program whose original rationale changed after Germany's defeat but whose capability and momentum persisted.
- Source of Oppenheimer's national authority and postwar moral burden.
- Institution whose participants did not control the final political decision to bomb Japan.

## Evidence

- Threat and mobilization: [[343-oppenheimer-the-father-of-the-atom-bomb-part-1-glt9202576882]] connects fission, fear of German research, secret committees, and the decision to pursue a bomb.
- Leadership and scale: [[343-oppenheimer-the-father-of-the-atom-bomb-part-1-glt9202576882]] follows Groves's selection of Oppenheimer and Los Alamos's growth into a community of thousands.
- Technical threshold: [[343-oppenheimer-the-father-of-the-atom-bomb-part-1-glt9202576882]] presents Trinity as the project's first successful atomic detonation.
- Decision boundary: [[344-oppenheimer-the-witch-hunt-part-2-glt1594914557]] states that Washington, not Oppenheimer, made the final use decision.
- Participant response: [[344-oppenheimer-the-witch-hunt-part-2-glt1594914557]] records both celebration and moral distress among Los Alamos scientists after Hiroshima.

## Qualifications

The paired episodes do not provide a complete history of the project's sites, personnel, industrial production, non-U.S. contributors, espionage, engineering, costs, displacement, environmental effects, or chain of command. Their strongest evidence concerns Oppenheimer, Los Alamos, and postwar interpretation rather than the total institutional record.

## What Changed

- Added the feared Nazi race, Groves's personnel decision, Los Alamos's rapid scale-up, and Trinity as the institutional prehistory of postwar responsibility.
- Distinguished the project's initiating rationale from the changed Japan-focused use context after Germany's defeat.
