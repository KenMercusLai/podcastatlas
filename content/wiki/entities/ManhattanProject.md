# Manhattan Project

## Overview

The Manhattan Project is the wartime atomic-bomb program represented in [[344-oppenheimer-the-witch-hunt-part-2-glt1594914557]] through [[JRobertOppenheimer|J. Robert Oppenheimer]]'s leadership at Los Alamos and the moral and political aftermath of Hiroshima and Nagasaki.

## Current Profile

The episode begins after the project's scientific success rather than reconstructing its full development. Its contribution is to distinguish institutional roles: scientists built and tested the weapon, while political leaders in Washington decided whether and how it would be used.

That division does not eliminate scientific responsibility. The Los Alamos response to Hiroshima ranged from celebration to sickness and guilt, and Oppenheimer's postwar career shows how technical achievement can create public authority, moral burden, and conflict over the next generation of weapons.

## Key Characteristics

- Wartime scientific-state program culminating in the atomic bomb and Trinity test.
- Source of Oppenheimer's national authority and postwar moral burden.
- Institution whose participants did not control the final political decision to bomb Japan.
- Starting point for the later divide over the hydrogen bomb, secrecy, and public warning.

## Evidence

- Leadership and transition: [[344-oppenheimer-the-witch-hunt-part-2-glt1594914557]] follows Oppenheimer as he leaves Los Alamos after directing the wartime laboratory.
- Decision boundary: [[344-oppenheimer-the-witch-hunt-part-2-glt1594914557]] states that Washington, not Oppenheimer, made the final use decision.
- Participant response: [[344-oppenheimer-the-witch-hunt-part-2-glt1594914557]] records both celebration and moral distress among Los Alamos scientists after Hiroshima.
- Postwar legacy: [[344-oppenheimer-the-witch-hunt-part-2-glt1594914557]] connects the project to the hydrogen-bomb dispute and Oppenheimer's public responsibility.

## Qualifications

The episode does not provide a complete history of the project's sites, personnel, espionage, engineering, costs, or chain of command. Its strongest evidence concerns postwar interpretation and Oppenheimer's role, not the total institutional record.

## What Changed

- Created the project as the institutional bridge between wartime technical success and postwar responsibility.
