# Raymond Poincaré

## Overview

Raymond Poincaré was the French president whose July 1914 visit to St Petersburg reinforced Franco-Russian firmness immediately before Austria-Hungary delivered its ultimatum to Serbia.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Poincaré as shaped by France's 1870 defeat, German occupation of Lorraine, and the loss of Alsace-Lorraine. He treated the Russian alliance as France's essential answer to German demographic and military strength and combined financial, military, and diplomatic coordination with a desire to bring Britain closer to the alignment.

In St Petersburg, Poincaré warned the Austro-Hungarian ambassador that Serbia had friends and overruled prime minister René Viviani's preference for a softer communiqué. His policy rested on a dangerous deterrent expectation: visible Franco-Russian solidarity and firmness would make Austria-Hungary retreat rather than widen the crisis.

## Key Characteristics

- Carried the political memory of German occupation and the loss of Alsace-Lorraine from his Lorraine childhood.
- Treated the Russian alliance as central to balancing Germany.
- Favored military readiness, including France's three-year service law.
- Used personally confrontational language toward the Austro-Hungarian ambassador in St Petersburg.
- Preferred a firm summit message over Viviani's caution.
- Expected allied solidarity to deter Austria-Hungary rather than trigger general escalation.

## Qualifications

This profile is grounded in one narrative podcast episode concentrated on a few days in July 1914. The source portrays Poincaré as hard-line and revanchist, but it also distinguishes wanting favorable strategic conditions from deliberately seeking an immediate general war. His private intent, the precise content of every summit exchange, and the relative weight of deterrence and escalation remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Created Poincaré as the French alliance manager whose firmness strengthened Russian confidence during the ultimatum crisis.
