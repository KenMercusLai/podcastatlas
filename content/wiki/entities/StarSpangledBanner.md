# The Star-Spangled Banner

## Overview

The Star-Spangled Banner appears in the wiki as the U.S. national anthem, presented by the source as a battle poem, borrowed-tune ballad, official state symbol, difficult sporting ritual, and contested memory object.

## Current Profile

The episode treats the anthem as a layered political symbol. [[FrancisScottKey]] wrote the poem after watching the British bombardment of [[FortMcHenry]] during the [[WarOf1812]], and the text was quickly printed, retitled, and sung to [[AnacreonticSong|The Anacreontic Song]]. It became the official U.S. anthem only in 1931, after more than a century of popular use, military ritual, parody, and institutional lobbying.

The song's hardest interpretive problem is the third verse. The source keeps the "hireling and slave" phrase uncertain, but it places the line beside [[ColonialMarines|British recruitment of escaped enslaved people]], Key's slaveholding and anti-abolitionist record, and later protest politics. That makes the anthem a U.S. case of [[NationalAnthemPoliticalPlasticity]] qualified by [[SlaveryNationalIdentityContradiction]].

## Key Characteristics

- U.S. national anthem originating in Key's 1814 poem about [[FortMcHenry]].
- Broadside ballad using the English [[AnacreonticSong|Anacreontic Song]] melody rather than an original American tune.
- Battle symbol built from rockets, bombs, dawn, and flag survival imagery.
- Official anthem only from 1931 after long military use and lobbying.
- Technically difficult song because of its unusually wide vocal range.
- Contested slavery symbol because of the third-verse "hireling and slave" phrase and Key's own politics.
- Reinterpreted through parody, sport, protest, and popular performance rather than fixed ceremonial use alone.

## Evidence

- Origin and publication: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says Key wrote the poem after the September 1814 bombardment and that the Baltimore Patriot published it as "The Defence of Fort McHenry."
- Melody reuse: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says the poem fit [[AnacreonticSong|The Anacreontic Song]], a tune already familiar in the United States.
- Flag imagery: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says [[MaryPickersgill]] made the large Fort McHenry flag raised after the bombardment.
- Official adoption: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says Woodrow Wilson ordered military use in 1916 and Herbert Hoover signed official adoption in 1931 after John Charles Linthicum and veterans pushed the cause.
- Slavery controversy: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] presents the "hireling and slave" line alongside [[ColonialMarines]], escaped enslaved people, and Key's slaveholding.
- Protest and reinterpretation: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] connects Jose Feliciano, Jimi Hendrix, Tommie Smith, John Carlos, [[ColinKaepernick]], and [[BlackLivesMatter]]-era kneeling to continuing anthem conflict.

## Qualifications

The page is bounded to one narrative podcast episode. The source does not settle Key's exact intention in the third verse, and it does not treat every performance or defense of the anthem as an endorsement of slavery. The core claim is narrower: the anthem's public meaning cannot be separated from slavery, wartime freedom, and protest disputes.
