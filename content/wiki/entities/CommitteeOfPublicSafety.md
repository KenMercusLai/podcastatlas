# Committee of Public Safety

## Overview

The Committee of Public Safety was the emergency executive body created by the National Convention on 6 April 1793 amid military defeat, revolt, scarcity, and fear of internal betrayal.

## Current Profile

The sources present the committee as one part of an improvised emergency architecture rather than a pre-existing Terror blueprint. [[GeorgesDanton|Danton]] initially dominated its nine members, but [[MaximilienRobespierre|Robespierre]] joined after Marat's assassination and became its defining political figure beside military and administrative specialists. The committee helped centralize repression, influence tribunal personnel, suspend constitutional government, and connect military survival to a single conspiracy framework. Military recovery did not dissolve emergency rule: the committee destroyed Hébertist and Dantonist opposition, while administrative exhaustion, Robespierre's absences, religious disagreement, and rivalry with the [[CommitteeOfGeneralSecurity|Committee of General Security]] eroded collective government. A failed July reconciliation and Saint-Just's unauthorized report helped turn internal rupture into the [[ThermidorianCoup|Thermidorian coup]].

## Key Characteristics

- It was created with nine members to coordinate military and political emergency response, initially under Danton's dominance.
- Its crisis role became durable coercive capacity as compromise grew suspect and revolutionary fear intensified.
- It operated alongside the [[RevolutionaryTribunal]], surveillance committees, and deputies on mission.
- Robespierre's moral authority helped supporters treat committee policy as an extension of personal virtue.
- Its influence over judges and jurors reduced the independence of political adjudication.
- It continued coercive government after battlefield and internal-security improvements weakened the original emergency rationale.
- Successive purges increased immediate authority while making members and deputies less confident about their own safety and ultimately splitting the committee itself.

## Evidence

### Emergency executive coordination

- [[707-the-terror-an-assassination-in-paris-part-1-glt6580043397]] dates the committee's creation to 6 April 1793 and places it amid war, defection, revolt, and economic crisis.

### Leadership transition

- [[707-the-terror-an-assassination-in-paris-part-1-glt6580043397]] contrasts Danton's initial dominance with Robespierre's entry after Marat's death and Danton's removal.

### Institutional accumulation

- [[707-the-terror-an-assassination-in-paris-part-1-glt6580043397]] treats the committee as one component of a wider system whose parts collectively enabled terror.

### Centralized terror policy

- [[708-the-terror-the-reign-of-robespierre-part-2-glt2099451333]] connects the committee to expanded tribunal chambers, influence over judicial personnel, the Law of Suspects, repression of revolts, and suspension of the Constitution of 1793.

### Leadership and moral authority

- [[708-the-terror-the-reign-of-robespierre-part-2-glt2099451333]] presents Robespierre, Saint-Just, and Couthon as a powerful alignment and explains how Robespierre's incorruptible reputation reassured supporters about committee action.

### Recovery, factional purges, and internal fear

- [[710-the-terror-death-at-the-guillotine-part-4-glt5038102951]] distinguishes collective committee government from personal dictatorship, describes military and administrative specialization, and follows the committee through the Hébertist and Dantonist purges.
- [[710-the-terror-death-at-the-guillotine-part-4-glt5038102951]] links the Danton executions to greater secrecy, internal conflict, and fear among surviving officials.

### Administrative exhaustion and institutional collapse

- [[712-the-terror-the-fall-of-robespierre-part-6-glt1154404105]] distinguishes Robespierre's allies, exhausted technocrats, and radical rivals, and contrasts their administrative work with his six-week absence.
- [[712-the-terror-the-fall-of-robespierre-part-6-glt1154404105]] follows accusations of dictatorship, a failed reconciliation, Saint-Just's report dispute, and committee members' decisive role in the Convention attack.

## Qualifications

- The sources cover formation through Robespierre's fall but not the committee's full Thermidorian reorganization or later abolition.
- The episode interprets institutional accumulation as a pathway to terror without claiming that every emergency measure was designed from the outset for that end.
- Committee members differed over the reach of repression, as Robespierre's rejection of Couthon's proposed Lyon deportation indicates.
- Continued terror after military recovery does not prove that every member shared one ideology, motive, or desired endpoint.
- The source explicitly rejects reducing collective committee government to Robespierre's sole dictatorship.
- Workload allocation, private quarrels, precise attendance, and the causal weight of exhaustion remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Extended the committee through administrative exhaustion, failed reconciliation, internal rupture, and Robespierre's fall.
- Added inter-committee rivalry and uneven routine work as constraints on the sole-dictator model.
- Completed the purge-security paradox by showing committee members join the overthrow of former colleagues.
