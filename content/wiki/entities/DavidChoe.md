# David Choe

## Overview
David Choe is an American artist, writer, podcaster, and television host whose [[HubermanLab]] conversation connects creative work and public success to trauma, shame, process addiction, self-sabotage, and recovery.

## Current Profile
Choe describes an artistic life powered partly by care, curiosity, risk tolerance, and broad visual study, but also by anger, revenge, validation seeking, and an inability to remain still with himself. Graffiti, commercial illustration, Facebook murals, travel media, music, podcasting, gambling, sex, shopping, and work became both real creative or professional activity and possible vehicles for avoidance.

His current profile is not a clean before-and-after success story. Choe says compulsions can change form, using a relapse-like pattern around Pokémon card packs as an example, and presents recovery as continued practice supported by therapy, friends, men's groups, rehab, twelve-step meetings, truth-telling, limits, rest, reduced electronics, and small acts of service that need not become content.

## Key Characteristics
- Treats art as emotional, embodied care rather than technical display alone.
- Built a career through graffiti, self-directed study, persistent pitching, commercial illustration, murals, media, and high-risk opportunities.
- Describes shame, anger, revenge, and external validation as productive fuel that also intensified self-destruction.
- Frames addiction broadly across gambling and other behaviors when emotional payoff overrides health, relationships, dignity, or life.
- Emphasizes help-seeking, honesty, connection, saying no, surrender, and tolerance for ordinary life in recovery.
- Questions legacy and public scale in favor of presence, family, and unrecorded service.

## Qualifications
The profile is based on Choe's own nonlinear public account. Psychiatric labels, health events, treatment experiences, financial values, career anecdotes, and motives are source-scoped and should not be converted into independent clinical or biographical findings. Creative intensity, artistic provocation, and career risk do not by themselves establish addiction, and recovery practices described here are not individualized medical advice.

## What Changed
- Created the profile around the tension between authentic creative capacity and shame-driven avoidance.
- Distinguished public achievement from recovery, connection, and self-worth.
- Recorded substituted compulsion and plural help-seeking as continuing rather than completed recovery themes.
