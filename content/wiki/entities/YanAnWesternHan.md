## Overview

严安 / Yan An is presented as a Western Han memorialist who warns [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] that conspicuous consumption and distant war can converge on the same political danger: households lose viable livelihoods, coercion and crime grow, and state extraction outruns the population's capacity to endure it.

## Current Profile

The current evidence consists of one compact podcast summary. Yan An first describes competitive luxury in transport, dress, housing, music, and food. When ordinary income cannot sustain the status race, he says people may leave farming for commerce and then, when profit still fails, turn toward fraud, robbery, or violence. He proposes legal restraint on display so wealth differences generate less hostility and public order can recover.

His second argument uses Qin's northern and southern expeditions as [[HistoricalCostPrecedentRemonstrance|历史成本先例式劝谏]]. Remote campaigning, disease or environmental mismatch, weak supply, mass conscription, transport labor, and civilian desperation become a warning against spending state capacity on avoidable war. The episode interprets this as an indirect criticism of Wudi's contemporary frontier and southern campaigns. Wudi nevertheless values the memorial and appoints Yan An as a palace gentleman alongside [[XuLeWesternHan|徐乐]] and [[ZhuFuYan|主父偃]].

## Key Characteristics

- Treats luxury competition as a livelihood and public-order problem rather than only a private moral fault.
- Connects household expenditure, exit from agriculture, uncertain commercial income, crime, punishment, and social resentment in one causal chain.
- Uses Qin's campaigns to make war costs visible through logistics, labor, mortality, and civilian exhaustion.
- Recommends anticipatory legal and policy correction before visible disorder becomes systemic.
- Gains direct imperial recognition through memorial writing, although the later extent of his influence is not established here.

## Qualifications

The profile rests on one popular-history summary rather than a collated memorial or full biography. The causal path from consumption to commerce to crime is the memorial's diagnosis, not an established general law; commerce is not inherently criminal, and sumptuary regulation may leave poverty, inequality, taxation, and access to productive work unchanged. Qin campaign details and their causal role in dynastic collapse require primary-source comparison. The episode does not settle Yan An's exact office chronology, later service, policy effect, or how closely Wudi's campaigns matched the Qin precedents.
