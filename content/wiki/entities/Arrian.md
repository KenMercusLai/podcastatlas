# Arrian

## Overview

Arrian was a Greek-speaking Roman-era historian whose detailed account of [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander the Great]] is militarily valuable but late, selective, and generally favorable to its subject.

## Current Profile

The episode introduces Arrian during Alexander's first campaign as king. His own military experience helps make his tactical narration unusually concrete, including troop deployments, terrain use, combined arms, withdrawals, and reported casualties. He wrote centuries after the events and relied especially on Ptolemy and Aristobulus, authors associated with favorable Alexander traditions.

That combination makes him indispensable without making him transparent. At [[Thebes]], Arrian attributes the opening assault to unauthorized action by Perdiccas, whereas Diodorus and Plutarch place greater responsibility on Alexander. Because Ptolemy later became Perdiccas's enemy, the source raises the possibility that narrative blame serves both Alexander's reputation and a successor-era rivalry, while preserving unauthorized attack as a live alternative.

## Key Characteristics

- Later historian rather than contemporary witness to Alexander's campaigns.
- Military author capable of preserving detailed accounts of formation, terrain, and maneuver.
- Relies heavily on Ptolemy and Aristobulus within a mostly favorable Alexander tradition.
- Supplies casualty figures and dramatic tactical sequences that require independent caution.
- Differs from other ancient authors over responsibility for the assault on Thebes.

## Qualifications

The bounded source does not provide a full study of Arrian's works, manuscripts, Roman context, or use of lost sources. Favorable selection is a reason for claim-level scrutiny, not proof that every tactical detail is invented. Casualties, speeches, exact sequence, motives, and the hypothesis of Ptolemaic score-settling remain uncertain.
