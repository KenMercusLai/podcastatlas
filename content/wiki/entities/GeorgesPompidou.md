# Georges Pompidou

## Overview

Georges Pompidou was the French prime minister whose May 1968 role moved from attempted conciliation and reopening the Sorbonne to advising parliamentary dissolution as the route out of the crisis.

## Current Profile

The two episode sources present Pompidou as the operational counterweight to [[CharlesDeGaulle|Charles de Gaulle's]] personalized authority. [[353-paris-1968-the-students-revolt-part-1-glt6124707164]] places him in the government's uneven early response: authorities underestimated student unrest, then reopened the Sorbonne in an attempt at conciliation only for students to occupy it as the strike wave spread.

[[354-paris-1968-the-return-of-de-gaulle-part-2-glt6246374874]] makes his later advice decisive. After negotiation and material concessions failed to end the strikes, Pompidou proposed dissolving the National Assembly, converting an uncontrolled contest in streets and workplaces into an election that the Gaullists won overwhelmingly.

## Key Characteristics

- Served as prime minister during the May 1968 crisis.
- Participated in an early strategy that alternated concession with coercive containment.
- Saw that reopening institutions could not by itself restore authority once occupations and strikes had widened.
- Advised de Gaulle to dissolve parliament and seek electoral legitimacy.
- Helped translate executive recovery into an order-versus-disruption political contest.

## Evidence

- Early response: [[353-paris-1968-the-students-revolt-part-1-glt6124707164]] connects Pompidou to reopening the Sorbonne after street confrontation, followed by student occupation.
- Failed settlement context: [[354-paris-1968-the-return-of-de-gaulle-part-2-glt6246374874]] describes continued strikes and rejection after the Grenelle negotiations.
- Electoral exit: [[354-paris-1968-the-return-of-de-gaulle-part-2-glt6246374874]] identifies Pompidou's dissolution advice as the move that shifted the crisis toward elections.

## Qualifications

The bounded sources profile Pompidou only through May 1968 and do not assess his broader premiership, later presidency, policy record, or the full institutional authorship of government decisions. Calling his advice decisive reflects the episodes' narrative emphasis rather than proof that dissolution alone caused the Gaullist victory.
