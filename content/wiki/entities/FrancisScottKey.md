# Francis Scott Key

## Overview

Francis Scott Key appears in the wiki as the Maryland lawyer who wrote the poem that became [[StarSpangledBanner|The Star-Spangled Banner]], while also embodying the anthem's slavery contradiction.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Key as a wartime observer rather than a battlefield commander. He went to the British fleet under a flag of truce to negotiate the release of Dr. William Beans, was detained because he had heard British plans for Baltimore, and watched the bombardment of [[FortMcHenry]] before writing "The Defence of Fort McHenry."

Key's public memory is morally unstable in the source. He came from a slave-owning family, owned enslaved people himself, represented some enslaved people seeking freedom, supported colonization, and later opposed abolitionism as Washington, D.C. district attorney. The episode therefore treats him as central to [[SlaveryNationalIdentityContradiction]] rather than only as an anthem author.

## Key Characteristics

- Maryland lawyer and wartime negotiator in the Fort McHenry episode.
- Author of the poem later retitled [[StarSpangledBanner|The Star-Spangled Banner]].
- Used an existing melody rather than composing a new anthem.
- Slaveholder from a slave-owning family.
- Legally and politically ambiguous figure who sometimes represented freedom seekers but also defended slaveholder interests.
- Colonization supporter and anti-abolitionist prosecutor in later public office.

## Evidence

- Wartime role: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says Key helped negotiate Dr. William Beans's release and was held aboard the British fleet until the Baltimore operation ended.
- Poem composition: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says Key finished the poem at Baltimore's Indian Queen Hotel and it was printed as "The Defence of Fort McHenry."
- Melody choice: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says Key fitted the poem to [[AnacreonticSong|The Anacreontic Song]], a melody he had used before.
- Slavery record: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says Key owned enslaved people, supported the American Colonization Society, represented slaveholders, freed some enslaved people, represented some freedom seekers, and prosecuted anti-slavery activists.
- Memory conflict: [[677-usa-the-star-spangled-banner-part-1-glt7890467734]] says activists toppled a Key statue in 2020 and that it was replaced with a slavery-memory installation.

## Qualifications

The episode does not reduce Key to a single motive or settle exactly what he intended by the anthem's third verse. Its profile is still severe: even ambiguity over wording sits inside a documented life tied to slaveholding and anti-abolitionist public action.
