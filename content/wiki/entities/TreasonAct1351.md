# Treason Act 1351/1352

## Overview

The Treason Act 1351/1352 is the medieval English statute that defined core forms of high treason and became the baseline to which later regimes repeatedly returned after periods of expansion.

## Current Profile

The statute emerged from a request by nobles for definition after royal and factional use had left treason unstable. Its core clauses covered compassing or imagining the death of the king, queen, or heir; violating specified royal women whose pregnancies affected succession; levying war; and adhering to the king's enemies. The episode presents its definition as both a constraint and an adaptable legal vocabulary: the text narrowed arbitrary prosecution but phrases about imagination, war, enemies, and royal succession still required interpretation.

The dual date is conventional rather than contradictory. Lawyers commonly call it the 1351 act, while the archival dating used in the episode places the relevant parliamentary record in 1352. Later rulers, especially [[HenryVIII]], added offences around religion, speech, supremacy, and poisoning, but the repeal of most Henrician additions confirmed the older statute's unusual durability.

## Key Characteristics

- Defined a core set of high-treason offences against monarch, succession, military authority, and allegiance.
- Answered pressure for greater legal certainty after flexible pre-statutory accusation.
- Survived as a baseline despite repeated statutory and interpretive expansion.
- Used language broad enough to create later disputes about intent, speech, collective violence, and foreign enemies.
- Exists in the episode through both Parliament-roll and statute-roll records written in Anglo-Norman French.

## Qualifications

The episode is an archival public-history account rather than a complete doctrinal commentary. It does not establish every later amendment, judicial construction, current provision, or precise distinction among surviving manuscript versions. Its modern relevance examples are analogies, not case-specific legal advice.
