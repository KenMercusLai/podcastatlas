## Overview

清朝 / Qing Dynasty enters this wiki page through Hanji 422-2 as the late-imperial setting where inherited tribute-order assumptions collide with treaty-era resident diplomacy.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Qing diplomacy during the Second Opium War as still shaped by the prestige and ritual assumptions of a tribute order. The Treaty of Tianjin's resident-envoy provision becomes threatening because it implies foreign states can maintain representatives in Beijing without performing subordinate ritual. In the source's reading, Qing status anxiety around "天朝上国" helps explain why [[XianfengEmperor|咸丰帝]] briefly considered sacrificing customs revenue and then accepted renewed war risk.

The wider Qing profile has two additional dimensions. Conquest of Mongolia, Xinjiang, and Tibet was central to the formation of China's current territorial map and distinguishes imperial expansion from the older Chinese heartland. The Opium Wars, postwar rural unrest, the [[TaipingRebellion]], and competing modernization proposals also form a late-dynastic crisis that foreign coercion aggravated but did not wholly cause. [[20-china-glt4329327216|20. China]]

## Key Characteristics

- Dynasty used by the source as the late-stage tribute-order case.
- Treaty-era state under military, fiscal, and diplomatic pressure.
- Court where resident-envoy diplomacy is interpreted through kneeling, face, and hierarchical recognition.
- Setting for internal tension between Xianfeng's status anxiety and officials' fiscal objections.
- Expansionary empire whose conquests substantially shaped the present territorial map.
- Dynasty whose nineteenth-century crisis joined foreign coercion to internal rebellion and contested modernization.

- Treaty pressure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-422-2-gudai-waiguoren-weisha-ai-dao-zhongguo-chaogong-yuanyin-papadalian-lr3erfvroy4b_ryu2sfsdgd-e4m1|Hanji 422-2]] says the Treaty of Tianjin allowed Western states to station envoys in Beijing.
- Status anxiety: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-422-2-gudai-waiguoren-weisha-ai-dao-zhongguo-chaogong-yuanyin-papadalian-lr3erfvroy4b_ryu2sfsdgd-e4m1|Hanji 422-2]] says Xianfeng feared non-kneeling foreigners would damage imperial face and weaken tribute relationships.
- Fiscal constraint: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-422-2-gudai-waiguoren-weisha-ai-dao-zhongguo-chaogong-yuanyin-papadalian-lr3erfvroy4b_ryu2sfsdgd-e4m1|Hanji 422-2]] says ministers blocked the idea of removing customs duties because customs revenue was crucial during fiscal stress.
- Territorial formation: [[20-china-glt4329327216|20. China]] attributes much of the present map to Qing conquest beyond the older Chinese heartland.
- Nineteenth-century crisis: [[20-china-glt4329327216|20. China]] connects the Opium Wars and Taiping conflict to rural unrest and competing plans for technological and political reform.

## Qualifications

The page still rests on two broad podcast interpretations rather than a comprehensive Qing history. The territorial account does not erase the agency or distinct histories of conquered peoples, and the nineteenth-century synthesis does not reduce Qing decline either to Western attack or to an automatic dynastic cycle. Opium War causation, reform constituencies, and the balance of internal and external pressures remain source-scoped.
