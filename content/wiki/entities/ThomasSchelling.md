# Thomas Schelling

## Overview

Thomas Schelling is the strategic theorist invoked in [[a-serious-game-a-serious-game|A Serious Game]] to explain why crisis planning must account for choices made by other actors and why analysis alone cannot list possibilities that never occur to the analyst.

## Current Profile

The episode uses one Schelling proposition as the conceptual hinge for professional war gaming: even a highly intelligent person cannot enumerate the things that would never occur to them. Interactive simulation responds by placing independent decision-makers inside a shared problem, allowing one participant's unexpected choice to alter the constraints and incentives faced by the rest.

This is a narrow source-scoped profile. Schelling provides the rationale for [[StrategicWarGaming]], while the RAND cases supply the operational examples.

## Key Characteristics

- Frames strategy as anticipation and influence among interdependent actors.
- Identifies unconsidered possibilities as a limit of solitary analysis.
- Supplies a conceptual rationale for interactive simulation.
- Is used in the episode as a theorist of strategic surprise rather than as a participant in the described games.

## Evidence

### Strategic interdependence and cognitive limits

- [[a-serious-game-a-serious-game|A Serious Game]] invokes Schelling to connect strategy with other actors' choices and states his observation that a person cannot list what would never occur to them.

## Qualifications

The source provides only a brief paraphrase and does not identify a specific Schelling publication or reconstruct his wider work on bargaining, deterrence, commitment, focal points, or arms control. The proposition supports searching for surprise but does not show that every game reveals the most important omitted possibility.
