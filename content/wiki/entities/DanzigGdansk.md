# Danzig / Gdańsk / 但泽—格但斯克

## Overview

Danzig/Gdańsk is the Baltic port city that anchors the episode's reading of [[GunterGrass|Günter Grass]] through overlapping German, Polish, Kashubian, Hanseatic, wartime, postwar, and labor-movement histories.

## Current Profile

The sources treat the city as a changing political landscape rather than a neutral hometown. Teutonic, Prussian, German, Free City, Nazi-war, and postwar Polish histories overlap with mixed local identities. Under the post-First World War settlement, Danzig became a League-supervised Free City with a German-speaking majority and defined Polish harbor, postal, customs, and military rights.

In 1938–39, Hitler demanded the city's incorporation into Germany together with German-controlled road and railway links across the Polish Corridor and Polish alignment with the Anti-Comintern Pact. Poland treated the package as a threat to trade access, foreign-policy autonomy, and sovereign survival rather than an isolated municipal border adjustment. The opening fighting of the Second World War in the area and the later rise of Solidarity at the Gdańsk shipyard make the city a recurring site where sovereignty, occupation, labor, and public memory meet. “Danzig” and “Gdańsk” can therefore signal different historical attachments, although a name alone does not prove a speaker's politics.

## Key Characteristics

- Baltic port shaped by trade and Hanseatic connections.
- Historically multilingual and multiethnic, including German, Polish, and Kashubian populations.
- Repeatedly transformed by conquest, state borders, war, and postwar settlement.
- League-supervised Free City whose German-speaking majority coexisted with protected Polish access and institutions.
- Territorial and corridor dispute used in a broader German attempt to subordinate Polish foreign policy.
- Literary geography central to Grass's Danzig trilogy and *The Flounder*.
- Political symbol extending from the 1939 invasion to the Solidarity movement.

## Qualifications

This page joins a literary-historical discussion to one diplomatic narrative rather than offering a full urban history. The 1939 source shows why Danzig mattered inside a larger sovereignty dispute but does not settle whether any narrower negotiated revision was possible. Chronology, demographic proportions, territorial terminology, leaders' motives, counterfactual settlements, family ancestry, and the political implications of particular place names require specialist verification and contextual use.
