# Andrea Fuentes

## Overview
Andrea Fuentes is presented in the current source as a Spanish Olympic artistic swimmer who later became a coach and led [[USAArtisticSwimming]]. Her profile joins elite performance, athlete-centered leadership, post-retirement reinvention, and the rescue of [[AnitaAlvarez]].

## Current Profile
Fuentes describes entering artistic swimming at nine and spending roughly twenty years in high-performance sport. She attributes durable excellence less to medal fixation than to enjoyment, curiosity, exacting practice, and small daily improvement. Retirement at thirty opened a five-year transition through travel, an artistic-swimming product business, training camps, international consulting, and eventual commitment to coaching.

As a coach, she says she moved away from authoritarian, outcome-first methods. With the American team, she replaced an imposed Olympic objective with the shared purpose “grow together,” explains why training is required, stops practice to address emotional conflict, models vulnerability, and treats athletes' independent problem-solving as evidence that coaching has worked. Her Budapest rescue of Alvarez adds a high-consequence safety role to that leadership profile.

## Key Characteristics
- Combines maximum ambition with humility, curiosity, and continued learning.
- Treats daily development and innovation as more controllable than medals.
- Uses transparency, vulnerability, and direct conflict discussion to build trust.
- Explains training purposes and invites athlete knowledge rather than demanding unexplained obedience.
- Defines coaching success partly as athletes becoming able to solve problems without her.
- Draws leadership ideas from sport, psychology, meditation, art, technology companies, and other coaches.
- Acts decisively in emergencies, as described in the rescue of [[AnitaAlvarez]].

## Qualifications
This profile rests on one autobiographical interview. Medal counts, organizational rankings, abuse and corruption claims, training loads, rescue timing, medical explanations, and causal claims about team performance remain source-scoped. Attention practices should not be read as permission to ignore pain, hypoxia, injury, or medical warning signs.
