# Sturmabteilung (SA)

## Overview

The Sturmabteilung, or SA, was the Nazi mass stormtrooper organization whose street violence, revolutionary expectations, scale, and military ambitions helped Hitler rise but later threatened his compact with the army and conservative elites.

## Current Profile

The SA began as meeting security and grew under [[ErnstRohm|Ernst Röhm]] into a mass paramilitary movement. Its culture privileged activist camaraderie and street confrontation, and its members expected the Nazi seizure of power to produce a deeper social and institutional revolution.

In early 1933, stormtroopers attacked communists, Social Democrats, trade unionists, and other opponents during the election campaign. [[HermannGoring|Göring's]] decision to recruit SA and SS members as auxiliary police converted party intimidation into state-backed coercion; SA presence, meeting bans, arrests, and violence shaped the conditions under which voting and legislation continued.

By 1933–34, the organization's reported millions of members made it far larger than the treaty-limited army. Röhm's proposal that it become Germany's principal defense force turned the tension into a direct institutional contest.

The [[NightOfTheLongKnives|Night of the Long Knives]] destroyed the SA leadership under a false coup pretext. The organization survived, but its independent political and military ambition was broken as Hitler chose the army relationship and the SS gained power.

## Key Characteristics

- It developed from meeting protection into mass paramilitary mobilization.
- It made street violence and intimidation central to Nazi political advance.
- Its incorporation into auxiliary policing fused paramilitary activism with state enforcement in 1933.
- Its members carried revolutionary expectations beyond Hitler's seizure of office.
- Its reported scale alarmed the professional army.
- Röhm's defense-force plan made it an institutional rival to the army.
- The 1934 purge subordinated rather than literally abolished it.

## Qualifications

The page is bounded to two episodes and does not provide a complete organizational history. Auxiliary-police status did not eliminate differences between party and state organizations, but it materially widened SA coercive authority. Reported membership, attack and arrest totals, internal opinion, and the balance between genuine revolutionary ambition and rhetorical pressure remain source-scoped. The absence of a real 1934 coup plot does not make the SA nonviolent or politically harmless.
