# Art Caplan

## Overview

Art Caplan is the bioethicist who places the [[MinnesotaStarvationExperiment]] against modern human-subject protections and argues that a comparable protocol would not now receive ethical approval.

## Current Profile

Caplan distinguishes the study's earlier research culture from contemporary systems of federal oversight, risk disclosure, consent, and withdrawal rights. His judgment focuses on the protocol: deliberately starving participants while they moved through an ordinary city full of food and experienced severe psychological stress would exceed what a modern review system would permit.

This is a protective boundary, not a denial that the volunteers had humanitarian motives or that the research generated useful knowledge. It makes survival and later benefit insufficient ethical tests and places continuing welfare, capacity, and institutional responsibility alongside initial willingness.

## Key Characteristics

- Interprets the experiment through the history of human-subject protections.
- Emphasizes oversight, disclosure, consent, and a meaningful right to withdraw.
- Treats the severity and psychological setting of the starvation protocol as disqualifying under modern review.
- Separates useful knowledge and participant survival from adequate ethical justification.
- Supplies a protective counterweight to arguments centered on volunteer altruism and retrospective benefit.

## Evidence

### Historical ethics contrast

- [[the-department-of-physiological-hygiene]] attributes to Caplan the contrast between earlier researcher discretion and modern oversight, disclosure, consent, and withdrawal safeguards.

### Modern protocol judgment

- [[the-department-of-physiological-hygiene]] reports his view that no contemporary ethics system would approve comparable deliberate starvation amid severe environmental and psychological stress.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to one structured podcast summary rather than Caplan's publications, complete interview, legal analysis, or a jurisdiction-specific review standard. “Would not be approved today” is a strong ethical judgment about a comparable protocol, not evidence that every modern institution uses identical rules or that no historical finding can ethically inform present care.
