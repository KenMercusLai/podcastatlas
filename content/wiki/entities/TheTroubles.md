# The Troubles

## Overview
The Troubles are the wiki's Northern Ireland conflict branch, grounded in The Rest Is History's sequence from partition and civil-rights escalation through armed republican, loyalist, policing, and British-government crises.

## Current Profile
The current synthesis rejects both timeless-hatred and single-cause explanations. The conflict grows from [[PartitionMinorityTrap]], [[InstitutionalSectarianDiscrimination]], unionist insecurity, civil-rights protest, policing distrust, and specific escalation choices by loyalist, republican, Stormont, and Westminster actors. By Part 4, the branch has moved from the first welcomed British troop deployment to a full [[CounterinsurgencyLegitimacyTrap]] through the [[FallsCurfew]], [[OperationDemetrius]], and [[BloodySunday]]. [[418-britain-in-1974-the-crisis-election-part-2-glt9690615062]] then shows violence entering mainland British electoral life through bombings, while public incomprehension of Northern Ireland and coercive wrongful-conviction practices widen the conflict's institutional damage.

## Key Characteristics
- Rooted in partition, minority governance, historical memory, and Protestant/Catholic institutional asymmetry rather than ancient hatred alone.
- Escalated through civil-rights protest, counter-mobilization, policing failure, and street conflict in [[Derry]], [[Belfast]], and the [[Bogside]].
- Shifted after British deployment from hoped-for neutral protection into contested military rule.
- Structured by reciprocal republican and loyalist violence, where atrocities, fear, and retaliation fed recruitment.
- Marked in 1972 by [[BloodySunday]], Stormont's suspension, direct rule, failed talks, and [[BloodyFriday]].
- Extended into Britain through bombings intended to force public and government attention.
- Repeatedly shaped by actors whose choices converted structural tension into sharper conflict.

## Evidence
- Partition and discrimination: [[697-the-troubles-murder-in-belfast-part-1-glt4877520736]] ties the conflict's pre-1968 roots to unionist state design, Catholic exclusion, policing distrust, and rival memories.
- Street-war threshold: [[698-the-troubles-the-brink-of-civil-war-part-2-glt2308408988]] follows civil-rights protest, Burntollet, the Battle of the Bogside, Belfast violence, and British troop deployment.
- Armed legitimacy trap: [[699-the-troubles-return-of-the-ira-part-3-glt9519811642]] links the Provisional IRA, Falls Curfew, internment, detention abuses, loyalist mobilisation, and bombing cycles.
- 1972 collapse: [[700-the-troubles-bloody-sunday-part-4-glt3909829334]] connects Bloody Sunday, direct rule, loyalist killings, IRA attacks, failed talks, and Bloody Friday to the bloodiest year of the conflict.
- Mainland political spillover: [[418-britain-in-1974-the-crisis-election-part-2-glt9690615062]] places the M62 bombing and wider 1973-1974 campaign beside the February election and the wrongful conviction of Judith Ward.

## Qualifications
The page is grounded in five podcast source notes, not a complete Troubles historiography. The current synthesis preserves source-scoped judgments: British troop deployment may have been unavoidable in August 1969, but later operations badly damaged Catholic trust; IRA violence could gain recruits after state violence, but civilian-harming attacks also damaged republican legitimacy. The 1974 episode compresses Northern Ireland policy and the mainland campaign because its primary subject is the election.

## What Changed
- Extended the conflict branch into mainland Britain's 1974 election atmosphere.
- Added coercive wrongful conviction as a second institutional harm of terrorism pressure.
- Preserved the distinction between Northern Ireland's conflict history and mainland public indifference.
