# George Armstrong Custer

## Overview

George Armstrong Custer was the Seventh Cavalry commander whose five-company battalion was destroyed at the [[BattleOfLittleBighorn]], becoming the center of both a command-accountability dispute and the [[HeroicLastStandMyth|heroic last-stand myth]].

## Current Profile

The episode portrays Custer as a commander whose proven dash and confidence became liabilities under poor intelligence and overwhelming opposition. He failed to establish the village's scale, divided the regiment, sent [[MarcusReno]] into a critical attack, dispatched [[FrederickBenteen]] away from the main axis, and later ordered Benteen to hurry while bringing slow ammunition packs. The hosts infer that he likely tried to reach the village and seize noncombatants as leverage, consistent with earlier cavalry practice, but preserve that purpose as a reconstruction rather than a witnessed fact.

No soldier with Custer survived, so his final route, timing, intent, and death cannot be narrated with certainty. Material evidence supports fighting across ridge positions, while later poetry and public memory converted the collapse into an orderly heroic tableau. The episode assigns Custer substantial responsibility without reducing the result to his errors: Lakota and Cheyenne leadership, numbers, confidence, and firepower were decisive.

## Key Characteristics

- Boldness and speed had previously supported success but became dangerous when joined to inadequate reconnaissance.
- Dividing the regiment reduced mutual support against a much larger force.
- His last Benteen order combined urgency with a logistically conflicting request.
- His likely hostage-seizure objective remains inferential rather than directly witnessed.
- His death is historically powerful but evidentially opaque.
- His posthumous image became a partisan and cultural object distinct from the recoverable battlefield record.

## Qualifications

- The absence of survivors from his battalion sharply limits claims about his intentions and conduct after the last message.
- Crow scout and Peter Thompson stories are preserved as contested recollections rather than settled observation.
- The exact purpose of his movement toward the river and whether he saw Reno's defeat remain unresolved.
- Accounts of his wounds and body do not establish the exact sequence or circumstances of death.
- This profile is grounded in one popular-history reconstruction, not a complete Custer biography.

## What Changed

- Created a profile separating command responsibility from later heroic mythology.
- Preserved the final battle as an evidence problem rather than a continuous witnessed narrative.
- Balanced U.S. command failure with Lakota and Cheyenne battlefield agency.
