# Paul von Hindenburg

## Overview

Paul von Hindenburg was the German president whose conservative authority helped place [[AdolfHitler]] in the chancellorship, legitimated and formally enabled the 1933 emergency transition, increased pressure for the 1934 purge, and whose death opened the way for Hitler to merge the head-of-state office into his personal rule.

## Current Profile

The 1933 episode places Hindenburg among the conservative elites who believed Hitler could be used and contained. His appearance during the 30 January torchlight procession and the Day of Potsdam ceremony gave symbolic continuity and presidential respectability to Hitler's ascent.

After the [[ReichstagFire1933|Reichstag fire]], the [[ReichstagFireDecree|emergency decree]] issued under Hindenburg's authority suspended liberties and expanded central intervention. The [[EnablingAct1933|Enabling Act]] then helped Hitler's cabinet escape dependence on both ordinary parliament and continuing presidential restraint.

By June 1934, Hindenburg's age and likely death made the army's position in the succession urgent. He and Defense Minister Blomberg pressed Hitler to end party conflict and disorder, with the implied possibility that the army might act if he failed.

After the [[NightOfTheLongKnives|Night of the Long Knives]], the army accepted Hitler's restoration of order. Hindenburg's death then allowed the presidency to be retired and joined to Hitler's offices, removing the last conservative figure above him.

## Key Characteristics

- His presidential authority helped conservative elites make Hitler chancellor.
- His public symbolism helped present Nazi power as continuity with older national authority.
- Emergency powers under his presidency enabled constitutional dismantling.
- His declining health made succession politics urgent in 1934.
- His pressure aligned with army demands to suppress party disorder.
- His death enabled the merger of presidency and chancellorship under Hitler.

## Qualifications

This two-episode profile is not a full account of Hindenburg's presidency or responsibility for Hitler's appointment. Formal presidential authority did not mean he controlled how the Nazis used emergency power, but attempted containment does not remove responsibility for appointment, symbolism, or decree. His private intentions, the exact form of the June 1934 threat, and the degree of control during consolidation remain episode-attributed.
