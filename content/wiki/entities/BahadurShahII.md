## Overview

巴哈杜尔·沙二世 / Bahadur Shah II (1775–1862) was the twentieth and final Mughal emperor, an Urdu poet, calligrapher, and religiously inclusive court figure whose residual prestige made him the nominal sovereign of the [[IndianRebellion1857|Indian Rebellion of 1857]].

## Current Profile

By Bahadur Shah's reign, effective Mughal sovereignty had largely vanished and the [[EastIndiaCompany|East India Company]] supported and constrained the emperor inside Delhi. The source presents him as an elderly cultural figure rather than an experienced commander: he wrote poetry, practiced calligraphy, patronized charity, and participated in both Muslim and Hindu observances.

Rebels who seized Delhi compelled or persuaded him to accept leadership because the imperial title still supplied legitimacy. His sons pursued restoration, but he could not control competing forces, prevent killings and looting, or impose unified command. Britain captured and tried him after retaking Delhi, rejected his claim that seals and orders had been coerced, and exiled him to Yangon. Secret burial attempted to suppress commemoration, yet the rediscovered grave later became a site of Muslim veneration.

## Key Characteristics

- Last Mughal emperor but a pensioned and closely constrained ruler without effective territorial power.
- Urdu poet, calligrapher, painter, and religious scholar whose importance was cultural as well as dynastic.
- Religiously inclusive figure presented as celebrating Hindu festivals and stressing common spiritual ground.
- Nominal rebellion leader selected for legitimacy but unable to direct fragmented armed groups.
- Defendant whose coercion argument did not prevent conviction and exile.
- Exile whose attempted erasure through secret burial failed to eliminate symbolic memory.

## Qualifications

The source distinguishes lack of command from absence of all agency but cannot resolve the degree of Bahadur Shah's voluntary cooperation, knowledge, or responsibility. Trial charges, rebel violence, exact religious views, family motives, burial policy, and later sanctification remain bounded to this popular-history account.

## What Changed

- Created the final Mughal emperor's profile around residual legitimacy, constrained agency, trial, exile, and memory.
