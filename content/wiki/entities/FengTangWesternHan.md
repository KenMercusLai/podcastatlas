## Overview

冯唐 / Feng Tang is an elderly Western Han palace officer who tells [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] that possessing famous generals would not matter if the court could not authorize, reward, and protect them.

## Current Profile

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-225-1-fengtang-xiuru-wendi-liuheng-weihe-bu-nu-fan-xi-linynx-dnz0rrptlafz__fch4cgd|Hanji 225-1]] introduces Feng Tang as a man over sixty, connected by ancestry to Zhao and by birth to Dai, who has remained a palace gentleman without military achievement. When Liu Heng longs for [[LianPo|廉颇]] and [[LiMu|李牧]], Feng Tang replies that the emperor would be unable to use them even if they were alive.

The remark initially angers Liu Heng, but a later private audience turns provocation into diagnosis. Feng Tang argues that frontier commanders need meaningful control over operations, provisioning, and rewards, uses Li Mu as the positive model, and presents [[WeiShangWesternHan|魏尚]] as the Han counterexample: a successful Yunzhong governor is imprisoned over six overreported enemy heads while the soldiers and commander receive too little credit for frontier performance.

Feng Tang's durable role is therefore not simply that of a fearless speaker. His criticism works because it connects a historical analogy to a reversible current decision. Liu Heng acknowledges the error, sends him to pardon Wei Shang, and appoints him chariot-and-cavalry commandant. The episode later makes his advanced age under Han Wudi the background to the literary image “冯唐易老.”

## Key Characteristics

- Elderly low-ranking official whose Zhao-Dai background gives him a plausible route into frontier history.
- Direct remonstrant willing to challenge the emperor's self-image in public.
- Institutional critic who shifts the problem from “Where are the great generals?” to “What authority and incentives make generals usable?”
- Evidence-based adviser who anchors abstract criticism in Li Mu's command model and Wei Shang's current punishment.
- Late-recognized official whose later age becomes a symbol of talent outlasting its opportunity.

## Qualifications

This profile rests on one popular-history podcast note rather than a complete biography. Feng Tang's exact age, offices, Zhao-Dai family background, public and private dialogue, mission to Wei Shang, Jingdi service, Wudi-era recommendation, friendship between his son Feng Sui and Sima Qian, and later literary reception require comparison with transmitted and literary sources. The page treats his criticism as institutionally useful without assuming that blunt public humiliation is always the safest or most effective form of advice.
