# Titus

## Overview

Titus was [[Vespasian]]'s son, siege commander at Jerusalem, and later Roman emperor whose public profile joined conquest, Flavian legitimacy, the [[EruptionOfVesuvius79|79 CE eruption of Vesuvius]], and the inauguration of the [[Colosseum]].

## Current Profile

In 70 CE Titus inherited command of the [[JudeanRevolt66To73|Judean campaign]] with four legions, auxiliaries, and artillery. His siege of Jerusalem moved from attempted intimidation and surrender through breached defenses, encirclement, starvation, crucifixion, assault on the Antonia fortress, destruction of the Temple, and demolition of most of the city. Judean figures including Julius Alexander, Herod Agrippa, Berenice, and [[Josephus]] stood with Rome, complicating a simple ethnic account. Josephus's claim that Titus wanted to save the Temple remains compromised by his dependence on the Flavians and providential interpretation.

Titus and Vespasian then used [[ImperialVictoryReframing]] to present provincial reconquest as foreign conquest through triumph, captives, Temple treasures, and Judea Capta coinage. As emperor, Titus faced Vesuvius, fire, and plague before the Colosseum's probable inauguration in 80 CE. The amphitheater allowed him to answer an earlier reputation for coercion and libertinism with generosity, punishment of informers, ordered spectatorship, spectacle, and public guardianship.

## Key Characteristics

- Flavian prince and commander who captured Jerusalem after a brutal siege.
- Conqueror whose conduct and intentions are filtered heavily through Josephus.
- Dynastic figure who helped recast Judean suppression as a foreign triumph.
- Emperor whose short reign opened amid the Vesuvius catastrophe, fire, and plague.
- Probable inaugurator of the Colosseum in 80 CE.
- Ruler who used public spectacle and disaster-era conduct for reputational repair.

## Evidence

- Jerusalem command: [[177-the-jewish-revolt-part-3-glt1109135413]] follows Titus through intimidation, encirclement, starvation, assault, Temple destruction, and demolition.
- Divided war and source problem: [[177-the-jewish-revolt-part-3-glt1109135413]] identifies Judean allies on the Roman side and qualifies Josephus's defense of Titus.
- Triumph and dynasty: [[177-the-jewish-revolt-part-3-glt1109135413]] and [[369-the-colosseum-romes-arena-of-death-glt7808118779]] connect Judean victory to Flavian legitimacy, display, and later monumentality.
- Eruption chronology: [[355-roman-apocalypse-pompeii-79-ad-glt6664993493]] places the Vesuvius catastrophe during Titus's short reign.
- Reputational and ceremonial repair: [[369-the-colosseum-romes-arena-of-death-glt7808118779]] contrasts Titus's earlier profile with the ordered, lavish inauguration of the Colosseum after multiple disasters.

## Qualifications

The sources do not settle Titus's responsibility for the Temple fire, sincerity about surrender or preservation, casualty figures, or the reliability of Josephus's speeches and scenes. The Vesuvius episode gives Titus little direct agency and does not establish a full relief policy. The Colosseum's inauguration date is probable, its political interpretation remains interpretive, and the sources reject a simple claim that Jerusalem loot alone funded the amphitheater.

## What Changed

- Reconstructed Titus's Jerusalem command as siege, terror, destruction, and an internally divided provincial conflict.
- Added Josephus's defense of Titus as an explicit source-critical limitation.
- Connected the Judean triumph directly to Flavian recategorization of civil-war legitimacy.
- Integrated conquest, catastrophe, spectacle, and reputational repair into one profile.

- [[JudeanRevolt66To73]] - conflict in which Titus commanded the capture of Jerusalem.

- [[ImperialVictoryReframing]] - mechanism that converted provincial reconquest into dynastic triumph.
- [[Colosseum]] - amphitheater whose inauguration became Titus's major imperial performance.
- [[EruptionOfVesuvius79]] - catastrophe near the opening of his reign.
