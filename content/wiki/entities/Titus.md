# Titus

## Overview

Titus is the Roman emperor presented as [[Vespasian]]'s successor, a conqueror of Jerusalem, and the ruler whose short reign joined the [[EruptionOfVesuvius79|79 CE eruption of Vesuvius]] to the later inauguration of the [[Colosseum]].

## Current Profile

The Vesuvius source places the destruction of [[Pompeii]] and [[Herculaneum]] near the opening of Titus's reign and follows the catastrophe through Plinian testimony, archaeology, and volcanology rather than through a detailed account of his policy response. The Colosseum source then contrasts Titus's earlier reputation as Praetorian commander and libertine with his self-presentation as a reformed emperor. Vesuvius, a major Roman fire, and plague made the amphitheater's inauguration more than celebration: it became a ritual and political opportunity to address the dead, punish figures associated with control, reorder spectators, and perform guardianship of city and empire.

## Key Characteristics

- Flavian successor associated with victory in Judea and the capture of Jerusalem.
- Emperor whose reign opened amid the Vesuvius disaster that destroyed Pompeii and Herculaneum.
- Emperor who probably inaugurated the Colosseum in AD 80 in the episode's chronology.
- Ruler carrying an earlier reputation for coercive office and libertine conduct.
- Disaster-era emperor presenting reform, generosity, moral order, and public guardianship.
- Patron of spectacles combining punishment, animals, mythic theater, and gladiatorial combat.

## Evidence

- Conquest and succession: [[369-the-colosseum-romes-arena-of-death-glt7808118779]] joins Titus to Jerusalem, Flavian wealth claims, and succession from Vespasian.
- Eruption chronology: [[355-roman-apocalypse-pompeii-79-ad-glt6664993493]] places the Vesuvius catastrophe during Titus's short reign.
- Disaster setting: [[369-the-colosseum-romes-arena-of-death-glt7808118779]] places inauguration after Vesuvius and amid fire and plague.
- Reputational repair: [[369-the-colosseum-romes-arena-of-death-glt7808118779]] contrasts Titus's earlier profile with his conduct as emperor.
- Ordered inauguration: [[369-the-colosseum-romes-arena-of-death-glt7808118779]] connects public punishment and ranked spectatorship to restored moral order.

## Qualifications

The Vesuvius episode gives Titus little direct agency and does not establish the details of imperial relief, so the eruption should not be converted into a complete disaster-response profile. The inauguration date is presented as probable, and the Colosseum source's account of Titus's motives, moral transformation, public punishment, ritual obligations, and audience response remains interpretive. It also qualifies a simple claim that Jerusalem loot alone paid for the amphitheater.

## What Changed

- Added the Vesuvius catastrophe as a distinct event in Titus's reign while keeping his direct response outside the source's evidence.
