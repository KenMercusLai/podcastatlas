# Miltiades

## Overview

Miltiades appears in the wiki as the Athenian commander whose earlier experience with Persian power in the Chersonese informs his later defensive containment and offensive gamble at Marathon.

## Current Profile

The pre-Marathon source introduces Miltiades before the battle as an Athenian aristocrat ruling the Thracian Chersonese under Persian pressure. During Darius's Scythian campaign, he proposes cutting the Danube bridge and trapping Darius, but [[Histias]] blocks the plan because Ionian tyrants depend on Persian backing. After the [[IonianRevolt]], Miltiades escapes Persian pursuit, reaches Athens, survives prosecution for tyranny in the Chersonese, and is elected one of the ten generals.

The Marathon source then explains why that background matters. Miltiades argues that the Athenians should march quickly to block the road from Marathon to [[Athens]], not because open battle is immediately desirable, but because containment prevents the Persians from moving freely on the city. When Persian cavalry appears absent and the force may be divided, he presses for a dawn attack, using strong wings and a thinned center to win a risky battle.

## Key Characteristics

- Miltiades is presented as an Athenian with unusually direct experience of Persian campaigning and client politics.
- His Danube-bridge proposal shows early willingness to strike Darius when opportunity appears.
- His return to Athens is politically uneasy because he has ruled as a tyrant elsewhere.
- His first Marathon aim is containment: block the road from Marathon to Athens and deny the Persians free movement.
- His offensive decision depends on the reported absence of Persian cavalry.
- His tactical design uses strong wings and a thin center to match the Persian frontage.
- The episode frames his plan as successful but extraordinarily risky.

## Qualifications

The profile is source-scoped to the Marathon pair. It does not cover Miltiades's wider career, later trial, all Chersonese politics, or all debates over his exact tactical authorship. The cavalry absence, battle vote, and Danube-bridge episode are preserved as the sources' reconstruction.
