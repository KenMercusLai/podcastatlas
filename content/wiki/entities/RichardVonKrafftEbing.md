# Richard von Krafft-Ebing

## Overview

Richard von Krafft-Ebing was a German-speaking psychiatrist whose *Psychopathia Sexualis* helped medicalize sexuality and cruelty through categories that shaped late-nineteenth- and twentieth-century public interpretation.

## Current Profile

Across the wiki's sources, Krafft-Ebing marks a shift from treating sexual conduct chiefly as sin or crime toward describing recurring desires, identities, and violent motives as psychiatric types. The Oscar Wilde episode places this vocabulary beside legal change and the emergence of homosexuality as a category of person. That conceptual shift could create forms of recognition while also exposing people to diagnosis, stigma, and institutional control.

The Marquis de Sade episode connects Krafft-Ebing's term “sadism” to Sade's later reception. The label made a historical name into a clinical category, but it does not make Sade's writing a neutral medical record or erase distinctions among consensual sexuality, fantasy, abuse, and coercion.

The Ripper episode adds “lust murder” and sexualized cruelty. It argues that psychiatric language helped later audiences interpret the Whitechapel murders as modern serial murder rather than violence without intelligible motive. This is a history of legibility, not a retrospective diagnosis of an unidentified offender.

## Key Characteristics

- Major figure in the medical classification of sexuality and sexual pathology.
- Associated by the supplied sources with identity categories including homosexuality and with the term “sadism.”
- Helped create a psychiatric vocabulary for sexualized cruelty and “lust murder.”
- Illustrates the double edge of classification: explanation and recognition can coexist with stigma and control.
- Became part of the cultural framework through which Sade and Jack the Ripper were interpreted retrospectively.

## Qualifications

These narrative sources summarize intellectual influence rather than providing a complete history of psychiatric terminology, editions, translation, or reception. A category's historical importance does not validate every diagnostic assumption behind it. Applying Krafft-Ebing's language to Jack the Ripper explains later interpretation but cannot establish the unknown killer's motive, identity, or mental state.
