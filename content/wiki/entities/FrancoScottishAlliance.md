# Franco-Scottish Alliance

## Overview

The Franco-Scottish Alliance is the long-running strategic relationship that first appears as a 1295 two-front agreement against [[England]] and later gives sixteenth-century Scotland military and dynastic alternatives to English coercion.

## Current Profile

The source presents the alliance as a two-front mechanism rather than a decorative diplomatic fact. If [[England]] attacks either France or Scotland, the other is expected to invade or pressure England. That makes Scottish war inseparable from the legal and territorial dispute over [[Gascony]] and [[Aquitaine]].

The alliance becomes active in Edward III's reign. After [[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]] supports the Balliol cause and wins at [[HallidonHill|Hallidon Hill]] in 1333, David II flees to France. [[PhilipVIOfFrance|Philip VI]] then insists that any Anglo-French treaty include Scotland and uphold Scottish rights.

The Mary Stuart episode supplies a later form. [[JamesVOfScotland|James V]]'s marriage to [[MaryOfGuise|Mary of Guise]] reinforced the connection, while the failed [[TreatyOfGreenwich1543|Treaty of Greenwich]] and [[RoughWooing|Rough Wooing]] made France the alternative protector of the infant queen. After [[BattleOfPinkie|Pinkie]], [[HenryIIOfFrance|Henry II]] sent troops, obtained Mary's betrothal to the Dauphin, and received her in France.

This later branch also shows the alliance's limits. French protection preserved Mary from English control, but it shifted the kingdom toward French troops, Guise influence, and a dynastic settlement that later helped provoke Protestant and anti-French resistance.

## Key Characteristics

- Agreement dated by the source to 1295.
- Strategic link between French and Scottish pressure on England.
- Makes Edward III's Scottish victories diplomatically costly.
- Connects northern war to Gascony and Aquitaine disputes.
- Helps explain why the Hundred Years' War origin is not only a French succession quarrel.
- Gives sixteenth-century Scotland an external alternative to compelled union with England.
- Joins military rescue to dynastic marriage and increased French leverage inside Scotland.

## Qualifications

The page covers two distant episodes rather than a continuous institutional history. The precise 1295 obligations, intervening renewals, sixteenth-century treaty law, military totals, Scottish popular opinion, and relative weight of French aid and self-interest remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Extended the alliance from its fourteenth-century two-front logic to sixteenth-century military protection and dynastic marriage.
- Added the qualification that French rescue also increased French leverage and future domestic resistance.
