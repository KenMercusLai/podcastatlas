# General Gordon

## Overview

Charles George Gordon was the British officer and administrator whose anti-slavery work in [[Sudan]], death during the [[SiegeOfKhartoum|siege of Khartoum]], and posthumous reputation made him a major Victorian Christian-imperial symbol.

## Current Profile

The source's Gordon is neither a spotless saint nor a cynical imperial mask. Evangelical conviction made his opposition to slavery, personal courage, austerity, and refusal to abandon Khartoum credible. The same religious certainty, isolation, eccentricity, and political impatience made him difficult to direct and inclined to reduce the [[MuhammadAhmad|Mahdist revolt]] to slavery and grievance rather than fully grasp its religious appeal.

His final mission magnified these traits because its purpose was unstable. London imagined inquiry or evacuation, Cairo restored him as governor-general, and Gordon treated withdrawal without those under his protection as dishonorable. His death after the relief expedition's delay then allowed newspapers, soldiers, the monarchy, and imperial memory to turn a failed policy into [[ImperialMartyrdomMemory|martyrdom]].

## Key Characteristics

- Sincere evangelical abolitionist whose moral authority was also useful to imperial projects.
- Courageous and personally austere administrator prepared to endure isolation and danger.
- Restless, religiously intense, and increasingly eccentric public figure.
- Politically impractical actor who resisted clear subordination and accepted an ambiguous mission.
- Humanitarian reformer willing to seek help from the slave trader Zubair when he saw no workable alternative.
- Besieged commander whose refusal to leave Khartoum made his death central to later public memory.

## Evidence

- Anti-slavery administration: [[142-general-gordon-and-the-siege-of-khartoum-glt6227664313]] follows Gordon from Equatoria to governor-general and links his campaigns against slavery and ivory to evangelical conviction.
- Religious character: [[142-general-gordon-and-the-siege-of-khartoum-glt6227664313]] describes daily Bible reading, moral absolutism, holy-geography theories, and refusal to convert or surrender.
- Ambiguous mission: [[142-general-gordon-and-the-siege-of-khartoum-glt6227664313]] distinguishes London's fact-finding or evacuation expectations from Cairo's governor-general appointment and Gordon's conduct in Khartoum.
- Siege and afterlife: [[142-general-gordon-and-the-siege-of-khartoum-glt6227664313]] records reform gestures, defense, refusal to abandon others, death, delayed relief, and the resulting martyr legend.

## Qualifications

The source is a British-centered narrative episode rather than a complete biography or Sudanese history. Gordon's motives, drinking, sexuality, mental state, private faith, understanding of the Mahdi, proposed use of Zubair, and exact death remain disputed or interpretive. His opposition to slavery should neither erase his imperial role nor be dismissed merely because governments and monarchs could exploit it.

## What Changed

- Created a profile holding Gordon's genuine abolitionism, religious intensity, strategic impracticality, and imperial use together.
- Distinguished the unclear 1884 mandate from Gordon's later refusal to leave Khartoum.
- Added martyr memory as a political afterlife rather than treating death as the end of the case.
