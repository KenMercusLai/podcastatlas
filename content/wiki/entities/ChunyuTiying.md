## Overview

淳于缇萦 / Chunyu Tiying is presented as the youngest daughter of [[ChunyuYi|淳于意]] whose petition to [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] joins filial rescue to a general argument against irreversible mutilating punishment.

## Current Profile

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-224-tiying-jiufu-14sui-shaonv-ruhe-gandong-hanwendi-lifxjdmrqoao7b8dq43w-xjh_dhy|Hanji 224]] says Tiying follows her condemned father from Qi to Chang'an after hearing him dismiss his five daughters as useless in an emergency. Her response is practical rather than merely emotional: she seeks access to the emperor and argues that death cannot be reversed and severed limbs cannot be restored, so a person subjected to permanent bodily punishment loses the ability to reform and become useful again.

She reportedly offers herself as a government slave to redeem her father's offense. Liu Heng is said to pardon Chunyu Yi and then issue a general decree ending specified mutilating punishments. The current profile therefore treats Tiying as both a filial-rescue figure and a petitioner who reframes one household's crisis as a problem of [[MutilatingPunishmentAbolition|punishment design and reformability]]. It does not establish her as the sole cause of the reform or validate every biographical detail.

## Key Characteristics

- Young Western Han petitioner who follows her condemned father to the imperial capital.
- Daughter whose action directly answers a gendered claim that daughters cannot help in crisis.
- Advocate who connects bodily irreversibility to the loss of any meaningful path to repentance.
- Filial figure willing to exchange her own status and liberty for her father's opportunity to reform.
- Individual supplicant whose case is presented as triggering a general institutional response.

## Qualifications

The profile rests on one popular-history podcast summary rather than a primary-text collation. Tiying's reported age of fourteen, family dialogue, travel conditions, access to the emperor, petition wording, offer of servitude, and causal role in reform require comparison with transmitted sources. Later praise of her as a filial exemplar can preserve the story while narrowing its institutional argument; conversely, recognizing the argument does not show that one petition alone produced the reform.

## What Changed

- Established Tiying as both a filial-rescue figure and an institutional critic of irreversible punishment.
- Separated the petition's reformability logic from the stronger claim that she single-handedly caused penal reform.
- Connected the abolition narrative to the lethal-substitution problem in later flogging reform.
