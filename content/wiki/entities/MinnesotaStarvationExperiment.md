# Minnesota Starvation Experiment

## Overview

The Minnesota Starvation Experiment is represented as a controlled World War II study of prolonged dietary restriction and recovery in young, relatively healthy male volunteers, intended to inform treatment of widespread postwar malnutrition.

## Current Profile

Within the source, the study has unusual humanitarian force because researchers expected millions of severely malnourished people to need rehabilitation and lacked reliable recovery knowledge. Its controlled food intake produced evidence that an uncontrolled famine could not supply in the same way.

That purpose does not settle its ethics. [[MariaKonnikova]] stresses unknown lifelong consequences, possible patriotic pressure, and the difficulty of calling consent fully informed when researchers cannot enumerate the harms. [[MalcolmGladwell]] gives greater weight to participant altruism and the number of future people the knowledge might help. The study therefore anchors [[UnknownRiskInformedConsent]] rather than serving as a simple example of either justified sacrifice or prohibited research.

## Key Characteristics

- Used controlled dietary restriction and recovery rather than observing ordinary diet variation.
- Recruited young, relatively healthy men who were not serving overseas during World War II.
- Pursued an urgent practical question about rehabilitating people after prolonged malnutrition.
- Combined potentially large public benefit with serious individual burden and uncertain long-term harm.
- Produced evidence from a narrow population whose applicability to other groups requires separate judgment.

## Qualifications

This profile is bounded to one structured podcast summary, not the study protocol, participant records, publications, later follow-up, or a historical ethics review. It does not establish recruitment details, diet quantities, measured outcomes, effect sizes, participant diversity, later morbidity, or current clinical relevance. The source discusses later famine and epigenetic research but explicitly warns that fetal famine exposure differs from adult male experimental restriction.
