# Magna Carta / 大宪章

## Overview

Magna Carta is the English charter first imposed on [[KingJohn|King John]] in 1215 and repeatedly reissued thereafter, becoming a durable symbol of lawful limits on governing and punishing power.

## Current Profile

The charter emerged from military defeat, fiscal extraction, baronial revolt, and mutual distrust rather than a peaceful democratic founding. It mixed specific feudal, commercial, ecclesiastical, and administrative provisions with broader promises of lawful judgment and justice not sold, denied, or delayed. Its twenty-five-barons enforcement clause made the original settlement especially threatening to royal power, and John's repudiation meant that later reissues under Henry III were crucial to survival.

Magna Carta's modern importance is an afterlife as well as an origin. Court circulation, [[EdwardCoke|Edward Coke]]'s fundamental-law interpretation, [[JohnLilburne|John Lilburne]]'s freeborn-rights argument, British radicalism, and transatlantic constitutional use widened an elite medieval settlement into an emblem of [[KingUnderLaw|government under law]] and individual liberty. The historically false Norman-yoke narrative aided this expansion, but did not create the charter's legal significance from nothing.

In modern criminal-law discussion, this accumulated meaning makes Magna Carta a concise warning that the authority to punish must itself be bounded. Its best use is therefore neither patriotic origin myth nor cynical dismissal, but a qualified language for asking what limits constrain coercive government.

## Key Characteristics

- Settlement produced by armed conflict between King John and rebel barons in 1215.
- Contains both narrow medieval provisions and durable language about lawful judgment and accessible justice.
- Tried to make royal promises enforceable through a council of twenty-five barons.
- Survived through reissue, legal circulation, commentary, and political mobilization rather than John's compliance.
- Became a symbol of state power constrained by law in British, American, Commonwealth, and modern criminal-law argument.

## Qualifications

Magna Carta was not a democratic constitution, did not name Parliament, and initially protected a stratified society through the language of free men. Many later claims depend on selective interpretation, historical myth, or indirect influence. Its original failure, elite origins, and narrow provisions qualify but do not erase the broader clauses and institutional history on which later meanings were built.

## What Changed

- Reframed Magna Carta from a single modern symbol into a synthesis of medieval crisis, mixed provisions, enforcement failure, reissue, and political afterlife.
- Distinguished genuine legal continuity from the myths and selective reinterpretations that widened its public meaning.
