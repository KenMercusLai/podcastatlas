## Overview
Marc Bloch was a French medieval historian, co-founder of the journal that anchored the [[AnnalesSchool|Annales tradition]], veteran of both world wars, and member of the French Resistance killed in 1944.

## Current Profile
The source presents Bloch's scholarly and civic lives as one continuous practice. First World War service sharpened his interest in rumor, collective psychology, and ordinary experience; works including *The Royal Touch* used varied evidence and problem-led inquiry to explain belief and social structure; and *Strange Defeat* joined eyewitness testimony to a severe diagnosis of France's political and social collapse. Under Vichy persecution he lost property and professional standing, continued writing *The Historian's Craft*, joined the Resistance, and was executed. His later admission to the [[FrenchPantheon|Panthéon]] recognizes that combined legacy while reopening disputes about identity, family consent, and political use.

## Key Characteristics
- Expanded historical inquiry beyond elite events and official archives toward ordinary people, collective mentalities, and social structures.
- Treated interdisciplinary evidence as a response to historical problems rather than a decorative accumulation of fields.
- Joined medieval scholarship to firsthand analysis of war, rumor, defeat, and institutional failure.
- Served in both world wars and moved from military service into active Resistance leadership under occupation.
- Maintained a French civic identity while being subjected to antisemitic exclusion and later framed through both French and Jewish remembrance.
- Became a posthumous object of state commemoration whose public meaning exceeds academic historiography.

## Qualifications
The page rests on one structured podcast summary rather than Bloch's complete works, military records, Resistance archives, family papers, or the full scholarship on Annales historiography. The source's causal link between war experience and historical method is plausible but interpretive. Its account of *Strange Defeat* preserves the difference between invaluable contemporary testimony and an exhaustive causal explanation of France's fall.
