# House of Slaves (Goree)

## Overview

The House of Slaves is a museum on [[Goree|Gorée Island]] whose “Door of No Return” has become an international symbol of Atlantic slavery, despite the episode's conclusion that the building was not a major slave-export warehouse and the door was not a practical embarkation point.

## Current Profile

The source describes a building constructed in 1776 by the son of a signare and a French surgeon, turned into a museum soon after Senegalese independence. Under [[BoubacarJosephNdiaye|Boubacar Joseph Ndiaye]], it promoted very large passage and export totals that [[PhilipDCurtin|Philip D. Curtin]] and other historians rejected.

Its importance therefore operates on two levels that should not be collapsed. The material and documentary claim about mass embarkation is presented as unsupported, while the door's visual form, ritual use, visitors, and association with ancestral loss give it enduring commemorative force.

## Key Characteristics

- Eighteenth-century building later made into a slavery museum.
- Site of the visually powerful “Door of No Return.”
- Promoted as a mass embarkation depot through disputed numerical claims.
- Major destination for African American mourning and ancestral pilgrimage.
- Case where symbolic significance and historical function diverge.

## Evidence

- Building and museum: [[272-senegal-the-door-of-no-return-glt8611353465]] supplies the construction, ownership, post-independence museum, and visitor account.
- Promoted narrative: [[272-senegal-the-door-of-no-return-glt8611353465]] attributes the one-million passage and 20-million export claims to Ndiaye's public storytelling.
- Historical challenge: [[272-senegal-the-door-of-no-return-glt8611353465]] says records, regional departure patterns, the building's use, and rocks beneath the door contradict the mass-embarkation account.
- Symbolic survival: [[272-senegal-the-door-of-no-return-glt8611353465]] compares the museum to pilgrimage sites whose emotional and ritual meaning is not reducible to literal verification.

## Qualifications

Calling the mass-embarkation narrative unsupported does not make the building irrelevant to slavery, erase the island's participation in coercive commerce, or invalidate visitors' grief. Conversely, emotional truth does not verify the episode's disputed totals or turn an impractical doorway into a documented loading point. Construction date, museum date, physical-use conclusions, and visitor claims remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Distinguished its disputed operational history from its strong commemorative afterlife.
