## Overview

邓训 / Deng Xun now has a public-works feasibility layer before his later Protector of the Qiang profile. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-749-guanliren-bikan-fa-jiangjin-shi-guanli-de-zuijia-shouduan-ma-lnhhoh0oxlstuwakl3rsqvcss-cf|Hanji 749]] shows him inspecting an impossible canal project and truthfully reporting that it should be stopped, [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-760-lishi-jiemi-huanghou-chugui-bei-zhua-xiachang-can-bucan-lgz2sc9xt8ulltvwemiz2uzfghqi|Hanji 760]] shows him protecting threatened Hu families and converting trust into frontier submission, and [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-767-congming-ren-dou-shanchang-yanxi-lq3ol7y-jdh7dnnfkmtfdg4ibryq|Hanji 767]] records the cross-community mourning that follows his death.

## Current Profile

Hanji 749 supplies Deng Xun's earliest current policy layer. He inspects the Hutuo/Shijiu canal project, finds that the work cannot be completed, and reports the field reality rather than protecting the project. [[EmperorZhangOfHan|Zhangdi]] cancels the project and uses donkey-cart transport instead, which the source frames as saving huge annual expense and many laborers' lives.

Hanji 760 then supplies Deng Xun's frontier-governance layer. After he is appointed Protector of the Qiang, Mi Tang leads more than ten thousand people toward the frontier and plans to coerce small Yuezhi and Hu groups. Some officials argue that Han should let Hu and Qiang groups weaken each other, but Deng rejects that approach. He says earlier official bad faith helped drive rebellion, opens his office to endangered Hu wives and children, and sends troops to protect them.

The protective stance produces operational trust. Hu leaders submit to Deng's direction, Deng uses surrendered Qiang to recruit other groups, Mi Tang's uncle Hao Wu brings more than eight hundred households over to Han, and Deng defeats Mi Tang with Han, Hu, and Qiang forces. After a later 89 CE attack across the river, the source says Mi Tang's people move more than a thousand li westward and adjacent smaller groups submit. Deng then reduces the ordinary garrison and uses convicts for farming and fortification repair, making pacification a post-battle governance problem rather than only a field victory.

Hanji 767 records Deng Xun's death as a political event, not only an obituary. Han officials, commoners, Qiang, Hu, and former Wuhuan-protector subordinates mourn him in large numbers. His successor Nie Shang also tries conciliation, but Mi Tang's renewed rebellion and killing of escorts makes the contrast sharp: Deng's legitimacy appears rooted in accumulated protection, credibility, coercive capacity, and settlement practice, while Nie's posture lacks the same trust base.

## Key Characteristics

- Honest field assessor who reports an infeasible canal project and enables burden-saving cancellation.
- Protector of the Qiang whose policy begins with protecting threatened Hu families rather than exploiting intergroup violence.
- Frontier official who connects grace, trust, surrenderer recruitment, and force against Mi Tang.
- Post-battle governor who shifts from campaign victory to reduced garrison, farming, and fortification repair.
- Eastern Han official whose death draws mourning from Han, Qiang, Hu, and former Wuhuan-linked subordinates.
- Contrast figure for Nie Shang's failed, impatient conciliation of Mi Tang.
- Source-bounded model of people-based political security in a frontier setting.

## Qualifications

This page is not a full biography of Deng Xun. Hanji 749 explains a public-works stop-loss decision under Zhangdi, Hanji 760 explains one policy sequence behind his frontier reputation, and Hanji 767 records posthumous legitimacy and a contrast with Nie Shang. Exact geography, Hutuo/Shijiu engineering details, reported savings, Hao Wu's later role, casualty totals, and the identities of smaller Hu and Qiang groups remain source-scoped.

## What Changed
