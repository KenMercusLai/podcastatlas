## Overview

邓训 / Deng Xun now appears as the Protector of the Qiang whose later mourning in Hanji 767 has an earlier policy basis in Hanji 760. Hanji 760 shows him protecting threatened Hu families, converting grace and trust into submission, using surrendered Qiang to weaken [[MiTangQiangLeader|Mi Tang]], and leaving a smaller farming-and-fortification garrison. Hanji 767 then contrasts the legitimacy visible at his death with [[NieShangLateHan|Nie Shang]]'s failed attempt to succeed him through quick conciliation.

## Current Profile

Hanji 760 supplies Deng Xun's first current policy layer. After he is appointed Protector of the Qiang, Mi Tang leads more than ten thousand people toward the frontier and plans to coerce small Yuezhi and Hu groups. Some officials argue that Han should let Hu and Qiang groups weaken each other, but Deng rejects that approach. He says earlier official bad faith helped drive rebellion, opens his office to endangered Hu wives and children, and sends troops to protect them.

The protective stance produces operational trust. Hu leaders submit to Deng's direction, Deng uses surrendered Qiang to recruit other groups, Mi Tang's uncle Hao Wu brings more than eight hundred households over to Han, and Deng defeats Mi Tang with Han, Hu, and Qiang forces. After a later 89 CE attack across the river, the source says Mi Tang's people move more than a thousand li westward and adjacent smaller groups submit. Deng then reduces the ordinary garrison and uses convicts for farming and fortification repair, making pacification a post-battle governance problem rather than only a field victory.

Hanji 767 records Deng Xun's death as a political event, not only an obituary. Han officials, commoners, Qiang, Hu, and former Wuhuan-protector subordinates mourn him in large numbers. Some frontier mourners cut themselves or slaughter animals as a sign of wanting to follow him in death, while households later build shrines and pray to him during epidemics.

The mourning evidence now has a clearer explanation. His successor Nie Shang also tries conciliation, but Mi Tang's renewed rebellion and killing of escorts makes the contrast sharp: Deng's legitimacy appears rooted in accumulated protection, credibility, coercive capacity, and settlement practice, while Nie's posture lacks the same trust base.

## Key Characteristics

- Protector of the Qiang whose policy begins with protecting threatened Hu families rather than exploiting intergroup violence.
- Frontier official who connects grace, trust, surrenderer recruitment, and force against Mi Tang.
- Post-battle governor who shifts from campaign victory to reduced garrison, farming, and fortification repair.
- Eastern Han official whose death draws mourning from Han, Qiang, Hu, and former Wuhuan-linked subordinates.
- Remembered through public grief, private household shrines, and epidemic prayers.
- Contrast figure for Nie Shang's failed, impatient conciliation of Mi Tang.
- Source-bounded model of people-based political security in a frontier setting.

## Qualifications

This page is not a full biography of Deng Xun. Hanji 760 explains one policy sequence behind his frontier reputation, while Hanji 767 records posthumous legitimacy and a contrast with Nie Shang. Exact geography, Hao Wu's later role, casualty totals, and the identities of smaller Hu and Qiang groups remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 760 as the policy layer before Deng's death: protection of Hu families, trust-building, Mi Tang defeat, and postwar farming/fortification.
- Reframed Hanji 767's mourning evidence as the afterlife of accumulated frontier credibility rather than an unexplained popularity notice.
- Preserved Nie Shang as the negative contrast for copying conciliation without Deng's trust base.
