# Watergate Scandal

## Overview

The Watergate scandal was the constitutional and political crisis that grew from the 1972 Democratic National Committee burglary into exposure of a White House cover-up, a struggle over presidential evidence, and [[RichardNixon|Richard Nixon]]'s 1974 resignation.

## Current Profile

The episode presents Watergate as a two-year escalation rather than an automatic consequence of the break-in. Hush money and false explanations depended on participants remaining silent; James McCord's letter and [[JohnDean|John Dean]]'s cooperation broke that arrangement. Televised Senate hearings widened public scrutiny, while disclosure of Nixon's taping system made the dispute testable against recorded evidence.

The later confrontation joined prosecutors, executive officials, [[UnitedStatesCongress|Congress]], the [[SupremeCourt]], and Republican leaders. Nixon's attempt to dismiss special prosecutor Archibald Cox intensified the crisis, the Court required the tapes, and the June 23 recording destroyed the remaining defense that Nixon had learned of the cover-up only later. The scandal therefore combines [[CoverUpEscalation]] with a [[ConstitutionalAccountabilityTrustParadox]]: institutions constrained the president, but the disclosures damaged confidence in presidential government.

## Key Characteristics

- Began with a burglary but became presidentially decisive through obstruction, payments, perjury, and evidence withholding.
- Unraveled when exposed participants stopped maintaining collective silence.
- Shifted from disputed testimony to recorded evidence after the taping system was revealed.
- Escalated through the Saturday Night Massacre into an overt conflict over prosecutorial and legal constraint.
- Ended politically when documentary proof and cross-party congressional judgment made Senate survival implausible.
- Produced both a successful accountability story and a lasting erosion of political trust.

## Qualifications

This profile is based on one conversational history episode rather than a complete legal or archival reconstruction. Its audience figures, motives, dialogue, psychological interpretations, comparisons with later scandals, and claims about the pardon or long-run political culture remain source-scoped. ## What Changed

- Established Watergate as a canonical event rather than a background reference on other pages.
- Distinguished the initiating burglary from the cover-up and evidence struggle that forced resignation.
- Preserved the dual legacy of effective constitutional constraint and damaged public trust.
