# A Dictionary of the English Language

## Overview

*A Dictionary of the English Language* was [[SamuelJohnson]]’s major collaborative lexicographical project, a source of literary authority, and an episode-level example of the Enlightenment dictionary as public education rather than mere word lookup.

## Current Profile

Johnson received the commission in 1746 after poetry, biography, translation, criticism, moral essays, and periodical work had demonstrated his command of English. He completed the Dictionary over nine years with six assistants, five of them Scottish, complicating the image of a solitary compiler and Johnson’s performed hostility toward Scots.

The project’s public meaning exceeded definition. The dictionary-history episode places it in the eighteenth-century transition toward dictionaries addressed to a wider reading public and intended to improve understanding of a complex world. That educational and normative ambition makes the work an early reference point for [[DictionaryDescriptionAndPrescription]], even though the current sources do not reconstruct its entry-level editorial method.

Its production history also joins public purpose to precarious institutions. Johnson’s definition of the lexicographer as a “harmless drudge” foregrounded labor, while the Earl of Chesterfield episode exposed patronage’s limits: after feeling neglected, Johnson rejected Chesterfield’s late praise and implied sponsorship. Completion brought fame and some relief from literary drudgery, but it did not immediately end debt.

## Key Characteristics

- Large lexicographical project grounded in Johnson’s learning and commercial writing experience.
- Collaborative work completed with six assistants rather than a solitary-genius artifact.
- Enlightenment-era educational reference intended for a widening public.
- Normative language project that precedes the modern descriptive consensus.
- Source of durable literary authority and public fame.
- Test case for conflict between labor already performed and patronage claimed after success.

## Evidence

- Commission and production: [[650-londons-golden-age-the-mad-life-of-dr-johnson-part-1-glt2687128958]] dates the commission to 1746 and describes nine years of work with six assistants.
- Public educational role: [[306-cidian-you-shenme-yong-lj-xsj-bvpmo0imc3qnv8njlgzso]] places Johnson’s dictionary in the Enlightenment emergence of the dictionary as a tool for educating and elevating a wider public.
- Labor, reputation, and patronage: [[650-londons-golden-age-the-mad-life-of-dr-johnson-part-1-glt2687128958]] uses the “harmless drudge” definition and Chesterfield conflict to connect lexicography to sustained labor, fame, and disputed credit.

## Qualifications

The sources do not provide a technical study of entries, citations, definitions, edition history, sales, errors, readership, or long-term linguistic influence. The public-education and prescriptive framing is a broad episode-level interpretation, while comparisons with academy projects and accounts of Chesterfield’s motives remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Added the Dictionary’s Enlightenment public-education and normative role to its existing labor, collaboration, patronage, and reputation profile.
- Positioned the work as a historical reference point for the later descriptive–prescriptive debate without assigning it a fully documented editorial doctrine.
