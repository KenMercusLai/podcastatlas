# PDVSA

## Overview
PDVSA is [[Venezuela]]'s state oil company and the institution where oil sovereignty, rent distribution, technical capacity, and political control collide. Across the sources, it shows that national ownership can capture more oil value without guaranteeing reinvestment, operational discipline, or insulation from political conflict.

## Current Profile
[[chevron-venezuela-and-the-paradox-of-plenty]] presents PDVSA as the company created when Venezuela fully nationalized its oil industry in 1976, keeping foreign firms such as [[Chevron]] and [[ExxonMobil]] under stricter contractor terms. [[trump-drinks-venezuelas-milkshake]] adds the internal arc: PDVSA became a powerful "state within a state," then was reshaped by the 2002-2003 strike, mass firings, Chávez-era contract reversals, later headcount growth, and a major loss of production capacity.

The current synthesis is that PDVSA is not just a nationalization symbol. It is also the operating system for Venezuela's [[OilRevenueDependence]]: when it underinvests, loses skilled workers, or becomes a political instrument, oil wealth turns into a reopening problem that can require foreign capital while reigniting sovereignty backlash.

## Key Characteristics
- State-owned oil company created through Venezuela's 1976 [[OilNationalization]].
- Sovereignty instrument that moved formal control from foreign oil companies toward the Venezuelan state.
- Operational bottleneck when political control, lost technical labor, degraded equipment, and underinvestment reduce production capacity.
- Contracting counterparty that still needs foreign companies for capital, technology, and market access.
- Political institution whose autonomy and workforce became central targets in the Chávez-era oil conflict.

## Evidence
National ownership and contractor control:
- [[chevron-venezuela-and-the-paradox-of-plenty]] describes PDVSA's creation during the 1976 nationalization and its use of stricter contractor terms for foreign firms.

Operational bottleneck:
- [[trump-drinks-venezuelas-milkshake]] says oil fields require constant reinvestment and describes PDVSA's later capacity losses, headcount expansion, and need for renewed capital and technology.

Political institution:
- [[trump-drinks-venezuelas-milkshake]] presents PDVSA as a company Chávez could criticize as too autonomous, then remake after the oil strike by firing about 20,000 workers and reversing foreign-company contracts.

Foreign-company counterparty:
- [[chevron-venezuela-and-the-paradox-of-plenty]] and [[trump-drinks-venezuelas-milkshake]] both show Chevron staying in Venezuela by negotiating through state-controlled arrangements while other major U.S. firms left.

## Qualifications
The sources focus on PDVSA's political economy, not its full corporate structure, field portfolio, finances, or current legal status. Investment figures and details of the Trump-era reopening remain source-scoped to [[trump-drinks-venezuelas-milkshake]].
