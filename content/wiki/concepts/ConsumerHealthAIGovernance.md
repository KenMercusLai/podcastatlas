# Consumer Health AI Governance

## Definition
Consumer health AI governance separates informational wellness tools from regulated medical claims and interventions according to validation, intended use, actionability, risk, and the consequences of error.

## Current Synthesis
The Makary source establishes a two-lane principle: consumer information can remain more flexible, while "medical grade" claims and automated interventions require validation against appropriate standards. Rapid software updates make static, slow guidance difficult, but speed does not remove the need for evidence when an output affects diagnosis, treatment, or escalation.

The WHOOP source makes the boundary concrete. Personal baselines, recovery scores, AI coaching, and blood-pressure insights may support wellness and prompt users to seek care, while AFib detection, ECG, heart-attack prediction, or disease diagnosis enter a stricter lane. The product's intended claim matters, but so do actual user reliance, algorithm performance, false alarms, and whether the system directs consequential action.

## Key Claims
- Consumer information and clinical intervention belong in distinct but connected regulatory lanes.
- "Medical grade" is a validation claim, not a synonym for sophisticated sensors or confident AI output.
- Intended use matters, but foreseeable user reliance and harm from error also shape the appropriate boundary.
- Rapidly updated AI and wearable features require governance that can evolve without waiving evidence standards.
- A wellness deviation can support proportionate follow-up without becoming a disease diagnosis.
- Cleared medical features should not transfer their regulatory credibility automatically to adjacent wellness outputs.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The WHOOP-FDA dispute is described from the company's perspective and does not supply the warning letter, current guidance text, performance data, or a regulator's full reasoning. A wellness label does not ensure low risk, while regulation does not guarantee perfect performance. Governance must consider intended use, evidence, update control, communication, privacy, user comprehension, and the consequences of both false reassurance and false alarms.
