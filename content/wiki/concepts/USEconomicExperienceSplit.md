## Definition
U.S. economic experience split is the pattern where the same U.S. economy produces sharply different lived conditions across asset owners, wage earners, retirees, sectors, income groups, and entry cohorts.

## Current Synthesis
The current synthesis is that the split is not only a mood-versus-data problem. AI capex, Big Tech earnings, stock ownership, pensions, and homeownership can make some households and sectors feel strong, while price-sensitive consumers, young workers, constrained professional households, and weak sectors experience stress. The [[PlanetMoney]] inflation episode adds listener-level evidence: [[LindsayCole]] faces constant household optimization, [[RickSchultz]] is comfortable through pensions and assets, and [[LeoVamaka]] is forming price-sensitive habits before adulthood.

## Key Claims
- Aggregate growth, equity strength, and cooling inflation can coexist with severe household stress.
- Asset ownership and retirement timing are central split mechanisms because home values, stocks, pensions, and 401(k)-style accounts are unevenly distributed.
- AI capex and Big Tech performance can make the U.S. economy look hot while benefits concentrate in technology, finance, and asset-owning households.
- Labor-market entry, job switching, and weak-sector employment can feel recession-like even without a simple national recession.
- Price-level pain matters even after inflation slows because households buy at today's higher level, not at the earlier base.
- The split becomes generational when shocks shape the first money habits or starting wages of younger cohorts.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The split does not mean headline indicators are false or that every household is struggling. It means averages need distributional interpretation. The listener cases are illustrative, while the market and AI claims remain source-dated to the relevant episodes.
