# Super-Slow Jogging / 超慢跑

## Definition
Super-slow jogging is a low-intensity running practice performed near walking speed with short steps, a relaxed body, conversational effort, and relatively high cadence. VOL.117 summarizes the pattern as “慢、小、高”: slow speed, small steps, and high step frequency.

## Current Synthesis
The wiki currently treats super-slow jogging as an adherence-oriented entry point to aerobic exercise. Its value in VOL.117 does not come from speed or distance targets; it comes from reducing setup, intensity, and intimidation enough that beginners, older adults, larger-bodied people, or people returning after inactivity may repeat it consistently.

The episode offers a practical pattern rather than a universal prescription. A metronome around 180 steps per minute can cue short, quick steps, while the talk test keeps effort low enough for conversation or a smile. New exercisers can begin with roughly 10 minutes and build toward 30 minutes as capacity and comfort improve. It can be done on a flat surface or in place, although footwear, noise, neighbors, symptoms, and individual physical capacity still matter.

VOL.117 attributes fat use, circulation, cardiopulmonary conditioning, shorter ground contact, lower injury risk, and easier next-day recovery to the practice. Those mechanisms and the exact cadence remain source-scoped because the episode does not provide comparative protocols, participant characteristics, effect sizes, or injury-specific assessment.

## Key Claims
- Super-slow jogging prioritizes sustainable low-intensity movement over speed, distance, or exhaustive effort.
- Conversational breathing and relaxed form are practical intensity checks; breathlessness indicates the pace may no longer be “super slow.”
- Short steps and relatively high cadence are intended to limit prolonged ground contact while maintaining rhythmic movement.
- A metronome can reduce pacing ambiguity, but 180 steps per minute is a source-level cue rather than a universal requirement.
- Gradual time progression makes the practice more accessible to people without a recent exercise base.
- Low equipment and location demands can reduce friction, although environment, footwear, noise, and health status constrain implementation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode is public education and personal experience, not a clinical exercise protocol. It does not establish that 180 steps per minute is optimal for every height, gait, age, surface, injury history, or health condition, and low intensity does not eliminate fall, joint, cardiovascular, or overuse risk. People with symptoms, chronic disease, pregnancy, frailty, balance problems, or recent injury may need individualized assessment. Claims about fat loss, cardiac output, gas exchange, lactate clearance, and injury reduction remain source-scoped.
