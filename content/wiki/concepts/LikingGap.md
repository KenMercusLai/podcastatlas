# Liking Gap

## Definition
The liking gap is the tendency to enjoy or positively evaluate a conversation partner while underestimating how much that person enjoyed the interaction or liked oneself in return.

## Current Synthesis
In [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-1dfa7e5aca191bd78c9a8028baf43cb1-50295125-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-1dfa7e5aca191bd78c9a8028baf43cb1-50295125|Mind Reading 2.0]], [[EricaBoothby]] presents the gap as a recurrent pessimistic error rather than proof that an interaction went perfectly. People inspect their own awkward remarks, omissions, intentions, and insecurity while lacking direct access to the partner's often favorable private reaction. Because both participants may hide interest and tell third parties instead, mutual liking can coexist with mutual fear of rejection.

The gap can persist when avoidance and indirect signals prevent correction. The episode reports it in initial conversations and for several months among new roommates, with disappearance near the end of the academic year. Outside perspective, respectful questions, curiosity, and expressed appreciation can supply missing evidence, but they do not guarantee reciprocity or override privacy and safety.

## Key Claims
- People can like one another while each underestimates the other's positive evaluation.
- Self-focused access to mistakes and insecurity biases inference when the partner's private reaction is hidden.
- High-stakes settings can enlarge self-monitoring and make favorable evidence harder to notice.
- Guardedness and third-party disclosure allow the error to persist by withholding corrective information.
- Familiarity can reduce the gap over time without making minds fully transparent.
- Direct evidence from questions, outside observers, and sincere compliments can improve calibration.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept describes an average tendency, not a promise that every person is liked, every conversation succeeds, or disclosure is safe. Cultural rules, power, harassment, prejudice, abuse, privacy, and relationship context can change both the true evaluation and the cost of testing it. The supplied summary does not provide methods, effect sizes, sample composition, replication evidence, or a full lifespan trajectory. Its proposed teenage, older-adult, social-anxiety, and narcissism patterns are not treated as established findings.
