# Cognitive Decline Advance Planning / 认知障碍提前规划

Cognitive decline advance planning is [[vol-118-danshen-renshi-yanglao-zhinan-bushi-gudu-de-daimingci-shi-gengzao-zhangwo-rensheng-de-zhudongquan-lhvkuveznxd4citlwf5marzaj-wt]]'s warning that dementia-like risk is especially hard for single elders. Physical illness can often be treated, accommodated, or outsourced through care services, but cognitive decline can remove the person's ability to manage money, sign documents, express preferences, or correct others' decisions.

The source therefore recommends arranging wills, mandates, entrusted decision-makers, and care intentions while cognition is clear. The concept extends [[EndOfLifeAutonomyAndDignity]] from assisted-death ethics into ordinary elder planning: autonomy must sometimes be protected before the moment when autonomy is most threatened.

[[working-memory-the-surprising-decline-of-dementia-6a6b0eeca585d3be78cd9d1b]] adds a prevention counterweight. [[AgeAdjustedDementiaDecline]] suggests dementia risk at a given age has fallen in rich countries, but the source also says ageing populations can still increase total cases. That means advance planning remains necessary even when [[ModifiableDementiaRiskFactors]] make dementia less fatalistic.

## Key Claims
- Cognitive decline is a planning risk even when the person is physically alive and medically supported.
- Legal and financial arrangements made after cognitive capacity is damaged may arrive too late.
- Single elders face a sharper coordination problem because there may be no default spouse or child managing daily decisions.
- Advance planning should connect legal paperwork, trusted people, medical preferences, and care-service options.
- The goal is not to control every outcome, but to reduce the chance that crisis hands decisions to unprepared relatives, strangers, or institutions.
- The dementia-decline source adds that planning and prevention are complementary: lower cohort risk does not remove population-level care demand.
