# First-Past-the-Post Bloc Splitting

## Definition

First-past-the-post bloc splitting occurs when ideologically adjacent parties divide votes across constituencies, allowing an opponent to win seats with pluralities and converting modest vote fragmentation into disproportionate parliamentary loss.

## Current Synthesis

The source compares the divided British left in 1983, when Labour competed with the SDP-Liberal Alliance, with division on the right between the Conservatives and Reform in 2024. It argues that first-past-the-post can punish divided blocs severely, but rejects the mechanical assumption that every vote for a smaller adjacent party would otherwise transfer to the largest party in that bloc.

The concept therefore combines an electoral arithmetic effect with a voter-behaviour qualification. Fragmentation changes seat efficiency, but ideological proximity does not erase candidate, class, protest, regional, turnout, or anti-party motivations.

## Key Claims

- Constituency plurality rules can magnify vote splitting into large seat losses.
- The effect depends on geographic distribution, not only national vote share.
- A divided left in 1983 and divided right in 2024 are structurally comparable without being politically identical.
- Counterfactual vote transfers cannot be inferred directly from party-family labels.
- Some minor-party voters might abstain or support the opposing major party if their preferred option disappeared.

## Evidence

- Electoral mechanism: [[464-modern-british-elections-part-2-glt4046145085]] describes first-past-the-post as brutally punishing divided political blocs.
- Historical comparison: [[464-modern-british-elections-part-2-glt4046145085]] compares Labour and the SDP-Liberal Alliance in 1983 with Conservatives and Reform in 2024.
- Transfer qualification: [[464-modern-british-elections-part-2-glt4046145085]] rejects the claim that Thatcher won only because the opposition split, noting that some Alliance voters might otherwise have voted Conservative.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source supplies no constituency model or voter-transfer survey for either election. “Bloc” is an analyst's category rather than proof of interchangeable preferences, and the 1983 and 2024 party systems differ in leadership, geography, issue alignment, incumbency, and voter motivation.

## What Changed

- Created the concept with an explicit distinction between seat-system penalties and uncertain voter-transfer counterfactuals.
