# Command Disobedience Under Battlefield Uncertainty

## Definition

Command disobedience under battlefield uncertainty is a subordinate's decision to disregard or reinterpret an order because local information suggests compliance would defeat the commander's wider purpose or create greater danger.

## Current Synthesis

At the [[BattleOfCapeStVincent1797|Battle of Cape St Vincent]], [[HoratioNelson]] left formation when he judged that smoke, missed signals, and the Spanish maneuver would defeat [[JohnJervis|Jervis]]'s wider purpose of preventing the enemy divisions from reuniting. The resulting victory makes this a strong case of locally informed, intent-preserving initiative, but outcome alone cannot prove that the risk was sound beforehand.

At Copenhagen, [[HydeParker]] saw a damaged British attack from farther away and signaled withdrawal, Nelson judged that disengaging under Danish fire would be more dangerous and kept “engage the enemy more closely” flying, and [[EdwardRiou]] reluctantly obeyed because his rank gave him less latitude. The contrast shows that celebrated initiative is not a general license to ignore orders. Its defensibility depends on information asymmetry, clarity of higher intent, consequences, rank, and whether success later biases judgment. Riou's death also shows that identical signals can impose unequal risks on differently positioned units. The failed [[TenerifeExpedition1797|Tenerife expedition]] supplies a nearby counterexample: persistence and honor rhetoric can intensify a poor plan when changing conditions should instead trigger reassessment.

At Lingling, [[LyuMeng|吕蒙]] receives [[SunQuan|孙权]]'s recall order but conceals it long enough to induce [[HaoPuLateHan|郝普]]'s surrender and then move toward Lu Su. Unlike Cape St Vincent, the source does not clearly establish that the ruler's wider intent required disobedience; unlike Copenhagen, the issue is not simply whether withdrawal is locally dangerous. The case adds a political boundary: initiative can improve the operational result while simultaneously threatening the superior's control over timing, risk, and strategy.

## Key Claims

- Formal authority and local situational awareness can diverge sharply during combat.
- Disobedience is most defensible when it preserves a credible higher intent rather than serving personal impulse.
- Rank and delegated trust distribute the practical freedom to reinterpret orders unevenly.
- Withdrawal can be more dangerous than continued engagement when units must turn or expose themselves under fire.
- Successful outcomes can romanticize disobedience and obscure the costs borne by officers who comply.
- Operational gain and command legitimacy must be judged separately when the subordinate withholds the order itself from the organization.

## Evidence

### Unequal information and judgment

- [[516-nelson-god-of-war-part-3-glt1686018300]] presents Cape St Vincent as an intent-preserving response to formation failure and Tenerife as a warning that bold persistence can also reinforce misjudgment.
- [[610-nelson-the-battle-of-copenhagen-part-3-glt9153225543]] contrasts Parker's distant withdrawal signal with Nelson's local judgment that disengagement would worsen the danger.

### Unequal discretion and consequences

- [[610-nelson-the-battle-of-copenhagen-part-3-glt9153225543]] contrasts Nelson's refusal with Riou's reluctant obedience and death during withdrawal.
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1152-sunquan-weishenme-tongma-liubei-shi-pianzi-lvyngihgs-obrqasnub4_lptryop|Hanji 1152]] presents Lyu Meng as hiding a recall order, taking Lingling through deception, and then complying with the wider redeployment.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept rests on three successful, commander-centered cases and one adjacent failure from popular-history accounts. Outcome bias can make successful insubordination look wiser than it was, while failed persistence may be judged more harshly on similar information. Jervis's exact orders and signals, Nelson's alternatives at Cape St Vincent, Parker's full reasoning, the feasibility of withdrawal at Copenhagen, and both counterfactual results remain uncertain. Hanji 1152's order timing, Sun Quan's intent, Lyu Meng's information, and the reported warning remain source-scoped; taking Lingling does not prove that delaying redeployment was the best ex ante choice. Tenerife was not the same formal command conflict, but it usefully shows why personal courage and national-honor language cannot substitute for reassessment. These cases do not support routine disregard of command authority.

## What Changed

- Added Lingling as a case where operational success coexists with a direct authority problem.
- Distinguished intent-preserving reinterpretation from concealment that changes the superior's control of timing.
- Strengthened the requirement to assess command legitimacy separately from the eventual result.
