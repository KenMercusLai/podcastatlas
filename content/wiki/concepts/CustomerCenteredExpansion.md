# Customer-Centered Expansion

## Definition
Customer-centered expansion is a growth strategy that begins with a defined customer and extends the ways the company serves that customer's needs, rather than beginning with a product and repeatedly searching for unrelated buyers.

## Current Synthesis
[[TopeAwotona]] derives the principle from thirteen years at [[Calendly]]. Once a company has acquired customers, served them well, and built trust, adjacent products or services for those customers may start with stronger distribution and context than a product aimed at a new group. The principle does not forbid customer expansion or product expansion; it proposes deeper service to the current customer as the more grounded initial direction.

## Key Claims
- An existing trusted customer relationship can reduce the discovery and acquisition burden for an adjacent offer.
- Customer-centered strategy requires a clear customer definition, not an abstract claim to serve everyone.
- Product expansion is useful when it solves additional real problems for the same customer rather than merely increasing catalog size.
- Customer trust and prior service create an advantage only if the adjacent offer remains relevant and credible.
- Companies eventually may need both new products and new customer groups, but sequencing affects risk and learning.

## Evidence
- Strategic lesson: [[advice-line-with-tope-awotona-of-calendly-vmp4254648132]] records Awotona's preference for building around customers rather than products.
- Trust and acquisition: [[advice-line-with-tope-awotona-of-calendly-vmp4254648132]] argues that serving acquired customers in additional ways is easier than repeatedly finding entirely new groups.
- Boundary: [[advice-line-with-tope-awotona-of-calendly-vmp4254648132]] acknowledges that both customer and product expansion eventually matter.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source supplies a strategic retrospective rather than comparative outcome data. Existing customers may not want adjacent products, and optimizing around them can narrow innovation, increase concentration, or stretch brand credibility. A company's current customer base may also reflect historical channel limits rather than the best future market.
