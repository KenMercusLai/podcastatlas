## Definition

Qiang official-abuse rebellion trigger is the frontier pattern where misconduct by a local or frontier official turns a manageable Qiang settlement into renewed armed resistance. The trigger is not the same as the whole rebellion; it is the escalation point where official behavior destroys trust, makes state protection unbelievable, or expands fear beyond the original incident.

## Current Synthesis

The current evidence now has two Western Han layers and one Eastern Han sharp-trigger case. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-435-xihan-sanchao-mingjiang-zhaochongguo-ruhe-daqi-wancheng-lgbznlirir1x0rgtzcj4oz0b1apr|Hanji 435]] supplies the opening Western Han escalation. Yi Qu Anguo first permits [[XianlingQiang|先零羌]] to cross the Yellow River for pasture without reporting upward, then later summons Qiang leaders, kills more than thirty he suspects, and pursues their people. The source presents this as the move that turns Zhao Chongguo's earlier coalition-risk warning into open rebellion by Yang Yu and others.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-440-dezui-xiaoren-pingqiang-mingjiang-zhaochongguo-ruhe-wannian-sangzi-lmu7jhfcslsdftyzxmx5glvioee3|Hanji 440]] shows the pattern after [[ZhaoChongguoWesternHan|赵充国]]'s Qiang settlement. Zhao warns [[EmperorXuanOfHan|汉宣帝]] that Xin Tang is drunken and impulsive and therefore unsuitable for Qiang affairs. Xin Tang later becomes Protector of the Qiang anyway, repeatedly abuses Qiang people while drunk, and helps trigger renewed rebellion. This version is a personnel-quality and trust-collapse case: the frontier had just moved toward surrender and dependent-state placement, but a bad official made the settlement unsafe.

Hanji 749 gives a later and sharper trigger sequence. A Han official abducts or takes a Qiang woman, the woman's husband kills the official, and the county head Zong Yan pursues the fleeing Qiang beyond the frontier. Other Qiang groups fear punishment, join together, and kill Zong Yan. Mi Wu and Buqiao then connect that panic to a broader attack on Longxi and Hanyang.

The source's governing lesson is that frontier rebellion can begin with a preventable administrative injury. Once state agents are seen as abusive and punitive, even groups not directly involved can decide that safety lies in collective resistance. Large imperial campaigns then become necessary to repair what local misconduct helped start.

This concept is narrower than general Qiang policy. It identifies the ignition mechanism before later military suppression, surrender, and pacification debates. Hanji 440 also shows that a trigger can appear after apparent success, when poor appointment decisions and misconduct undo the trust needed to hold a settlement.

## Key Claims

- Local official abuse can become a strategic frontier problem when it converts personal injury into group fear.
- Bad personnel choices before or after pacification can open or reopen a frontier crisis when an official's conduct makes Qiang groups unsafe.
- Punitive pursuit can escalate rather than restore order when communities expect collective punishment.
- Coalition leaders such as Mi Wu and Buqiao become more dangerous once state misconduct supplies a shared grievance.
- Military victory can end a revolt cycle without proving that the initial governance failure was repaired.
- The pattern should be kept distinct from later Qiang pacification methods that start after rebellion is already underway.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The page does not claim that all Qiang unrest came from official abuse, that Yi Qu Anguo or Xin Tang alone caused the Western Han rebellions, or that Mi Wu and Buqiao had no independent political motives in the later Eastern Han case. Hanji 435 provides an initial Western Han mismanagement-and-mass-killing trigger; Hanji 440 provides a compact post-pacification misconduct trigger; Hanji 749 provides one later abuse-and-pursuit escalation path within a broader Qiang-frontier history. Yi Qu Anguo, Yang Yu, Diao Ku, Xin Tang, Xin Linzhong, the specific Qiang victims, the woman, the initial official, Zong Yan, and Dian Wu remain source-scoped until further notes warrant standalone pages or disambiguation.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 435 as the Western Han origin layer where unauthorized frontier handling, mass killing of Qiang leaders, and pursuit turn Zhao Chongguo's warning into open rebellion.
- Preserved Hanji 440 as a Western Han post-pacification version where a bad Protector-of-Qiang appointment and drunken abuse help reignite rebellion.
- Preserved Hanji 749's official-abuse-to-rebellion sequence as the sharpest current escalation example.
- Kept the trigger analytically separate from later campaign victory and pacification strategy.
