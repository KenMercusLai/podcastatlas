# Teacher Assessment Bias

## Definition

Teacher assessment bias is the risk that judgments made by teachers incorporate expectations, behavior history, disability perceptions, class signals, or relationship context in ways that systematically affect awarded outcomes.

## Current Synthesis

The episode uses pandemic-era English assessment to argue that removing common exams does not remove unfairness. Teacher knowledge can capture work and circumstances missed by a timed test, but it can also expose pupils to unequal expectations. [[DaisyChristodoulou|Daisy Christodoulou]] specifically identifies poorer students, disadvantaged students, students with behavioral issues, and students with special educational needs as groups at risk.

This is a comparative institutional claim, not an argument that exams are neutral. Common tests can reflect unequal preparation, content choice, disability access, and high-stakes pressure; teacher assessment and exams distribute judgment and error differently.

## Key Claims

- Replacing exams moves evaluative discretion rather than eliminating it.
- Contextual familiarity can improve interpretation and introduce expectation bias at the same time.
- Disadvantage may be reproduced through behavior, class, and special-needs perceptions.
- Fair assessment requires comparing error patterns across methods, not idealizing either one.

## Evidence

- Pandemic case: [[84-exams-glt6507641896]] describes two disrupted exam cycles as evidence in the debate over retaining GCSEs and common assessment.
- Distributional claim: [[84-exams-glt6507641896]] reports Christodoulou's warning about poorer, disadvantaged, behaviorally marked, and special-needs students.
- Comparative boundary: [[84-exams-glt6507641896]] presents exams as flawed institutions whose alternatives carry different risks.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source does not provide the underlying datasets, effect sizes, mechanisms, subject differences, moderation practices, or comparison countries. Teacher assessment may add valid longitudinal evidence and accommodation, while standardized exams can also encode socioeconomic and cultural advantage.
