# Early Portuguese Atlantic Plantation Slavery

## Definition

Early Portuguese Atlantic plantation slavery names the episode's connection among African captive raiding and trading, Atlantic island settlement, sugar cultivation, and coerced plantation labor before 1492.

## Current Synthesis

The episode argues that Atlantic globalization did not begin suddenly with Columbus. Portuguese movement into Madeira, the Azores, Cape Verde, São Tomé, and the West African coast created an earlier system in which navigation, conquest, commodities, and human trafficking developed together.

Sugar cultivation on Madeira supplied a plantation model, while the 1444 Arguin raid and later captive sales in Lisbon show enslavement becoming an organized part of the expansion. Captive Africans were also transported to Atlantic islands, linking metropolitan markets to coerced production and foreshadowing the later Atlantic slave system without treating the mature system as already complete.

## Key Claims

- Portuguese Atlantic island expansion preceded Columbus and formed part of early globalization.
- Sugar cultivation and plantation organization developed alongside maritime route-building.
- African raiding, Lisbon captive sales, and island labor were connected parts of the same coercive expansion.
- The early system foreshadowed later Atlantic slavery but should not be collapsed into its fully developed later scale and institutions.

## Evidence

- Island evidence: [[227-portugal-on-the-edge-of-the-world-part-1-glt1834337589]] connects Madeira, the Azores, Cape Verde, and São Tomé to early Portuguese expansion.
- Commodity evidence: [[227-portugal-on-the-edge-of-the-world-part-1-glt1834337589]] identifies Madeira as a sugar-cultivation and early plantation site.
- Captive-trade evidence: [[227-portugal-on-the-edge-of-the-world-part-1-glt1834337589]] describes a 1444 raid at Arguin and the sale of African captives in Lisbon.
- Labor evidence: [[227-portugal-on-the-edge-of-the-world-part-1-glt1834337589]] says enslaved Africans were transported to Atlantic plantations.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode gives limited attention to enslaved people's lives, African political contexts, legal transformation, and differences among the islands. Its reported captive total and chronology remain source-scoped. “Foreshadowing” marks institutional continuity without claiming that the later transatlantic slave system already existed in complete form.

## What Changed

- Created a pre-1492 framework joining island colonization, sugar, captive trading, and plantation labor.
