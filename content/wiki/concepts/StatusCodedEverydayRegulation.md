## Definition

身份编码式日常管制 / status-coded everyday regulation is the practice of making political and social hierarchy visible through ordinary life rules: clothing, color, fabric, motifs, residence, utensils, footwear, headwear, hair, and other daily material signals.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 400-2 creates the concept through [[ZhuYuanzhang|朱元璋]]'s Ming rules. The episode presents the system as more than sumptuary etiquette. It argues that a ruler can use daily objects to make status legible, lower certain occupations, restrict imitation of elite or imperial symbols, and identify people who violate the expected order.

This concept sits between household administration and ritual order. [[HouseholdOccupationControl|户籍与职业管控]] fixes where people belong and what work they inherit; status-coded everyday regulation makes that belonging visible in fabrics, colors, houses, vessels, boots, hats, and hairstyles. In the episode's reading, [[LiAsPoliticalOrder|礼制政治秩序]] becomes politically intrusive when the court treats everyday form as a marker of loyalty and disorder risk.

## Key Claims

- Everyday objects can become political signs when the state assigns them by rank, occupation, or gendered status.
- Status coding makes hierarchy enforceable because improper fabric, color, motif, housing, or footwear can reveal rule-breaking.
- Anti-merchant hierarchy can be materialized through dress restrictions rather than stated only as ideology.
- Ritual clarity can stabilize rule in the ruler's eyes while reducing ordinary people's mobility and autonomy.
- The concept is source-scoped to this Ming example until additional sources add other dynastic cases.

## Evidence

- Clothing hierarchy: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-400-2-yidai-qiong-shidai-qiong-nandao-qiongren-jiu-gai-daidai-qiong-lluvrlqzbzx4bb_aatfmtyqibdym|Hanji 400-2]] says status rules covered clothing style, color, motif, material, sleeve length, and other details from emperor and princes down to commoners.
- Occupational fabric coding: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-400-2-yidai-qiong-shidai-qiong-nandao-qiongren-jiu-gai-daidai-qiong-lluvrlqzbzx4bb_aatfmtyqibdym|Hanji 400-2]] contrasts farmers' permitted fabrics with merchants' narrower permissions and treats this as explicit discrimination against merchants.
- Daily object control: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-400-2-yidai-qiong-shidai-qiong-nandao-qiongren-jiu-gai-daidai-qiong-lluvrlqzbzx4bb_aatfmtyqibdym|Hanji 400-2]] extends regulation to boots, hats, house size and decoration, drinking vessels, and women's hairstyles.
- Order logic: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-400-2-yidai-qiong-shidai-qiong-nandao-qiongren-jiu-gai-daidai-qiong-lluvrlqzbzx4bb_aatfmtyqibdym|Hanji 400-2]] says Zhu Yuanzhang viewed these details as tied to distinguishing obedient subjects from disorderly people and keeping upper-lower hierarchy clear.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not claim that every Ming rule was uniformly enforced or that daily-life coding fully prevented mobility. The source is a short explanatory episode, so exact legal wording, regional variation, gender categories, and enforcement frequency remain source-scoped.

## What Changed
