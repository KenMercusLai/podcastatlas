# Coercive Reservation Assimilation

## Definition

Coercive reservation assimilation is the use of confinement, material dependency, schooling, religious conversion, ceremony suppression, policing, land division, and status rewards to replace an Indigenous people's social and political order with one designed by the governing state.

## Current Synthesis

The episode presents the Great Sioux Reservation as more than a bounded place. After railroad and settler expansion and the destruction of the bison herds undermined independent subsistence, agencies controlled rations and organized schools, churches, farming, and police. Federal reformers sought to break collective structures, suppress ceremonies such as the Sun Dance, Christianize adults, and remove children to boarding schools.

The system also remade authority from within. [[JamesMcLaughlin]] elevated accommodationist figures and recruited former warriors as badge-wearing agency police, while [[SittingBull]] experienced dependence and public display as political and spiritual humiliation. The result was not passive assimilation: [[LakotaPeople|Lakota]] actors accommodated, resisted, converted, policed, farmed, performed, and joined the [[GhostDance]] under sharply constrained choices.

## Key Claims

- Material dispossession and cultural assimilation functioned as a joined system rather than separate policies.
- Ration dependency gave administrators leverage over people whose prior food system had been destroyed.
- Schools, Christianity, farming, ceremony bans, land division, and policing targeted institutions as well as individual behavior.
- Recruiting Indigenous police and favored intermediaries internalized federal authority within community relationships.
- Constrained participation in the reservation order does not by itself demonstrate consent to that order.
- Assimilation pressure could deepen spiritual grief and make renewal movements more compelling.

- Authority conversion: [[455-fall-of-the-sioux-the-ghost-dance-part-2-glt2320133118]] contrasts Sitting Bull's humiliation with Gall's status inside McLaughlin's agency order.
- Religious consequence: [[455-fall-of-the-sioux-the-ghost-dance-part-2-glt2320133118]] links the Ghost Dance's appeal to confinement, hunger, grief, and the loss of an older world.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The source distinguishes sincere reform belief from pure bad faith, but sincerity does not remove coercion or unequal power.
- Lakota responses were diverse; the concept should not divide all participants into simple collaborators and resisters.
- The page is grounded in one reservation-era account and is not a complete comparison of U.S. Indigenous policy or boarding-school systems.
- Environmental destruction, congressional policy, local administration, and settler expansion had distinct actors even when their effects reinforced one another.

## What Changed

- Established assimilation as a joined material, institutional, cultural, and political system.
- Added Indigenous policing and factional status as mechanisms that internalized reservation authority.
- Connected coerced social transformation to the spiritual and emotional appeal of the Ghost Dance.
