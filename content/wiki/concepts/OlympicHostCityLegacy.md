# Olympic Host-City Legacy

## Definition

Olympic host-city legacy is the long-run balance of public cost, infrastructure, debt, displacement, image, participation, political signaling, and shared experience left after a city stages the [[ModernOlympicGames]].

## Current Synthesis

[[81-modern-olympics-part-2-glt4846758176]] uses London 1948 as a low-build baseline and Montreal 1976 as a warning about cost overruns and long-lived debt. Later examples broaden the account: Atlanta adds commercialization and organizational criticism; Athens adds fiscal burden; Beijing adds state propaganda; London adds disputed participation benefits; and Rio adds the absence of community facilities alongside mass displacement. Barcelona is presented as the favorable exception whose city reputation improved.

The strongest synthesis is distributive rather than categorical. A successful broadcast, memorable competition, tourist image, or national celebration does not by itself prove durable resident welfare. Equally, weak conventional economic returns do not erase happiness, shared attention, or the cultural value some viewers and athletes receive. Legacy assessment therefore asks which benefits persist, who receives them, who pays, and what alternatives the same resources displaced.

The Los Angeles 1932 material supplies an earlier promotional and institutional-success case. In [[hitler-s-olympics-part-9-a-plague-on-both-your-houses-hitler-s-olympics-part-9-a-plague-on-both-your-houses|Hitler’s Olympics, Part 9]], tourism, investment, Hollywood, the Coliseum, the athletes' village, and a reported profit helped revive the city and sustain the Olympic movement. Yet the same spectacle model was transferable to Berlin, while the contemporaneous [[ScottsboroBoys]] injustice remained outside the city's international presentation. A positive delivery or balance-sheet result can therefore coexist with exclusion from the legacy account and with politically dangerous model transfer.

## Key Claims

- Host-city evaluation must extend beyond construction completion and event-time spectacle, including injustice excluded from the international presentation.
- Cost overruns and specialized venues can create long fiscal tails.
- Image, tourism, skills, employment, productivity, and sports participation should be tested rather than assumed.
- Displacement and missing community facilities make distribution central to legacy accounting.
- Political prestige may be valuable to a regime without being equivalent to public welfare.
- Barcelona's favorable reputation is an exception in the episode, not proof that hosting usually transforms cities.
- Emotional and civic value remain real but do not cancel opportunity cost or coercive burdens.

## Evidence

- Low-build baseline: [[81-modern-olympics-part-2-glt4846758176]] describes London 1948 as austere and almost entirely reliant on existing infrastructure.
- Debt and venue risk: [[81-modern-olympics-part-2-glt4846758176]] cites Montreal's cost overrun, debt, and troubled stadium.
- Later-city comparison: [[81-modern-olympics-part-2-glt4846758176]] contrasts Atlanta, Sydney, Athens, Beijing, London, Rio, and Barcelona.
- Participation and growth limits: [[81-modern-olympics-part-2-glt4846758176]] reports David Goldblatt's criticism of promised economic and sporting-participation benefits.
- Nonfinancial value: [[81-modern-olympics-part-2-glt4846758176]] retains audience inspiration and athlete stories as benefits outside narrow GDP accounting.
- Los Angeles promotional model: [[hitler-s-olympics-part-9-a-plague-on-both-your-houses-hitler-s-olympics-part-9-a-plague-on-both-your-houses]] connects the 1932 Games to tourism, investment, Hollywood, new facilities, profit, and Berlin's later borrowing.
- Excluded moral cost: [[hitler-s-olympics-part-9-a-plague-on-both-your-houses-hitler-s-olympics-part-9-a-plague-on-both-your-houses]] places the internationally protested Scottsboro case beside Los Angeles's unboycotted presentation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The city judgments and numerical estimates come from two conversational sources and are not independently audited here. Host-city outcomes can vary within a city and across time, and Barcelona's reputation may reflect conditions not reproducible elsewhere. Los Angeles's reported profit does not establish distribution, opportunity cost, or the complete public balance sheet, while Berlin's borrowing of spectacle techniques does not make the two host regimes equivalent. The concept does not presume that every nonfinancial benefit can be monetized or that every public cost is unjustified.

## What Changed

- Added model transfer and excluded injustice to the legacy framework through Los Angeles 1932 and Berlin 1936.
- Distinguished profitable event delivery from complete public, moral, and political accounting.
