# Siege of Cusco (1536–1537)

## Definition

The siege of Cusco was Manco Inca's large-scale attempt to destroy the Spanish position in the former Inca capital, beginning with the assault of 6 May 1536 and ending as military stalemate gave way to Diego de Almagro's seizure of the city in April 1537.

## Current Synthesis

[[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] treats the siege as the conquest's closest point to reversal. [[MancoInca]] and [[VilacUmu]] exploit surviving Inca organization to assemble and provision a reported force of about 100,000, enter Cusco, set roofs alight with heated sling stones, and confine a small Spanish-Indigenous garrison to two buildings.

The failure to eliminate that enclave lets [[HernandoPizarro]] and [[GonzaloPizarro]] retake Sacsayhuaman. Spanish armor, steel, cavalry, Indigenous allies, African firefighting labor, and seaborne reinforcement then combine with the seasonal dispersal of Manco's agrarian army. By late 1536 neither side can force a decision; Almagro's return changes the conflict from siege to a three-way contest and ultimately pushes Manco toward Vilcabamba.

## Key Claims

- The siege was a planned imperial mobilization enabled by communications, food storage, weapons preparation, and surviving political authority.
- Inca forces penetrated Cusco and used fire effectively, bringing the Spanish position close to collapse.
- Retaking Sacsayhuaman preserved the defenders' foothold while imposing mass death and damaging Inca morale.
- Spanish survival depended on a mixed coalition and logistical system, not only cavalry or steel.
- Inca success varied by terrain: mountain ambushes devastated relief columns, while open ground near Lima favored cavalry.
- Seasonal labor demands and family obligations weakened the ability to sustain a large agrarian army indefinitely.
- Almagro's return ended the binary siege without resolving the larger conquest.

## Evidence

- Mobilization and assault: [[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] describes Lares, the May attack, urban penetration, and incendiary sling stones.
- Defensive survival: [[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] places Spaniards, Indigenous auxiliaries, and African slaves inside the defense.
- Sacsayhuaman: [[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] follows Juan Pizarro's death, Titucusi Gualpa's resistance, and the fortress's capture.
- Strategic limits: [[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] connects seasonal dispersal, Quiso Yupanqui's contrasting campaigns, and Spanish reinforcement to stalemate.
- Three-way ending: [[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] follows Almagro's failed negotiation with Manco and occupation of Cusco.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode relies substantially on Spanish and later chroniclers, so force totals, casualties, speeches, miracles, timing, and motives remain source-scoped. Calling the campaign a siege compresses urban assault, fortress fighting, relief-column ambushes, the Lima offensive, foraging, and factional negotiation into one frame. Manco's failure does not show that revolt was hopeless from the outset, while his initial advantage does not prove victory was inevitable.

## What Changed

- Added the missing military bridge between Manco's humiliation and the Vilcabamba resistance.
- Reframed Spanish survival as a coalition-logistics outcome under changing terrain and time.
- Identified Almagro's return, rather than a clean battlefield victory, as the siege's political termination.
