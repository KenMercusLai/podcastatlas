## Definition

Behavior-focused police reform treats observable conduct, institutional routines, situational risk, and measurable outcomes as primary targets for reducing racial disparities and force, without making proof of an individual officer's inner prejudice a prerequisite for action.

## Current Synthesis

[[PhilipAtibaGoff]]'s framework separates moral diagnosis from operational reform. Arguments about whether an officer is racist, good, or evil may matter to responsibility, but they are difficult to measure consistently and can trap public debate at the final moment of a tragedy. Behavior-focused reform instead asks what preceding choices, policies, resources, and environments made the harmful encounter more likely.

The framework joins measurement to redesign. The [[CenterForPolicingEquity]] uses force and disparity data to identify high-risk practices, while Las Vegas foot-pursuit training illustrates changing officer behavior inside an encounter. [[PoliceContactReduction]] goes further by asking whether the encounter or armed responder was necessary. The approach therefore spans accountability, training, process redesign, and investment in non-police capacity.

## Key Claims

- Racially disparate outcomes can be measured and addressed without proving private animus in every case.
- Final-moment judgments often conceal earlier institutional choices that created avoidable danger.
- Performance management should define desired and prohibited outcomes, track them, and assign responsibility for improvement.
- Situational risk factors can be redesigned through policy, training, supervision, staffing, or alternative response.
- Individual accountability and structural reform are compatible rather than mutually exclusive.
- Reform should be judged by harm reduction, not by whether it establishes moral purity or eliminates every tragedy.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Behavioral metrics can be incomplete, manipulated, or detached from lived harm. Aggregate disparity does not adjudicate every encounter, and before-and-after changes do not establish causality without a credible design. Moving away from motive analysis should not erase intent where it is legally or morally relevant, excuse individual misconduct, or narrow racism to only what an agency chooses to count. Community governance, transparent definitions, and rights-based constraints remain necessary.
