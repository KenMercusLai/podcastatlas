# Revolutionary Justice-Mercy Conflict

## Definition

Revolutionary justice-mercy conflict is the legitimacy struggle over whether a defeated ruler may receive ordinary legal protection, popular appeal, delay, reprieve, or compassion when revolutionaries treat his continued life as a political threat.

## Current Synthesis

Louis's trial separated guilt from punishment without making either question politically neutral. The defence appealed to French humanity, the [[Girondins]] sought a referendum and reprieve, and the Convention considered several non-capital penalties; opponents treated delay and mercy as concealed royalism because the king embodied an order the Republic meant to destroy.

## Key Claims

- Agreement on guilt does not settle punishment, procedure, or the political meaning of mercy.
- A referendum can be framed either as democratic legitimation or as evasion of representative responsibility.
- Mercy becomes suspect when a defeated ruler remains a usable symbol for counterrevolution.
- Public roll-call voting exposes individual representatives to demands for visible loyalty.
- Street petitioning and revelations of prior correspondence can narrow the space for procedural moderation.
- Revolutionary legitimacy is weakened if every non-maximal penalty is automatically reclassified as treason.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The source does not establish that mercy was costless or that all calls for procedural delay had identical motives.
- Girondin opposition to immediate execution did not make the faction monarchist.
- The episode's moral framing does not substitute for a complete legal analysis of constitutional inviolability or jurisdiction.

## What Changed

- A canonical concept now separates disagreement over punishment and ratification from disagreement over guilt.
