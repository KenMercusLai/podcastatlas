## Definition

边疆册封信用权衡 / frontier recognition credibility tradeoff is the statecraft problem where recognizing, visiting, installing, or visibly dignifying one frontier actor may stabilize or flatter that actor while damaging earlier commitments, allied-group trust, neighboring frontier relations, ritual hierarchy, and public finances.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 738 now supplies the earlier and lighter-contact version of the problem. [[ZhengZhongFrontierEasternHan|Zheng Zhong]] argues that [[EmperorMingOfHan|Mingdi]] should not send a return mission to [[NorthernXiongnuLateHan|Northern Xiongnu]] while the chanyu is still raiding Han borders. His worry is not only safety. A Han envoy would let the chanyu advertise reconciliation, reinforce Western Regions loyalty to himself, unsettle [[SouthernXiongnu|Southern Xiongnu]], and affect [[Wuhuan|Wuhuan]] alignment. The credibility damage appears before formal title recognition: envoy presence and ritual posture can already function as a recognition signal.

Hanji 765 defines the fuller title-recognition version through [[YuanAnEasternHan|Yuan An]] and [[RenKuiEasternHan|Ren Kui]] opposing [[DouXianEasternHan|Dou Xian]]'s proposal to recognize [[YuChuNorthernXiongnu|Yu Chu / 于除]] as a new Northern Xiongnu chanyu. Dou treats recognition as an extension of the Southern Xiongnu precedent; Yuan treats it as a breach of the older Southern Xiongnu commitment structure.

The core tradeoff is cumulative. A contact or title package may create a visible diplomatic win, but it can also tell long-serving partners that their loyalty is expendable, give the rival prestige among dependent states, anger neighboring groups with their own conflict history, and add provisioning or supervision costs. The current concept therefore covers both Zheng's envoy-refusal warning and Yuan's later title-recognition objection.

## Key Claims

- Frontier recognition must be judged against existing commitments, not only the newest claimant's or rival's request.
- Envoy exchange can operate as recognition even when no formal title is granted.
- A title or visit granted to one frontier actor can damage trust with older allies who supplied prior military cooperation.
- Neighboring frontier groups matter because visible recognition can reopen grievances or make them doubt Han's alignment.
- Fiscal and supervisory capacity become credibility issues when recognition implies long-term support.
- Powerful court patrons or ruler misreadings can push risky recognition signals through despite developed objections.
- Later defection, rebellion, or hostile prestige gain can vindicate the original credibility warnings.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not reject all frontier recognition, visits, or title packages. Other pages preserve cases where recognition stabilizes submission or restores local order. The key condition here is credibility damage: the new signal must threaten older allies, neighboring groups, existing commitments, fiscal capacity, or ritual hierarchy. Hanji 738 is also a warning case rather than a completed policy failure; Mingdi stops only after imprisoning and later rehabilitating Zheng, so the source grounds risk diagnosis more than outcome measurement.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 738 as the earlier envoy-contact and ritual-recognition variant before Hanji 765's formal Yu Chu title-recognition dispute.
- Broadened the concept from formal title grants to visible diplomatic acts that a frontier rival can use as recognition.
- Preserved Hanji 765's Southern Xiongnu, Wuhuan-Xianbei, fiscal, and rebellion evidence as the fuller failure case.
