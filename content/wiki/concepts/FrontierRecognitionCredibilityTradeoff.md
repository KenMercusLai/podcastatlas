## Definition

边疆册封信用权衡 / frontier recognition credibility tradeoff is the statecraft problem where rewarding, answering, recognizing, visiting, installing, or visibly dignifying one frontier actor may stabilize or flatter that actor while damaging earlier commitments, allied-group trust, neighboring frontier relations, ritual hierarchy, and public finances.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 719 now supplies the earliest Western Regions version of the problem. [[ShacheKingdomLateHan|Shache]] king Xian asks [[LiuXiu|刘秀]] for restored Western Regions authority and initially receives a protectorate-level seal, vehicle, flags, gold, and silk. [[PeiZunEasternHan|Pei Zun]] objects that such recognition gives too much authority to a frontier ruler and will disappoint other states. Liu Xiu accepts the objection and downgrades Xian to Great General, but Xian resents Han and continues to posture as regional protector. This case shows that both overrecognition and reversal carry credibility costs.

Hanji 727 supplies the earliest hard-refusal Xiongnu version. [[NorthernXiongnuLateHan|Northern Xiongnu]] seeks heqin at Wuwei while [[SouthernXiongnu|Southern Xiongnu]] submission is newly dependent on Han protection. The crown prince argues that if Han does not cut off the northern relationship, Southern Xiongnu will doubt Han commitment and potential defectors will hesitate. Liu Xiu accepts the advice and refuses to receive the northern envoys.

Hanji 729 supplies the next soft-contact version. Northern Xiongnu sends tribute, renews heqin language, asks for music, and proposes bringing Western Regions envoys. [[BanBiaoEasternHan|Ban Biao]] argues that the repeated and costly approach signals fear after Southern Xiongnu submission, so he advises Liu Xiu to avoid rupture but answer with matched rewards, careful praise, refusal of instruments, and wording that avoids fully siding with either Xiongnu branch.

Hanji 737 supplies the later lighter-contact version. [[EmperorMingOfHan|Mingdi]] accepts trade with Northern Xiongnu while the northern branch is still raiding, hoping commerce may reduce violence. The contact is not neutral: [[ZhengZhongFrontierEasternHan|Zheng Zhong]]'s mission faces ritual coercion, and [[XuguDuhouSouthernXiongnu|Xugu Duhou]] resents Han-Northern contact enough to seek northern help. [[DuliaoCampEasternHan|Duliaoying]] is created to contain that Southern/Northern collusion risk.

Hanji 738 supplies the later return-mission version. Zheng argues that Mingdi should not send a return mission to Northern Xiongnu while the chanyu is still raiding Han borders. His worry is not only safety. A Han envoy would let the chanyu advertise reconciliation, reinforce Western Regions loyalty to himself, unsettle Southern Xiongnu, and affect [[Wuhuan|Wuhuan]] alignment. The credibility damage appears before formal title recognition: envoy presence and ritual posture can already function as a recognition signal.

Hanji 765 defines the fuller title-recognition version through [[YuanAnEasternHan|Yuan An]] and [[RenKuiEasternHan|Ren Kui]] opposing [[DouXianEasternHan|Dou Xian]]'s proposal to recognize [[YuChuNorthernXiongnu|Yu Chu / 于除]] as a new Northern Xiongnu chanyu. Dou treats recognition as an extension of the Southern Xiongnu precedent; Yuan treats it as a breach of the older Southern Xiongnu commitment structure.

The core tradeoff is cumulative. A contact or title package may create a visible diplomatic win, but it can also tell long-serving partners that their loyalty is expendable, give the rival prestige among dependent states, anger neighboring groups with their own conflict history, and add provisioning or supervision costs. The current concept therefore covers Pei Zun's Shache objection, Zheng's envoy-refusal warning, and Yuan's later title-recognition objection.

## Key Claims

- Frontier recognition or refusal must be judged against existing commitments and the motives behind the newest approach, not only the newest claimant's or rival's request.
- Rewards, reply wording, envoy exchange, trade contact, seal packages, and ritual posture can operate as recognition even when no final protectorate or chanyu settlement is durable.
- A title or visit granted to one frontier actor can damage trust with older allies or neighboring states who read the signal as changed alignment.
- Reversing an overbroad recognition can limit unsupported obligations while still creating resentment in the actor being downgraded.
- Fiscal and supervisory capacity become credibility issues when recognition implies long-term support.
- Powerful court patrons or ruler misreadings can push risky recognition signals through despite developed objections.
- Later defection, rebellion, hostile prestige gain, or alliance distrust can vindicate the original credibility warnings.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not reject all frontier recognition, visits, trade, or title packages. Other pages preserve cases where recognition stabilizes submission or restores local order. The key condition here is credibility damage: the new signal must threaten older allies, neighboring groups, existing commitments, fiscal capacity, or ritual hierarchy. Hanji 719 is also a mixed case because Liu Xiu corrects the overrecognition but still produces Shache resentment. Hanji 727 and Hanji 729 strengthen the timing qualification because the correct signal changes by context: Liu Xiu cuts off Northern Xiongnu envoys in 51 CE when Southern Xiongnu confidence is newly fragile, then accepts Ban Biao's 52 CE advice to manage contact through reward and wording while withholding stronger recognition signals. Hanji 738 is mainly a warning case rather than a completed policy failure, so it grounds risk diagnosis more than outcome measurement.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 719 as the earliest current Western Regions variant: Shache overrecognition, Pei Zun's warning, title downgrade, and resentment after reversal.
- Broadened the concept from Xiongnu-centered contact and title disputes to include Western Regions seal and protectorate-level authority signals.
- Preserved Hanji 727 as the hard-refusal variant protecting Southern Xiongnu confidence and defector signaling.
- Preserved Hanji 729, Hanji 737, and Hanji 738 as soft-contact, trade-contact, and envoy-contact variants.
- Preserved Hanji 765's Southern Xiongnu, Wuhuan-Xianbei, fiscal, and rebellion evidence as the fuller failure case.
