## Definition

A citizens' assembly is a temporary public body that uses random selection, often adjusted for demographic composition, to bring ordinary residents into structured learning, deliberation, and recommendation on a defined policy question.

## Current Synthesis

The Connecticut case suggests that citizens' assemblies can broaden representation beyond election candidates and convert diverse residents into capable policy deliberators. Their value depends on more than sortition: composition, compensation, expert instruction, facilitation, self-governed speaking rules, sufficient time, and a credible path from recommendation to response all matter. Advisory status remains the central weakness because elected institutions retain publication, agenda-setting, and implementation power.

## Key Claims

- Random selection can diversify political participation beyond the traits and incentives associated with electoral candidacy.
- Demographic composition can improve descriptive breadth but necessarily introduces design choices into the lottery.
- Policy competence can be developed through structured learning rather than assumed to exist only among professionals.
- Procedural rules affect whether quieter participants can contribute and whether disagreement remains workable.
- Repeated deliberation can produce civic relationships as well as policy recommendations.
- Advisory assemblies need explicit response and implementation mechanisms if they are to influence binding policy.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- A random sample of willing respondents is not identical to a random sample of the full public; invitation response and availability can still select participants.
- Demographic balancing improves some forms of representation without reproducing every affected interest or viewpoint.
- One completed recommendation process does not show that proposals are substantively superior or politically implementable.
- Friendship, learning, and procedural satisfaction do not substitute for transparent drafting, stable promises, or formal authority.
- International precedents named in the source are examples, not comparative outcome evidence.
