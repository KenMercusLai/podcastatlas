# Portuguese Crusading-Commercial Expansion

## Definition

Portuguese crusading-commercial expansion is the source's framework for treating spice profit, royal prestige, Christian mission, and anti-Muslim warfare as mutually reinforcing motives in Portugal's African and Asian expansion.

## Current Synthesis

The episode rejects a clean division between an economic “Age of Discovery” and an older religious age. The fall of Constantinople, the Iberian Reconquista, warfare in North Africa, the search for Christian allies, the spice trade, and competition with Muslim and Venetian commercial networks formed one strategic imagination.

That fusion helps explain why navigation, diplomacy, and trade repeatedly coexist with coercion. [[ManuelIOfPortugal|Manuel I's]] universal titles and papal hopes, [[VascoDaGama|Vasco da Gama's]] attacks on pilgrims and fishermen, [[AfonsoDeAlbuquerque|Albuquerque's]] reported killings in Goa, and [[SebastianOfPortugal|Sebastian's]] Moroccan campaign appear as different expressions of the same unstable formation.

## Key Claims

- Religious war and commercial ambition were integrated rather than competing explanations.
- The search for Prester John made Christian alliance-seeking part of geographic exploration.
- Muslim merchants were commercial rivals and confessional enemies in the source's Portuguese frame.
- Royal titles and papal language converted route success into claims of providential global authority.
- Violence against pilgrims, fishermen, urban populations, and coastal communities was structural to expansion as narrated.
- Sebastian's defeat shows the same crusading energy becoming strategically ruinous.

## Evidence

- Motive formation: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] links spices, Prester John, Constantinople, Christian militancy, and rivalry with Muslim and Venetian trade.
- Royal program: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] connects Manuel's titles and papal correspondence to commerce, conquest, and hopes of Christian expansion.
- Coercive practice: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] describes violence by da Gama and Albuquerque against pilgrims, fishermen, Muslims, and conquered cities.
- Late reversal: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] presents Sebastian's Moroccan campaign as a destructive return of crusading ambition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept summarizes one narrative source and should not reduce every voyage, commander, merchant, missionary, or policy choice to a single motive. Local alliances, practical bargaining, internal political differences, and African and Asian agency are underdeveloped. Reported intentions, casualty figures, and religious declarations remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Established a framework that keeps commerce, religion, monarchy, and organized violence in the same causal account.
