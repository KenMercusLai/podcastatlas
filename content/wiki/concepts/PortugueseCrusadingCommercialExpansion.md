# Portuguese Crusading-Commercial Expansion

## Definition

Portuguese crusading-commercial expansion is the source's framework for treating spice profit, royal prestige, Christian mission, and anti-Muslim warfare as mutually reinforcing motives in Portugal's African and Asian expansion.

## Current Synthesis

The sources reject a clean division between an economic “Age of Discovery” and an older religious age. Before the Cape route, [[HenryTheNavigator|Henry the Navigator's]] project already joined Ceuta and Tangier warfare, the search for Prester John, spices, land, African routes, and papal permission to conquer and enslave non-Christians. The fall of Constantinople, the Iberian Reconquista, warfare in North Africa, and competition with Muslim and Venetian commercial networks then reinforced the same strategic imagination.

That fusion helps explain why navigation, diplomacy, and trade repeatedly coexist with coercion. Early captive raiding and [[EarlyPortugueseAtlanticPlantationSlavery]] precede [[ManuelIOfPortugal|Manuel I's]] universal titles, [[VascoDaGama|Vasco da Gama's]] attacks on pilgrims and fishermen, [[AfonsoDeAlbuquerque|Albuquerque's]] reported killings in Goa, and [[SebastianOfPortugal|Sebastian's]] Moroccan campaign. They appear as different expressions of the same unstable formation rather than a late corruption of neutral exploration.

## Key Claims

- Religious war and commercial ambition were integrated rather than competing explanations.
- The search for Prester John made Christian alliance-seeking part of geographic exploration.
- Papal conquest and enslavement language gave religious authorization to commercial and territorial violence.
- Muslim merchants were commercial rivals and confessional enemies in the source's Portuguese frame.
- Royal titles and papal language converted route success into claims of providential global authority.
- Violence against pilgrims, fishermen, urban populations, and coastal communities was structural to expansion as narrated.
- Sebastian's defeat shows the same crusading energy becoming strategically ruinous.

## Evidence

- Motive formation: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] links spices, Prester John, Constantinople, Christian militancy, and rivalry with Muslim and Venetian trade.
- Royal program: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] connects Manuel's titles and papal correspondence to commerce, conquest, and hopes of Christian expansion.
- Coercive practice: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] describes violence by da Gama and Albuquerque against pilgrims, fishermen, Muslims, and conquered cities.
- Late reversal: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] presents Sebastian's Moroccan campaign as a destructive return of crusading ambition.
- Early motive evidence: [[227-portugal-on-the-edge-of-the-world-part-1-glt1834337589]] joins Henry's wealth and route aims to Prester John and militant Christian purpose.
- Authorization and slavery evidence: [[227-portugal-on-the-edge-of-the-world-part-1-glt1834337589]] connects papal permission, African captive raiding, sales, and plantation labor to the expansion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept summarizes two related narrative sources and should not reduce every voyage, commander, merchant, missionary, or policy choice to a single motive. Local alliances, practical bargaining, internal political differences, enslaved people's lives, and African and Asian agency are underdeveloped. Reported intentions, papal meaning, captive and casualty figures, and religious declarations remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Extended the framework backward from the Cape route to Henry, papal authorization, captive trading, and island plantations.
