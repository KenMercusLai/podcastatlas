# Commitment–Deliberation Rubicon

## Definition
The commitment–deliberation Rubicon is the psychological shift from evaluating whether to pursue a goal toward planning, defending, and implementing the chosen course.

## Current Synthesis
[[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-db5f9d45fb650a533486e6eb38780ebf-49695184-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-db5f9d45fb650a533486e6eb38780ebf-49695184|Wellness 2.0: Who Do You Want To Be?]] presents commitment as a useful change in cognitive mode. Once a decision is made, less attention goes to comparing alternatives and more goes to routes, reasons, and protections that help the goal survive doubt. Without that narrowing, repeated reconsideration can prevent action.

The same mechanism can preserve a poor choice. Goal-supporting attention may reinterpret discomfort as a temporary obstacle and discount evidence that the destination itself is wrong. The practical boundary is not permanent indecision but planned permeability: strong enough commitment to act, paired with review points at which dissonance, changed circumstances, or sustained value mismatch can reopen deliberation.

## Key Claims
- Deliberation and implementation are different cognitive tasks.
- Commitment narrows attention toward planning, goal protection, and supportive reasons.
- This narrowing can reduce paralyzing doubt and improve coordinated action.
- The same process can filter out evidence that the goal is no longer fitting or viable.
- Discomfort is ambiguous: it can signal ordinary difficulty or a reason to reconsider the destination.
- Review points can preserve revision without forcing every difficult moment back into open-ended choice.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The supplied source does not provide the underlying studies or establish a universal threshold for commitment, dissonance, or review. Frequent reconsideration can become avoidance, while rigid persistence can become escalation of commitment. Dissonance may reflect fear, learning difficulty, social threat, value conflict, or a genuinely bad decision; interpretation requires context, external evidence, and attention to safety and opportunity cost.

## What Changed
- Established commitment as a useful implementation mode with a built-in revision risk.
- Added planned review as a bridge between chronic doubt and rigid goal protection.
