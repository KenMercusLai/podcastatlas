## Definition

传财有度 / Wealth Transmission Restraint is the family-governance idea that leaving descendants enough to live and work may be safer than leaving excessive assets that blunt capable children, empower foolish ones, and attract resentment.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 431 creates the concept through [[ShuGuangShuShouWesternHan|疏广与疏受]]. After retirement, Shu Guang and Shu Shou spend imperial gifts on daily feasts with neighbors, kin, old friends, and guests. When advised to buy more property or store money for descendants, Shu Guang refuses. His argument is practical rather than anti-family: existing land and houses are enough if descendants work; if descendants are worthy, too much wealth weakens their will, and if they are foolish, too much wealth helps them do wrong.

The episode then links this to a Sima Guang family-teaching line: gold may not be kept and books may not be read, so hidden virtue is presented as the more reliable long-term provision. The page keeps that as source-scoped moral reasoning while preserving the more general concept: inheritance is not only asset transfer, but risk design for character, envy, and household durability.

## Key Claims

- More inheritance is not always better for descendants.
- Existing livelihood assets may be sufficient when paired with work capacity.
- Excess wealth can weaken worthy descendants by reducing striving.
- Excess wealth can empower foolish descendants by funding worse choices.
- Visible wealth can attract resentment and make a family more exposed.
- Moral or social capital may be treated as a competing form of long-term provision.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This is not a general argument against saving, education, property, or intergenerational help. The source's claim is moral and contextual: the problem is excessive, visible, unearned wealth that changes incentives and exposure. Modern application would depend on family needs, social safety nets, disability, debt, and legal context not discussed in the episode.
