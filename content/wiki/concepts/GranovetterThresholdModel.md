# Granovetter Threshold Model

## Definition

The Granovetter threshold model explains participation by the number of other people who must already perform an action before a particular person will join, separating private belief about the action from socially contingent willingness to perform it.

## Current Synthesis

[[the-big-man-can-t-shoot-the-big-man-can-t-shoot|The Big Man Can’t Shoot]] introduces the model through collective behavior and then applies it to adoption of an unconventional basketball technique. A low-threshold person may act with little support, while a high-threshold person can know that an action works and still wait for broad peer acceptance. Because people have different thresholds, early actors can change the social conditions faced by later ones.

The [[RickBarry]]–[[WiltChamberlain]] comparison makes the belief–action distinction concrete. Barry accepts ridicule after performance validates the underhand shot; Chamberlain abandons it despite direct success because the technique remains stigmatized. NFL draft and fourth-down examples broaden the analogy to institutions, although organizational incentives, authority, implementation costs, and career risk cannot be reduced to individual thresholds.

## Key Claims

- A threshold is the amount of visible participation required before an actor joins an action.
- Private approval or evidence of effectiveness does not by itself predict public adoption.
- Heterogeneous thresholds make collective behavior dependent on sequence and visible precedent.
- Low thresholds can enable useful innovation but do not guarantee good judgment or prosocial conduct.
- High thresholds can preserve convention even when individuals privately recognize a better option.
- Organizational resistance may resemble threshold behavior while also reflecting incentives, power, skills, and coordination.

## Evidence

### Belief–action distinction

- [[the-big-man-can-t-shoot-the-big-man-can-t-shoot]] reports Granovetter’s riot example and explicitly distinguishes what people believe from how many peers they need before acting.

### Athlete contrast

- [[the-big-man-can-t-shoot-the-big-man-can-t-shoot]] contrasts Barry’s willingness to withstand ridicule with Chamberlain’s abandonment of a method that had improved his results.

### Institutional analogy

- [[the-big-man-can-t-shoot-the-big-man-can-t-shoot]] connects threshold-like conformity to NFL teams’ resistance to trading down in the draft and attempting more fourth-down conversions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode does not measure any actor’s threshold or test whether peer participation caused the reported choices. Perceived masculinity, motor habit, coaching, incentives, career risk, accountability, uncertainty, and local implementation constraints can produce similar behavior. Low thresholds can support error, recklessness, or antisocial cascades as readily as beneficial innovation, while waiting for others can sometimes aggregate legitimate information.

The model should not be confused with [[MovementParticipationThreshold]], which summarizes a population-level association between mobilization share and campaign success. Granovetter’s threshold is actor-specific and concerns the condition for joining.

## What Changed

- Established the actor-level distinction between private belief and socially contingent adoption.
- Added athlete and institutional examples while preserving their status as analogies rather than measurements.
- Separated low-threshold innovation from a general claim of superior judgment or character.
