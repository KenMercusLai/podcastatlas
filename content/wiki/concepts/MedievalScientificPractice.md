# Medieval Scientific Practice

## Definition

Medieval scientific practice is the source's joined field of textual study, mathematical calculation, observation, instrument construction, skilled tinkering, and argument about nature before science became a distinct modern profession.

## Current Synthesis

Astronomy was especially precise because celestial position could be measured, represented geometrically, and calculated. Astrolabes, equatoria, and clocks turned inherited models into usable devices, while manuscripts carried instructions that practitioners copied, corrected, glossed, translated, and adapted.

These devices served mixed purposes. They could tell time, regulate monastic life, locate direction, estimate height, calculate planetary positions, support astrology, and model the cosmos. Their practical value and theological meaning were complementary within the source's medieval setting.

## Key Claims

- Knowledge work joined books, hands, materials, measurement, and calculation.
- Astronomy provided an early domain of mathematical precision without being separated cleanly from astrology.
- Astrolabes represented the heavens while supporting time, direction, height, and stellar calculations.
- Equatoria mechanically transformed inputs into calculated planetary positions and can be described functionally as computers.
- Mechanical clocks grew from practical timekeeping needs but could also reproduce a clockwork cosmos.
- Incremental tinkering and correction mattered alongside famous theories and authors.

## Evidence

- **Text and construction:** [[112-medieval-science-glt3839934134]] presents John of Westwick as copyist, translator, commentator, and equatorium designer.
- **Multipurpose instruments:** [[112-medieval-science-glt3839934134]] describes astrolabe functions and the equatorium's dials, strings, and celestial-longitude output.
- **Practical and cosmological clocks:** [[112-medieval-science-glt3839934134]] connects monastic schedules to timekeeping while noting clockmakers' more elaborate astronomical ambitions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Calling an equatorium a “computer” is a functional analogy, not a claim of electronic or general-purpose computation. Early written clock references do not always identify the mechanism clearly, and most early machines do not survive. Astrology's medieval seriousness does not establish its validity under modern evidentiary standards.

## What Changed

- Established a practice-centered account of medieval scientific work.
- Joined textual transmission to instrument construction and computation.
- Distinguished historical importance from modern validation of every medieval belief.
