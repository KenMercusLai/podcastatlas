# AI Industrial Capture

## Definition
AI industrial capture is the concentration of consequential AI decisions inside a small network of leading companies and government actors, with limited legislative, public, or independent technical scrutiny.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources treat capture as a structural allocation-of-authority and information problem rather than proof of bad intent by one company. Frontier labs possess models, expertise, infrastructure, and large volumes of user-contributed material; governments possess procurement, security, and coercive authority. When firms filter information or negotiate deployment boundaries privately with officials, practical governance can emerge before democratic institutions have established who may decide, what values shape outputs, what evidence outsiders may inspect, or how affected publics can challenge the result.

## Key Claims
- Concentrated capability can turn a small number of executives and officials into de facto AI governors.
- Public-private cooperation can be necessary while still producing an accountability deficit.
- Defense procurement is a high-salience capture risk because commercial negotiations can settle warfare boundaries before public law does.
- User-contributed documents, questions, ideas, and decisions can deepen informational dependence on a small number of providers.
- Presentation and ranking choices can exercise political influence even without a formal government mandate.
- Independent evidence access, legislative scrutiny, disclosure, and public deliberation are complementary checks on capture.
- The remedy is institutional redesign, not assigning every governance failure to one firm or individual.

## Evidence
- Concentrated authority - [[ep-35-who-actually-controls-ai-the-governance-gap-explained]] says a small group of companies and CEOs make choices with civilizational implications while no international authority has effective control.
- Closed defense channel - [[ep-35-who-actually-controls-ai-the-governance-gap-explained]] uses negotiations involving [[Anthropic]] and U.S. defense officials to argue that military-AI boundaries were discussed without adequate public, congressional, or independent technical review.
- Structural diagnosis - [[ep-35-who-actually-controls-ai-the-governance-gap-explained]] explicitly frames the problem as institutional rather than as the failure of one company.
- Information concentration - [[defaultmp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-725b059f65d6ae95b04d1dbc8a6cf2dd-104571730-defaultmp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-725b059f65d6ae95b04d1dbc8a6cf2dd-104571730]] argues that a small group of AI companies can shape what information users receive while accumulating documents, questions, ideas, and decision context.
- Values and legitimacy - [[defaultmp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-725b059f65d6ae95b04d1dbc8a6cf2dd-104571730-defaultmp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-725b059f65d6ae95b04d1dbc8a6cf2dd-104571730]] argues that capability, efficiency, and profitability do not settle which ethical assumptions should be embedded in systems or who may legitimately choose them.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The supplied summaries do not document the underlying defense negotiations, provider data holdings, ranking systems, or every formal oversight channel. Governments may need confidential technical and national-security discussions, and frontier labs may hold expertise that public bodies lack. Users also choose to submit some information and may receive substantial value in return. The concept identifies legitimacy, dependency, privacy, and accountability risks; it does not establish corrupt intent, prove uniform company behavior, or show that every public-private AI decision is captured.

## What Changed
- Expanded the concept from closed public-private decision channels to provider control over user-contributed information and output presentation.
- Added embedded ethical assumptions and information dependence to the legitimacy test.
