## Definition
Power-system flexibility and market design is the joint technical and institutional capacity to move electricity across time and place, respond to variable renewable output, and reward resources for acting when the system needs them.

## Current Synthesis
[[nengyuan-zhuanxing-jinru-xiabanchang-dang-huanbao-yu-jingji-zengzhang-buzai-shi-erxuanyi-llpsxe4uv4diznyyxmdfgrzaais]] distinguishes owning flexible assets from operating a flexible system. Batteries, grids, demand response, software, virtual power plants, and modified coal units can all contribute, but only when dispatch, contracts, and price signals align commercial behavior with system conditions.

The source makes this the institutional core of the transition's “second half.” When renewable deployment begins to displace rather than merely supplement fossil generation, coordination and distribution become more important: storage must act at useful times and locations, coal's role must change without locking in inefficient operation, and consumers need reliable service and tolerable prices.

## Key Claims
- Storage capacity is not equivalent to delivered flexibility; charging and discharging must correspond to renewable surplus, scarcity, location, and grid need.
- Price signals, dispatch rules, contracts, and market access determine whether privately rational behavior supports system reliability.
- Coal units may temporarily supply flexibility after technical modification, but low-load efficiency, operating cost, compensation, and retirement incentives determine whether this is a bridge or a lock-in.
- Flexibility includes grids, demand response, virtual power plants, software, and responsive end use, not batteries alone.
- Stable and affordable electricity is an outcome constraint, so transition speed cannot be assessed only through renewable capacity additions.

## Evidence
- Storage-use distinction - [[nengyuan-zhuanxing-jinru-xiabanchang-dang-huanbao-yu-jingji-zengzhang-buzai-shi-erxuanyi-llpsxe4uv4diznyyxmdfgrzaais]] argues that China's large battery fleet still needs better timing, location, dispatch, and price signals to serve the grid.
- Transitional coal role - [[nengyuan-zhuanxing-jinru-xiabanchang-dang-huanbao-yu-jingji-zengzhang-buzai-shi-erxuanyi-llpsxe4uv4diznyyxmdfgrzaais]] describes modified coal generation as a possible lower-load support resource while noting the associated efficiency, cost, contract, and market-design problems.
- Multi-resource balancing - [[nengyuan-zhuanxing-jinru-xiabanchang-dang-huanbao-yu-jingji-zengzhang-buzai-shi-erxuanyi-llpsxe4uv4diznyyxmdfgrzaais]] joins batteries to transmission, software, virtual power plants, and demand-side response rather than treating storage construction as the complete solution.
- Evaluation boundary - [[nengyuan-zhuanxing-jinru-xiabanchang-dang-huanbao-yu-jingji-zengzhang-buzai-shi-erxuanyi-llpsxe4uv4diznyyxmdfgrzaais]] makes market health and price stability one of three transition indicators alongside renewable capacity and end-use electrification.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- The source gives a system framework rather than comparative evidence about which market design or regulatory structure performs best.
- Flexible coal can support variable renewables in the short run while preserving emissions, fixed costs, and political dependence that delay retirement.
- Stronger price variation may improve dispatch incentives while exposing households or small firms to volatility unless tariffs, hedging, and consumer protection are designed alongside it.
- AI may improve forecasting and dispatch, but it also raises electricity demand and does not resolve physical transmission, generation, or storage constraints by itself.

## What Changed
- Distinguished storage construction from storage utilization.
- Added contracts, dispatch, and prices as coequal components of technical flexibility.
- Preserved flexible coal as a qualified transitional option rather than a settled endpoint.
