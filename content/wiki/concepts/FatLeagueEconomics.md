# Fat League Economics

## Definition
Fat league economics names a sports structure in which a central commercial company retains substantial revenue-generating rights and enterprise value, rather than simply collecting money for distribution to participating teams. The term comes from the [[FormulaOneGroup]] case under [[LibertyMedia]]; it does not make every centralized sports organization the same kind of company.

## Current Synthesis
F1 is the clearest instance: coordinated [[RacePromotionFees|race promotion]], broadcasting, sponsorship and hospitality make the commercial-rights holder valuable in its own right, while team commitments and [[CostCapEconomics|cost controls]] help keep the [[FormulaOne]] product viable. This is a commercial-rights arrangement, not identical to the [[FIA]]'s sporting governance. The comparison cases separate *centralization* from *where value resides*. The [[NationalFootballLeague|NFL]] pools rights and governance but builds much of the resulting value into franchises. [[FIFA]] monetizes the [[FIFAWorldCup]] through rights and [[WorldCupExpansion|expanded match inventory]] and redistributes proceeds through a voting-based federation, not an F1-like public equity vehicle. Nor does a valuable league or tournament guarantee profitable clubs or a settled division of gains with players. Fatness is thus a question about the location of rights, residual income and bargaining power, not a synonym for a rich sport.

## Key Claims
- F1's centralized commercial-rights holder captures value from packaging races and media, while team alignment and cost caps support the joint product.
- NFL-wide rights and competitive governance can be highly centralized even when the main enterprise-value story concerns member franchises rather than separately traded league equity.
- FIFA's expanded World Cup illustrates central-event monetization joined to political redistribution, but its member-association governance differs from F1's corporate rights structure.
- Central commercial growth can diverge from club cash flows: opaque lower-tier control and contracts, or elite-club wages, debt and fan obligations, can consume or contest the apparent upside. Elite rights and scarcity may attract capital without making routine club operations profitable.
- Growing league revenue reopens the labor bargain; the [[WNBA]] players' revenue-sharing demand illustrates why fixed raises may not secure a proportionate share of future growth.

## Evidence
- **Central-rights capture:** [[formula-1-1]] describes Ecclestone's consolidation of team, promoter and broadcast arrangements, Liberty Media's later commercialization, and cost caps; its hosts call the retained league-company economics “fat league.”
- **Centralization without identical ownership:** [[the-nfl-1]] traces pooled television rights, shared revenues and parity rules, but presents franchise scarcity as the key valuation locus. Both it and the F1 account are episodes of *Acquired*, distinct cases rather than independent confirmations of a universal model.
- **Tournament governance:** [[shangye-xiaoyang-44-shijiebei-kuojun-yu-fifa-de-quanli-douzheng-993354969]] links additional World Cup match inventory, media, ticketing and hospitality to association votes and distributions; that political mechanism is not corporate league equity.
- **Club-level limits:** [[vol-262-qu-xibanya-mai-zuqiu-julebu-yichang-huangdan-de-shangye-maoxian-992614191]] recounts [[JumillaCF]]'s weak investor control and hard-to-realize player rights: local identity and player-development utility do not by themselves produce investable free cash flow. [[e243-te-lang-pu-huanxing-hongpai-zhiwai-meiguo-ziben-ruhe-yingkong-quanqiu-zutan]] contrasts [[PremierLeague]] club valuations and [[UEFA]] competition-rights opportunities with leverage, spending and supporter conflict. The *[[Soccernomics]]* discussion in [[22-zuqiu-jingjixue-duzhe-bubi-shi-qiumi-570874427]] adds soft-budget, community-asset reasons why some clubs persist despite thin profits. The Jumilla and FIFA discussions come from the same show, not separate statistical samples.
- **Labor's claim on upside:** [[diary-of-a-wnba-negotiator]] follows [[WNBAPlayersAssociation|player]] negotiators arguing for shared basketball revenue rather than fixed salary increases alone, using projected media-rights growth and a strike threat; the account is predominantly from the players' side.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The NFL is a counterexample to equating powerful central rights with a separately valuable league company; FIFA is a comparison in central capture and allocation, not a matching legal entity. Club anecdotes cannot establish that football as a whole is unprofitable, and high club valuations need not imply high operating profit. F1's own history includes promoter pressure and breakaway threats, so retaining revenue depends on continuing stakeholder consent; team distributions, rising valuations and cost caps may help sustain that bargain but do not guarantee it. World Cup budgets and WNBA agreement terms in the source notes are dated episode accounts, not current verified financial statements or a neutral assessment of both bargaining sides.

## What Changed
- The definition now anchors on F1's retained commercial-company value rather than on centralization alone.
- NFL and FIFA become structurally different comparison cases, separating franchise value and federation politics from F1 equity.
- Club-level losses and control problems limit the inference from tournament or league growth to investor returns.
- Player revenue sharing becomes a separate distributional test of whether central growth aligns stakeholders.
