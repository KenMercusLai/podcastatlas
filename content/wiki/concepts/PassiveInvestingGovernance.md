# Passive Investing Governance

## Definition
Passive investing governance is the market-wide control and coordination problem created when rule-based funds become major owners, voters, liquidity channels, and default household investment products. It extends [[PassiveInvesting]] beyond an individual's low-cost allocation choice to the effects of concentrated asset-manager power, benchmark rules, flows, and continuing dependence on active price discovery.

## Current Synthesis
[[vanguard-1]] establishes the ownership and voting side: [[Vanguard]], [[BlackRock]], and [[StateStreet]] can hold large stakes across public companies on behalf of dispersed fund investors, concentrating stewardship authority and raising common-ownership questions. [[xin-yidai-touzizhe-sihu-bu-ai-mai-gupiao-le-zhujian-bei-etf-zhudao-de-shichang-he-weilai-lk1lxwniotregvs9euhepkm9nruo]] adds the flow and attention side: benchmark inclusion may create stable demand, platforms and themes can direct investor attention, and active-fund redemptions may weaken the research capital that passive prices still rely on. Neither source treats passive investing as inherently broken. The current judgment is conditional: its private benefits remain strong while its market-wide governance burden grows with scale and with the weakness of countervailing active capital.

## Key Claims
- Passive funds depend on enough active investors still doing price discovery around them.
- Common ownership may create concerns when the same large asset managers hold major stakes across competing companies.
- Voting authority can concentrate in asset managers even when the economic ownership is dispersed among millions of fundholders.
- [[FundholderMutualOwnership]] improves Vanguard's customer alignment but does not eliminate market-wide governance questions.
- Benchmark inclusion, theme design, and distribution can direct attention and marginal demand even without an active stock-selection decision.
- Active-fund redemptions may increase the time needed for neglected fundamental value to attract enough capital for repricing.
- These are scale-dependent governance issues, not reasons to reject low-cost index investing outright.

## Evidence
- Ownership and voting concentration: [[vanguard-1|Vanguard]] identifies large index managers as cross-market owners whose voting and stewardship authority exceeds the apparent simplicity of the household product.
- Common ownership and price discovery: [[vanguard-1|The Vanguard source]] treats both as unresolved long-term questions while preserving the cost and diversification case for index funds.
- Flow and inclusion effects: [[xin-yidai-touzizhe-sihu-bu-ai-mai-gupiao-le-zhujian-bei-etf-zhudao-de-shichang-he-weilai-lk1lxwniotregvs9euhepkm9nruo|The 面基 episode]] argues that index membership supplies recurring demand and that firms outside major benchmarks can lose liquidity and attention.
- Active-capital boundary: [[xin-yidai-touzizhe-sihu-bu-ai-mai-gupiao-le-zhujian-bei-etf-zhudao-de-shichang-he-weilai-lk1lxwniotregvs9euhepkm9nruo|The same episode]] connects ETF inflows and active-fund outflows to a possible weakening of stock-level research and value realization.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not identify a passive-ownership threshold at which voting concentration, common ownership, or price discovery becomes harmful. Asset managers can build stewardship capacity, active investors can earn larger rewards when mispricing widens, and index changes still respond to market prices and published rules. ETF flows can improve liquidity and reduce household costs, while net creations may reflect transfers from other vehicles rather than wholly new capital. The 面基 episode's endpoint of nearly all trading concentrating in constituents is explicitly a thought experiment, and its AI-recommendation evidence is anecdotal.

## What Changed
- Expanded the concept from voting and common ownership to benchmark-flow and attention governance.
- Added active-fund redemptions as a possible constraint on the price discovery that passive funds require.
- Preserved low cost, diversification, and improved liquidity as counterweights to the governance risks.
