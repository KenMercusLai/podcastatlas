# California Affordability Governance Reset

## Definition
California affordability governance reset is [[SteveHilton|Steve Hilton]]'s All-In campaign frame that treats high living costs as the product of taxes, spending growth, regulation, special-interest capture, and weak execution rather than as isolated policy failures.

## Current Synthesis
The concept ties Hilton's tax, spending, regulation, housing, energy, transport, school, crime, and homelessness claims into one electoral argument. California's cost problem is presented as a governance-system problem: relief for working households requires lower direct costs plus changes to the political and administrative machinery that produces them.

The later interview broadens the April platform and raises a revision question. Hilton now proposes exempting the first $150,000 of income rather than $100,000, and connects it to a 10% state-workforce reduction, regulatory cuts, high-speed-rail cancellation, cheaper gasoline, a $73 registration cap, and ten new cities. The central tension remains implementation: the sources do not supply an updated fiscal model or show how a Republican governor would secure legislative and legal authority for the package.

## Key Claims
- The platform links affordability to governance structure, not only to one tax or program.
- The income-tax exemption threshold moved from $100,000 in April to $150,000 in September, indicating an expanded proposal without a revised cost estimate in the later source.
- Spending restraint is framed through audits, a return toward pre-pandemic levels, a 10% state-headcount cut, regulatory reduction, and project cancellation.
- Direct-cost relief now includes gasoline, vehicle registration, housing expansion, and the tax exemption.
- The concept treats California's working-class cost squeeze as a possible cross-party coalition issue.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are candidate interviews, so the fiscal savings, household impact, gasoline-price path, job figures, regulatory counts, and program-effectiveness claims remain source-scoped. The higher exemption threshold creates an unresolved fiscal revision. Neither source models legal constraints, ballot-measure pathways, official revenue effects, transition costs, or the probability that Democratic legislators would enact the package.

## What Changed
- Incorporated the revised $150,000 income-tax exemption while retaining the earlier $100,000 proposal as a documented change.
- Expanded the frame to state staffing, regulatory volume, high-speed rail, gasoline, vehicle registration, and new-city development.
- Sharpened the cross-party working-voter coalition theory and its implementation limits.
