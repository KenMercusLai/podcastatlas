# Territorial Slavery Expansion

## Definition

Territorial slavery expansion is the recurring U.S. conflict over whether slavery could enter western lands, converting continental growth into a crisis of sectional power and constitutional authority.

## Current Synthesis

The episode connects cotton geography to political geography. Rising cotton demand and new land made enslaved labor more profitable, while territory acquired after the Mexican-American War forced Congress and settlers to decide whether slavery would be legal where Mexico had prohibited it. The Missouri Compromise line temporarily bounded the question, but the Kansas-Nebraska Act replaced that settlement with popular sovereignty and produced armed migration and Bleeding Kansas.

[[DredScott|Dred Scott]] then deepened the crisis by treating enslaved people as constitutionally protected property in the territorial dispute. The issue was therefore not only local settlement preference: territorial rules affected the balance of sections and raised the fear that slavery could become national.

## Key Claims

- Cotton profitability made western land central to slavery's future.
- Mexican cession territory reopened a question that prior compromise had geographically managed.
- Popular sovereignty moved the conflict into settler competition and organized violence.
- Territorial status affected national sectional power, not merely local labor arrangements.
- Dred Scott suggested constitutional property protection could override geographic restriction.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not claim cotton could grow everywhere or that every western settler prioritized slavery. The episode notes arguments that geography would contain the institution, but judges that this underestimated the political importance of border and territorial status. Legal details, settlement motives, and the counterfactual durability of earlier compromises remain source-scoped.
