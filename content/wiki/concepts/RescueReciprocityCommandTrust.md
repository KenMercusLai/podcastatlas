## Definition

救援互惠式统帅信任 / rescue reciprocity command trust names the command expectation that leaders and subordinates will share danger enough to rescue one another when the position can still be saved. It is not sentimental loyalty; it is an operating condition for making dispersed forces believe that holding out, taking risk, and coordinating under pressure are rational.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 746 adds the positive frontier version before the Gongsun Zan failure cases. [[FanQiangEasternHan|Fan Qiang]] insists on rescuing [[GengGongEasternHan|Geng Gong]] because he had previously followed him, even though other commanders judge the route too dangerous. His arrival through deep snow turns twenty-six exhausted defenders from an abandoned holdout into a rescued force, though only thirteen survive to Yumen. [[ZhengZhongFrontierEasternHan|Zheng Zhong]] then memorializes their endurance, and Geng receives appointment after reaching Luoyang.

The later Yijing evidence shows the opposite pattern. [[GongsunZan|Gongsun Zan]] refuses to rescue isolated commanders because he wants them to rely on themselves, but that policy teaches the force that danger is individualized and that the center may not reciprocate. Hanji 1031 shows the terminal consequence: when outside help does approach, the command network depends on a secret fire signal rather than durable trust, [[YuanShao|Yuan Shao]] captures the message, and the failed sortie confirms that late rescue cannot easily repair earlier non-rescue.

The concept therefore now has both sides. Fan Qiang's action shows why rescue can make followership credible, while Gongsun Zan shows how repeated non-rescue makes even strong walls, family concentration, and outside relief brittle.

## Key Claims

- Rescue reciprocity is a form of command infrastructure: soldiers and subordinate officers fight differently when they believe risk will be shared.
- Positive rescue makes endurance rational by showing that isolated defenders are not merely expendable symbols.
- Recognition after rescue matters because memorials, care, and reward turn suffering into organizational memory.
- Refusing rescue can be tactically defensible once, but repeated non-rescue becomes a signal about what the center values.
- Gongsun Zan's Yijing policy converts self-reliance rhetoric into isolation, morale decay, and reluctance to believe in common fate.
- The final fire-signal plan shows that external relief still needs trustworthy channels, timing, and morale inside the fortress.
- Guan Jing's advice and death show the tragic edge of the concept: leadership movement can look like abandonment once trust is already damaged.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Not every rescue is wise, possible, or worth the loss of the whole force. Hanji 746 shows a successful arrival but also preserves the cost: half of Geng Gong's remaining defenders die before reaching Yumen, and the broader route, force, and siege details remain source-scoped. In Yijing, the issue is not a single missed rescue but a pattern that makes later coordination brittle.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 746 as the positive Fan Qiang and Geng Gong rescue case.
- Reframed the concept around rescue, recognition, and organizational memory as well as non-rescue failure.
- Preserved Hanji 1030-1031 as the negative Gongsun Zan/Yijing branch.
