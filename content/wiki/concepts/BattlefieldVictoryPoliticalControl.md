# Battlefield Victory and Political Control

## Definition

Battlefield victory and political control describes the gap between defeating or capturing an enemy in battle and building a settlement that can raise revenue, command allegiance, administer territory, and survive after the victorious army leaves.

## Current Synthesis

The first episode shows how the leverage is created. French noble alienation and shifting alliances let English armies penetrate from Normandy and Gascony, while the [[EdwardTheBlackPrince|Black Prince's]] destructive [[Chevauchee|chevauchée]] creates wealth and strategic shock without occupation. At [[BattleOfPoitiers|Poitiers]], a trapped English-Gascon army captures [[JohnIIOfFrance|John II]] after French command breaks down.

The later episode shows why that leverage is not control. Holding John, accumulating loot, and demanding ransom and territory create bargaining dominance, but France's political collapse removes the administrative capacity needed to fulfill those terms. [[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]] can raid to Paris yet cannot take Reims, secure coronation, capture the capital, or make conquest self-enforcing.

The [[TreatyOfBretigny|Treaty of Brétigny]] narrows ambition from the French crown to sovereign [[Aquitaine]], but sovereignty on paper still depends on taxation, local loyalty, security, and accepted appellate authority. The Black Prince's fiscal pressures and [[CharlesVOfFrance|Charles V's]] rebuilding reverse the settlement without a single equivalent French battlefield triumph.

## Key Claims

- Capturing a ruler can weaken the state needed to pay ransom and implement concessions.
- Destructive raiding can generate wealth and fear without stable territorial administration.
- Raiding and humiliation do not necessarily provide garrisoned, administered, or legitimate control.
- Treaty gains remain conditional on revenue, local allegiance, demobilization, and enforcement.
- Expensive occupation can turn subjects toward a rival claimant or appellate authority.
- A defeated state can recover by rebuilding capacity and refusing the victor's preferred battle form.

## Evidence

- Leverage creation: [[320-hundred-years-war-the-black-prince-part-3-glt6004693022]] links chevauchée, French factional instability, Poitiers, and royal capture.
- Captive-king paradox: [[321-hundred-years-war-a-storm-of-swords-part-4-glt1570526345]] links John's captivity to terms France cannot finance or enforce.
- Failed conquest: [[321-hundred-years-war-a-storm-of-swords-part-4-glt1570526345]] records Edward's failure at Reims and Paris despite raids and military prestige.
- Treaty fragility: [[321-hundred-years-war-a-storm-of-swords-part-4-glt1570526345]] shows enlarged sovereign Aquitaine destabilized by damage, taxation, companies, and appeals.
- Reversal without decisive battle: [[321-hundred-years-war-a-storm-of-swords-part-4-glt1570526345]] links French recovery to Charles V's state rebuilding and du Guesclin's attrition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept is grounded in two podcast episodes' compressed account of a medieval war phase. It does not show that battlefield victory is unimportant: Poitiers created real diplomatic leverage and Brétigny produced substantial gains. The narrower claim is that raids, capture, and victory were insufficient without durable institutions and allegiance.
