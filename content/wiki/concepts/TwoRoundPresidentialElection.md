# Two-Round Presidential Election

## Definition

A two-round presidential election is a system in which an initial multi-candidate vote selects finalists for a head-to-head runoff when no candidate wins outright.

## Current Synthesis

The French sources show that two-round systems do not automatically protect the center from an organized populist plurality. The first episode treats [[MarineLePen]]'s majority acceptability as the main uncertainty; the newer one adds [[CentristFragmentationRunoffRisk]], under which the center may fail to reach the runoff at all. The two stages therefore pose different coordination problems: first select a viable finalist, then assemble a majority coalition.

## Key Claims

- First-round plurality strength does not by itself prove majority acceptability.
- Candidate fragmentation can determine who reaches the runoff before transfer behavior matters.
- A stable organized base gains leverage when rivals cannot coordinate candidacies.
- Legal eligibility, party normalization, campaign style, and establishment identity affect both rounds differently.
- A runoff between populist-right and populist-left candidates can occur even when a broad center exists.

## Evidence

- Majority uncertainty: [[marine-warfare-le-pen-runs-for-president-6a4e1f2781fe1f6460b7117c]] separates Le Pen's first-round strength from second-round uncertainty.
- Fragmentation risk: [[national-front-runner-who-can-beat-le-pen-6ab3960493749e4c0ba24e60]] says at least 15 potential candidates could split the broad center.
- Runoff testing: [[national-front-runner-who-can-beat-le-pen-6ab3960493749e4c0ba24e60]] reports Le Pen winning every tested pairing in the cited polling, including 55%-45% against Philippe.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Early polling has failed before, candidates can withdraw or coordinate, and runoff transfer behavior may change during the campaign. The French case illustrates system dynamics but does not establish a universal outcome for all two-round elections.
