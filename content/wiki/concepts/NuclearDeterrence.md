# Nuclear Deterrence

## Definition

Nuclear deterrence is the attempt to prevent attack by making credible the prospect that nuclear aggression will bring unacceptable retaliation, including mutual national destruction.

## Current Synthesis

[[92-nuclear-weapons-glt2236644203]] presents the strongest case and the central qualification together. Fear of retaliation may have prevented deliberate nuclear launch and a conventional Third World War, including during [[AbleArcher83|Able Archer 83]], but deterrence did not eliminate danger. Its operation depended on leaders perceiving signals accurately, command systems functioning, forces remaining controlled, and crises not converting ambiguity into panic. Deterrence is therefore a conditional restraint system, not proof of safe or fully rational nuclear governance.

## Key Claims

- Credible retaliatory destruction can make initiating nuclear war appear suicidal.
- Deterrence may inhibit both nuclear attack and conventional conflict among nuclear powers.
- Retaliatory capability also requires readiness, which can shorten decision time during a false warning or misread exercise.
- Stable deterrence depends on communication, restraint, legible limits, technical reliability, and some degree of luck.
- More nuclear actors and flashpoints may multiply pathways for misunderstanding without making escalation inevitable.

## Evidence

### Restraint through unacceptable retaliation

- [[92-nuclear-weapons-glt2236644203]] argues that Soviet leaders did not launch during the 1983 scare because retaliation would have amounted to national suicide.

### Conditional and fragile stability

- [[92-nuclear-weapons-glt2236644203]] pairs deterrence with Able Archer misperception, aircraft accidents, damaged weapons, false alarms, and crisis panic.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The absence of nuclear war does not by itself prove deterrence caused peace; diplomacy, conventional conditions, institutional restraint, contingency, and luck may also matter. The source is a broad historical conversation rather than a systematic comparison of deterrence theories. Its multipolar-risk discussion is cautionary, not a prediction that a particular present-day dispute will cross the nuclear threshold.

## What Changed

- Established deterrence as a conditional restraint model whose claimed success must be evaluated alongside misperception and command-system fragility.
