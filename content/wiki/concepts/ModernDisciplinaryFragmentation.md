# Modern Disciplinary Fragmentation / 现代学科分割

## Definition

Modern disciplinary fragmentation is the division of older, overlapping bodies of learning into specialized university fields with separate methods, departments, curricula, and professional identities.

## Current Synthesis

The source treats fragmentation as a productive loss. Specialization enables deeper research in literature, history, philosophy, linguistics, philology, politics, and other fields, but it can make materials that once belonged to one learned practice appear to have only one disciplinary home. The *Book of Songs*, rites, histories, and classical commentaries remain simultaneously textual, political, historical, philosophical, performative, and linguistic.

The educational answer is not to abolish expertise or restore an undefined totality called “national learning.” It is to keep disciplinary competence while crossing boundaries when the object requires it. For humanities students, that can include language, history, philosophy, social science, science, and practical experience; the governing standard is intellectual integration rather than interdisciplinary branding.

## Key Claims

- Modern departments produce depth by narrowing objects, methods, and training pathways.
- Classical texts and lived problems often exceed the borders assigned by those departments.
- Chinese and English departments contain theory, criticism, linguistics, and research rather than only creative writing or translation.
- Cross-disciplinary study is most useful when it restores dimensions required by the object, not when breadth substitutes for competence.
- Appeals to an undivided “national learning” can express a real desire for wholeness while remaining institutionally and conceptually ambiguous.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode supplies an interpretive history and two graduates' experience, not a comparative survey of departments or proof that older education was more complete for all learners. Pre-modern learning also had exclusions, hierarchies, and specialist traditions. Integration can become superficial if it avoids methods, evidence standards, or the sustained practice that specialization provides.
