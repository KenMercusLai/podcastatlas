# Judeo-Christian National Identity

## Definition

Judeo-Christian national identity is the episode's name for a historically constructed American coalition that grouped Protestants, Catholics, and Jews inside a shared religious nation opposed first to Nazism and later to atheistic communism.

## Current Synthesis

[[153-god-and-the-american-empire-glt8752091834]] dates common American use of “Judeo-Christian” language to the late 1930s rather than treating it as an ancient inheritance. [[FranklinDRoosevelt|Franklin D. Roosevelt]] and wartime propaganda are presented as helping narrate Catholics, Protestants, and Jews as partners in one cause; the Cold War then shifted a major boundary from Protestant-versus-Catholic-or-Jew toward religious-versus-nonreligious.

The coalition widened national belonging but did not erase exclusion. Its label can describe a strategic and political construction without proving theological unity, equal membership, or inclusion of traditions outside the three named groups.

## Key Claims

- “Judeo-Christian” identity is historically produced rather than timeless.
- Anti-Nazi mobilization helped make interfaith unity part of American national storytelling.
- Cold War anti-communism strengthened a religious-versus-irreligious political boundary.
- Coalition language can widen membership while still defining outsiders and masking internal inequality.
- Domestic interfaith inclusion and foreign-policy moral framing can reinforce one another.

## Evidence

### Wartime construction

- [[153-god-and-the-american-empire-glt8752091834]] attributes late-1930s and Second World War prominence to Roosevelt-era language and propaganda representing Protestants, Catholics, and Jews as fighting together.

### Cold War consolidation

- [[153-god-and-the-american-empire-glt8752091834]] says the principal divide increasingly moved from conflict among faiths to religion against irreligion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The dating and causal account come from one short expert interview and are not independently established here. The synthesis does not imply that antisemitism, anti-Catholicism, denominational rivalry, racial exclusion, or non-Christian marginalization disappeared when the coalition language gained force.

## What Changed

- Created the concept as a contingent wartime and Cold War coalition rather than a timeless description of the United States.
