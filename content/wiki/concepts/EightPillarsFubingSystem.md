## Definition
The Eight Pillars–Fubing System is the episode's model of Western Wei high command: eight pillar generals connected to six effective field commands, twelve great generals, and twenty-four armies.

## Current Synthesis
The structure turns a famous roster into an organizational hierarchy. [[YuwenTai|Yuwen Tai]] stood above the whole, one Yuan imperial clansman carried mainly symbolic status, and six other pillars each led two great generals, whose commands divided again into two armies. This architecture helped integrate military elites but did not eliminate personal politics. Yuwen Tai could contain senior commanders; after his death, Yuwen Hu's weaker standing made the same pillars potential rivals and led to purges. [[LiHu|Li Hu]] also shows that later dynastic memory can distort the roster's internal rank.

## Key Claims
- “Pillar of state” changed from an exceptional title into a patterned high-command structure.
- Eight nominal pillars did not mean eight equivalent field commanders.
- Six effective pillars linked through twelve great generals to twenty-four armies.
- Hierarchy helped bind heterogeneous forces to the Western Wei political order.
- Formal organization remained dependent on personal authority and succession management.
- Later genealogical prestige can reorder remembered rank within the system.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source provides an explanatory summary rather than a full institutional chronology or scholarly debate over fubing origins. The neat hierarchy should not imply stable equivalence among offices, armies, and actual wartime commands across the whole period.

## What Changed
- Established the Eight Pillars as a layered command system with a personal-authority succession limit.
