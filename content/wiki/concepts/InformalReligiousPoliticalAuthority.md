## Definition

Informal religious-political authority is public influence exercised through preaching, charisma, moral reputation, followers, and access to decision-makers without holding the offices that formally make or execute state policy.

## Current Synthesis

The episode calls [[GirolamoSavonarola|Savonarola]] Florence's most powerful man in 1495 while emphasizing that he could not sit on the grand council because he was neither Florentine nor a lay politician. His sermons, apparent prophetic success, negotiation with [[CharlesVIIIOfFrance|Charles VIII]], Piagnoni supporters, and moral program shaped state action without making him a formal ruler.

This distinction clarifies reach and constraint. Civic authorities enacted harsher morality laws, but refused the organized boys' request for power to punish homosexuals and prostitutes. The episode finds no evidence that Savonarola personally controlled executions. Rival factions, the Signoria, religious orders, the pope, and changing crowds retained agency.

Informal authority can still legitimate constitutional change, define moral agendas, mobilize supporters, and make religious disobedience a civic crisis. Its dependence on belief, preaching access, coalition support, and performance also makes it vulnerable to bans, excommunication, economic failure, disappointed expectations, and spectacle-driven reversal.

## Key Claims

- Political importance does not require formal office.
- Preaching and moral reputation can influence legislation and constitutional settlement.
- Supporters and sympathetic officeholders transmit informal authority into state action.
- Independent institutions constrain the claim that charismatic influence equals dictatorship.
- Informal authority can be extensive while responsibility for coercive acts remains distributed.
- Dependence on reputation and access makes this authority vulnerable to public failure and exclusion.

## Evidence

### Influence without office

- [[575-the-medici-the-bonfire-of-the-vanities-part-4-glt2280694489]] contrasts Savonarola's lack of office with his role in negotiation, constitutional debate, preaching, and reform.

### Civic limits and distributed coercion

- [[575-the-medici-the-bonfire-of-the-vanities-part-4-glt2280694489]] reports the Signoria's refusal to empower the boys and the absence of evidence that Savonarola controlled executions.

### Reputation-dependent collapse

- [[575-the-medici-the-bonfire-of-the-vanities-part-4-glt2280694489]] links declining prophecy, papal restrictions, faction, material crisis, and the failed ordeal to lost influence.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The distinction must not minimize harm. Savonarola's campaigns helped legitimate coercive regulation, including severe anti-sodomy penalties, even if implementation remained distributed. Lack of office does not make influence innocent. The division of initiative among Savonarola, Piagnoni, magistrates, youth companies, and the public requires evidence beyond one episode.

## What Changed

- Created a framework separating broad influence from formal rule and direct administrative control.
- Made distributed responsibility and institutional constraint explicit without absolving moral agenda-setters.
