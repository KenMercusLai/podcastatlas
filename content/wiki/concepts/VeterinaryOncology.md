# Veterinary Oncology / 兽医肿瘤学

## Definition
Veterinary oncology is the diagnosis, staging, treatment, symptom management, and quality-of-life care of cancer in animals, represented here through canine and feline clinical practice.

## Current Synthesis
The current source frames veterinary oncology as a whole-patient specialty built on general medicine rather than a single tumor technique. Animals cannot narrate symptoms, may conceal pain, vary substantially by species and breed, and depend on human caregivers for access, budget, environment, and end-of-life decisions. Diagnosis therefore depends heavily on examination, imaging, sampling, pathology quality, staging, and the owner's observations.

Treatment choice is an optimization problem rather than a technology ranking. Surgery, chemotherapy, imaging, molecular tests, and minimally invasive ablation may all be useful, but only when indication, anatomy, evidence, expected burden, local capability, cost, longevity, and quality of life align. [[ComparativeMedicine]] can expand options, yet human oncology remains a source of hypotheses and methods rather than a directly portable rulebook.

## Key Claims
- Veterinary oncology requires broad general-medicine knowledge because tumors and treatment effects can involve any organ system.
- Apparent increases in disease can reflect improved recognition, imaging, sampling, and differential diagnosis as well as true incidence change.
- Pathology grade, surgical margins, and TNM-style staging can materially change treatment and prognosis discussions.
- Early detection is difficult because animals cannot report subtle symptoms and often conceal pain; incidental imaging findings therefore matter but do not create a universal screening protocol.
- Molecular or breed-specific screening can be useful only when positive and negative results are interpreted within prevalence, indication, and follow-up limits.
- Minimally invasive treatment may reduce burden for selected older or fragile animals, but human thresholds cannot be mechanically scaled to smaller bodies.
- The best plan balances expected benefit, treatment burden, caregiver constraints, and quality of life rather than maximizing price or technical intensity.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This page is based on one clinician interview summary, not a systematic review or veterinary guideline. Individual claims about lymphoma, tumor markers, genetic tests, ablation, cure, survival, and complication rates remain source-scoped. Diagnosis, staging, and treatment require a qualified veterinarian and cannot be inferred from this synthesis.
