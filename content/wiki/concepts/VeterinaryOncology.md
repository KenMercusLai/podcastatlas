# Veterinary Oncology / 兽医肿瘤学

## Definition
Veterinary oncology is the diagnosis, staging, treatment, symptom management, and quality-of-life care of cancer in animals, represented here through canine and feline clinical practice.

## Current Synthesis
The sources frame veterinary oncology as a whole-patient specialty built on general medicine rather than a single tumor technique. Animals cannot narrate symptoms, may conceal pain, vary substantially by species and breed, and depend on human caregivers for access, budget, environment, and end-of-life decisions. Diagnosis therefore depends heavily on examination, imaging, sampling, pathology quality, staging, clinician sensitivity, and caregiver observations. Incidental early detection can improve an individual outcome, but it does not establish a universal screening package.

Treatment choice is an optimization problem rather than a technology ranking. Surgery, chemotherapy, imaging, molecular tests, minimally invasive ablation, lower-cost medication, comfort care, and euthanasia can occupy different places as indication, anatomy, evidence, expected burden, comorbidity, local capability, cost, longevity, and quality of life change. [[ComparativeMedicine]] can expand options, yet human oncology remains a source of hypotheses and methods rather than a directly portable rulebook. [[CompanionAnimalEndOfLifeDecisionMaking]] adds that technical feasibility is not sufficient when rescue cannot alter the trajectory or another disease now dominates suffering and prognosis.

## Key Claims
- Veterinary oncology requires broad general-medicine knowledge because tumors and treatment effects can involve any organ system.
- Apparent increases in disease can reflect improved recognition, imaging, sampling, and differential diagnosis as well as true incidence change.
- Pathology grade, surgical margins, and TNM-style staging can materially change treatment and prognosis discussions.
- Early detection is difficult because animals cannot report subtle symptoms and often conceal pain; incidental examination or imaging findings can matter but do not create a universal screening protocol.
- Molecular or breed-specific screening can be useful only when positive and negative results are interpreted within prevalence, indication, and follow-up limits.
- Minimally invasive treatment may reduce burden for selected older or fragile animals, but human thresholds cannot be mechanically scaled to smaller bodies.
- The best plan balances expected benefit, comorbidity, treatment burden, caregiver constraints, and quality of life rather than maximizing price or technical intensity.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
These pages are clinician interview summaries, not systematic reviews or veterinary guidelines. Individual claims about lymphoma, tumor markers, genetic tests, ablation, screening yield, cure, survival, complications, costs, and end-of-life thresholds remain source-scoped. Diagnosis, staging, treatment, and euthanasia decisions require qualified veterinary context and cannot be inferred from this synthesis.

## What Changed
- Added multimorbidity and changing disease priority to the treatment-selection frame.
- Added lower-cost symptom relief and comfort care without equating budget limits with abandonment.
- Sharpened the boundary between technically possible rescue and intervention likely to change outcome.
- Preserved incidental early detection without promoting a universal screening protocol.
