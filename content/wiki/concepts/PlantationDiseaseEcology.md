# Plantation Disease Ecology

## Definition

Plantation disease ecology is the interaction of coerced labor, forced migration, cash crops, water storage, settlement patterns, mosquitoes, pathogens, climate, and unequal living conditions in producing disease risk within plantation systems.

## Current Synthesis

The Atlantic plantation was not only an economic institution placed in a tropical disease environment. In the episode's account, the slave trade moved people and diseases from West Africa, while sugar production assembled human bodies, sugar-rich environments, and standing water in ways favorable to *Aedes aegypti* and yellow fever. Malaria, hookworm, yaws, labor conditions, and regional vector limits formed part of the same system.

Disease then shaped imperial geography without acting alone. High European mortality constrained settlement in West Africa, while differences among New Orleans, the Deep South, the mid-Atlantic, New England, Canada, and Australia influenced the relative viability of plantation, trading, and settler-colonial arrangements.

## Key Claims

- Forced migration can transport pathogens and vectors as well as labor.
- Crops, water practices, housing, coercion, and settlement density jointly shape exposure.
- Plantation profitability can depend on disease environments that distribute mortality unequally.
- Regional vector limits can influence labor systems and colonial settlement patterns.
- Medical and military technologies can change where direct colonial rule becomes feasible.
- Disease ecology interacts with political economy and violence rather than determining them mechanically.

## Evidence

- Sugar-and-vector ecology - [[147-disease-the-new-world-and-modern-pandemics-glt1023018457]] links plantations, standing water, mosquitoes, yellow fever, and enslaved populations in Jamaica and Barbados.
- Regional gradient - [[147-disease-the-new-world-and-modern-pandemics-glt1023018457]] contrasts endemic risk in New Orleans and the Deep South with lower vector suitability farther north.
- Imperial constraint - [[147-disease-the-new-world-and-modern-pandemics-glt1023018457]] treats falciparum malaria and European mortality as barriers to direct settlement in West Africa before nineteenth-century medical and military changes.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode supplies a broad ecological interpretation rather than comparative mortality data or a complete account of Atlantic slavery. Climate and vectors did not create enslavement, erase political choice, or determine a single colonial form. Claims about regional boundaries, settlement feasibility, and the weight of particular diseases remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to join plantation political economy to vector, water, labor, and settlement ecology.
