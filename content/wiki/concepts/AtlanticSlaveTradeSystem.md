# Atlantic Slave Trade System

## Definition

The Atlantic Slave Trade System is the episode's model of how older practices of captive-taking and human sale were enlarged by European maritime power, colonial demand, commercial finance, state support, and plantation production into a durable transoceanic machine.

## Current Synthesis

The system did not begin with one actor or institution. African wars, rulers, and intermediaries supplied captives through pre-existing practices; European traders supplied expanding overseas demand, ships, capital, insurance, chartered firms, and imperial protection; American plantations converted coerced labor into sugar, tobacco, rice, cotton, and other valuable commodities. Disease-driven Indigenous collapse and continuing colonial labor demand strengthened the turn toward African captives.

Responsibility is therefore distributed but not flattened. Supplier participation matters, yet it does not cancel the role of buyers who enlarged demand, organized transoceanic transport, and built a state-backed commercial infrastructure. Nor does economic explanation displace moral judgment: the machine operated through forced marches, confinement, shipboard death and sexual violence, public sale, family separation, surveillance, and punishment.

## Key Claims

- Atlantic slavery scaled older enslavement through oceanic transport, capital, insurance, companies, naval capacity, and state support.
- Plantation demand for high-value commodities made coerced labor a recurring economic input rather than an incidental byproduct of war.
- Indigenous demographic catastrophe intensified labor substitution without mechanically determining the African trade.
- African sellers and European buyers held different positions in a connected chain of capture, demand, finance, transport, and exploitation.
- The captive process extended from inland seizure and forced movement through ocean crossing, sale, and plantation control.
- Systemic explanation identifies mechanisms and responsibility without reducing victims to economic units or excusing individual action.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

African societies were not uniform, and participation in capture or sale varied across places and periods. Disease, crop profitability, agricultural knowledge, malaria adaptation, maritime competition, state policy, and racial ideology are interacting explanations rather than a single sufficient cause. The episode's migration totals and commercial examples are not independently verified here, and the machine metaphor can obscure differences in agency and power if treated too literally.

## What Changed

- Established an end-to-end model connecting captive supply to finance, maritime transport, commodity production, and state power.
- Distinguished distributed participation from equalized responsibility.
- Extended the unit of analysis beyond the Middle Passage to pre-embarkation and plantation coercion.
