# Civilian Followership as Political Capital / 百姓追随型政治资本

## Definition
Civilian followership as political capital is the pattern in which people's voluntary willingness to move with, endure costs for, or visibly choose a leader becomes evidence of trust and a reusable legitimacy asset, even when the same followers create immediate logistical burdens.

## Current Synthesis
Hanji 1093 defines the pattern through [[LiuBei|刘备]]'s 208 CE retreat from [[Jingzhou|荆州]]. The episode presents civilians as making a comparative safety choice between Liu Bei's humane reputation and fear associated with [[CaoCao|曹操]]'s earlier violence in [[Xuzhou|徐州]]. Their movement is therefore not only a leader's performance: it begins with perceived protection and the followers' own risk judgment.

Once visible at mass scale, however, followership changes political meaning. A slow column of civilians and carts exposes Liu Bei to pursuit before [[ChangbanBattle|长坂坡之战]], yet it also makes popular trust observable to officials, local elites, and future allies. The source's strongest synthesis is that humanitarian obligation, military liability, and reputational gain can coexist without proving whether the leader's private motive was sincere, strategic, or mixed.

## Key Claims
- Voluntary civilian movement can serve as observable evidence that a leader is trusted to provide comparatively safer rule.
- Atrocity reputation and humane reputation shape political alignment before formal administration is settled.
- The same followers who increase transport and defense costs can increase a leader's legitimacy and future recruitment value.
- Political benefit does not by itself prove that protective conduct was insincere or staged from the outset.
- Followership becomes stronger political capital when third parties can see the costs civilians are willing to bear.

## Evidence
### Comparative safety choice
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1093-weihe-jingzhou-baixing-ningyuan-taonan-ye-yao-zhuisui-liubei-lhnmyfjgxy8lmioghbsfwoquzg-o|Hanji 1093]] attributes civilian movement to Liu Bei's humane reputation and fear linked to Cao Cao's Xuzhou record.

### Operational burden and visible commitment
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1093-weihe-jingzhou-baixing-ningyuan-taonan-ye-yao-zhuisui-liubei-lhnmyfjgxy8lmioghbsfwoquzg-o|Hanji 1093]] describes a large, slow civilian column whose carts and mixed ages make rapid withdrawal impossible before Changban.

### Legitimacy conversion
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1093-weihe-jingzhou-baixing-ningyuan-taonan-ye-yao-zhuisui-liubei-lhnmyfjgxy8lmioghbsfwoquzg-o|Hanji 1093]] explicitly interprets mass followership as a public demonstration of Liu Bei's moral claim and future attraction power.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not independently verify the scale of the following, the transmission of Xuzhou memories into Jingzhou, or the later return of local elites. Its “image project” language is a modern interpretation of political effect, not proof of Liu Bei's private intent. Coercion, lack of alternatives, kinship networks, and local elite coordination could also shape civilian movement, but this episode does not examine them in depth.

## What Changed
- Established a distinction between civilian risk choice, the military cost of protection, and the later conversion of visible followership into legitimacy.
- Preserved mixed motive as an open question rather than reducing the episode to either pure benevolence or pure performance.
