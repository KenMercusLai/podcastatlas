# Risk Perception

## Definition

Risk perception is the subjective way people feel and judge danger before or alongside formal probability, expected value, causal evidence, or technical analysis.

## Current Synthesis

[[vol-110-touzi-jiushi-dui-shijieguan-de-toupiao-maichu-zichan-peizhi-di-yi-bu-wanjiepian-lk5jkgnzvzerymcixylsdqdtn7f8]] introduces the mechanism through [[PaulSlovic]] and [[DanielKahneman]]. People can be strongly affected by vivid detail while struggling to distinguish a small probability from an extremely small one. In finance, a person may understand risk intellectually yet act through fear, excitement, trust, disgust, or imagined regret; suitability must therefore account for what an investor can understand and hold, not only theoretical return.

[[blame-game-blame-game|Blame Game]] extends the mechanism from personal choice to public controversy. A fatal 911 recording made sudden acceleration concrete and memorable, repeated coverage increased the salience of a technological threat, and institutional responses reinforced the perceived pattern before causal disagreement was settled. The two domains share a judgment: risk communication is shaped by vividness, worldview, and trust, so better decisions require both quantitative evidence and attention to how danger is experienced.

## Key Claims

- Affect and vivid examples often make a risk salient before numerical analysis begins.
- People can underweight large differences between low and extremely low probabilities.
- Perceived risk influences whether a person can sustain an investment strategy despite understanding it abstractly.
- Repeated coverage and institutional action can reinforce a public risk narrative independently of settled causation.
- Worldview, trust, and familiarity shape which evidence feels credible or actionable.
- Good risk management must address both measured danger and the human experience of that danger.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Neither bounded source provides a complete risk-perception literature review. The investment episode uses secondary explanation rather than the cited studies, and the automotive episode does not quantify the effect of media exposure on reporting or belief. Vividness does not make a risk false, institutional response can reveal real danger, and emotional concern can be rational when stakes are severe or institutions are mistrusted. The concept should supplement rather than displace probability, causal investigation, and domain expertise.
