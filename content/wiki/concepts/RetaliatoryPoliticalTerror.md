## Definition

Retaliatory political terror is the pattern where a powerholder converts refusal, warning, omission, lawful enforcement, property resistance, or association with critics into lethal punishment, using exemplary killings and family liability to make ordinary correction or noncompliance unsafe.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 846 grounds the concept in [[LiangJiLateHan|梁冀]]'s late-Han crime record. The pattern is broader than anger at one opponent: [[WuShuWanMagistrateLateHan|Wu Shu]] is poisoned after enforcing against Liang's guests, [[HaoMengDongjunLateHan|Hao Meng]] is executed after failing to visit Liang first, [[YuanZhuRemonstrantLateHan|Yuan Zhu]] and [[CuiQiLateHan|Cui Qi]] are killed after warnings, [[HuWuTaiyuanLateHan|Hu Wu]]'s household is exterminated, [[HaoJieTaiyuanLateHan|Hao Jie]] kills himself to save kin, and [[GengChengLinruHouLateHan|Geng Cheng]] is killed with family after refusing a grave-goods demand.

The current synthesis treats terror as a governance mechanism. When a powerholder can punish both action and omission, both speech and silence become politically legible risks. This helps explain why [[EmperorHuanOfHan|Emperor Huan]] waits and why later anti-Liang action depends on palace conspiracy rather than normal remonstrance or legal process.

## Key Claims

- Terror begins when private grievance can be converted into official accusation, execution, poisoning, or household punishment.
- The victim does not need to rebel; refusal, warning, omitted deference, or lawful enforcement can be enough.
- Protected retainers make local office dangerous because public law collides with the powerholder's private network.
- Family liability extends the terror beyond the named actor and forces kin-preservation calculations.
- Corrective speech fails when warning a powerholder is treated as exposing or threatening him.
- Retaliatory terror makes ordinary institutional repair unlikely, pushing opponents toward secrecy, delay, or coup mechanics.

## Evidence

- Retainer enforcement punished: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-846-bahu-jiangjun-bingshan-yijiao-de-fanzui-jilu-lv7fbvoqr1nureqgvwiwixjyk4pq|Hanji 846]] says Wu Shu kills harmful Liang guests after refusing protection and is later poisoned by Liang.
- Omitted deference punished: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-846-bahu-jiangjun-bingshan-yijiao-de-fanzui-jilu-lv7fbvoqr1nureqgvwiwixjyk4pq|Hanji 846]] says Hao Meng is executed before taking office after he has not yet visited Liang.
- Corrective speech punished: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-846-bahu-jiangjun-bingshan-yijiao-de-fanzui-jilu-lv7fbvoqr1nureqgvwiwixjyk4pq|Hanji 846]] says Yuan Zhu is beaten to death after warning Liang to reduce power, and Cui Qi is hunted down after admonitory essays and direct criticism.
- Family liability: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-846-bahu-jiangjun-bingshan-yijiao-de-fanzui-jilu-lv7fbvoqr1nureqgvwiwixjyk4pq|Hanji 846]] says Hu Wu's household is exterminated and Geng Cheng is killed with more than ten family members.
- Kin-preservation pressure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-846-bahu-jiangjun-bingshan-yijiao-de-fanzui-jilu-lv7fbvoqr1nureqgvwiwixjyk4pq|Hanji 846]] says Hao Jie drinks poison at Liang's gate to exchange his life for family safety.
- Strategic consequence: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-846-bahu-jiangjun-bingshan-yijiao-de-fanzui-jilu-lv7fbvoqr1nureqgvwiwixjyk4pq|Hanji 846]] says Emperor Huan cannot yet preside openly but is forming the intent to destroy Liang.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is currently grounded in one late-Han source note and should not be treated as a universal model of all political violence. Hanji 846 gives a concentrated podcast summary, so the detailed counts, legal forms, and exact chronology of each case remain source-scoped. The concept also differs from lawful punishment: its diagnostic feature is retaliation for offense to an overmighty actor, not proof-based public adjudication.

## What Changed

- Hanji 846 creates the concept from Liang Ji's chain of poisoned, executed, hunted, or household-punished opponents.
