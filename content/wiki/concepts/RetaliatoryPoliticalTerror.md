## Definition
Retaliatory political terror is the pattern where a powerholder converts omen accusation, refusal, warning, omission, lawful enforcement, property resistance, partial compliance, elite reputation, failed service, or association with critics into official punishment or lethal coercion. Its tools include fabricated accusation, imprisonment, dismissal, prison torture, exemplary killing, public display, mourning threats, confiscation, and family liability.

## Current Synthesis
The 145 CE layer now shows the pattern before the better-known prison deaths. [[ZhongHaoLateHan|种暠]] reports [[LiuJunshiLateHan|刘君世]]'s gold-snake gift to [[LiangJiLateHan|梁冀]], Liang resents him, and the later failed suppression of [[FuZhiLateHan|服直]] in [[BaCommanderyLateHan|巴郡]] becomes an arrest channel against Zhong Hao and [[YingChengLateHan|应承]]. [[LiGuLateHan|李固]] prevents execution by arguing that indiscriminate punishment of failed service will make officials stop exerting themselves, but both men still lose office.

Hanji 830 gives the court-ministerial branch. Li Gu and [[DuQiaoLateHan|杜乔]] are accused of hidden conspiracy, and public pressure can only briefly interrupt the case. Liang Ji reasserts the accusation, both men die in prison, and their bodies are displayed with mourning forbidden; terror therefore targets reputation, followers, and funeral memory as well as the officials themselves.

Hanji 833 supplies a wealth-extortion branch. [[SunFenFufengLateHan|孙奋]] is targeted because he is rich; Liang turns a gift and loan request into coercive payment, treats partial compliance as offense, then uses a false claim against Sun Fen's mother, prison torture, and property seizure to destroy the household. The same source's rabbit-park deaths show Liang making private pleasure legally lethal for outsiders.

Hanji 843 provides an omen-accusation branch. [[ChenShouTaishiLingLateHan|Chen Shou the Taishi ling]] blames a solar eclipse on Liang through [[XuHuangLateHan|徐璜]], after which Liang has the Luoyang magistrate arrest and torture him until he dies; [[EmperorHuanOfHan|Emperor Huan]] knows but does not visibly act.

Hanji 846 grounds the concept in a larger victim chain. [[WuShuWanMagistrateLateHan|Wu Shu]] is poisoned after enforcing against Liang's guests, [[HaoMengDongjunLateHan|Hao Meng]] is executed after failing to visit Liang first, [[YuanZhuRemonstrantLateHan|Yuan Zhu]] and [[CuiQiLateHan|Cui Qi]] are killed after warnings, [[HuWuTaiyuanLateHan|Hu Wu]]'s household is exterminated, [[HaoJieTaiyuanLateHan|Hao Jie]] kills himself to save kin, and [[GengChengLinruHouLateHan|Geng Cheng]] is killed with family after refusing a grave-goods demand.

## Key Claims
- Terror begins when private grievance can be converted into official accusation, imprisonment, dismissal, execution, poisoning, prison death, or household punishment.
- The victim does not need to rebel; reputation, warning, lawful enforcement, omitted deference, partial compliance, refusal, failed service, or mourning can be enough.
- Public display and mourning bans extend retaliation from the condemned person to followers and memory.
- Omen or disaster-responsibility claims become dangerous when they name an overmighty actor rather than staying abstract.
- Protected retainers make local office dangerous because public law collides with the powerholder's private network.
- Family liability extends the terror beyond the named actor and forces kin-preservation calculations.
- Corrective speech fails when warning a powerholder is treated as exposing or threatening him.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This concept is currently grounded in five late-Han source notes and should not be treated as a universal model of all political violence. Hanji 826 broadens the pattern from lethal punishment to retaliatory imprisonment and office loss, but it also shows limits: Li Gu can argue against execution and Liang Na can reduce the penalty. Hanji 830 likewise shows partial limits through petitions, Wu You's defense, and later permission for burial. Detailed counts, legal forms, property figures, final-letter wording, and exact chronology of each case remain source-scoped. The concept differs from lawful punishment: its diagnostic feature is retaliation for offense to an overmighty actor, not proof-based public adjudication.

## What Changed
- Added the 145 CE Zhong Hao and Ying Cheng case as an early non-lethal branch where corruption reporting and failed service become punishment material.
- Preserved Hanji 830 as the ministerial-persecution and funeral-intimidation branch through Li Gu and Du Qiao.
- Refined the concept to include imprisonment and office loss as well as killing, torture, confiscation, and family liability.
