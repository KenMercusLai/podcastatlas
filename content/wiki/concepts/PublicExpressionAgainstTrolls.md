## Definition
Public expression against trolls / 面对喷子的公共表达 is the choice to keep speaking in hostile online environments because silence can make loud, aggressive, or distorted views appear more representative than they are.

## Current Synthesis
The source frames public expression as both exhausting and socially meaningful. One side says an individual voice can feel like a stone dropped into the sea, so withdrawing expression may protect attention and dignity. Luo Yonghao's counterclaim is that if ordinary or more reasonable people stop speaking, the visible public square can be occupied by hostile voices that make abnormal positions look normal.

The concept is not a blanket instruction to answer every troll. The panel distinguishes pure abuse, structured disagreement, long but poor logic, and comments that miss the point. It also keeps practical tactics available: sarcasm, deletion, blocking, ignoring, searching one's own name, and moving to offline conversation are all treated as possible responses. The core issue is whether expression is only private catharsis or also a public signal that other voices exist.

## Key Claims
- Online hostility can make expression feel futile, especially when a single person cannot visibly shift the crowd.
- Withdrawal has a public cost when it leaves only the loudest hostile voices visible.
- Not all disagreement deserves the same response; abuse, bad logic, and argued opposition are different cases.
- Creators are especially vulnerable to misread intention because jokes and public posts can be interpreted outside their intended frame.
- Offline encounter is imagined as a way to restore complexity, but the source treats its feasibility as highly contested.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not prove that responding online changes public opinion. It presents expression as a live panel's contested value judgment. The concept should also not override harassment, doxxing, safety, or mental-health boundaries.
