## Definition

谗言疑心式反馈失灵 / slander-suspicion feedback collapse is a court-politics pattern in which a ruler's suspicion and susceptibility to slander make truthful advice unstable: worthy people are withdrawn quickly, petty people gain room to coordinate accusations, and the feedback environment deteriorates even before formal policy collapses. Its positive inverse appears when a ruler tests accusations against evidence, timing, and motive before making them actionable.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-477-xihan-shouwei-jianchen-da-huanguan-shi-ruhe-raoluan-chaozheng-de-lq_tjdwttakvkciban4sptyyhuhb|Hanji 477]] grounds the concept in [[LiuXiang|刘向 / 刘更生]]'s warning to [[HanYuandi|汉元帝]] after [[ShiXianWesternHan|石显]] repeatedly slanders [[ZhouKanWesternHan|周堪]] and [[ZhangMengWesternHan|张猛 / 张萌]]. Liu Xiang does not treat slander as a private quarrel alone. He links it to court disharmony, disasters, the ruler's unstable judgment, and the wider danger that petty people can turn rumor and factional language into governing signals.

The key mechanism is speed and asymmetry. Worthy people and good measures are abandoned quickly after denunciation, while removing petty men is described as difficult. That makes the ruler's feedback channel progressively worse: loyal recommendation can be misread as faction, slander can be read as vigilance, and ministers who see the danger must use disaster, classical precedent, and indirect warning to speak.

The countercase is [[EmperorZhaoOfHan|汉昭帝刘弗陵]]'s handling of a forged memorial accusing [[HuoGuangWesternHan|霍光]] of dangerous overreach. Han Zhao refuses to let the accusation become policy because the alleged information could not have traveled from Chang'an to distant [[LiuDanYanKingWesternHan|刘旦]] so quickly and because the supposed rebel motive is implausible. The episode uses [[LiDeyu|李德裕]]'s praise of Han Zhao to show that ruler discernment can interrupt slander before it becomes court feedback collapse.

The pattern also has a crown-prince variant before the [[WuguIncidentWesternHan|巫蛊之祸]]. [[SuWenWesternHan|苏文]] and Chang Rong do not initially need a full legal case; they try to reshape [[HanWudi|汉武帝]]'s perception of [[LiuJuCrownPrinceWesternHan|刘据]] through sexual slander and a false emotional reading of Liu Ju's response to Wudi's illness. The danger is sharpened because Liu Ju trusts Wudi's discernment and does not remove Su Wen, leaving hostile reports inside the father-son feedback channel.

## Key Claims

- Feedback collapse can begin when the ruler treats denunciation as more actionable than sustained evidence of worth.
- Slander becomes structurally dangerous when it pairs with ruler suspicion, not only when the slanderer is powerful.
- Disaster language can serve as an indirect warning that the court's human feedback system has already become disordered.
- Loyal mutual recommendation and petty factional collusion must be distinguished; collapsing them benefits slanderers.
- Ruler discernment can stop the pattern when accusations are tested against concrete timing, distance, and motive rather than treated as instant evidence.
- The pattern drives upright ministers toward retreat, indirect remonstrance, or self-protective speech.
- Heir-directed slander can work by attacking perceived affection, filiality, and moral conduct before it becomes a formal treason or witchcraft case.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is still a court-feedback pattern, not a complete theory of Western Han factional politics. Hanji 477 presents Liu Xiang's memorial through a podcast summary, not the full classical text. Hanji 399 supplies a positive countercase, but it does not prove that Han Zhao was generally immune to all factional manipulation. Hanji 384-2 supplies a compact heir-directed slander example, not full biographies of Su Wen, Chang Rong, Wang Bi, or the complete Wugu prosecution. Yuan's inner motives, Shi Xian's exact accusation record, Zhou Kan and Zhang Meng's legal status, and the forged memorial investigation remain source-scoped.
