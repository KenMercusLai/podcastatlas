# Housing Scarcity as Electoral Agenda Setting

## Definition
Housing scarcity as electoral agenda setting is the attempt to make a visible affordability or eviction crisis the dominant campaign issue so that voters judge parties through housing rather than through less favorable political questions.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents [[PedroSanchez]]'s snap-election decision as a high-risk effort to convert Spanish housing anger into a new electoral dividing line. A reported shortage, slow construction, rapid household growth, and a salient eviction made the issue politically potent, but the proposed tenant protections also carried supply tradeoffs and had already exposed coalition weakness. Agenda control therefore cannot be separated from policy credibility, governing record, or competing concerns about corruption and migration.

## Key Claims
- A severe and personally legible housing problem can give an incumbent a plausible issue on which to reset a campaign.
- Calling an early election does not erase other liabilities; it changes which issue the incumbent hopes voters will use to organize them.
- Tenant protections can offer immediate security while rent controls or purchase restrictions may reduce rental supply or access for future tenants.
- A failed housing decree can simultaneously demonstrate policy intent and advertise parliamentary weakness.
- Polling advantage is a dated campaign condition rather than proof of the eventual result.

## Evidence
- Scarcity and salience: [[house-call-spains-snap-election-24e23fedc70442e36d4ea43c405cbb9c]] cites a 750,000-home shortage, low annual building, rapid household growth, and the eviction of an elderly long-term tenant.
- Electoral reset: [[house-call-spains-snap-election-24e23fedc70442e36d4ea43c405cbb9c]] says Sanchez framed the contest as ordinary people against property speculators while corruption, migration, and weak polling burdened his government.
- Policy and coalition tradeoff: [[house-call-spains-snap-election-24e23fedc70442e36d4ea43c405cbb9c]] describes contract extensions, temporary eviction protection, and corporate-purchase limits, but also supply concerns and the decree's defeat by a coalition partner.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode offers a campaign interpretation, not direct evidence of Sanchez's private motive or proof that housing will dominate voting. Its construction, household, shortage, and polling figures are source-dated, and the effect of each proposed rule is not separately estimated. Housing supply constraints, tenant vulnerability, coalition arithmetic, corruption, and migration can all matter at once.
