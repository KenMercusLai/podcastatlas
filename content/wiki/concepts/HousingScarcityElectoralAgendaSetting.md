# Housing Scarcity as Electoral Agenda Setting

## Definition
Housing scarcity as electoral agenda setting is the attempt to make an affordability, availability, or eviction crisis the dominant campaign issue so voters judge parties through housing rather than through less favorable political questions.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources present [[PedroSanchez]]'s snap-election decision as a high-risk attempt to convert Spanish housing anger into a favorable dividing line. A large reported shortage, slow construction, rapid household growth, and a salient eviction made the issue politically potent, but his tenant-protection package exposed both supply tradeoffs and coalition weakness. The newer late-campaign account suggests the reset did not clearly displace migration, transport failures, corruption allegations, or dissatisfaction with how growth was experienced; agenda setting created a plausible contest, not control over how voters integrated the governing record.

## Key Claims
- A severe and personally legible housing problem can give an incumbent a plausible issue on which to reset a campaign.
- Calling an early election changes the issue the incumbent hopes voters prioritize but does not erase other liabilities.
- Tenant protections can offer immediate security while rent controls or purchase restrictions may reduce rental supply or access for future tenants.
- A failed housing decree can demonstrate policy intent while advertising parliamentary weakness.
- Strong aggregate growth may intensify rather than resolve housing politics when labor-force and household expansion outpace construction.
- Polling and issue salience are dated campaign conditions rather than proof of the eventual result.

## Evidence
- Scarcity and salience: [[house-call-spains-snap-election-24e23fedc70442e36d4ea43c405cbb9c]] cites a 750,000-home shortage, low annual building, rapid household growth, and the eviction of an elderly long-term tenant.
- Electoral reset: [[house-call-spains-snap-election-24e23fedc70442e36d4ea43c405cbb9c]] says Sanchez framed the contest as ordinary people against property speculators while corruption, migration, and weak polling burdened his government.
- Policy and coalition tradeoff: [[house-call-spains-snap-election-24e23fedc70442e36d4ea43c405cbb9c]] describes contract extensions, temporary eviction protection, and corporate-purchase limits, along with supply concerns and defeat by a coalition partner.
- Late-campaign qualification: [[left-in-the-lurch-spains-general-election-f9a9608c40ea7371c98aaab648d8d8b0]] says inadequate homebuilding prevented many voters from feeling Spain's strong aggregate growth and that the election remained burdened by migration, train crashes, and corruption cases.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episodes offer campaign interpretations, not direct evidence of Sanchez's private motive or proof that housing determined voting. Construction, household, shortage, growth, and polling figures are source-dated, and the effect of each proposed rule is not separately estimated. Housing supply, tenant vulnerability, coalition arithmetic, corruption, migration, public safety, and party choice can all matter at once.

## What Changed
- The newer source qualifies the agenda-reset thesis by showing that housing remained embedded in a wider judgment on governing competence.
- Added labor-force-led growth and inadequate construction as a mechanism connecting headline success to voter dissatisfaction.
- Preserved the distinction between campaign salience and the eventual election result.
