# CBD Evidence-and-Dose Boundary

## Definition
The CBD evidence-and-dose boundary separates well-supported, indication-specific clinical effects at meaningful doses from low-dose retail claims, uncertain mechanisms, and effects that may be explained by expectancy or placebo.

## Current Synthesis
The source gives CBD a nonbinary assessment. It is structurally related to THC but not intoxicating, and it has strong evidence for selected pediatric epilepsies such as Dravet syndrome, where pharmaceutical-scale dosing can substantially reduce seizures in some children. That success does not validate every commercial CBD product or wellness claim.

Hill says there is no established dedicated CBD receptor and discusses inhibition of adenosine uptake as one plausible mechanism. He also argues that clinical effects usually require much larger doses than those in many retail products, making placebo or expectancy a strong alternative explanation for low-dose benefits. Product composition, dose, indication, interactions, and outcome measurement therefore matter more than the presence of “CBD” on a label.

The earlier solo episode treats CBD as biologically active, less associated with intoxication than THC, and inappropriate as a presumed-safe developmental exposure during pregnancy, lactation, or youth. It does not supply the indication-specific dosing evidence needed to weaken the later clinical-versus-retail boundary, and its broad receptor-binding language remains less differentiated than Hill's mechanism account.

## Key Claims
- CBD is not intoxicating in the way THC is.
- Selected pediatric epilepsies have strong indication-specific evidence for clinically dosed CBD.
- A dedicated CBD receptor is not established in the source.
- Adenosine uptake inhibition is one plausible mechanism rather than a complete settled explanation.
- Many commercial CBD doses are far below doses associated with clinical effects.
- Low-dose retail benefits may reflect placebo, expectancy, natural symptom variation, or unmeasured product composition.

## Evidence
- Epilepsy indication: [[how-cannabis-impacts-health-the-potential-risks-dr-matthew-hill-scim3230094101]] describes strong CBD evidence and dramatic seizure reduction for some children with Dravet syndrome.
- Mechanism uncertainty: [[how-cannabis-impacts-health-the-potential-risks-dr-matthew-hill-scim3230094101]] rejects a known dedicated receptor and discusses adenosine uptake inhibition.
- Dose mismatch: [[how-cannabis-impacts-health-the-potential-risks-dr-matthew-hill-scim3230094101]] contrasts high clinical doses with low-dose commercial products.
- Placebo boundary: [[how-cannabis-impacts-health-the-potential-risks-dr-matthew-hill-scim3230094101]] argues that many low-dose perceived effects are likely expectancy-driven.
- Earlier caution: [[the-effects-of-cannabis-marijuana-on-the-brain-body-scim1203833427]] distinguishes CBD from THC intoxication while warning against assuming that non-intoxicating or plant-derived means developmentally safe.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode notes do not supply exact product assays, complete dose-response trials, interaction data, regulatory status, or evidence across every proposed indication. “Likely placebo” is Hill's evidence judgment, not proof that every low-dose user report is false. The earlier episode's developmental precaution does not establish a quantified risk threshold, and its broad mechanism description does not establish a dedicated CBD receptor. Strong epilepsy evidence cannot be generalized to anxiety, pain, sleep, inflammation, or other uses without indication-specific trials and clinical oversight.
