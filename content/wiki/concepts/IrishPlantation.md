# Irish Plantation

## Definition

Irish plantation was the policy of confiscating land from existing Irish owners and granting it to English or Scottish settlers for security, political control, and profit.

## Current Synthesis

The source presents plantation as a long, uneven process rather than a single event. Early projects in Leix-Offaly and Munster often failed to create durable settler control, while Ulster plantation proved more consequential because large-scale English and Scottish settlement interacted with proximity to Scotland and a strategically resistant region.

Plantation joined land to confession and state security. The episode estimates about 100,000 settlers and says roughly two thirds of Irish land passed from native to settler ownership between the 1560s and 1690s. That transformation hardened political identity because Catholicism increasingly marked dispossession and exclusion while Protestant identity became entangled with property, ascendancy, and loyalty to British rule.

After reconquest, atrocities against surrendered people and civilians formed part of the coercive setting, while [[WilliamPetty]]'s survey made territory measurable for redistribution. Plantation therefore involved not only soldiers and settlers but also the conversion of conquest into maps, categories, and transferable claims.

## Key Claims

- Plantation combined land confiscation, migration, security policy, and profit.
- Its implementation varied sharply by region and period rather than following one uniform design.
- Ulster's settlement became especially durable and politically consequential.
- Land transfer helped bind religious identity to wealth, law, and political power.
- Later conflict cannot be reduced to plantation alone, but plantation made confessional division materially durable.
- Survey and measurement helped convert military conquest into an administrable property settlement.

## Evidence

- Policy and motives: [[336-ireland-celts-conquest-and-cromwell-part-1-glt6280042699]] defines plantation through confiscation and resettlement and identifies both security and profit.
- Uneven outcomes: [[336-ireland-celts-conquest-and-cromwell-part-1-glt6280042699]] contrasts weaker Leix-Offaly and Munster projects with lasting Ulster consequences.
- Scale and identity: [[336-ireland-celts-conquest-and-cromwell-part-1-glt6280042699]] supplies the settler and land-transfer estimates and connects property to Protestant ascendancy and Catholic exclusion.
- Conquest and administration: [[333-the-republic-of-britain-life-under-cromwell-glt3360112790]] links Cromwellian atrocities and Petty's land survey to mass redistribution.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources compress multiple plantations and later confiscations into a broad explanatory arc. Numerical estimates, the distinction between “native” and “settler,” atrocity classification, the phrase “ethnic cleansing,” and the survey's exact causal role require more specialized evidence before being treated as final. Security motives help explain English policy but do not morally justify confiscation or the coercive order it produced.

## What Changed

- Added survey-based administrative legibility as the bridge from reconquest to land redistribution.
