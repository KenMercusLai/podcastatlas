# Medical Imaging Modality Principles / 医学影像成像原理

## Definition
Medical imaging modality principles are the physical and interpretive distinctions that explain why X-ray, CT, and MRI represent anatomy differently and why no modality is universally the clearest or best examination.

## Current Synthesis
[[vol-225-qu-yiyuan-paipianzi-meiren-gaosu-ni-de-jijianshi-yingxiangke-gei-ni-zhuge-jiangmingbai-lhhurs_v1d-vp6rr4eycunr0zmhs|VOL.225]] distinguishes the modalities by both acquisition and representation. X-ray records the combined attenuation of structures along one beam path, so different depths overlap in a two-dimensional projection. CT acquires attenuation data around the body and reconstructs thin cross-sections; sufficiently thin data can then be reformatted into other planes or three-dimensional views without turning the reconstruction into a separate observation of the body.

MRI does not measure X-ray attenuation. It uses magnetic and radiofrequency interactions to collect hydrogen-related signals whose appearance changes with sequence and timing. T1- and T2-weighted images can emphasize different tissue properties, anatomy, fluid, edema, or lesion contrast, so a bright or dark region has meaning only in the context of the sequence, anatomy, and clinical question.

These mechanisms make modality choice functional rather than hierarchical. Projection, cross-sectional localization, multiplanar reconstruction, tissue contrast, acquisition time, motion, anatomy, safety, and the question being asked all affect usefulness. A low-quality phone photograph of film also cannot preserve the full image data, display conditions, series, or clinical context needed for reliable interpretation.

## Key Claims
- X-ray is a projection method in which structures at different depths overlap along the beam path.
- CT reconstructs cross-sectional attenuation data and can reformat sufficiently thin acquisitions into multiple planes or three-dimensional views.
- MRI uses hydrogen-related signals shaped by magnetic fields, radiofrequency energy, timing, and sequence rather than X-ray attenuation.
- Image brightness is modality- and sequence-dependent; “bright” is not a diagnosis or a stable tissue meaning across T1, T2, CT, and radiography.
- Modality usefulness follows the clinical question, anatomy, safety constraints, motion, protocol, and required form of contrast rather than technological prestige.
- Diagnostic interpretation requires the original study, its series and protocol, and clinical context; a casual photograph of displayed film may omit decisive information.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source uses simplified analogies and a pig-trotter demonstration, not a complete account of image physics, acquisition, reconstruction, artifacts, protocol design, or modality indications. T1 and T2 descriptions are introductory rather than exhaustive, and three-dimensional reconstructions do not automatically add diagnostic value. The page does not tell a patient which examination to request or how to interpret an image.

## What Changed
- Established a mechanism-first comparison of projection radiography, cross-sectional CT, and sequence-dependent MRI.
- Made original image data and protocol context part of the interpretation boundary.
