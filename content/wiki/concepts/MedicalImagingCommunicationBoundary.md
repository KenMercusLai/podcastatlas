# Medical Imaging Communication Boundary / 影像检查沟通边界

## Definition
Medical imaging communication boundary is the patient-facing rule that image acquisition, preliminary observation, formal reporting, record access, and result explanation belong to different roles and should not be collapsed during the scan.

## Current Synthesis
The sources show why imaging communication is clinically and emotionally difficult. Patients want immediate answers and caregivers fear radiation, yet image acquisition, preliminary observation, formal reporting, risk-benefit choice, and clinical interpretation remain different tasks. Radiographers may identify urgent findings and escalate them without owning the final diagnosis; diagnostic physicians inspect large image sets and approve formal reports, while treating clinicians interpret those findings with history, examination, labs, prior studies, and follow-up.

VOL.115 adds a modality and cooperation layer. X-ray and CT use ionizing radiation, MRI does not, and contrast material is not automatically the same thing as radiation. Shielding choices depend on anatomy and the scan: unnecessary protection may obscure the target or interfere with automatic exposure control. In children, correct positioning, immobilization, sleep preparation, and medication instructions can reduce failed studies and repeats. VOL.01 further distinguishes enhanced CT contrast from PET radiotracers, explains why bedside access can trade against image quality, and makes the technologist-versus-diagnostic-physician division visible. The communication goal is not “no risk,” but a clear reason for the test, modality-specific precautions, accurate cooperation, and a route from images to qualified clinical interpretation.

## Key Claims
- Patients need explanations, but the person acquiring images may not be the person authorized to give diagnostic conclusions.
- Imaging reports are safer when interpreted with clinical history, symptoms, examination, and next-step planning.
- Urgent findings require rapid clinician notification even if routine reporting is delayed.
- Privacy rules limit casual record lookup even for staff with system access.
- Radiation, MRI, contrast, shielding, positioning, and repeat-scan questions require modality- and anatomy-specific explanation rather than one generic reassurance.
- Communication boundaries work best when paired with clear report routes, cooperation instructions, and follow-up expectations.
- Contrast enhancement, radioactive tracers, machine-generated radiation, and magnetic-field hazards need separate explanations rather than one undifferentiated warning.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The boundary should not excuse poor communication. Patients still need a clear reason for testing, a pathway for results, and rapid escalation of urgent findings. Dose comparisons, shielding practice, automatic exposure behavior, sedation, sleep deprivation, and contrast risks vary by modality, anatomy, machine, age, condition, country, and workplace; podcast examples are not instructions to refuse or demand a particular test or protection method.

## What Changed
- Clarified the multi-step path from positioning and acquisition through report review and treating-clinician interpretation.
- Distinguished ordinary contrast enhancement from PET radiotracers and MRI magnetic-field hazards.
- Added bedside access versus image-quality tradeoffs to the communication frame.
