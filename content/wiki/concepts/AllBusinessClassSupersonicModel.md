# All-Business-Class Supersonic Model

## Definition
The all-business-class supersonic model is [[BoomSupersonic]]'s first-market thesis that much shorter flights can serve travelers already paying premium international fares while using less cabin space per passenger than long-duration lie-flat service.

## Current Synthesis
The two sources frame the model as a way to narrow a capital-intensive market before attempting universal access. Speed substitutes partly for sleeping space, enabling a smaller aircraft and denser premium cabin; overwater routes initially avoid dependence on overland sonic-boom reform. The newer source adds a claimed break-even round-trip transatlantic fare near $3,500 and a long-run ambition to reach every traveler category over multiple aircraft generations. That figure makes the proposition more concrete but does not turn an unbuilt, uncertified aircraft into proven airline economics.

## Key Claims
- A premium segment can be a plausible entry market when broad affordability would require later generations and much larger scale.
- Travel time can substitute for cabin space because shorter flights reduce the value of lie-flat sleeping accommodation.
- Overwater service narrows the initial regulatory problem by allowing full-speed operation away from populated land.
- A $3,500 break-even round-trip, if achieved, would be more accessible than Concorde but still premium and below the likely retail fare.
- The model does not resolve certification, production, fuel, environmental, airline-adoption, or schedule risk.

## Evidence
- Market origin and cabin substitution: [[tsr-s5-blakescholl-v3-finalaudio-tsr-s5-blakescholl-v3-finalaudio]] describes the spreadsheet logic, premium customer segment, and decision not to require boomless overland flight at launch.
- Updated price and accessibility thesis: [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-blake-scholl-why-plane-speed-stalled-supersonic-commercial-flight-revolutionizing-the-engine-42987238]] supplies the founder's $3,500 break-even estimate and multi-generation ambition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The fare is a founder projection, not an observed airline price, and Scholl says airlines would likely charge more. Faster travel does not guarantee that seat density, fuel burn, maintenance, airport operations, certification, demand, and capital costs combine into a viable route. The mass-accessibility ambition belongs to future aircraft generations, not the initial premium model.

## What Changed
- Added a specific founder-estimated break-even fare and separated it from likely retail pricing.
- Clarified that broad accessibility is a multi-generation ambition rather than the initial market.
