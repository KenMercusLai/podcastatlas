# Mountain Scenic-Area Growth Constraint / 山岳景区增长约束

## Definition
The mountain scenic-area growth constraint is the gap between efficiently moving visitors through a famous landscape and building a destination that raises dwell time, spending, repeat visitation, and satisfaction without exceeding ecological or physical capacity.

## Current Synthesis
Traditional mountain scenic areas were organized around enclosed admission, parking, fixed sightseeing routes, cableways, and viewing platforms. That system can process tour groups and support a profitable access asset, yet visitor growth may still fail to become proportional revenue growth. New projects do not automatically close the gap: mountain hotels face difficult logistics and environmental constraints, base hotels face ordinary hospitality competition, and novelty attractions can remain disconnected from the landscape. Meanwhile, hikers and younger visitors may want slower forest contact, multi-day traverses, route clarity, safety, and supplies. Sustainable growth therefore requires redesigning the full visit rather than maximizing transport throughput alone.

## Key Claims
- Visitor volume and destination revenue can diverge when per-capita spending, dwell time, or ancillary conversion remains weak.
- Cableways solve access friction but do not by themselves create repeat demand or a differentiated reason to stay longer.
- Adding hotels, performances, vehicles, or novelty attractions introduces new capital and operating risks rather than reproducing cableway margins.
- Fixed-route sightseeing infrastructure can become misaligned with demand for hiking, nature immersion, and multi-day outdoor experiences.
- Product redesign must combine open routes, legible information, safety, supplies, accommodation, ecological limits, and capacity management.

## Evidence
- Revenue conversion - [[suodao-zhuanqian-nengli-kanbi-maotai-shanyue-jingqu-weihe-hai-zai-wei-zengzhang-fachou-1020019950]] reports a first-half revenue decline across a listed scenic-area sample and cases where visitor growth at Huangshan and Jiuhua exceeded revenue growth.
- Access-product limit - [[suodao-zhuanqian-nengli-kanbi-maotai-shanyue-jingqu-weihe-hai-zai-wei-zengzhang-fachou-1020019950]] argues that cableways lose novelty, face capacity ceilings, and do not reliably drive dining, lodging, or other consumption.
- Hospitality tradeoff - [[suodao-zhuanqian-nengli-kanbi-maotai-shanyue-jingqu-weihe-hai-zai-wei-zengzhang-fachou-1020019950]] contrasts scarce-view mountain hotels with their weather, ecological, transport, and renovation burdens, and accessible base hotels with chain and homestay competition.
- Demand transition - [[suodao-zhuanqian-nengli-kanbi-maotai-shanyue-jingqu-weihe-hai-zai-wei-zengzhang-fachou-1020019950]] says some younger visitors want forests, longer stays, and two- or three-day traverses supported by clearer routes, safety, and supplies.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode does not segment visitors by age, fitness, trip purpose, season, or destination, and it provides no completed transformation case with audited returns. Group sightseeing, cableway use, and short visits can remain valuable, especially for accessibility and time-constrained travelers. The proposed shift toward deeper outdoor experience is therefore a product hypothesis grounded in reported demand signals, not proof that every mountain destination should open more terrain or deprioritize conventional tourists.

## What Changed
- Defines destination growth as visitor conversion and experience design rather than raw traffic or one profitable line of business.
- Adds hotel economics and outdoor-demand change as linked, but distinct, constraints on scenic-area diversification.
