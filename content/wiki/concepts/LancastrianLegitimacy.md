# Lancastrian Legitimacy

## Definition

Lancastrian legitimacy is the episode's account of how [[BattleOfAgincourt|Agincourt]] stabilized a dynasty still shadowed by Henry IV's usurpation.

## Current Synthesis

Victory allowed [[HenryVOfEngland|Henry V]] to join battlefield competence, divine favor, public ritual, national identity, and parliamentary support. Legitimacy work began before the battle: anti-plunder ordinances presented the invader as a rightful ruler of French subjects, while visible punishment asserted control over his own army. Confession, masses, a crowned helmet, appeals to justice and English victories, and confidence before an outnumbered army then made command performance part of the providential story. Prayer at Canterbury and London's elaborate reception turned the campaign into a sacred and civic confirmation of rule.

The effect was not only personal popularity. Promotion of St George's and St David's days, acknowledgment of Welsh bowmen, and the later financing of renewed war helped translate royal victory into a broader English political cause.

## Key Claims

- Lancastrian rule began with an inherited legitimacy problem after usurpation.
- Anti-plunder discipline extended Henry's sovereignty claim from dynastic right into army conduct.
- Agincourt gave Henry a powerful performance of providential and competent kingship.
- Pre-battle discipline, visibility, religious ritual, and rhetoric made command itself a legitimacy performance.
- Religious and civic ritual converted distant battle into domestic authority.
- National symbolism widened ownership of the victory beyond the aristocracy.
- Parliamentary taxation indicates that prestige could be converted into governing capacity.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept describes political effect, not proof of divine favor or a claim that every subject accepted Lancastrian rule. Henry's protections did not eliminate siege destruction, coercive surrender, expulsion, exemplary punishment, hunger, or later prisoner killing. National celebration rested on a campaign whose French consequences included civilian displacement, mass death, captivity, looting, and grief.

## What Changed

- Extended legitimacy work backward from battlefield command to invasion discipline and treatment of claimed subjects.
- Added Harfleur's destruction, surrender terms, expulsions, and public punishment as limits on the restrained-kingship narrative.
