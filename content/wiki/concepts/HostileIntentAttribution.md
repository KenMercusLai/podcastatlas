## Definition

Hostile-intent attribution is the tendency to interpret ambiguous behavior as deliberately threatening, increasing the likelihood of aggressive response.

## Current Synthesis

[[KennethDodge]]'s account in [[memorial-day-2020-part-1-memorial-day-2020-part-1|Memorial Day, 2020 | Part 1]] treats threat attribution as learned and context-sensitive. A child in a violent home may survive by reading weak cues as danger; if that rule generalizes to school, work, or ordinary interaction, accidental touch, fear, distress, or ambiguity can be perceived as attack.

The policing application is demanding because officers must distinguish actual hostility from conduct that only resembles it. The episode argues that respiratory struggle, fear, and medical deterioration were repeatedly read through a threat frame during Floyd's restraint, but its analogy to Chauvin's development remains speculative.

## Key Claims

- Ambiguous behavior supports multiple intention hypotheses, including benign, accidental, playful, defensive, and hostile explanations.
- Chronic danger can make hostile interpretation locally protective without making it accurate across contexts.
- Generalized threat attribution can generate aggression and social isolation.
- Social feedback helps children learn both intention discrimination and aggression regulation.
- Policing requires threat sensitivity together with rapid correction when observable evidence supports fear or medical crisis instead.

## Evidence

### Ambiguous cue treated as threat

- [[memorial-day-2020-part-1-memorial-day-2020-part-1]] recounts Dodge's example of an aggressive teenager who struck after unexpected touch and interpreted the response as necessary vigilance.

### Context-bound adaptation

- [[memorial-day-2020-part-1-memorial-day-2020-part-1]] describes a child learning to monitor a violent stepfather for danger, then explains how the same interpretive rule can misfire elsewhere.

### Policing application

- [[memorial-day-2020-part-1-memorial-day-2020-part-1]] argues that Chauvin treated Floyd's breathing efforts, a firefighter's intervention, and other warnings as resistance or threat rather than reasons to revise.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Not every hostile inference is biased, and credible danger can justify proportionate safety action before motive is certain. The episode supplies examples rather than the underlying research record and cannot establish Chauvin's childhood, diagnosis, or internal reasoning. Developmental explanation must not become exoneration, and the framework is incomplete as an account of racism, policing, or institutional responsibility.

## What Changed

- Established the distinction between contextually adaptive vigilance and harmful generalization.
- Added policing as a high-stakes setting requiring threat detection and active interpretive correction.
