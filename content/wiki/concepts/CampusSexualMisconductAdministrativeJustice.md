# Campus Sexual-Misconduct Administrative Justice

## Definition

Campus sexual-misconduct administrative justice is the university system for receiving, investigating, hearing, and sanctioning sexual-misconduct complaints under [[TitleIX]], operating alongside criminal, civil, state, and federal processes.

## Current Synthesis

The Cornell Seven case shows why this system cannot be assessed through a single value or proceeding. Strong procedure may reduce erroneous findings and protect accused students, yet repeated hearings, cross-examination, changing federal rules, and weak case throughput can make participation inaccessible. The newer source adds that campus discipline, criminal investigation, and civil litigation may all operate while producing fragmented results: differentiated sanctions can remain opaque, evidence may be disputed in transit between campus police and prosecutors, and a stopped criminal inquiry may restart only after civil filings generate national attention. A credible system therefore needs fair evidence rules, practicable participation, auditable evidence handoffs, proportionate and sufficiently transparent sanctions, trauma-informed investigation, and independent oversight without treating an administrative finding, civil allegation, or criminal threshold as interchangeable.

## Key Claims

- Due process and complainant accessibility are simultaneous design requirements, not mutually exclusive slogans.
- Repeated administrative reinterpretation makes institutional procedure unstable and politically contingent.
- Procedural complexity can reduce participation even when introduced to improve reliability.
- Institution-specific punishment creates room for proportionality but also disparity and weak deterrence.
- University, criminal, civil, state, and federal proceedings apply different powers and standards.
- Complaint and survey prevalence cannot by themselves establish case-specific guilt, but they remain relevant to institutional safety.
- Evidence custody and transfer between campus police and prosecutors are part of accountability, not merely administrative detail.

## Evidence

- Burden and access: [[gilets-jeunes-frances-school-protests-2959cbdca16de39a43a75666b5bc7057]] reports twelve Cornell hearings and a 14% New York investigation rate for incidents reported to Title IX coordinators in 2024.
- Rule instability: [[gilets-jeunes-frances-school-protests-2959cbdca16de39a43a75666b5bc7057]] describes repeated executive reinterpretation and the 2020 rule's extensive procedural protections.
- Sanctions and deterrence: [[gilets-jeunes-frances-school-protests-2959cbdca16de39a43a75666b5bc7057]] contrasts suspension or expulsion for sexual misconduct with lighter attempted-exploitation sanctions and cites a Cornell survey reporting high assault prevalence.
- Oversight: [[gilets-jeunes-frances-school-protests-2959cbdca16de39a43a75666b5bc7057]] links a large federal complaint backlog with a state attorney-general investigation into the Cornell case.
- Fragmented proceedings: [[cong-kangnaier-dao-sitanfu-xiaoyuan-xingbaoli-weihe-nan-zhuize-cf30978cd2963cabea566e9c8cfc6616]] distinguishes seven students' varied campus outcomes, the initial decision not to prosecute, the civil complaint, and the later appointment of a special prosecutor.
- Evidence and transparency: [[cong-kangnaier-dao-sitanfu-xiaoyuan-xingbaoli-weihe-nan-zhuize-cf30978cd2963cabea566e9c8cfc6616]] reports conflicting accounts of whether fraternity chats reached the prosecutor and limited disclosure of conduct findings behind different sanctions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Neither source adjudicates the Cornell allegation. The newer episode reports a reopened investigation, not a decision to prosecute, and presents unresolved conflicts over early statements and evidence transfer. Investigation rates do not reveal the merits of reports that did not proceed, while survey prevalence does not identify perpetrators or determine individual cases. More disclosure can improve accountability but must still respect lawful privacy, and faster or simpler proceedings can create error and fairness risks. The concept therefore preserves a multi-objective design problem rather than endorsing maximal procedure, publicity-driven intervention, or unchecked institutional discretion.

## What Changed

- Added evidence handoffs, sanction transparency, fragmented proceedings, and publicity-dependent reopening to the framework.
