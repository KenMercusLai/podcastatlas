## Definition

White-deer-skin ritual currency / 白鹿皮币 is the Han Wudi-era device in which a decorated piece of white-deer skin received a state-assigned price of forty wan cash and became a required base for certain gifts presented by kings, marquises, and imperial relatives. It is best understood here as ritualized fiscal extraction, not ordinary circulating money.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-320-yi-kuai-pi-mai-40-wan-hanwudi-zhuanqian-you-duo-sun-lhpgz2peskxn466cyxeqjwc-ym0z|Hanji 320]] supplies the mechanism. Amid fiscal pressure, [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] controls access to court, sacrificial, and tributary rituals; [[ZhangTangWesternHan|张汤]] is credited with adapting an older elite practice of presenting gifts with skin or textile material; and a one-square-foot decorated skin is assigned a forty-wan price. The purchase is therefore enforced by ceremonial eligibility: the court need not make the skin useful in open exchange if elite participants cannot complete required presentation without it.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-337-gudai-zuiming-keyi-you-duo-huangtang-lqoqdtg35ua6m9y4shcz9u8zygp1|Hanji 337]] supplies opposition and proportionality. [[YanYiWesternHan|颜异]] argues that using a forty-wan skin to support tribute worth only several thousand cash reverses the relation between accessory and principal. His later prosecution does not prove that the fiscal objection alone caused his death, but it shows that disagreement over the device enters a wider field of imperial displeasure and coercive court feedback.

The episode's paper-money comparison is narrow. State power assigns a very high nominal value to a lightweight object, but the supplied evidence also says the object is nontransferable and does not circulate. That makes it an instructive case of administered value and compulsory ritual purchase, not evidence that it performed the full functions of currency or directly generated later paper money.

## Key Claims

- The mechanism converts compulsory elite ritual participation into revenue by attaching an expensive required substrate to otherwise separate gifts.
- Its enforcement comes from control of ceremonial acceptance, not from general market demand or ordinary currency circulation.
- The forty-wan valuation is politically assigned and sharply exceeds the physical object's ordinary utility, making administered value central to the design.
- Reusing an older gift convention lowers the institutional distance between recognizable ritual form and a new fiscal burden.
- Yan Yi's proportionality objection exposes the distinction between ritual accessory and tribute principal and places policy disagreement inside a coercive advice environment.
- Resemblance to paper money is limited to lightweight form and state-backed nominal valuation; circulation, transferability, general acceptance, redemption, and monetary continuity are unproved.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept rests on two short podcast notes, not a direct institutional or numismatic study. The exact date, mandate, buyer population, transaction procedure, revenue yield, duration, regional reach, abolition, and relationship to white-metal coinage remain unresolved. Hanji 320 reports older and later textual parallels without directly comparing the relevant passages, and its explanation of 币 as textile or gift material is useful but not a complete semantic history. Hanji 337 supplies an objection and later prosecution but does not prove that opposition to the device alone caused Yan Yi's death. Because the object is described as noncirculating and nontransferable, calling it currency or a paper-money precursor can mislead unless those limits remain explicit.

## What Changed

- Created the concept by joining Hanji 320's mechanism with Hanji 337's proportionality objection.
- Distinguished ritualized compulsory purchase from ordinary monetary circulation.
- Narrowed the paper-money analogy to administered nominal value rather than direct institutional ancestry.
