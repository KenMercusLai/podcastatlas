# Social Homeostasis

## Definition
Social homeostasis is the source's model of social need as a regulated state in which perceived contact is compared with a flexible baseline and deficits can motivate corrective social behavior.

## Current Synthesis
Loneliness can function like an aversive need signal: acute isolation increases the drive to reconnect, much as hunger motivates food seeking. The solo bonding episode makes the control model more explicit by distinguishing detection of social contact, hypothalamic control, dopamine-linked action, and prefrontal interpretation of meaning and hierarchy. Yet the response is not simply “more contact is better.” When correction repeatedly fails or isolation becomes chronic, the social baseline may adapt; re-entry can then produce avoidance, territoriality, aggression, irritability, or overload rather than uncomplicated affiliation. Social resilience therefore involves both access to meaningful connection and capacity for chosen solitude, while the precise neural mapping and the roles of deficit detection, failed correction, and baseline resetting remain unresolved.

## Key Claims
- Social need can be modeled as an aversive motivational state rather than only the pleasure of contact.
- Social regulation combines need detection and action drive with contextual interpretation of meaning, expectation, and hierarchy.
- Acute isolation can produce rebound affiliative behavior when contact returns.
- Chronic isolation can produce avoidance, aggression, territoriality, or other anti-social responses.
- A social set point can adapt after prolonged change, altering what level of contact feels normal.
- Quality, expectation, identity, history, and context affect whether contact registers as nourishing.
- Comfortable solitude and meaningful connection can both support a flexible social system.

## Evidence
- Neural discovery - [[the-biology-of-social-interactions-and-emotions-dr-kay-tye-scim4105255786]] describes isolation-sensitive dorsal-raphe dopamine neurons discovered through a separated control group.
- Acute/chronic distinction - [[the-biology-of-social-interactions-and-emotions-dr-kay-tye-scim4105255786]] contrasts affiliative rebound after acute isolation with anti-social re-entry after chronic isolation.
- Baseline adaptation - [[the-biology-of-social-interactions-and-emotions-dr-kay-tye-scim4105255786]] uses Tye's pandemic experience and experimental model to motivate flexible social set points.
- Control-system account - [[science-of-social-bonding-in-family-friendship-romantic-love-scim2455538543]] describes detector, control-center, effector, and prefrontal interpretation layers and connects acute deprivation to dorsal-raphe dopamine-related social craving.
- Chronic-state qualification - [[science-of-social-bonding-in-family-friendship-romantic-love-scim2455538543]] says prolonged isolation may reduce approach motivation while increasing stress chemistry and irritability.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not establish whether harmful outcomes arise primarily from detecting a deficit, failing to correct it, adapting to a new baseline, or interacting stress systems. The episode's named human circuit components are a public-education synthesis rather than a complete causal map. Animal results, short human isolation experiments, and pandemic experience do not establish a universal threshold, treatment, or ideal quantity of contact.

## What Changed
- Added the detector-control-effector-interpretation model and a stronger distinction between acute social craving and chronic stress-linked withdrawal.
