# Ethical Supply-Chain Certification

## Definition
Ethical supply-chain certification is a market intervention that uses monitoring, standards, labeling, and guarantees to let purchasers distinguish products represented as meeting a labor or social condition and to shift incentives through demand.

## Current Synthesis
The source's carpet case begins when direct rescue reveals a replenishing trafficking pipeline and exported demand offers leverage beyond the production region. [[GoodWeave]] gives consumers an affirmative child-labour-free option, translating concern into purchasing behavior and requiring verification infrastructure across the chain. Certification is therefore neither a label alone nor proof of complete reform; its credibility depends on monitoring, remediation, worker protection, commercial incentives, and attention to displacement.

## Key Claims
- Certification can turn diffuse consumer concern into a repeatable market choice.
- An affirmative alternative may preserve lawful trade while changing labor incentives more precisely than a blanket boycott.
- Monitoring and guarantees are essential because a label without verification cannot carry the promised condition.
- Commercial viability and labor protection need not be inherently opposed when demand rewards compliant production.
- Headline reductions do not prove causal impact, schooling, durable freedom, or absence of displacement into less visible work.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode does not supply audit protocols, false-negative rates, remediation rules, worker testimony, current coverage, or causal evaluation. Certification can create audit gaming, cost shifting, exclusion of small producers, or movement of abuse outside the monitored boundary. Consumer choice also cannot replace labor law, social protection, schooling, community income, worker organization, or criminal enforcement.
