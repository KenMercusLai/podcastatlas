# Full-Pay Student Cross-Subsidy

## Definition

Full-pay student cross-subsidy is a college financing model in which tuition collected from students receiving little or no aid helps fund institutional operations and grants for students who cannot pay the listed price.

## Current Synthesis

In [[food-fight-food-fight|Food Fight]], [[VassarCollege|Vassar College]] expands access by replacing some full-paying students with students requiring substantial aid. That choice reduces net tuition revenue even when enrollment and academic standards remain stable. The college then relies more heavily on its endowment and on the remaining full-paying group.

The mechanism turns admissions composition into a financial constraint. A full-paying student's value to the budget is not a judgment of educational worth, but losing too many such students can shrink the aid the institution can sustain. Because those students can choose among competing colleges, the cross-subsidy links access policy to [[CollegeAccessAmenityCompetition]].

## Key Claims

- The listed tuition price and the net revenue collected from each student can differ sharply.
- Replacing a full-paying student with a fully aided student carries a large revenue opportunity cost.
- A college can widen access while increasing dependence on endowment draws and affluent enrollment.
- Small changes in the full-paying cohort can matter when the operating margin is narrow.
- The model makes admissions, pricing, aid, amenities, and endowment policy interdependent.
- Cross-subsidy can support access but leaves that access exposed to applicant preferences and economic inequality.

## Evidence

### Revenue concentration

- [[food-fight-food-fight]] reports that about 1,000 of Vassar's roughly 2,450 undergraduates paid full or nearly full price and generated about $60 million annually.

### Composition shift

- [[food-fight-food-fight]] reports that 75 to 80 percent of students once paid full tuition and that the later access strategy replaced hundreds with students needing aid.

### Marginal fragility

- [[food-fight-food-fight]] attributes to Vassar finance officers the judgment that losing even 50 full-paying students would be damaging.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode supplies a simplified institutional account rather than a complete net-tuition model. Merit aid, partial pay, price discrimination, restricted funds, fundraising, debt, auxiliary revenue, retention, discount rates, and fixed versus marginal costs complicate the mechanism. A full-pay cohort can also concentrate privilege, and dependence on it does not prove that every affluent student directly funds a particular aid recipient.

## What Changed

- Established the revenue mechanism beneath the episode's food-versus-aid framing.
- Distinguished students' educational worth from their different short-run budget effects.
- Connected enrollment composition to endowment dependence and amenity competition.
