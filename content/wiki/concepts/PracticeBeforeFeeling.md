# Practice Before Feeling

## Definition
Practice before feeling is Brooks' claim that repeated committed action can precede and sustain belief or feeling, especially in faith, marriage, discipline, and service.

## Current Synthesis
[[the-art-of-true-happiness-dr-arthur-brooks-scim2290485637]] reverses a common sequence. Rather than waiting to feel devotion, certainty, love, or motivation before acting, Brooks argues that practice can come first. He applies this to daily Mass, marriage, exercise, teaching, and the broader work of living by values.

The concept is not a denial of feeling. It treats feeling as something that may follow structured practice, public commitment, humility, and repeated orientation toward what one loves. This makes it a neighbor of self-control, discipline, and meaning-making rather than a purely religious idea.

## Key Claims
- Practice can precede belief and feeling rather than merely expressing them.
- Marriage, faith, exercise, and service may need repeated action when emotion is absent.
- Waiting for the right feeling can undermine commitments that require steadiness.
- Practice turns values into embodied routines and public or private identity.
- Later life can shift attention from scoreboards toward service, spirituality, and meaning.

## Evidence
- Faith practice - [[the-art-of-true-happiness-dr-arthur-brooks-scim2290485637]] has Brooks describe daily Mass attendance and faith as practice rather than constant feeling.
- Marriage analogy - [[the-art-of-true-happiness-dr-arthur-brooks-scim2290485637]] compares faith practice with sustaining marriage beyond feeling alone.
- Explicit sequence - [[the-art-of-true-happiness-dr-arthur-brooks-scim2290485637]] states the order as practice, belief, feeling.
- Life-stage branch - [[the-art-of-true-happiness-dr-arthur-brooks-scim2290485637]] uses later-life religious and transcendent openness to frame movement beyond worldly scoreboards.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Practice without reflection can become empty routine, coercion, or avoidance. The source's claim is strongest where the practice expresses chosen love, faith, service, or moral aspiration, and weakest where safety, consent, or clinical care are at stake.
