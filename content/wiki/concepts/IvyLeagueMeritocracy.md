# Ivy League Meritocracy

## Definition
Ivy League meritocracy is the institutional belief and selection system through which elite U.S. universities present admission and status as measures of earned ability while inherited advantage, unequal opportunity, prestige markets, and changing definitions of merit continue to shape who succeeds.

## Current Synthesis
Elite universities moved from regional and class-bound institutions toward quantified and holistic merit without leaving status reproduction behind. They did not simply discover natural excellence; they built admission rules, tests, interviews, rankings, and prestige markets that shaped what students learned to perform. This historical branch comes from [[160-youxiu-de-mianyang-qing-ba-shuo-bu-de-quanli-huan-gei-wo-933088014]].

The episode describes a sequence: older elite schools served particular class and religious groups; public-school and immigrant applicants pushed into the pipeline; interviews, recommendations, sports, leadership traits, and alumni-family advantages were used as filters; SAT-style testing then promised measurable merit; later rankings and admissions industries turned the whole system into a wider family competition.

The developmental and policy branch asks what admissions can infer when starting polish reflects opportunity and coaching as well as capability. Grant favors removing legacy preference and considering individual obstacles, but he also warns that demographic categories do not reveal identical adversity and declines to prescribe one college choice without comparative evidence and the student's values. This supports scrutiny of inherited advantage while leaving affirmative-action design and institution-specific outcomes unresolved. [[project-dillard-project-dillard|Project Dillard]] extends the argument from admissions into institutional comparison. A prestige system can reward a college for wealth, selectivity, high test scores, and affluent students' uninterrupted completion while overlooking developmental contribution. Its Dillard simulation makes the mission conflict explicit: becoming more elite by ranked criteria can require serving fewer of the students the institution exists to educate.

## Key Claims
- Elite-school prestige is historically constructed through class formation, exclusion, admissions rules, rankings, and market demand.
- SAT-style meritocracy changed the selection language but did not remove inherited advantages or performance expectations.
- Holistic admissions can expand what counts as merit, but it can also make students optimize sports, clubs, leadership, volunteering, and personality signals.
- Rankings compress complex institutional differences into a single competitive ladder, feed parental anxiety and optimization, and can rise through wealth and student composition without demonstrated teaching improvement.
- Legacy preference conflicts with a merit claim because inherited institutional connection is not individual achievement.
- Individual obstacles and developmental trajectory may reveal potential that polished starting performance hides, but no single demographic category describes every applicant's adversity.
- College choice depends on values, goals, support, and desired life as well as institutional status.

- Developmental evaluation - [[unlocking-hidden-potential-with-adam-grant-unlocking-hidden-potential-with-adam-grant]] argues that institutions should examine individual obstacles rather than infer potential only from starting performance or group identity.
- Choice beyond status - [[unlocking-hidden-potential-with-adam-grant-unlocking-hidden-potential-with-adam-grant]] has Grant refuse a universal Ivy-versus-HBCU prescription without outcome evidence, goals, and values.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are structured podcast summaries rather than admissions archives, applicant datasets, institutional outcome comparisons, or a balanced policy review. The historical account uses U.S. elite education to illuminate Chinese status competition without establishing that the systems are identical. Grant's stigma concern does not establish that affirmative action generally harms beneficiaries, and individualized adversity review can introduce its own measurement, privacy, consistency, and bias problems. The Dillard simulation is an incentive critique rather than a causal estimate of teaching quality. Ending legacy preference, broadening evidence, or replacing rankings does not by itself make selection fully meritocratic when schooling, wealth, coaching, geography, discrimination, institutional resources, and mission differences remain unequal.

## What Changed
- Extended the prestige critique from student admissions to institutional ranking incentives.
- Added the distinction between selecting high-input students and developing capability.
