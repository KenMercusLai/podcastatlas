# King Under Law

## Definition

King Under Law is the principle that a monarch's authority is constrained by law and lawful process rather than being identical with the ruler's personal will.

## Current Synthesis

[[MagnaCarta|Magna Carta]] gave this principle practical form during the 1215 crisis by restricting [[KingJohn|King John's]] revenue practices, protecting lawful judgment, promising that justice would not be sold, denied, or delayed, and authorizing baronial enforcement. The settlement sought to correct a bad king without abolishing monarchy: lawful rule, not dynastic replacement or democratic sovereignty, was the immediate aim.

Its significance does not depend on imagining a complete modern constitution at Runnymede. The charter arose from elite armed bargaining, excluded much of the population from its language of free men, and failed almost immediately. Reissue and reinterpretation nevertheless allowed the claim that the ruler is not above law to support progressively broader arguments about liberty and state coercion.

## Key Claims

- Constraint can target how a ruler taxes, judges, detains, dispossesses, and administers justice without rejecting monarchy itself.
- Legal protection requires enforceability, not only a ruler's promise; the twenty-five-barons clause exposed this problem in unusually direct form.
- A failed settlement can preserve a durable constitutional principle when later institutions reissue, circulate, and apply it.
- The medieval principle is an ancestor of later rule-of-law arguments, not a complete equivalent of modern democracy or universal rights.

## Evidence

- Substantive limits: [[62-magna-carta-glt5613809659]] identifies revenue restrictions, lawful judgment, the law of the land, and justice not sold, denied, or delayed.
- Enforcement problem: [[62-magna-carta-glt5613809659]] describes the council of twenty-five barons and its power to seize royal resources after breach.
- Reform rather than deposition: [[62-magna-carta-glt5613809659]] explains why the rebels sought to bind a bad king rather than simply replace or abolish monarchy.
- Survival beyond failure: [[62-magna-carta-glt5613809659]] connects reissue under Henry III to the principle's later national and transatlantic life.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The 1215 charter was an elite settlement made during civil war, many clauses addressed narrow feudal problems, and "free man" did not mean universal legal equality. John repudiated it, and Parliament was not named in it. Later rule-of-law meanings are historically connected to Magna Carta but cannot all be read directly back into the original document.

## What Changed

- Established the concept as lawful restraint on monarchy whose later breadth depended on enforcement, reissue, and reinterpretation.
