# 家庭角色与责任边界 / Family Role Responsibility Boundary

## Definition
家庭角色与责任边界 / Family Role Responsibility Boundary is the distinction between care or obligation that fits a person's role and chosen commitments, and burdens imposed through family expectation, role reversal, or another adult's transferred responsibility.

## Current Synthesis
The concept does not make autonomy equivalent to refusing responsibility. A useful boundary asks whose role a task belongs to, whether the person meaningfully chooses it, whether accepting or refusing it predictably harms self or others, and whether care has become substitution for another adult's responsibility. The new family-of-origin discussion adds a distributional test: repeated demands that the more successful child equalize siblings' lives can convert kinship support into open-ended liability, especially when gender norms direct a daughter's income and labor toward brothers. The evidence remains public conversation rather than a diagnostic rule or universal formula.

## Key Claims
- Family expectations can become internal duties even without an explicit command.
- Role confusion is especially strong when a child becomes caregiver, mediator, or emotional parent.
- Care for family can remain a real responsibility without extending to every debt, conflict, or life project of another adult.
- A norm that the stronger family member must keep transferring resources until outcomes are equal can erase effort, consent, and adult accountability.
- Boundary setting and basic social responsibility can coexist; freedom is practiced within rules that protect others.
- Self-blame can add a second burden after an ordinary setback, making responsibility feel larger than the event warrants.

## Evidence
### Inherited and misplaced burdens
- [[vol-188-fengkuang-dongwucheng-2-beihou-cangzhe-de-chengnianren-xinlike-lltxpvji9zeewoygycdyvrnjbn4c]] connects family “dispatch,” demands to bring honor, and [[Parentification|parentification]] to responsibilities that may not properly belong to the child.

### Care without total takeover
- [[vol-188-fengkuang-dongwucheng-2-beihou-cangzhe-de-chengnianren-xinlike-lltxpvji9zeewoygycdyvrnjbn4c]] contrasts caring for parents with assuming a sibling's bride-price, mortgage, or car costs, using role and harm as rough tests.
- [[ep114-meige-haizi-de-guisu-doushi-yuansheng-jiating-wkgo1v6jm4rqp7q-bvyzzt7v_ia573]] describes expectations that a better-off child finance siblings, arrange work or marriage, and continue helping until living standards converge.

### Gendered recognition and over-giving
- [[ep114-meige-haizi-de-guisu-doushi-yuansheng-jiating-wkgo1v6jm4rqp7q-bvyzzt7v_ia573]] suggests that disproportionate giving may seek family recognition after earlier neglect, while explicitly treating that motive as interpretation rather than diagnosis.

### Rules and psychological freedom
- [[vol-188-fengkuang-dongwucheng-2-beihou-cangzhe-de-chengnianren-xinlike-lltxpvji9zeewoygycdyvrnjbn4c]] uses public-behavior and basketball examples to argue that refusing excess burden does not erase civic rules or consequences for others.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The evidence comes from two podcasts and illustrative examples, not a validated decision procedure. Role, consent, dependency, culture, disability, money, safety, and power can change what care is reasonable. The “harm” test is deliberately broad and may not resolve conflicts where burdens or benefits are delayed, unequal, or disputed. Unequal outcomes do not by themselves prove exploitation, and gendered obligation can overlap with broader family norms that burden whichever member is more successful.

## What Changed
- Added open-ended sibling equalization as a transferred-responsibility pattern.
- Added gender and recognition-seeking as possible intensifiers without treating them as universal causes.
