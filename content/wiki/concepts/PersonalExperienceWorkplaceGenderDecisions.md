# Personal Experience and Workplace Gender Decisions

## Definition
Personal experience and workplace gender decisions describes the hypothesis that changes in a manager's private life can alter how comparably qualified workers are evaluated, affecting hiring, promotion, representation, and pay outcomes without a formal policy change.

## Current Synthesis
[[foiled-plot-did-iran-plan-british-base-attack-6aba32b170b66274d96fb345]] reports a 25-year study of small Danish firms in which women's relative earnings and workforce share rose by more than 4% after a male manager had a daughter, while a son's birth produced no measured equivalent. The changes appeared mainly through hiring and, to a lesser extent, promotion rather than automatic raises for women already employed.

The evidence is consistent with a shift in how some managers assess women: new male and female hires reportedly had similar qualifications and experience, firm performance barely changed, and the probability that the highest-paid employee was a woman rose. It does not directly observe the manager's beliefs, establish one psychological mechanism, or supply a workplace policy by itself.

## Key Claims
- Managerial bias can appear through selection and advancement decisions even when explicit pay-setting for incumbents does not change.
- A private-life event may change attention or empathy, but observed outcomes do not by themselves prove the mental mechanism.
- Similar incoming qualifications and stable firm performance weaken simple claims that more women were hired at the expense of worker quality.
- Representation, promotion, and relative earnings should be analyzed together because workforce composition can change aggregate pay outcomes.
- A demographic association is a research clue, not a prescription that managers should have daughters.

## Evidence
- Earnings and representation: [[foiled-plot-did-iran-plan-british-base-attack-6aba32b170b66274d96fb345]] reports increases exceeding 4% after a male manager had a daughter.
- Decision channel: [[foiled-plot-did-iran-plan-british-base-attack-6aba32b170b66274d96fb345]] locates most of the change in hiring and some in promotion, not automatic raises for current female staff.
- Candidate comparison and performance: [[foiled-plot-did-iran-plan-british-base-attack-6aba32b170b66274d96fb345]] reports that new hires were broadly similar in qualifications and experience, while sales and other firm measures barely changed.
- Leadership outcome: [[foiled-plot-did-iran-plan-british-base-attack-6aba32b170b66274d96fb345]] reports that the chance of the firm's highest-paid employee being a woman rose by two percentage points.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The supplied source does not provide the paper's identification strategy, uncertainty intervals, effect heterogeneity, institutional setting, or replication evidence. Small Danish firms may not generalize to larger organizations or other countries. Too few female managers were available to test an equivalent sons effect, and the study does not show that fatherhood changes every manager or directly reveal why the association occurs.

## What Changed
- Added a qualified mechanism linking personal experience to hiring and promotion decisions.
- Distinguished workforce-composition effects from automatic pay rises for existing employees.
