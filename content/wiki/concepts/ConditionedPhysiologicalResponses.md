# Conditioned Physiological Responses

## Definition
Conditioned physiological responses occur when a previously neutral cue acquires the capacity to evoke a bodily response after repeated pairing with a drug, food, or other biologically active event.

## Current Synthesis
The episode uses Pavlovian learning to explain why placebo responses can be biologically specific. An injection context paired with sumatriptan was later associated with increased growth hormone and reduced cortisol after saline, while food-related smells or neutral signals paired with eating could later cue insulin release. Expectation therefore operates partly through learned predictive relationships, not only conscious persuasion.

## Key Claims
- A treatment ritual can become a predictive cue for a previously experienced drug effect.
- Conditioned responses can reproduce a directional hormonal pattern rather than only a vague feeling of improvement.
- Food-associated cues can prepare metabolism before nutrients arrive.
- Conscious information alone need not produce the same effect as prior biological pairing.
- Conditioning explains some specificity but does not imply that every drug action can be recreated by an inert cue.

## Evidence
### Drug-context conditioning
- [[how-placebo-effects-work-to-change-our-biology-psychology-scim2208611274]] reports that learning about growth hormone and cortisol did not itself change them, whereas sumatriptan exposure followed by saline produced the previously learned hormone pattern.

### Meal-cue conditioning
- [[how-placebo-effects-work-to-change-our-biology-psychology-scim2208611274]] describes food smells and neutral signals paired with eating as later cues for insulin release.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source note does not provide sample sizes, duration, replication status, or the conditions under which extinction occurs. Anticipatory hormone or insulin release is not proof that an inert cue supplies the therapeutic or nutritional effects of the original event.
