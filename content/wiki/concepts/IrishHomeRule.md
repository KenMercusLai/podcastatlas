# Irish Home Rule

## Definition

Irish Home Rule was the project of devolved self-government for Ireland within the United Kingdom: a major constitutional reform short of an independent republic and, for some nationalists, a step toward greater sovereignty.

## Current Synthesis

Home Rule grew from the increasing Westminster strength of [[IrishConstitutionalNationalism]]. In the 1870s the Home Rule Association became the Irish Parliamentary Party; [[CharlesStewartParnell]] linked its parliamentary campaign to land agitation and cooperation with Gladstone; and [[JohnRedmond]] later used the post-1910 balance of power and the Parliament Act to overcome the House of Lords' veto. The proposed settlement retained the monarchy and limited Irish powers, especially over defence and taxation.

Legislative progress did not create political consent. Nationalists repeatedly underestimated Ulster unionist attachment to the United Kingdom, prosperity, and freedom as they understood them. By 1912-1914, unionist resistance had helped militarize politics through the Ulster Volunteer Force, gun-running, and the Curragh incident, while nationalists answered through the [[IrishVolunteers|Irish Volunteers]].

For [[PatrickPearse]] and other future rebels, limits over finance and defence and the likely exclusion of six Ulster counties made the settlement incapable of satisfying sovereignty claims. Pearse's movement from support in 1912-1913 to IRB planning in 1915 demonstrates changing judgment rather than inevitable revolutionary opposition from the outset.

## Key Claims

- Home Rule offered devolution inside the United Kingdom rather than an independent republic.
- Parliamentary balance and reform of the Lords' veto made enactment possible after earlier bills failed.
- Some nationalists treated limited self-government as a stepping stone rather than a final settlement.
- Unionist resistance normalized volunteer armies and gun-running before 1914.
- Nationalist volunteer organization partly copied and answered Ulster mobilization.
- Limits over finance, defence, and territorial inclusion weakened the settlement's credibility for separatists.

- Legislative route and intended powers: [[337-ireland-union-famine-and-parnell-part-2-glt4375415331]] connects Parnell, Redmond, Gladstone, the Parliament Act, limited devolution, and the third bill.
- Unionist non-consent: [[337-ireland-union-famine-and-parnell-part-2-glt4375415331]] emphasizes nationalist and Liberal failure to grasp the depth of Ulster resistance.
- Militarization: [[339-ireland-the-easter-rising-1916-part-4-glt7095481415]] connects the UVF, Larne gun-running, Curragh incident, and Irish Volunteers to the Home Rule crisis.
- Constitutional limits: [[339-ireland-the-easter-rising-1916-part-4-glt7095481415]] identifies finance, defence, and likely exclusion of six Ulster counties as republican objections.
- Changing allegiance: [[339-ireland-the-easter-rising-1916-part-4-glt7095481415]] follows Pearse from Home Rule support into wartime separatist planning.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources do not reconstruct every bill, parliamentary negotiation, unionist argument, regional interest, exclusion proposal, or wartime suspension. The Rising does not prove that Home Rule was doomed or that its supporters were merely delayed separatists; nor does statutory enactment prove that implementation would have produced consent. The sources describe multiple Irish positions rather than unanimous nationalism or unionism.

## What Changed

- Added the parliamentary route from Parnell and Gladstone through Redmond and the Parliament Act.
- Distinguished statutory success from consent, implementation, and acceptance in Ulster.
