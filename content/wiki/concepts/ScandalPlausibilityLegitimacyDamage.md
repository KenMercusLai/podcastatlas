# Scandal Plausibility and Legitimacy Damage

## Definition

Scandal plausibility and legitimacy damage is the process by which a false allegation weakens an institution because audiences already consider the alleged conduct characteristic of its leaders, making exposure and adjudication amplify the story even when the targeted leader is innocent.

## Current Synthesis

The [[DiamondNecklaceAffair|Diamond Necklace Affair]] supplies the initial case. [[JeanneDeLaMotte|Jeanne de la Motte]] did not need genuine access to [[MarieAntoinette|Marie Antoinette]]; she needed [[CardinalDeRohan|Rohan]], the jewelers, and later publics to find secret royal extravagance plausible. A disguised lookalike, forged letters, court secrecy, and elite intermediaries made that belief actionable. Once the fraud became an open trial, legal memoirs, acquittals, punishment, and pamphlets gave the story more channels of circulation. The queen's factual innocence could not answer the different political question the scandal encouraged: why had so many people found the deception believable?

The concept therefore distinguishes truth from political fit. Prior hostility does not validate an allegation, but it can determine which falsehoods travel, which anomalies are excused, and whether public correction restores trust. It also distinguishes scandal from structural causation: reputational damage can supply images and emotions to a crisis whose fiscal, social, and institutional drivers lie elsewhere.

## Key Claims

- False allegations spread most effectively when they match an existing public model of the target.
- Motivated intermediaries can make weak evidence look mutually corroborating.
- Public investigation may amplify a scandal when proceedings widen circulation faster than they establish trusted facts.
- An acquittal can damage an uninvolved target when audiences infer that the accused person's belief was socially reasonable.
- Gendered, sexual, foreignness, and consumption stereotypes can combine into one legitimacy attack.
- Scandal is an accelerant and interpretive frame, not necessarily a sufficient cause of institutional collapse.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Plausibility is not evidence of truth. Marie Antoinette's innocence in the necklace transaction remains central, and later documented royal conduct cannot retroactively validate de la Motte's fabrication. Nor does a scandal explain the French Revolution by itself: fiscal breakdown, privilege, hunger, harvest failure, representative conflict, and court opposition remain distinct causes and contexts. The scale and composition of public response are reconstructed through partisan printed sources rather than comprehensive opinion data.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to distinguish factual guilt from the political effects of expectation-matched allegations.
- Added open adjudication as a possible amplification mechanism rather than an automatic route to trust repair.
- Bounded scandal as an accelerant within a larger structural crisis.
