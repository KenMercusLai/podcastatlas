# Scandal Plausibility and Legitimacy Damage

## Definition

Scandal plausibility and legitimacy damage is the process by which a false allegation weakens an institution because audiences already consider the alleged conduct characteristic of its leaders, making exposure and adjudication amplify the story even when the targeted leader is innocent.

## Current Synthesis

The case begins before the [[DiamondNecklaceAffair|Diamond Necklace Affair]]. [[MarieAntoinette|Marie Antoinette]] arrived as an Austrian dynastic bride, resisted [[Versailles]] etiquette, created an intimate circle, used the [[PetitTrianon|Petit Trianon]] as a sentimental retreat, and exercised visible influence over dress and hair. Court rivals interpreted those choices through foreignness, sexual impropriety, favoritism, and extravagant consumption. [[JeanneDeLaMotte|Jeanne de la Motte]] therefore did not need genuine access to the queen; she needed [[CardinalDeRohan|Rohan]], the jewelers, and later publics to find secret royal extravagance plausible. A disguised lookalike, forged letters, court secrecy, and elite intermediaries made that belief actionable. Once the fraud became an open trial, legal memoirs, acquittals, punishment, and pamphlets gave the story more channels of circulation. The queen's factual innocence could not answer the different political question the scandal encouraged: why had so many people found the deception believable?

The concept therefore distinguishes truth from political fit. Prior hostility does not validate an allegation, but it can determine which falsehoods travel, which anomalies are excused, and whether public correction restores trust. It also distinguishes scandal from structural causation: reputational damage can supply images and emotions to a crisis whose fiscal, social, and institutional drivers lie elsewhere.

## Key Claims

- False allegations spread most effectively when they match an existing public model of the target.
- Institutions can generate their own hostile priors when internal factions circulate gossip that wider publics later inherit.
- Motivated intermediaries can make weak evidence look mutually corroborating.
- Public investigation may amplify a scandal when proceedings widen circulation faster than they establish trusted facts.
- An acquittal can damage an uninvolved target when audiences infer that the accused person's belief was socially reasonable.
- Gendered, sexual, foreignness, and consumption stereotypes can combine into one legitimacy attack.
- Scandal is an accelerant and interpretive frame, not necessarily a sufficient cause of institutional collapse.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Plausibility is not evidence of truth. The sources do not establish the sexual relationships attributed to Marie Antoinette, and her innocence in the necklace transaction remains central; later documented royal conduct cannot retroactively validate court rumor or de la Motte's fabrication. Nor does a scandal explain the French Revolution by itself: fiscal breakdown, privilege, hunger, harvest failure, representative conflict, and court opposition remain distinct causes and contexts. The scale and composition of public response are reconstructed through partisan printed sources rather than comprehensive opinion data.

## What Changed

- Backfilled the hostile expectations that existed before the Diamond Necklace Affair.
- Added internal court faction and role ambiguity as sources of later public scandal plausibility.
- Preserved the distinction between unsupported rumor, factual innocence in the fraud, and later documented royal conduct.
