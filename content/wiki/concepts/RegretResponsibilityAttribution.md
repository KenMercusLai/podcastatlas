# Regret Responsibility Attribution

## Definition
Regret responsibility attribution is the task of judging one's real contribution to a harmful outcome without converting an imaginable alternative action into proof of total control or using context to erase personal agency.

## Current Synthesis
Regret naturally centers the self: “If I had acted differently” makes one intervention vivid while other people, conditions, uncertainties, and causal paths recede. After an unpredictable loss, that narrowed story can restore a feeling that the event was controllable, but emotional usefulness is not causal proof.

The same correction can run too far. Peer pressure and social context may help explain childhood bullying without excusing it, just as recognizing multiple causes of suicide can reduce unwarranted self-blame without denying ordinary duties of care. Accurate responsibility therefore requires both agency and context: identify the person's available choices and standards, then place them inside the broader causal network.

## Key Claims
- Vivid self-focused alternatives can make one person's causal contribution appear larger than the evidence supports.
- A simple “what if” may reduce uncertainty by creating perceived control over a senseless or unpredictable event.
- Personal agency and situational influence are compatible rather than mutually exclusive explanations.
- Context can qualify blame without automatically excusing harmful conduct or removing duties of repair.
- Victim blaming can arise when attention fixes on one action by the harmed person while neglecting the offender and surrounding circumstances.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Situational explanation can be abused as evasion, while responsibility language can be abused as totalizing shame. The episode does not provide a formula for assigning causal or moral shares, and a public summary cannot assess any listener's clinical condition, legal duty, knowledge, opportunity to intervene, or actual causal impact. Suicide and victimization require particular care: counterfactual review is not evidence that a survivor or victim controlled the outcome.

## What Changed
- Established a balanced attribution frame that preserves accountability while limiting control illusions and self-blame.
