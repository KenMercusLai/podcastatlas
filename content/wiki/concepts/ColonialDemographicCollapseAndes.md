# Colonial Demographic Collapse in the Andes

## Definition

Colonial demographic collapse in the Andes was the severe sixteenth-century population and social catastrophe produced by interacting epidemic disease, war, forced labor, displacement, food-system damage, violence, and despair after Spanish invasion.

## Current Synthesis

[[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] rejects a one-cause account. Smallpox and measles probably caused most deaths in the hosts' interpretation, but epidemic mortality occurred after Inca civil war and amid Spanish and Indigenous warfare, ruined canals and terraces, disrupted roads and food supplies, forced movement, mining, encomienda, and widespread coercion. Biological exposure and colonial structure therefore cannot be separated cleanly.

The episode also distinguishes catastrophe from a narrowly intentional extermination claim. Las Casas's language supplies a contemporary moral indictment, while reports of suicide, refusal to eat, poisoning, and infanticide indicate social despair beyond battlefield deaths. Calling the result multi-causal should preserve responsibility for violence and exploitative conditions rather than convert disease into exoneration.

## Key Claims

- Epidemic disease was probably the largest mortality mechanism described by the episode.
- War and destruction of irrigation, terraces, roads, food stores, and settlement systems amplified vulnerability.
- Forced labor, mining, displacement, hunger, and direct violence interacted with disease.
- Social despair appears in reports of suicide, self-starvation, poisoning, and infanticide.
- Catastrophic destruction does not require one cause or a proven unified intent to exterminate.
- Contemporary Spanish criticism makes moral condemnation historically rooted rather than purely retrospective.

## Evidence

- Mortality mechanisms: [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] identifies smallpox, measles, civil war, conquest war, hunger, and infrastructure destruction.
- Coercive institutions: [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] reports extensive forced labor and especially severe mining conditions at Potosí.
- Social despair: [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] attributes reports of suicide, refusal to eat, poisoning, and newborn killing to Philip II's later account.
- Moral interpretation: [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] uses [[BartolomeDeLasCasas|Las Casas]] to establish a contemporary Spanish language of destruction.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode does not supply a demographic series capable of allocating deaths among disease, violence, labor, hunger, migration, or fertility change. Its “four out of five men” labor claim and Potosí death estimates remain source-scoped. Rejecting “genocide” as the preferred label is one host's interpretive judgment and does not settle legal, comparative, or historiographical debate; nor does disease absolve conquerors of responsibility for imposed conditions.

## What Changed

- Established a multi-causal model joining disease to war, subsistence collapse, coercion, and despair.
- Preserved the distinction between catastrophic responsibility and disputed exterminatory intent.
