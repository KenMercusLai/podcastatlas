# Growth-to-Livability Transition / 从增长速度到城市宜居性

## Definition

Growth-to-livability transition is the shift a rapidly expanding city must make from maximizing construction, jobs, capital, and newcomer attraction toward enabling residents to afford housing, use mature public services, form families, enjoy public life, and remain over the long term.

## Current Synthesis

[[ep138-laile-jiushi-shenzhenren-cmcoosydpdsgbanyaqbwgizp|EP138]] uses [[Shenzhen]] to show why the institutions that create a growth city do not automatically create a mature home. Experimentation, infrastructure, finance, technology, youth, mobility, and fast physical change can attract people and opportunities. As the city grows larger and its earlier growth rate becomes harder to sustain, however, residents increasingly judge it through rent and home prices, commutes, school places, medical depth, cultural life, work pressure, street-level convenience, and time with family.

The transition is not a rejection of growth. It asks whether productive strength is converted into everyday capacity and whether informal urban life survives modernization. EP128 adds a planning mechanism: polycentric services, fifteen-minute access, urban-village affordability, and renewal that improves infrastructure without erasing low-cost housing. A city that continues to recruit workers while shifting housing to distant municipalities, redeveloping work-proximate rental supply, or leaving services behind population growth may preserve labor inflow without achieving durable settlement.

## Key Claims

- Infrastructure and industrial growth can outpace education, healthcare, housing, culture, and other resident-facing capacity.
- Slower percentage growth in a larger economy is not itself decline, but it changes what residents expect from government and urban development.
- Housing affordability is a metropolitan-life issue when lower-cost residence requires long cross-city commutes.
- Informal commerce and urban villages can supply affordability, convenience, and social texture even while requiring safety and infrastructure improvement.
- Distributed public services and short-distance daily access turn spatial growth into usable livability.
- A mature city's competitiveness includes whether workers can become long-term residents with family and non-work lives.

## Evidence

- Capacity mismatch: [[ep138-laile-jiushi-shenzhenren-cmcoosydpdsgbanyaqbwgizp|EP138]] contrasts rapid economic and physical development with shortages in school places, healthcare depth, housing, and cultural provision.
- Metropolitan housing tradeoff: [[ep138-laile-jiushi-shenzhenren-cmcoosydpdsgbanyaqbwgizp|EP138]] describes residents moving toward Dongguan or Huizhou while retaining costly cross-city commutes.
- Everyday-city value: [[ep138-laile-jiushi-shenzhenren-cmcoosydpdsgbanyaqbwgizp|EP138]] treats urban villages, breakfast stalls, boxed meals, stir-fries, and barbecue as housing and social infrastructure rather than disposable disorder.
- Planning mechanism: [[ep128-shiwunianyigao-de-chengshi-guihua-jiujing-zai-guihua-shenme-cmcocqgc36nfb4cpjgba98wr|EP128]] connects secondary centers, work-housing proximity, urban villages, service completion, and fifteen-minute access to everyday urban quality.
- Long-term settlement test: [[ep138-laile-jiushi-shenzhenren-cmcoosydpdsgbanyaqbwgizp|EP138]] says the decisive question is whether young workers can stay after marriage or children rather than merely arrive for work.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Both sources offer 2020 conversational diagnoses rather than current administrative or comparative evidence. Their reported GDP growth, school progression, talent-housing supply, medical travel, rent, planning effects, and project outcomes require official data and later evaluation. Public-service expansion takes time and may improve after the recording date. Urban villages and informal commerce provide access but can also involve safety, tenancy, labor, and service-quality problems that functional defense should not obscure.

## What Changed

- Added distributed centers, fifteen-minute services, and work-proximate affordable housing as planning mechanisms for the transition.
- Clarified that renewal must evaluate replacement affordability rather than assume redevelopment improves livability.
