# Founding Liberty-Slavery Self-Correction

## Definition

Founding liberty-slavery self-correction is the pattern where a founding-era figure who participated in slavery later revises his public position against it, without erasing the earlier contradiction.

## Current Synthesis

The Franklin episode grounds the concept in Benjamin Franklin's late life. Franklin owned enslaved people, brought enslaved people to London, considered seeking the return of one who absconded, and wrote a 1770 anonymous defense of slavery that the hosts call the lowest point of his career. Yet by February 1790 he petitioned Congress to extend liberty to enslaved people, and his final published satire reversed pro-slavery arguments by applying them to Europeans enslaved by North African powers. The current judgment is double-sided: self-correction matters, but it is meaningful only because the prior harm remains visible.

## Key Claims

- Late moral change can be historically important without functioning as absolution.
- A founder's liberty language becomes sharper when tested against his own participation in slavery.
- Satire can expose slavery apologetics by moving the same arguments into a reversed setting.
- The concept differs from pure reputation revision because the actor himself changes position, not only later interpreters.
- Franklin's path contrasts with Jefferson's unresolved dependence on slavery while remaining part of the same founding contradiction.

## Evidence

- Earlier participation: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says Franklin brought two enslaved people to London and had formerly owned slaves.
- Lowest point: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says Franklin wrote an anonymous 1770 defense of slavery, which the hosts call the lowest point of his career.
- Abolition petition: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says Franklin petitioned Congress in February 1790 to extend liberty to enslaved people.
- Final satire: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says Franklin published a satire under the pseudonym Sidi Mehmet Ibrahim using pro-slavery arguments to defend enslaving Europeans by North African powers.
- Source judgment: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] describes the satire as repentance and Franklin's final published work before death.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept must not treat late abolitionism as cancellation of prior ownership, attempted recapture logic, or pro-slavery writing. It also remains source-scoped to Franklin until additional sources add other cases. Jefferson's page supplies an adjacent founding contradiction, but not the same self-correction arc.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to hold Franklin's slaveholding, pro-slavery writing, late abolition petition, and final satire together.
