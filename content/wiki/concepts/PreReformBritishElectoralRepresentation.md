# Pre-Reform British Electoral Representation

## Definition

Pre-reform British electoral representation was a historically layered system in which parliamentary participation depended on property, local franchise custom, corporate privilege, and inherited borough status rather than equal individual citizenship or population.

## Current Synthesis

The source traces representation from medieval demands for consent to taxation, while stressing that the selection of early burgesses was not democratic in the modern sense. County voting became tied to the forty-shilling freehold, and boroughs used divergent rules involving rate payment, household hearths, closed corporations, or manufactured nominal freeholds.

This patchwork created broad variation in participation and extreme territorial mismatch. [[OldSarum]] could retain two MPs with only a handful of electors while Manchester had none, showing that representational rights attached to inherited places and interests more readily than to current population.

## Key Claims

- Representation emerged partly from the political need to secure consent for taxation.
- The franchise was structured by property, status, local custom, and exclusion rather than universal citizenship.
- Borough rules varied enough to create substantially different electorates across places.
- Land subdivision and corporate control could manufacture or restrict voting power.
- Inherited seat allocation could persist despite population and economic change.
- Reform expanded the electorate but did not immediately establish universal adult suffrage.

## Evidence

- Medieval origin: [[463-mad-elections-part-1-glt4220133865]] connects Simon de Montfort's 1265 parliament and the summoning of burgesses to taxation and consent while preserving uncertainty about selection.
- Franchise variation: [[463-mad-elections-part-1-glt4220133865]] describes forty-shilling county freeholders, scot-and-lot boroughs, potwalloper boroughs, corporation boroughs, and faggot voting.
- Territorial mismatch: [[463-mad-elections-part-1-glt4220133865]] contrasts [[OldSarum]]'s two MPs and tiny controlled electorate with Manchester's lack of MPs.
- Incomplete democratization: [[463-mad-elections-part-1-glt4220133865]] presents the 1832, 1867, and 1884 franchise expansions as large changes that still excluded women and many men.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode offers a broad narrative rather than a complete constitutional history. Local rules, actual turnout, eligibility enforcement, women's participation, and the representativeness of individual borough types varied over time; the suggestion that some wealthy widows voted under Tudor and Stuart monarchs is explicitly presented as probable rather than certain.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to distinguish inherited representation from modern population-based electoral equality.
