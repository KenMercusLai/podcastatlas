## Definition

弃边失屏 / Strategic Frontier Abandonment is the strategic mistake of treating a costly frontier, corridor, or outer buffer as removable budget pressure while ignoring its shield, depth, alliance, enemy-resource, and future-defense-cost functions.

## Current Synthesis

The abandonment error appears most clearly when a court frames frontier policy as a binary choice between expensive expedition and retreat. In the 120 CE Western Regions debate, Cao Zong wants a costly punitive expedition while other ministers want to close the western passes and abandon the region. [[BanYongLateHan|Ban Yong]] rejects both. He argues that the Western Regions deny Xiongnu taxes, manpower, and strategic reach, but that a treasury weakened by long Qiang war cannot support a deep campaign. His alternative is constrained retention through a forward Shanshan post backed by Dunhuang.

That case also makes implementation quality part of the concept. [[EmpressDowagerDengLateHan|Empress Dowager Deng]] accepts only the deputy-commandant and Dunhuang-garrison part of Ban Yong's proposal, leaving out the Shanshan forward post. The result is strategically weak: an office and an inner-edge garrison cannot deliver the same deterrent, reassurance, and route-control effect as the forward position.

The later Western Regions recurrence after renewed Northern Xiongnu and Cheshi pressure on Hexi confirms the same strategic accounting. [[ZhangDangLateHan|张珰]] answers from Dunhuang that the [[HexiFourCommanderiesLateHan|河西四郡]] cannot stand if the Western Regions are abandoned, and [[ChenZhongLateHan|陈忠]] argues that leaving Shanshan unsupported would push the Western Regions toward Northern Xiongnu, increasing Xiongnu resources, courage, and possible connection with Qiang pressure. [[EmperorAnOfHan|Emperor An]] accepts Chen Zhong's anti-abandonment argument and sends Ban Yong to Liuzhong.

The Liangzhou version shifts the geography but not the mechanism. [[CuiLieLateHan|崔烈]] proposes abandoning [[LiangzhouLateHan|凉州]] because rebellion and taxation have made it expensive, and [[FuXie|傅燮]] answers that the proposal would endanger the state. Liangzhou is described as a western gate and major route. If the court withdraws, mounted non-Han forces could take the region, gain horses and space, and return with a larger threat.

Together, the sources define the concept as a frontier-accounting error. Withdrawal may look like savings from the capital, but the abandoned space can become an enemy base, dissolve allied confidence, expose core corridors, and destroy the strategic depth that made the frontier worth holding. The remedy is not maximal war; the stronger sources all preserve cost discipline, graded options, and forward-position analysis.

## Key Claims

- A frontier region can be expensive and still be strategically cheaper to hold than to abandon.
- Tax burden, rebellion, treasury limits, and pass-defense costs are real constraints, but they do not by themselves settle whether withdrawal reduces danger or compounds it.
- Outer-buffer polities matter because abandonment can shift their alignment, taxes, soldiers, and legitimacy toward the enemy.
- Constrained retention depends on credible position, not only on naming an office or stationing troops too far from the pressure point.
- Frontier geography can convert a current local problem into a future core-region threat if enemy forces inherit the corridor, horses, alliance network, and depth.
- The concept supports limited commitment, graded alternatives, and strategic-depth analysis rather than a blanket rule that every border must always be held.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept should not become an automatic anti-withdrawal rule. Hanji 791 itself rejects an immediate large expedition, Hanji 799 preserves fallback options and limited garrisoning, and Hanji 934 occurs in a later fiscal and rebellion context with different capacity constraints. The shared claim is narrower: before abandoning a frontier, the court must price the buffer, corridor, alliance, enemy-resource, and future-defense effects, not only the immediate tax and deployment burden. Hanji 791 also cautions that token retention may fail if the part adopted is not the part that creates strategic leverage.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 791 as the earlier Western Regions case before the Hanji 799 Liuzhong decision.
- Made the cost-aware middle path explicit: Ban Yong rejects both deep expedition and total abandonment.
- Added partial implementation as a qualification because Deng's Dunhuang-only adoption omits Ban Yong's forward Shanshan mechanism.
- Preserved Hanji 934 as the Liangzhou, horse, corridor, and western-gate version.
