# Carthaginian Constitution / 迦太基政体

## Definition

The Carthaginian constitution is the mixed aristocratic order reconstructed from external evidence as combining wealthy elite rule with sufets, councils, judicial supervision, military commands, and some popular participation.

## Current Synthesis

The episode presents [[Carthage]] as an aristocratic republic in which wealth, breeding, and Phoenician inheritance structured power without reducing government to a single hereditary monarchy. Two sufets served as senior magistrates; a Council of Elders, Tribunal of 104, generals, officials, courts, and popular assembly distributed authority. Aristotle's admiration places Carthage alongside constitutions he considered stable, while stories of Hanno the Great's failed coup suggest that resistance to individual tyranny was an active institutional commitment.

Commercial agreement among ruling families supplied policy continuity: protect sea routes, secure markets and monopolies, negotiate treaties, suppress piracy, and limit rivals. Yet institutional names and functions remain uncertain because Carthaginian political writing is lost and Greek and Roman authors translated offices into their own categories. Herodotus calling Hamilcar a king, for example, may describe an elected or appointed leadership element rather than straightforward hereditary monarchy.

## Key Claims

- Carthage combined aristocratic dominance with multiple magistracies and collective bodies.
- Sufets, elders, judicial supervisors, commanders, and an assembly distributed political functions.
- The ruling elite's shared commercial interests supported stable external policy.
- Institutional resistance to individual tyranny helps explain Aristotle's favorable comparison.
- Military failure could be investigated through bodies such as the Tribunal of 104.
- Greek and Roman terminology makes exact constitutional equivalence unsafe.

## Evidence

- Institutional structure: [[422-ancient-carthage-rise-of-a-superpower-part-2-glt7946297144]] names the sufets, Council of Elders, Tribunal of 104, generals, courts, and popular assembly.
- External assessment: [[422-ancient-carthage-rise-of-a-superpower-part-2-glt7946297144]] cites Aristotle's admiration for Carthage's constitution.
- Anti-tyranny boundary: [[422-ancient-carthage-rise-of-a-superpower-part-2-glt7946297144]] uses Hanno the Great's failed coup and punishment as evidence that elite dominance did not license personal monarchy.
- Policy coherence: [[422-ancient-carthage-rise-of-a-superpower-part-2-glt7946297144]] connects governing consensus to trade, treaties, sea control, market protection, and suppression of piracy.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The constitution survives through external authors using Greek and Roman political vocabulary after Carthaginian writings were destroyed or dispersed. Office functions, selection procedures, chronology, popular power, Magonid dominance, and the historicity of coup narratives remain incompletely recoverable. Aristotle's praise is evidence of comparative reputation, not proof that the system was equitable or consistently stable.
