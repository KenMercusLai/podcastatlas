# Scientist Mode Thinking

## Definition
Scientist mode thinking is a stance in which opinions are treated as hypotheses and decisions as experiments, so the aim becomes learning what is true rather than defending identity or winning approval.

## Current Synthesis
[[AdamGrant]] contrasts scientist mode with three common social postures: preaching to protect a belief, prosecuting to defeat another view, and politicking to win approval from an existing audience. Scientist mode changes the success condition from appearing right to updating well.

The practice therefore depends on exposure to reasoned disagreement, explicit tests, and willingness to revise. Grant describes a rethinking cycle of humility, doubt, curiosity, and learning as an alternative to conviction, confirmation bias, and arrogance. Following people with intellectual integrity can reveal blind spots, but social-media signals are easily mistaken for endorsement and stripped of context.

[[PrincipleStrategySeparation]] supplies an important boundary: scientist mode need not make core values disposable, but it should make plans and causal assumptions testable. The method works better when claims, tests, and revision criteria are concrete rather than when "experiment" is used as a metaphor for unstructured trial and error.

## Key Claims
- Opinions become easier to revise when separated from moral identity and social status.
- Decisions can generate evidence when predictions and outcomes are made explicit.
- Reasoned disagreement is useful when the other person's process has integrity.
- Preaching, prosecuting, and politicking can each substitute identity defense for inquiry.
- Updating well is a distinct achievement from having guessed correctly at the outset.
- Stable principles can coexist with revisable plans and causal assumptions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Not every decision can be safely or ethically experimental, and ambiguous outcomes can reinforce prior beliefs rather than correct them. Social media rarely supplies full context, while following an account may be publicly interpreted as endorsement. The sources offer a reasoning posture, not evidence that adopting the label reliably removes bias; principles themselves can also require scrutiny rather than being protected from all revision.
