# Colonial Source Mediation

## Definition

Colonial source mediation is the problem of reconstructing colonized people's lives and categories through evidence shaped by translation, conquest reports, legal self-defense, journalism, military and settler archives, later interviews, folklore, retrospective memory, and performance for outsider audiences.

## Current Synthesis

The sources do not discard mediated evidence, but they refuse to treat translated quotations, conquest narratives, or polished biographies as transparent access to Indigenous thought. In the Mexico-conquest opening, Spanish accounts may misunderstand unfamiliar political forms, translate strategically, or reshape events to justify authority. The gods-and-surrender tradition is therefore not a secure baseline merely because later victors repeated it, while reversal alone does not prove an alternative.

The Caribbean and coastal stories show different mediation layers. [[Hatuey]]'s gold warning and baptism refusal preserve an Indigenous moral indictment whose exact wording and transmission remain uncertain. Reports of sacrifice, the alleged origin of “Yucatan,” Totonac statements, gift exchanges, and remembered dialogue combine language gaps, fear, genre, and later colonial purpose.

In the Plains case, [[SittingBull]]'s reported prenatal vision came through an 1877 newspaper interview, and his later participation in Buffalo Bill's show complicates any effort to separate self-presentation from a role expected by white audiences.

[[CrazyHorse]] presents a different but related problem. His avoidance of photography and public performance leaves fewer apparently direct records, while accounts of his appearance, visions, rituals, youth, personality, and tactics survive through later narration and legend. Less performance does not automatically mean unmediated truth; it changes the form of uncertainty.

The method also requires conceptual caution. Lakota accounts did not necessarily divide natural from supernatural, religion from ordinary life, or “medicine” from power in the ways modern Western writing does. Translation can therefore distort categories even when a speaker, interpreter, or reporter acts sincerely.

Journal-based Pacific first contact extends the same problem. Banks can record surfing, tattooing, language, bodily display, and exchange with genuine curiosity while still sexualizing or objectifying what he sees. Tupia's translation restores some Indigenous agency, but Tahitian, Māori, and Aboriginal perspectives remain mostly reconstructed through British observers. Apparent indifference, theft, threat, friendship, or consent must therefore be read as encounter interpretations rather than transparent local motives.

## Key Claims

- A translated newspaper quotation is evidence of an encounter among speaker, interpreter, reporter, genre, and audience, not a verbatim transparent voice.
- A conquest report can record real encounter while also serving legal authority, moral justification, personal reputation, and claims to reward.
- Later performance or refusal of spectacle changes the form of mediation without making every statement false or eliminating retrospective legend.
- Oral and visionary accounts should be preserved as evidence while their recording conditions and cultural categories remain explicit.
- Western natural-supernatural and religion-secular divisions can misdescribe Lakota accounts before factual evaluation even begins.
- Source scarcity makes graded confidence more responsible than either romantic acceptance or wholesale dismissal.
- Scientific journals can preserve indispensable detail while still projecting European categories, desire, property norms, and imperial purpose.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- Mediation does not make a source useless; it changes what can responsibly be inferred and how precisely wording can be attributed.
- All three episodes are outsider-produced popular-history sources and therefore participate in the problem they identify.
- Crazy Horse's distance from public performance does not prove that later stories about him are less shaped by political memory or audience desire.
- Indigenous oral histories are not intrinsically less reliable than written records; both require attention to transmission, purpose, genre, and power.
- The page does not independently compare Lakota-language testimony, original newspaper text, military records, or specialist biographies.
- The Cook source does not provide a complete Tahitian, Māori, or Aboriginal counterarchive against which British journals can be checked.

## What Changed

- Extended the framework from translated Plains evidence to conquest reports shaped by language gaps, legal interest, Christian justification, and victory memory.
- Added the distinction between preserving an Indigenous moral tradition and claiming verbatim certainty for its remembered speech.
- Established a source-criticism framework for translated, journalistic, retrospective, and performed Indigenous evidence.
- Distinguished explicit mediation from evidentiary dismissal.
- Extended the framework to scientific journals, bodily interpretation, trade categories, and translated Pacific first contact.
