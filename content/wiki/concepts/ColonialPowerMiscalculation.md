# Colonial Power Miscalculation / 殖民强权误判

## Definition
Colonial power miscalculation is the strategic error of judging an expanding industrial-colonial power through an older regional hierarchy, tribute logic, or past military confidence, causing a state to underestimate the opponent's capacity and escalation threshold.

## Current Synthesis
The Myanmar episode uses early nineteenth-century Burma as its case. Burma had been a formidable regional military power, but the episode argues that this prior strength became misleading once expansion put it beside British India. Demands that British Bengal submit, plans to take Calcutta, and confidence before the First Anglo-Burmese War are presented as signs that the Burmese court interpreted Britain through a familiar dominance frame rather than through the material and imperial capacity Britain could actually mobilize.

The Ye Mingchen episode adds an individual-institutional case. Ye's earlier success against local rebellion and a limited British retreat gave him evidence that firmness worked, while an amateur information network supplied false reports about British weakness in Crimea, Hong Kong, and India. Real administrative ability and prior success therefore did not protect judgment; they helped create a model that was repeatedly confirmed by poor information and then misapplied to renewed Anglo-French escalation.

The pattern is not simply "small state loses to large empire." Its analytical value is in the transition: a state or official with real regional capability can still make a disastrous judgment if inherited maps of status, past success, distance, information, and force no longer match the adversary's operating system.

## Key Claims
- Past regional dominance can become a cognitive trap when the strategic environment shifts.
- Territorial expansion can create new border contact with an opponent whose scale and methods are poorly understood.
- Diplomatic demands based on older hierarchy can escalate conflict when the other side operates from a different sovereignty and empire logic.
- A single military defeat can become a sovereignty shock when it produces indemnities, territorial concessions, and permanent diplomatic intrusion.
- Prior success can become an experience trap when tactics suited to local rebellion or limited confrontation are generalized to industrial-imperial war.
- Intelligence systems can worsen error when unprofessional collection and confirmation bias repeatedly validate an official's preferred forecast.

- Misjudgment signal: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-395-3-dongnanya-bazhu-miandian-shi-ruhe-shuailuo-de-lizilinr6epqdeglne27rrb7eipf]] presents Burmese demands toward Bengal and plans against Calcutta as overconfident misunderstanding of British strength.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both sources are popular-history summaries rather than specialist military histories. They do not fully document British logistics, Burmese or Qing deliberation, local politics, casualty figures, treaty law, or competing explanations. In Ye's case, force shortages, imperial coercion, divided local leadership, and the disputed Arrow incident limit any personality-first explanation. Use the concept as a comparative mechanism, not a complete causal account of either defeat.
