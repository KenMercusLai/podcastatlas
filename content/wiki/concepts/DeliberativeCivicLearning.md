## Definition

Deliberative civic learning is the development of public-policy knowledge and judgment through shared instruction, procedural participation, exchange across lived experience, and repeated collective decision-making.

## Current Synthesis

The bounded sources show civic competence as an institutional product rather than a fixed entry credential. Connecticut assembly participants moved from limited tax knowledge toward system-level reasoning by studying revenue, school finance, municipal services, and tradeoffs, while also learning how speaking rules and group norms shape collective judgment.

The Topical Talk classroom adds an earlier-age, non-decision setting. Current-affairs knowledge, question formulation, AI-output checking, explanation, and respectful disagreement are practiced together, and teachers report that the format can widen participation beyond conventionally academic pupils. Across both cases, substantive knowledge and procedural voice reinforce each other only when institutions allocate time, structure turns, and make evidence review explicit. The cases support feasibility, not proof that every assembly or classroom will learn accurately, participate equally, or reach superior conclusions.

## Key Claims

- Ordinary participants can acquire substantial domain knowledge when a process supplies time and instruction.
- Personal tax frustration becomes more analytically useful when connected to revenue, spending, redistribution, and institutional structure.
- Learning includes procedural competence, such as regulating speaking turns and protecting quieter participation.
- Diverse experience can complement expert instruction by revealing how policy burdens are distributed.
- Ongoing disagreement is compatible with collective learning and recommendation.
- Social trust can emerge from repeated collaboration, though it is not guaranteed.
- Children can practice deliberative competence before formal citizenship when discussion joins topical knowledge, evidence checking, explanation, and respectful disagreement.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The source does not provide pre/post knowledge tests, participant-level survey data, or independent measures of reasoning quality.
- Expert selection and curriculum design can shape what participants learn and which options appear feasible.
- Stronger relationships do not establish factual accuracy, fair drafting, or policy effectiveness.
- The revision dispute shows that learning and goodwill can coexist with contested process legitimacy.
- The classroom evidence is one observed lesson plus participant, teacher, and programme testimony; it does not independently establish durable transfer, equal benefit, or the reported comparative skill gain.

## What Changed

- Added a policy-specific case in which civic competence developed through designed participation.
- Distinguished substantive learning, procedural learning, and social trust as related but separate outcomes.
- Extended the framework to primary-school current-affairs discussion and AI-answer scrutiny.
- Added protected curriculum time as an institutional input rather than treating discussion as spontaneous.
