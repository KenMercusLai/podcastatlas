## Definition

Deliberative civic learning is the development of public-policy knowledge and judgment through shared instruction, procedural participation, exchange across lived experience, and repeated collective decision-making.

## Current Synthesis

The Connecticut assembly shows civic competence as an institutional product rather than a fixed entry credential. Participants moved from limited tax knowledge toward system-level reasoning by studying revenue, school finance, municipal services, and tradeoffs, while also learning how speaking rules and group norms shape collective judgment. The case supports feasibility, not proof that every assembly will learn accurately or reach superior conclusions.

## Key Claims

- Ordinary participants can acquire substantial domain knowledge when a process supplies time and instruction.
- Personal tax frustration becomes more analytically useful when connected to revenue, spending, redistribution, and institutional structure.
- Learning includes procedural competence, such as regulating speaking turns and protecting quieter participation.
- Diverse experience can complement expert instruction by revealing how policy burdens are distributed.
- Ongoing disagreement is compatible with collective learning and recommendation.
- Social trust can emerge from repeated collaboration, though it is not guaranteed.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The source does not provide pre/post knowledge tests, participant-level survey data, or independent measures of reasoning quality.
- Expert selection and curriculum design can shape what participants learn and which options appear feasible.
- Stronger relationships do not establish factual accuracy, fair drafting, or policy effectiveness.
- The revision dispute shows that learning and goodwill can coexist with contested process legitimacy.

## What Changed

- Added a policy-specific case in which civic competence developed through designed participation.
- Distinguished substantive learning, procedural learning, and social trust as related but separate outcomes.
