# Digital Olfaction

## Definition
Digital olfaction is the effort to measure, model, transmit, or reproduce perceived smell by connecting chemical composition and molecular structure to human odor perception.

## Current Synthesis
The source frames digitizing smell as a mapping problem between molecules, mixtures, and perceptual similarity. A useful system does not merely identify one molecule; it must predict how mixtures will smell, find different chemical mixtures that produce similar perceptions, and recreate a target odor under controlled conditions.

The reported Germany-to-Israel demonstration is a proof of concept rather than a general smell codec. Violet was recognized by 15 of 16 participants, but attempted rose and anise reconstructions were perceived mainly as bubble gum and cherry, while gasoline performed only slightly above chance. The long-term diagnostic idea is plausible because disease can alter metabolism and emitted chemicals, but turning odor signatures into dependable clinical decisions would require standardized sampling, validated classifiers, specificity, replication, and comparison with existing tests.

## Key Claims
- Digital olfaction requires a quantitative mapping between chemical structure and perceived odor.
- Mixtures matter because real-world smells are rarely single molecules.
- Olfactory metamers are chemically different mixtures intended to produce similar perceptual experiences.
- Remote odor recreation is possible in bounded demonstrations but is not yet equivalent to high-resolution universal smell transmission.
- Success with one target odor does not establish reliable reconstruction across odor classes.
- Medical use requires stronger validation than perceptual matching alone.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The supplied summaries do not establish generalization across odors, devices, people, environments, or disease states. Incorrect rose and anise perceptions and near-chance gasoline performance show that one violet success is not a general codec. Perceptual similarity is not chemical identity, and a disease-associated odor signature may lack the specificity needed for diagnosis. The medical application is prospective and should not be read as an available clinical test.
