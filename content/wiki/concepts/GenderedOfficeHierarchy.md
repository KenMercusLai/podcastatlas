# Gendered Office Hierarchy

## Definition
Gendered office hierarchy is an organizational pattern in which lower-status clerical and support roles are feminized while managerial authority, promotion control, credit, and acceptable workplace behavior remain male-dominated.

## Current Synthesis
The episode uses [[NineToFiveFilm|9 to 5]] and its production research to make this hierarchy visible. Clerical workers were numerous and often women, while bosses and upper-level roles were men; the result was not only lower pay but a daily structure of servitude, harassment, rumor, promotion exclusion, and personal tasks being treated as secretarial work.

The hierarchy also appears behind the camera. [[PatriciaResnick]] says she was kept off the set after writing from worker research, and the episode compares that to the film's own theme of men taking credit for women's work. The concept therefore links formal hierarchy with symbolic control: who performs office labor, who gets public credit, and who has the language to name mistreatment.

## Key Claims
- Gender hierarchy in offices can be built into role allocation before any individual boss acts badly.
- Personal-service requests, humiliation, harassment, and credit-taking become easier when support work is treated as naturally female.
- Promotion exclusion and unequal pay are structural expressions of the same hierarchy, not separate side issues.
- Cultural representation can expose hierarchy by showing ordinary office routines as systematic rather than normal.
- The lack of settled vocabulary can hide abuse even when the behavior is widely recognized by workers.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source focuses on 1970s and 1980s U.S. clerical work as represented through a film and organizer interviews. It should not be generalized to every office, industry, or time period without additional evidence, and the episode itself notes limits around race because the film centered white women.
