# Palace Scandal Power Consolidation / 宫廷丑闻式收权

Palace scandal power consolidation / 宫廷丑闻式收权 is the pattern [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-108-3-huangtaihou-he-qingfu-laoai-qingse-beihou-de-qishi-lthiqkpc0nbnlkxhcmivufvgl25b|Qinji 108-3]] draws from the [[LaoAi|嫪毐]] affair. A private relationship between [[ZhaoJi|赵姬]] and Lao Ai becomes politically usable once it produces children, rumor, retainers, fief power, and succession talk around [[QinShiHuang|嬴政 / 秦始皇]]'s personal-rule threshold.

The concept does not say scandal automatically creates power. It names a narrower sequence: an intimate court secret becomes public, the exposed actor panics or rebels, the ruler can then frame a larger purge as defense of legitimacy, and adjacent power holders become easier to remove or discipline. In this source, the same crisis lets Ying Zheng destroy Lao Ai's faction, confine Zhao Ji, kill the two sons, and push [[LuBuwei|吕不韦]] out of the center after postponing harsher punishment.

The pattern also has an interpretation risk. If the scandal is remembered only as sexual disorder, the power-consolidation work becomes invisible; if it is remembered only as rational consolidation, [[GenderedHistoriographicalDesireFraming|性别化史书欲望叙事]] and Zhao Ji's constrained position disappear. The useful reading keeps both layers active.

## Key Claims
- A palace scandal becomes a power event when it touches succession, seals, military mobilization, court factions, or royal legitimacy.
- The ruler's punitive response can be both personal rage and institutional consolidation.
- Scandal can create a politically acceptable path to remove actors who were already dangerous but not yet easy to attack directly.
- The same event can be gendered in memory, with women reduced to sexual cause while the surrounding male power struggle is treated as statecraft.
