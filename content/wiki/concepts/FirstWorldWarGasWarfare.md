# First World War Gas Warfare

## Definition

First World War gas warfare is the chemical-weapons branch in [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]], where gas appears as a frightening technological shortcut against trench stalemate that became morally symbolic but failed to produce decisive breakthrough.

## Current Synthesis

The episode treats gas as both tactical experiment and moral escalation. Germany's chemical expertise and strategic desperation encouraged chlorine use at Ypres in April 1915, while lawyers and officers debated legality, retaliation, wind risk, and scandal. Gas terrified soldiers because it could move silently and destroy lungs, but the episode stresses that it did not break the deadlock.

The British use of gas at [[BattleOfLoos|Loos]] sharpens the lesson. Gas cylinders, bad wind, fogged masks, and rigid offensive planning turned the weapon back against British troops. Gas therefore matters in this source less as an efficient killer than as a symbol of modern war's physical and moral contamination.

## Key Claims

- Gas was pursued as a hoped-for way around trench stalemate.
- Legal and moral arguments around gas were contested from the start.
- Gas caused terror disproportionate to its overall share of deaths because it attacked breath, visibility, and bodily control.
- Defensive adaptation reduced its tactical surprise through improvised cloths, pads, and later respirators.
- British gas at Loos shows that new weapons could intensify disaster when used under bad conditions and poor tactical understanding.

## Evidence

- German use at Ypres: [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]] describes chlorine released from thousands of cylinders toward French-Algerian troops on 22 April 1915 and later Canadian exposure.
- Tactical limits: [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]] says gas did not deliver breakthrough at Ypres and caused about 6,000 British and Imperial gas deaths across the war.
- British failure at Loos: [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]] describes British gas blowing back, masks fogging, and high casualties at Loos.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This page does not yet cover all gases, delivery systems, armies, casualty estimates, treaties, medical effects, or later chemical-weapons history. The source treats gas as symbolically central but tactically limited, not as the main killer of the war.
