# China Neurotechnology Industrial Policy

## Definition
China neurotechnology industrial policy is the coordinated use of strategic designation, local investment, regulation, reimbursement infrastructure, education, clinical trials, and commercialization to build a neurotechnology ecosystem.

## Current Synthesis
The episode argues that China’s route to closing the American neurotechnology lead is ecosystem-scale rather than dependent on one frontier company. National priority draws local capital and regulatory attention; ethical guidelines, billing codes, reimbursement work, and university training build institutional support; and competition across implants, prosthetics, suppliers, and consumer devices may reduce costs and generate stronger firms. This is a capacity-building thesis, not evidence that technological leadership has already changed hands.

## Key Claims
- Strategic designation can coordinate investment, regulation, education, and commercialization around an emerging industry.
- Billing and reimbursement infrastructure matter because a clinical device needs an adoption path as well as technical feasibility.
- A broad supplier and competitor base can lower costs even when few firms operate at the frontier.
- Medical and consumer neurotechnology follow different evidence, risk, and adoption paths.
- Consumer openness may accelerate commercialization without proving efficacy or safety.

## Evidence
- Policy stack - [[independence-eh-canadas-referendum-d1db236273c4dba7fc1e1a7b66cd08a1]] cites strategic priority since 2015, later planning, 2024 ethical guidance, billing codes, reimbursement work, and Tianjin University training.
- Company ecosystem - [[independence-eh-canadas-referendum-d1db236273c4dba7fc1e1a7b66cd08a1]] names BrainCo, NeuroXS, Stermed, and NeuroCyber across non-invasive and implanted approaches.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode says America still leads and that few Chinese firms operate at the technological frontier. Trial progress, ethical guidance, training programs, and market size do not by themselves establish clinical efficacy, safety, reimbursement uptake, consumer benefit, or durable global leadership.
