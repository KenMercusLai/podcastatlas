# Role-Asymmetric Surrender Incentives / 身份不对称的投降激励

## Definition
Role-asymmetric surrender incentives describe a decision in which people inside the same regime face materially different outcomes from capitulation. Officials may preserve life, office, or status by serving the victor, while the defeated ruler's independent authority makes demotion, confinement, humiliation, or death a distinct risk.

## Current Synthesis
[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1101-tongwei-geju-zhuhou-sunquan-pingsha-gan-gen-caocao-jiaoban-lo1cdnjb5iljou-1ojzxso10eqcv|Hanji 1101]] develops the concept through [[LuSu|鲁肃]]'s private response to [[SunQuan|孙权]] during [[CaoCao|曹操]]'s post-[[Jingzhou|荆州]] pressure. [[ZhangZhaoLateHan|张昭]] assesses military and legitimacy asymmetry at regime level: Cao Cao invokes the Han court, has acquired Jingzhou's fleet, and threatens Jiangdong with overwhelming force. Lu Su does not answer that military case in the surviving transcript. He changes the unit of analysis.

Lu Su argues that Jiangdong's ministers and Sun Quan do not share one surrender payoff. A minister can plausibly enter Cao Cao's order and seek another office; Sun Quan is the rival sovereign center whose command must be neutralized. The episode therefore treats “what preserves the organization?” and “what preserves the ruler?” as related but non-identical questions. This does not prove resistance is militarily sound. It shows why advice from a comparatively portable official may underweight the principal's non-portable political exposure.

## Key Claims
- Surrender payoffs vary by role even when actors belong to the same regime.
- Officials with portable skills or status may have a credible post-surrender career path that the ruler lacks.
- A ruler's authority is itself the object the victor must neutralize, making personal safety inseparable from strategic choice.
- Regime-level prudence can therefore diverge from ruler-level prudence without either side being reducible to courage or cowardice.
- Persuasion becomes stronger when it identifies whose risk is being optimized rather than debating aggregate danger alone.

## Evidence
- Unequal exit options: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1101-tongwei-geju-zhuhou-sunquan-pingsha-gan-gen-caocao-jiaoban-lo1cdnjb5iljou-1ojzxso10eqcv|Hanji 1101]] has Lu Su contrast his own plausible survival and reemployment with Sun Quan's uncertain fate after surrender.
- Regime-level case for capitulation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1101-tongwei-geju-zhuhou-sunquan-pingsha-gan-gen-caocao-jiaoban-lo1cdnjb5iljou-1ojzxso10eqcv|Hanji 1101]] gives Zhang Zhao's court-legitimacy, naval-strength, and lost-river-barrier arguments, showing that surrender advocacy is grounded in a real strategic asymmetry rather than mere panic.
- Principal-specific exposure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1101-tongwei-geju-zhuhou-sunquan-pingsha-gan-gen-caocao-jiaoban-lo1cdnjb5iljou-1ojzxso10eqcv|Hanji 1101]] repeatedly frames Sun Quan's dignity, independent command, and life as risks that cannot be inferred from what happens to ordinary ministers.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is incomplete: it ends before Lu Su's full case and Sun Quan's final decision. The concept should not imply that advisers always prefer surrender, that rulers always rationally resist, or that personal survival automatically aligns with civilian or regime welfare. Zhang Zhao's motives are interpreted by the host; the surviving material establishes his argument more securely than his private intent. Cao Cao's troop total is also a coercive claim in a threatening letter, not a verified force count.

## What Changed
- Created the concept from the episode's explicit minister-versus-ruler comparison.
- Separated aggregate military prudence from the distribution of surrender risk inside a regime.
- Preserved the incomplete-transcript boundary and the uncertainty around private motives.
