# Role-Asymmetric Surrender Incentives / 身份不对称的投降激励

## Definition
Role-asymmetric surrender incentives describe a decision in which people inside the same regime face materially different outcomes from capitulation. Officials may preserve life, office, or status by serving the victor, while the defeated ruler's independent authority makes demotion, confinement, humiliation, or death a distinct risk.

## Current Synthesis
Hanji 1092 adds the surrendering side before the later Jiangdong debate. [[LiuCongLateHan|刘琮]] is a young successor dependent on a Jingzhou elite coalition whose members can plausibly survive regime change. [[FuXunLateHan|傅巽]] argues that relying on [[LiuBei|刘备]] cannot protect Liu Cong: if Liu Bei loses, Jingzhou falls; if Liu Bei wins, his superior force makes the nominal ruler dispensable. The case shows that role asymmetry can exist not only between adviser and ruler after surrender, but also between a weak ruler and the stronger protector invited to save him.

Hanji 1101 develops the concept through [[LuSu|鲁肃]]'s private response to [[SunQuan|孙权]] during [[CaoCao|曹操]]'s post-[[Jingzhou|荆州]] pressure. [[ZhangZhaoLateHan|张昭]] assesses military and legitimacy asymmetry at regime level: Cao Cao invokes the Han court, has acquired Jingzhou's fleet, and threatens Jiangdong with overwhelming force. Lu Su changes the unit of analysis.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1102-lusu-yijuhua-chuoxing-sunquan-toujiang-caocao-de-houguo-you-duo-kepa-lh0fwhkq9g9imfm1esblolh5gzjz|Hanji 1102]] completes the comparison. Lu Su says that his reputation and skills might still yield office under Cao Cao, whereas Sun Quan as the rival sovereign center faces demotion, surveillance, and possible elimination. The episode therefore treats “what preserves the organization?”, “what preserves the advisers?”, and “what preserves the ruler?” as related but non-identical questions. This does not by itself prove resistance is militarily sound; Zhou Yu supplies that separate case. It shows why advice from a comparatively portable official may underweight the principal's non-portable political exposure.

## Key Claims
- Surrender payoffs vary by role even when actors belong to the same regime.
- Officials with portable skills or status may have a credible post-surrender career path that the ruler lacks.
- A weak ruler may also face role asymmetry with a stronger protector whose success can erase the ruler's remaining autonomy.
- A ruler's authority is itself the object the victor must neutralize, making personal safety inseparable from strategic choice.
- Regime-level prudence can therefore diverge from ruler-level prudence without either side being reducible to courage or cowardice.
- Persuasion becomes stronger when it identifies whose risk is being optimized rather than debating aggregate danger alone.
- Role-risk diagnosis and military feasibility remain separate tests; a ruler's surrender danger does not make every resistance plan sound.

## Evidence
- Unequal exit options: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1101-tongwei-geju-zhuhou-sunquan-pingsha-gan-gen-caocao-jiaoban-lo1cdnjb5iljou-1ojzxso10eqcv|Hanji 1101]] has Lu Su contrast his own plausible survival and reemployment with Sun Quan's uncertain fate after surrender.
- Weak-ruler dilemma: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1092-fu-ke-di-guo-de-lougui-yin-yiju-gankai-bei-caocao-cisi-lutwkvmtn_7cv4r5l-mwknmgyjhs|Hanji 1092]] has Fu Xun argue that Liu Bei's defeat cannot preserve Liu Cong, while Liu Bei's victory would leave the stronger protector unlikely to defer to him.
- Elite portability: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1092-fu-ke-di-guo-de-lougui-yin-yiju-gankai-bei-caocao-cisi-lutwkvmtn_7cv4r5l-mwknmgyjhs|Hanji 1092]] places Liu Cong's decision inside a Jingzhou elite coalition seeking family and status survival under Cao Cao.
- Completed outcome contrast: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1102-lusu-yijuhua-chuoxing-sunquan-toujiang-caocao-de-houguo-you-duo-kepa-lh0fwhkq9g9imfm1esblolh5gzjz|Hanji 1102]] specifies office continuity for the adviser against lost authority, surveillance, and possible death for the ruler.
- Regime-level case for capitulation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1101-tongwei-geju-zhuhou-sunquan-pingsha-gan-gen-caocao-jiaoban-lo1cdnjb5iljou-1ojzxso10eqcv|Hanji 1101]] gives Zhang Zhao's court-legitimacy, naval-strength, and lost-river-barrier arguments, showing that surrender advocacy is grounded in a real strategic asymmetry rather than mere panic.
- Principal-specific exposure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1101-tongwei-geju-zhuhou-sunquan-pingsha-gan-gen-caocao-jiaoban-lo1cdnjb5iljou-1ojzxso10eqcv|Hanji 1101]] repeatedly frames Sun Quan's dignity, independent command, and life as risks that cannot be inferred from what happens to ordinary ministers.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept should not imply that advisers always prefer surrender, that rulers always rationally resist, or that personal survival automatically aligns with civilian or regime welfare. Hanji 1092's claims about Jingzhou elite motives and Liu Bei's hypothetical post-victory behavior are interpretations and counterfactuals, not observed outcomes. Zhang Zhao's motives are likewise less secure than his stated argument, while Lu Su's career forecast and Sun Quan's projected fate remain counterfactual. Cao Cao's troop total is a coercive claim in a threatening letter, not a verified force count.

## What Changed
- Added Liu Cong's pre-surrender case, extending the concept from adviser-ruler exit asymmetry to weak-ruler versus strong-protector asymmetry.
- Preserved the distinction between stated strategic arguments and unverified private motives or counterfactual outcomes.
