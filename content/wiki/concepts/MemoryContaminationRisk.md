# Memory Contamination Risk

## Definition
Memory contamination risk is the danger that remembered experience can be altered by labels, suggestion, social expectations, hypnosis, therapeutic framing, fear, later identification, or the interpreter's preferred story.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded evidence covers interacting contamination routes. Labels, stereotypes, priming, and distraction can shape later recall; cultural scripts, hypnosis, and emotionally satisfying explanations can supply material; and an eyewitness lineup can replace the weak original trace with a selected face and greater confidence. The Eagleman interview adds weapon focus, repeated recall, post-event information, and suggestion-based false-memory formation. Stress can strengthen salience while narrowing what was encoded, so a memory can be emotionally durable without being complete or exact.

The current judgment is careful rather than dismissive. Sincerity and accuracy are different questions, but memory caution should not turn into contempt for witnesses or people describing real harm. The practical response is corroboration, context protection, slower inference, and awareness that confident memory can still be reconstructed.

## Key Claims
- Sincerity and accuracy are different questions.
- Labels, stereotypes, and distraction can make later recall feel like evidence for a story introduced from outside.
- Hypnosis, fear, media templates, or emotionally satisfying explanations can amplify memory authority while adding suggestion.
- Eyewitness identification can contaminate later memory, especially when confidence rises after a lineup or confirmation.
- Memory caution should protect truth-seeking and vulnerable people rather than erase real harm.
- Extraordinary or high-stakes claims need corroboration beyond confident testimony.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept does not mean all testimony is false or that memory evidence has no value. It means memory reports need context, corroboration, non-leading interview design, preservation of early accounts, and caution about later suggestion. The “lost in the mall,” weapon-focus, and post-9/11 examples are summarized through a public-neuroscience interview rather than assessed here as a full forensic-psychology literature.
