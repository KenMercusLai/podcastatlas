## Definition

Disaster-omen interpretive skepticism is the court-speech pattern where an adviser accepts that disasters may carry political meaning but resists a shallow one-to-one reading that turns earthquakes, eclipses, or other anomalies into immediate blame against a selected person or faction.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 548 creates the concept through [[ZhangYuWesternHan|张瑜 / 张禹]] advising [[HanChengdi|汉成帝]]. After earthquakes and eclipses are interpreted by some officials as signs that Wang-family outer relatives have overreached, Chengdi asks Zhang Yu whether he should act against his uncles. Zhang Yu's answer does not deny the moral universe of Heaven's warnings. Instead, he argues that the causes of disasters in classical records are multiple and hard to know, that sages speak cautiously about Heaven and spirits, and that the ruler should respond through upright conduct rather than a shallow personnel attack.

The concept matters because omen language can become a weapon. Zhang Yu's skepticism blocks a disaster-based accusation against [[WangGenWesternHan|王根]] and the Wang side, while still preserving a moral demand on Chengdi to cultivate virtue.

## Key Claims

- Disaster interpretation can become politically dangerous when a broad cosmic warning is narrowed into blame against a current rival.
- Skepticism can operate inside the omen worldview rather than rejecting it outright.
- A strong skeptical response separates moral self-correction from opportunistic certainty about who caused the sign.
- Advisers can use interpretive humility to reduce factional escalation while still preserving pressure for upright rule.
- This pattern differs from favorable-omen skepticism because the target is not celebratory legitimacy but punitive accusation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept does not claim disasters have no political meaning in the source world. Zhang Yu's move is narrower: resist confident factional assignment of blame when the evidence is interpretively unstable. The same strategy could also protect entrenched power from needed criticism, so its value depends on whether it prevents opportunistic accusation or suppresses valid accountability.
