# Scottish Reformation Royal Resistance

## Definition

Scottish Reformation royal resistance is the combination of Protestant religious obligation, noble organization, and foreign-supported political action through which subjects could oppose, constrain, or remove a ruler associated with the unreformed religious order.

## Current Synthesis

The revolutionary prehistory shows the principle in action before it was stated directly to Mary. The Lords of the Congregation organized against [[MaryOfGuise|Mary of Guise]], Knox's preaching helped widen mobilization and iconoclasm, and resentment of French troops joined Protestant belief to defense of Scottish liberties. The Treaty of Berwick and English intervention demonstrate that resistance depended on diplomacy and force as well as theology.

The later episode locates the explicit principle in [[JohnKnox|John Knox]]'s first meeting with [[MaryQueenOfScots|Mary, Queen of Scots]] in September 1561. Knox told the Catholic queen that princes exceeding their bounds could be resisted, restrained, and even imprisoned. Mary understood the political consequence: religious interpreters and the subjects following them could place conditions on sovereign authority.

The claim drew strength from more than Knox's personality. Scottish Protestants are presented as confident that their national church formed a providential community, while Mary's private Mass, French household, and reluctance to reverse the settlement left a Catholic monarch ruling an officially Protestant country. Religious accommodation reduced immediate conflict but did not reconcile the rival sources of authority.

## Key Claims

- Obedience to God could override unconditional obedience to a monarch.
- Noble covenant, preaching, anti-foreign politics, and English support converted doctrine into revolutionary capacity.
- Resistance theory turned confessional disagreement into a constitutional limit on royal power.
- A confident Protestant national identity gave the doctrine collective political force.
- Mary's private Catholic worship preserved personal practice without restoring Catholic public authority.
- Strategic accommodation could postpone conflict while leaving the authority dispute unresolved.

- Institutional setting: [[586-mary-queen-of-scots-the-battle-for-scotland-part-3-glt1545905247]] describes an officially Protestant Scotland ruled by a Catholic queen and repeated conflict around her private Mass.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episodes' description of the Scottish Reformation as an extraordinary or possibly first modern European revolution is interpretive rather than a settled classification. Knox's preaching did not independently cause the revolt; noble interest, anti-French sentiment, English strategy, military force, and religious commitment overlapped. One confrontation does not establish how consistently resistance theory was understood, accepted, or applied across Scottish society. Mary's accommodation also reflected overlapping constraints and aims; the source's emphasis on English succession strategy does not exclude youth, unfamiliarity, military balance, widowhood, or pragmatic government.

## What Changed

- Extended the concept from Knox's 1561 doctrine to its 1559-1560 revolutionary practice.
- Added noble organization, anti-French politics, English intervention, and treaty settlement as conditions turning resistance theory into power.
