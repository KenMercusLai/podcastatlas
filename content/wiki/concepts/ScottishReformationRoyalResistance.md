# Scottish Reformation Royal Resistance

## Definition

Scottish Reformation royal resistance is the claim that obedience to God sets limits on obedience to a monarch, allowing subjects to resist or restrain a ruler who violates the reformed religious order.

## Current Synthesis

The episode locates this principle in [[JohnKnox|John Knox]]'s first meeting with [[MaryQueenOfScots|Mary, Queen of Scots]] in September 1561. Knox told the Catholic queen that princes exceeding their bounds could be resisted, restrained, and even imprisoned. Mary understood the political consequence: religious interpreters and the subjects following them could place conditions on sovereign authority.

The claim drew strength from more than Knox's personality. Scottish Protestants are presented as confident that their national church formed a providential community, while Mary's private Mass, French household, and reluctance to reverse the settlement left a Catholic monarch ruling an officially Protestant country. Religious accommodation reduced immediate conflict but did not reconcile the rival sources of authority.

## Key Claims

- Obedience to God could override unconditional obedience to a monarch.
- Resistance theory turned confessional disagreement into a constitutional limit on royal power.
- A confident Protestant national identity gave the doctrine collective political force.
- Mary's private Catholic worship preserved personal practice without restoring Catholic public authority.
- Strategic accommodation could postpone conflict while leaving the authority dispute unresolved.

## Evidence

- Doctrinal confrontation: [[586-mary-queen-of-scots-the-battle-for-scotland-part-3-glt1545905247]] recounts Knox's defense of resistance and Mary's recognition that it subordinated royal command to a religious test.
- Institutional setting: [[586-mary-queen-of-scots-the-battle-for-scotland-part-3-glt1545905247]] describes an officially Protestant Scotland ruled by a Catholic queen and repeated conflict around her private Mass.
- Political confidence: [[586-mary-queen-of-scots-the-battle-for-scotland-part-3-glt1545905247]] connects Protestant identity, anti-Catholic pageantry, and criticism of Mary's French court.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode's description of the Scottish Reformation as possibly the first modern European revolution is interpretive rather than a settled classification. One confrontation does not establish how consistently resistance theory was understood, accepted, or applied across Scottish society. Mary's accommodation also reflected overlapping constraints and aims; the source's emphasis on English succession strategy does not exclude youth, unfamiliarity, military balance, or pragmatic government.

## What Changed

- Established religious resistance as a direct limit on Mary's royal authority rather than only a dispute over worship.
- Added the unresolved coexistence of private Catholic monarchy and public Protestant settlement.
