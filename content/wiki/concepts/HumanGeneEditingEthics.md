# Human Gene-Editing Ethics

## Definition
Human gene-editing ethics evaluates somatic treatment, embryo editing, and embryo selection through therapeutic necessity, technical precision, causal certainty, consent, inheritance, enhancement, inequality, and respect for human variation.

## Current Synthesis
The sources distinguish somatic editing from germline intervention. Editing a patient's immune cells can target a serious disease without intentionally changing future generations, although off-target effects, chromosome damage, delivery, consent, and clinical evidence still matter. Germline editing adds an intergenerational consequence that the future person and descendants cannot consent to.

The CRISPR-babies case makes necessity central. The episode describes an attempted CCR5 embryo edit in a setting where established HIV-risk-reduction methods existed, while consent, imperfect edits, non-peer-reviewed results, and heritable consequences remained disputed. Technical feasibility therefore does not establish ethical justification.

Embryo selection can create similar value pressure without editing sequence. Avoiding a severe mutation or chromosomal abnormality differs from ranking embryos through uncertain polygenic predictions, yet the boundary between prevention and enhancement can shift with social expectations. Preserving diversity, limiting inequality, and refusing probabilistic overpromise remain part of the ethical test.

## Key Claims
- Somatic and germline editing differ ethically because only the latter intentionally transmits changes to future generations.
- Disease severity, available alternatives, evidence maturity, and expected benefit should determine whether editing risk is justified.
- Off-target changes, nearby DNA damage, chromosome injury, and incomplete causal knowledge remain technical as well as ethical constraints.
- Consent is especially limited for embryos, children, descendants, and participants given incomplete risk disclosure.
- Preventing severe disease is ethically distinct from optimizing preferred traits, even when the boundary is contested.
- Embryo selection can create enhancement pressure and inequality without directly editing DNA.
- Human diversity has value that can be lost when probabilistic genetic associations are treated as a blueprint for perfection.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
These are high-level podcast discussions, not current legal, regulatory, reproductive-medicine, or technical reviews. CRISPR precision, approved somatic therapies, embryo-testing methods, and jurisdictional rules can change. The page does not treat all somatic editing, preimplantation testing, disease prevention, or research as ethically equivalent, and it does not establish the detailed facts or outcomes of the embryo-editing case independently.

## What Changed
- Made the somatic-versus-germline boundary the organizing ethical distinction.
- Added therapeutic necessity and available alternatives to the test for embryo-editing justification.
- Expanded precision risk beyond off-target cuts to nearby changes and rare chromosome damage.
- Added preservation of human diversity as a qualification on enhancement and embryo-ranking projects.
