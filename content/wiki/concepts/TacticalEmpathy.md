# Tactical Empathy

## Definition
Tactical empathy is the practice of actively demonstrating that one understands another person's perspective and emotional frame without implying agreement, approval, sympathy, or concession.

## Current Synthesis
In [[how-to-succeed-at-hard-conversations-chris-voss-scim5142864264]], [[ChrisVoss]] treats understanding as information transmission rather than private intention. A negotiator offers a provisional account of the other person's view, uses labels such as "sounds like," mirrors a few words, and lets correction reveal what actually matters. The method is tactical because it improves diagnosis and cooperation, but it is still empathy because the other person must experience their perspective as recognized.

## Key Claims
- Demonstrated understanding is separable from agreement or liking.
- A provisional interpretation invites useful correction and can be more revealing than direct interrogation.
- Labels can name negative emotion without endorsing or feeding it.
- Mirroring one to three words can prompt elaboration and help a speaker recover their own thought.
- Calm, playful, or non-accusatory delivery affects whether the same words feel collaborative or controlling.

## Evidence
- Definition and distinction - [[how-to-succeed-at-hard-conversations-chris-voss-scim5142864264]] separates empathy from agreement, sympathy, compassion, and endorsement.
- Hypothesis testing - [[how-to-succeed-at-hard-conversations-chris-voss-scim5142864264]] says a best guess allows the counterpart to correct the negotiator and disclose priorities.
- Emotional labeling - [[how-to-succeed-at-hard-conversations-chris-voss-scim5142864264]] uses "sounds like" to address frustration, venting, and predictable negative reactions.
- Mirroring - [[how-to-succeed-at-hard-conversations-chris-voss-scim5142864264]] presents short repetition as an invitation to expand rather than a demand to repeat.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Understanding can be used manipulatively, and the episode does not establish that these techniques work equally across cultures, power imbalances, neurotypes, abusive relationships, or emergency contexts. Naming an emotion incorrectly can feel presumptuous; the method depends on treating the label as a corrigible hypothesis rather than a diagnosis.
