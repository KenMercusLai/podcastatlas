# Death-Ground Tactical Preconditions / 死地战术前提

Death-ground tactical preconditions / 死地战术前提 enters the wiki through [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-157-hanxin-beishuiyizhan-bingfa-jiedu-1-loiz8ge-a39tmigajool2spzri5i|Hanji 157 part 1]] as the broader lesson behind [[HanXin|韩信]]'s [[BackwaterBattle|背水一战]] at [[JingxingPass|井陉口]]. The episode accepts the [[SunziBingfa|《孙子兵法》]] idea that troops placed in a no-retreat situation may fight harder, but treats that as only one layer of the victory.

The concept's core claim is conditional. Putting an army on death ground can create forced resolve, especially when the soldiers are recently assembled and might scatter in ordinary danger. But that commitment becomes reckless if the commander has not already reduced the main external risks, understood the enemy's likely choice, and arranged a surprise that prevents the opponent from exploiting the exposed position.

In Han Xin's case, the preconditions are explicit. He knows his own force is not deeply trained, uses spies to learn that [[ChenYu|陈馀]] has rejected [[LiZuoChe|李左车]]'s supply-line plan, confirms that the pass is not ambushed, and places two thousand hidden cavalry where they can seize the empty Zhao camp. The backwater formation forces Han soldiers to fight, while the camp seizure forces Zhao soldiers to misread their rear as lost.

This keeps death-ground tactics separate from simple inspiration. The episode's criticism of later imitators is that they copy the visible form but miss the invisible preparation: intelligence, timing, enemy psychology, terrain, and an independent surprise layer.

The concept also clarifies the relationship to [[BreakingCauldronsAndSinkingBoats|破釜沉舟]]. Xiang Yu's Julu commitment device is also a death-ground action, but that source already ties it to prior supply-corridor disruption. Han Xin's backwater case makes the same rule more explicit: the commander creates no-retreat morale only after the battle has been shaped enough for that morale to matter.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-157-hanxin-beishuiyizhan-bingfa-jiedu-2-lowgtomc-sfmdnshzlscm26d-d3|Hanji 157 part 2]] extends the concept after the battle. The same prudence that makes death-ground tactics usable also limits what should follow victory: Han Xin accepts Li Zuoche's warning that tired troops should not be thrown directly at [[YanState|燕国]] and [[QiState|齐国]] fortifications. Death-ground success therefore leads into rest, pacification, and envoy pressure rather than automatic escalation.

## Key Claims
- Death-ground morale is a real mechanism, but it is not a complete battle plan.
- The tactic is most coherent when the commander's own troops might otherwise scatter or fail to sustain contact.
- Prior intelligence is mandatory because an enemy that refuses the expected fight can turn the no-retreat position into a trap.
- A second surprise or information shock is needed so the enemy experiences collapse rather than simply grinding down the committed force.
- Later imitation fails when commanders copy the visible "no retreat" posture without reproducing the hidden conditions.
- Han Xin's case joins knowing oneself, knowing the enemy, regular deployment, and surprise; it is not reducible to courage.

- Hanji 157 part 2 adds a post-victory precondition: after forced-resolve combat, the commander must account for troop exhaustion before choosing the next method of expansion.
