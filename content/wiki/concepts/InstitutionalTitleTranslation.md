## Definition

Institutional-title translation is the practice of translating a political title by comparing its function, authority, succession setting, territorial scope, and contemporary usage before selecting a familiar target-language term. It treats readability and historical precision as a managed tradeoff rather than assuming one-to-one equivalence.

## Current Synthesis

The source shows why literal similarity and conventional translation are both incomplete. Emperor can be a useful rendering of several supreme rulers, but it can also import a Roman-European or Chinese hierarchy into a different political order. Regent names a temporary governing function more broadly than 摄政王, while steward in a fictional monarchy can exercise inherited substitute authority without originating as a royal prince. Queen can mean a reigning sovereign or a king's wife; context must distinguish the functions.

Good translation therefore starts from institutional questions: Who exercises rule? Is authority temporary, delegated, territorial, dynastic, marital, or sacerdotal? What office does the source language name, and what associations does the target term introduce? A concise familiar word may remain appropriate, but qualification is necessary when it hides the original office, invents rank, or encourages false East-West symmetry.

## Key Claims

- Conventional equivalence is a translation choice, not proof that institutions share an origin or constitutional structure.
- Political function, succession relation, territorial scope, and contemporary usage should be checked before lexical resemblance.
- A readable familiar title can be retained when its lost distinctions are not material, but consequential differences require explanation.
- Regent is broader than 摄政王 because substitute rulers need not be princes or hold a royal title.
- Reigning sovereign, consort, dowager, heir, retired ruler, and officeholding deputy must remain distinct even when target-language vocabulary overlaps.
- Translation can create cultural imagery: familiar native terms may come to feel foreign through translated literature and screen media.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- No translation can preserve every institutional distinction without becoming commentary; precision must be proportional to the question being answered.
- The source offers practitioner judgments, not a complete corpus study of translation conventions or reader response.
- Translating a regent as 摄政王 can be locally defensible when royal status and actual authority are central, even if the source title is broader.
- Using 太子 for a European heir improves functional clarity in some contexts but can erase fixed territorial styles such as Prince of Wales, Prince of Asturias, Prince of Orange, or Dauphin.
- The concept should not replace established proper titles mechanically; it is a test for when convention needs qualification.

## What Changed

- Created a functional test for translating political titles across non-equivalent institutions.
- Added explicit boundaries for regents, heirs, female sovereigns, consorts, dowagers, and retired rulers.
- Added translation-created cultural imagery as a consequence distinct from factual mistranslation.
