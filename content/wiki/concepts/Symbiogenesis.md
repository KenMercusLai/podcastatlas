# Symbiogenesis

## Definition
Symbiogenesis is the evolutionary process in which formerly separate organisms become so integrated that they form a new living whole with differentiated parts.

## Current Synthesis
The episode uses eukaryotic cells and mitochondria to make symbiogenesis a general account of major evolutionary transitions: simpler entities can merge, specialize, and become components of a larger unit that can combine again at another level. The frame does not replace natural selection, competition, or branching. It widens the evolutionary story by making partnership, merger, and division of labor visible as sources of complexity, while preserving the possibility that coordination at one level harms a larger system.

## Key Claims
- Evolutionary novelty can arise through integration as well as competition and divergence.
- A successful merger creates a new level of organization rather than merely a loose alliance.
- Differentiation and division of labor make interdependence useful.
- Human intelligence can be understood as similarly dependent on communities, accumulated knowledge, and technology.
- Integration is not inherently beneficial when the new coalition damages its host or wider network.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The supplied source is a conceptual podcast summary, not an evolutionary-biology review. Symbiogenesis should not be generalized into a claim that cooperation dominates evolution, that all mergers are voluntary, or that biological integration directly justifies a political or technological arrangement. Competition and cooperation can operate simultaneously at different levels.
