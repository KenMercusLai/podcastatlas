# Sex Work as an Economic Spectrum

## Definition

Sex work as an economic spectrum is a framework for treating prostitution, concubinage, mistress arrangements, brothel life, street work, and elite courtesanship as varied positions shaped by money, coercion, family strategy, urban markets, reputation, and unequal bargaining power.

## Current Synthesis

The episode rejects a single route into a fixed occupation. Poverty, insufficient dowries, domestic service, upbringing in a brothel, coercion, and strategic pursuit of patronage could all lead women into sexual commerce under sharply different conditions. London's changing red-light geography also placed the trade inside ordinary urban development and class relations rather than outside society.

Agency and exploitation can coexist without canceling each other. A famous courtesan might market herself, select among keepers, influence fashion, and function as a status symbol, yet still depend on men's wealth and face poverty when attention or support ended. Reproductive risk and stigma further limited exit and security across the spectrum.

## Key Claims

- Sex work comprises heterogeneous arrangements rather than one stable occupation or social identity.
- Entry can combine coercion, poverty, family calculation, socialization, and strategic choice.
- Urban location and client markets connect sexual commerce to broader patterns of development and class geography.
- Celebrity and patron competition can increase leverage without eliminating dependency.
- Pregnancy, abortion risk, disease, violence, stigma, and loss of patronage make apparent income an incomplete measure of security.
- Moral labels can hide movement into, within, and out of sexual commerce.

## Evidence

- Varied entry routes: [[24-sex-in-the-city-glt9221135938]] contrasts poor women, servant girls, daughters without dowries, women raised in brothels, mistresses, and courtesans.
- Urban integration: [[24-sex-in-the-city-glt9221135938]] traces sex work through the Strand, Covent Garden, Piccadilly, St James's, Fitzrovia, and class-specific client markets.
- Reproductive risk: [[24-sex-in-the-city-glt9221135938]] presents pregnancy and attempted abortion as recurring occupational hazards in a period without reliable contraception.
- Uneven leverage: [[24-sex-in-the-city-glt9221135938]] describes famous courtesans as marketed celebrities and male status symbols who could choose patrons yet still end in poverty.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

A spectrum model must not blur legal distinctions, age, consent, trafficking, violence, or the difference between occasional exchange and sustained occupation. Evidence is filtered through class and male-authored records, while the episode's brief examples cannot establish prevalence or typical outcomes. Recognizing constrained choice does not minimize coercion; recognizing coercion does not erase every form of strategy or bargaining.

## What Changed

- Established a framework that holds heterogeneous entry routes, constrained agency, urban markets, reproductive risk, and economic insecurity together.
