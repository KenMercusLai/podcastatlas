# Political Accent Prestige

## Definition
Political accent prestige is the way a leader's speech pattern affects whether audiences hear them as authoritative, intelligent, authentic, regional, common, elite, or prime-ministerial.

## Current Synthesis
The episode develops the concept through [[LaneGreen]]'s segment on [[AndyBurnham]]'s northern accent. Burnham's voice is treated as politically meaningful because received pronunciation once dominated Oxbridge, the BBC, and Westminster, making certain southern or elite sounds seem closer to authority.

The source does not say accent barriers have disappeared. It argues that working-class and regional accents gained wider cultural status from the 1960s onward, but research and political commentary still suggest that accent can shape perceived intelligence and credibility. Burnham's "generic northern" sound therefore becomes both a symbol of change and evidence that class-coded listening still matters.

The historical episode adds a formation mechanism. Nineteenth-century ruling-class education could smooth regional speech, while the BBC later helped institutionalize received pronunciation as a national broadcast norm. Chaucer's mockery of northern speech shows that regional accent hierarchy predates broadcasting, even though modern schooling and media gave standardization new reach.

## Key Claims
- Accent can function as a political status signal even when no explicit policy issue is at stake.
- Received pronunciation historically supplied a prestige template for British elite authority.
- Regional and working-class accents can gain authenticity and cultural value while still facing intelligence or class bias.
- A politician's voice can widen who sounds plausible in high office, but one successful figure does not remove the underlying hierarchy.
- Schools and national media can convert one class-regional speech pattern into an apparently neutral standard.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are podcast discussions, not a full sociolinguistic literature review. Their examples support a British class, region, media, and politics frame but do not measure the relative influence of schools, broadcasting, mobility, or audience prejudice. Chaucer, Peel, Gladstone, the BBC, and Burnham illustrate different periods and cannot by themselves establish an unbroken hierarchy. Burnham's case also carries a chronology tension with earlier wiki sources that already place him in Downing Street; the accent concept does not settle that timeline.
