# Small-State Enforcement Capacity

[[taking-the-shine-off-albedo-and-global-temperatures-6a730fdaca067b295bab9760]] adds a narcotics version through [[WestAfricaCocaineTransit]]. In [[Liberia]], [[SierraLeone]], and neighboring routes, weak port screening, limited maritime response, low official pay, corruption, and fast-boat tactics make enforcement capacity the difference between one seizure and sustained control.

[[SmallStateEnforcementCapacity]] is the state-capacity problem [[sand-heists-and-property-rights-in-the-caribbean-summer-school]] raises through beaches, sand, air, rivers, seas, and other resources that are large relative to a small government's budget. The issue is not only legal authority; it is whether monitoring, evidence collection, witness protection, and prosecution can work at the required scale.

In the Jamaican sand case, capacity limits show up as nighttime extraction, uncertainty over where sand was taken, difficult forensic proof, witness intimidation, and dropped criminal charges. In the broader Caribbean frame, the same capacity problem interacts with [[SmallOpenEconomyVulnerability]] and [[TourismDependentSmallEconomy]].

## Key Claims
- A government can have rules on paper while lacking enough resources to watch open-access spaces continuously.
- Resource crimes become harder when the stolen material is mixed into ordinary economic activity.
- Enforcement capacity is part of environmental policy; without it, externality pricing and property rights remain incomplete.
- Drug-route enforcement adds a maritime and corruption layer: traffickers can move routes, use faster boats, and exploit officials whose pay and tools are weaker than cartel resources.
