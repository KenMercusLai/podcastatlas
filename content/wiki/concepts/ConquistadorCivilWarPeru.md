# Conquistador Civil War in Peru

## Definition

The conquistador civil war in Peru was the linked sequence of jurisdictional, factional, dynastic, and royal conflicts that followed the initial Spanish seizure of Inca political centers.

## Current Synthesis

[[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] shows the feud developing before open civil war. Disputes over titles, ransom shares, Cusco, and new opportunities push [[FranciscoPizarro]] to send [[DiegoDeAlmagro]] toward Chile. Almagro returns without the expected wealth during [[SiegeOfCusco1536|the siege of Cusco]], unsuccessfully tests an alliance with [[MancoInca]], then occupies the city and imprisons [[HernandoPizarro]] and [[GonzaloPizarro]].

In [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]], conquest still does not create a coherent settler regime. The Pizarro and Almagro camps manipulate arbitration, mobilize Spanish and Indigenous forces, and turn Las Salinas into a battle whose execution of Almagro makes revenge structurally likely. Pizarro's assassination and Diego de Almagro the Younger's rebellion continue the feud until royal forces defeat him at Chupas.

Royal victory does not immediately settle the order. Gonzalo Pizarro mobilizes settler opposition to the New Laws, kills a viceroy, and rules Lima until Pedro de la Gasca wins through desertion and royal legitimacy. The sequence shows that imperial control was constructed against conquistadors as well as Indigenous resistance, while [[MancoInca]] and [[NeoIncaState|Vilcabamba]] tried to survive amid Spanish division.

## Key Claims

- Disputed jurisdiction over Cusco converted conquest partnership into organized war.
- The failed Chile expedition returned Almagro to Peru with grievance, troops, and a need for a new political settlement.
- Negotiation and arbitration functioned inside factional power rather than above it.
- Las Salinas settled a battle but Almagro's execution reproduced conflict through revenge.
- Indigenous auxiliaries and spectators were present across supposedly Spanish civil wars.
- Royal officials eventually defeated both the Almagrist succession and Gonzalo Pizarro's settler revolt.
- Resistance to the New Laws linked civil war to the defense of colonial labor privileges.

## Evidence

- Factional prehistory: [[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] links the Chile expedition, failed Manco negotiation, seizure of Cusco, and Pizarro-family imprisonment.
- Factional escalation: [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] connects prisoner politics, failed arbitration, and mobilization to Las Salinas.
- Revenge cycle: [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] follows Almagro's execution, Pizarro's assassination, and Almagro the Younger's defeat at Chupas.
- Royal consolidation: [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] describes Vaca de Castro's intervention and La Gasca's later defeat of Gonzalo Pizarro.
- Colonial stakes: [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] links Gonzalo's rebellion to settler anger at the New Laws and threatened encomienda interests.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episodes compress distinct conflicts into one narrative chain. Legal jurisdictions, local loyalties, Almagro's intentions toward Manco, troop numbers, battle tactics, royal instructions, and the degree to which the New Laws caused Gonzalo's revolt require fuller evidence. “Civil war” names organized conflict among Spanish-led factions but should not obscure Indigenous participation, coercion, strategic choice, or suffering.

## What Changed

- Added the failed Chile venture and seizure of Cusco as the transition from rivalry to open war.
- Clarified that Spanish factionalism created tactical openings without making either faction a reliable ally for Manco.
