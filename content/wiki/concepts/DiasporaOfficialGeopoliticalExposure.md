# Diaspora Official Geopolitical Exposure

## Definition
Diaspora official geopolitical exposure is the source's frame for how elected officials with family, ethnic, language, business, or community ties to another country can face heightened legal and reputational risk when relations between that country and their state of office deteriorate.

## Current Synthesis
[[404-shaoyang-yiwei-huaren-shiyiyuan-qinlide-meiguo-difang-zhengzhi-f96bdc7a-c667-4594-8719-2070d24f8231]] develops the concept through [[ShaoYang|邵阳 / Shao Yang]]'s discussion of Chinese American elected officials in the context of U.S.-China tension. Shao argues that China's stronger global position does not automatically make overseas Chinese political participation easier; for Chinese American officials, it can make disclosure, relationship management, and foreign-government boundaries more delicate.

The [[AileenWangArcadia|Aileen Wang]] case is the episode's concrete example. Shao stresses that the case, in his telling, involved foreign-agent registration rather than espionage. His lesson is procedural: officials need to know which relationships require disclosure or registration, when to recuse, and which ties create avoidable risk.

## Key Claims
- Diaspora officeholders can become symbolically linked to foreign states even when their offices are local.
- U.S.-China tension can make Chinese American officials more sensitive to foreign-government contacts, disclosure, registration, and recusal.
- Foreign-agent registration is distinct from espionage, but failure to register can still create serious legal exposure.
- Community solidarity and legal-defense fundraising can coexist with a practical lesson about red lines.
- A stronger origin country can increase suspicion toward diaspora officials rather than straightforwardly improving their political comfort.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is source-scoped. It should not generalize from Chinese American officials to all diaspora officeholders without additional sources, and it does not independently verify the Wang case, FARA doctrine, selective-enforcement claims, or comparative examples involving other countries.
