# Creatine Monohydrate Evidence

## Definition
Creatine monohydrate evidence is the episode's frame that creatine monohydrate is an unusually well-tested sports supplement for healthy people, with practical dosing and tolerance mattering more than expensive forms or fast saturation.

## Current Synthesis
The Norton source treats creatine as a rare supplement where evidence is comparatively strong. Creatine monohydrate is presented as safe and effective for healthy people, with benefits around phosphocreatine stores, exercise performance, recovery, lean mass, strength, and possible cognition. Other forms are framed as unlikely to justify extra cost relative to monohydrate.

The dosing stance is pragmatic. Loading saturates stores faster, usually within about a week, but five grams per day without loading reaches the same endpoint more slowly. If creatine causes gastrointestinal irritation, Norton favors avoiding loading and splitting doses. The concept therefore belongs next to [[ContextDependentBiomedicalInterventions]] and [[SupplementLabelAccuracy]] while still being more positive than the wiki's weaker-evidence supplement examples.

## Key Claims
- Creatine monohydrate is treated as the most tested, safe, and effective sports supplement in the source.
- Other creatine forms are presented as probably not worth extra cost compared with monohydrate.
- The source links creatine to phosphocreatine stores, exercise performance, recovery, lean mass, strength, and possible cognition.
- Kidney and liver concerns are treated as debunked for healthy people, while the hair-loss concern rests mainly on one unreplicated DHT study.
- Loading saturates stores faster, but five grams per day without loading can reach the same endpoint over two to four weeks.
- Gastrointestinal tolerance and adherence can justify skipping loading or splitting doses.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This concept is not individualized supplement advice. Kidney disease, liver disease, pregnancy, adolescence, medication interactions, competitive-sport rules, product contamination, gastrointestinal intolerance, diet pattern, and clinical conditions can change the risk calculation. "Healthy people" is part of the source's safety boundary, not a universal claim.
