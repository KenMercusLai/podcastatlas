# 宫市游乐式权力错位 / Palace Market Theater

宫市游乐式权力错位 / palace market theater enters the wiki through [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-920-mingwu-zhege-daoli-caineng-zhuan-daqian-lqmabg3bpyz6ct2v4arx-iun57ws|Hanji 920]] as the source's pattern for rulers or royal households staging marketplace life inside palace space. The main late-Han case is [[EmperorLingOfHan|汉灵帝]] building shops in the harem, making palace women trade, and enjoying quarrels, fights, theft, bargaining, drink, music, and spectacle as if the palace were a miniature city.

The concept is not ordinary [[MarketCoordination|market coordination]]. A palace market has no normal equality of buyers and sellers, no ordinary outside discipline, and no clear separation between office, household, play, and extraction. The source therefore treats it as role confusion: the ruler who should govern the public order imitates small trade and turns the court into entertainment.

Hanji 920 broadens the pattern through comparison. Sima Yu, Liu Yifu, Tang Zhongzong, Ming imperial shops, and [[QianlongEmperor|乾隆]]'s Tongleyuan buy/sell street show different intensities of the same palace-market impulse, from amusement to revenue-seeking. The late-Han branch connects most directly to [[UntimelyExtravagance|时绌举赢]] and [[ImperialPrivateTreasuryExtraction|帝王私库进奉盘剥]] because the market play appears amid rebellion, frontier pressure, disasters, and public/private treasury anxiety.

## Key Claims
- Palace market play turns commercial imitation into a visible signal of ruler role confusion.
- The problem is not commerce itself; it is a ruler using palace power to simulate or capture commerce while neglecting public responsibility.
- Market scenes inside palace space can range from amusement to revenue extraction, but all blur household, state, and market roles.
- In Hanji 920, the pattern is untimely because it appears beside rebellion, frontier invasion, hail, eclipse, palace fire, and office churn.
