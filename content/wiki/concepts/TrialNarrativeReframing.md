# Trial Narrative Reframing

## Definition

Trial Narrative Reframing is the use of admissible evidence to replace an opponent's causal and moral account with a more coherent explanation of disputed conduct.

## Current Synthesis

In U.S. v. Khoury, reframing occurred in two stages. [[BrendaSmithGeorgetown]]'s emails first weakened the prosecution's clean moral contrast by showing Georgetown's own attention to wealthy recruits, but did not directly explain why Catherine stopped playing tennis. Her later testimony and contemporaneous messages recast that fact from evidence of a planned fake injury into evidence of a family rupture, linking institutional context, documentary timing, witness agency, and emotional plausibility to reasonable doubt.

## Key Claims

- Contextual hypocrisy can weaken moral contrast without disproving a specific theory of intent.
- Cross-examination is strongest when contemporaneous records expose a gap between institutional testimony and practice.
- A first-person witness can replace an abstraction with an account the jury can evaluate directly.
- Contemporaneous messages can carry more weight than a later denial because they predate the litigation narrative.
- Effective reframing connects evidence to the disputed causal link rather than merely attacking an institution.
- Emotional force can aid comprehension but can obscure missing evidence or invite overgeneralization.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

A compelling narrative is not necessarily true, and an acquittal does not identify which argument each juror accepted. The sources' credibility and emotional descriptions come largely from defense participants and Gladwell's interpretation; the full record would be needed to compare evidentiary completeness and alternative explanations.
