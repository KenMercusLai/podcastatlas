## Definition
Spatial historical layering is the practice of reading a present-day place as the visible surface of accumulated institutions, populations, infrastructures, memories, and economic regimes.

## Current Synthesis
The concept begins from familiar space rather than from a period label. A school, bridge, park, market, or district can retain traces of Qing planning, colonial construction, industrial employment, deindustrialization, tourism, consumption, and private memory at once. Historical inquiry follows those traces without assuming that the newest use cancels earlier layers.

The method becomes explanatory only when a line joins the fragments. In [[XitaBook|《西塔》]], railways help connect religious space, settlement, administration, community, and commerce; elsewhere a political movement, ecology, infrastructure, or social institution might provide the organizing relation.

## Key Claims
- Present landscapes are interfaces produced by multiple unfinished historical processes.
- Distance or deliberate estrangement can make an overfamiliar home place observable again.
- Private memory and public history can illuminate the same space without being treated as identical evidence.
- Any place can yield rich historical material, but accumulation alone does not create an explanation.
- A strong spatial history identifies a defensible connective logic among heterogeneous traces.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Visible traces do not explain themselves, and a compelling route can overfit selected landmarks while excluding displaced people or erased structures. Personal recognition, maps, architecture, and archives have different evidentiary status and require comparison rather than seamless fusion.
