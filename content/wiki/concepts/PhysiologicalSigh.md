# Physiological Sigh

## Definition
The physiological sigh is a breathing pattern using a deep nasal inhale, a second shorter inhale, and a long mouth exhale as a rapid state-regulation tool.

## Current Synthesis
The sources distinguish a single or small number of physiological sighs for acute stress from a repeated cyclic-sighing practice performed for several minutes. The first is presented as an in-the-moment way to reduce autonomic arousal; the second is linked to a laboratory comparison of brief daily breathwork and meditation that reportedly favored cyclic sighing on stress, mood, sleep, and resting-heart-rate measures.

A third use context is training: one sigh at the start of a rest interval is intended to lower arousal and improve readiness for the next set. Several minutes of exhalation-weighted breathing after a workout is a broader recovery practice and remains distinct from the physiological sigh itself. Breathing can change state quickly, but it does not replace adequate rest, programming, sleep, medical evaluation, or mental-health treatment.

## Key Claims
- A double inhale followed by a longer exhale can be used during acute stress.
- One cycle may be enough for some moments, while several cycles may be used when arousal persists.
- Brief daily cyclic sighing is distinct from the single real-time maneuver.
- Exhalation-weighted breathing is linked in the episode to respiratory-heart-rate coupling and autonomic regulation.
- Breathing changes state but does not by itself resolve causes, trauma, or psychiatric illness.
- Between-set use is intended to improve the quality of a rest interval without reducing the preceding training effort.
- The episode also proposes a small number of sighs for a right-sided exercise stitch, but that mechanism and benefit remain source-scoped.

## Evidence
- Real-time protocol - [[mental-health-toolkit-tools-to-bolster-your-mood-mental-health-scim1991900083]] describes a deep nasal inhale, a second brief inhale, and a long mouth exhale as the preferred acute-stress tool.
- Practice distinction - [[mental-health-toolkit-tools-to-bolster-your-mood-mental-health-scim1991900083]] separately describes five minutes of daily cyclic physiological sighing and reports mood-related benefit from a trial associated with David Spiegel.
- Autonomic context - [[mental-health-toolkit-tools-to-bolster-your-mood-mental-health-scim1991900083]] links inhalation and exhalation to heart-rate acceleration and slowing while discussing vagal tone and respiratory sinus arrhythmia.
- Training context - [[science-supported-tools-to-accelerate-your-fitness-goals-scim7037527631]] recommends one physiological sigh at the start of a rest interval and separately proposes three to five minutes of exhalation-weighted breathing after training.
- Comparative and exercise context - [[how-to-breathe-correctly-for-optimal-health-mood-learning-performance-scim7880960950]] describes the brief daily comparison and proposes two or three sighs for right-sided exercise stitches.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source summaries do not provide the trial's full methods, effect sizes, durability, adverse events, or evidence that between-set or side-stitch use improves performance or recovery. The source attributes right-sided stitches to phrenic-nerve referral near the diaphragm and liver, but the summary does not establish that as the cause of an individual's pain. Breathwork can be uncomfortable or destabilizing for some people, and this simple protocol should not be treated as crisis care, trauma treatment, or a substitute for medical evaluation of breathing, cardiac, exercise-related, or panic symptoms.
