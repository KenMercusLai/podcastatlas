# Cognitive Dissonance And Accommodation / 认知失调与顺应

## Definition

Cognitive dissonance is the discomfort created when beliefs, expectations, evidence, or behavior conflict. Accommodation changes the underlying model or behavior; assimilation and rationalization reduce the discomfort while preserving more of the existing commitment.

## Current Synthesis

[[132-zhenglun-yu-shuofu-women-weishenme-chaojia-zenme-cai-suan-ying-807583318]] treats dissonance as a normal learning problem rather than a disease or insult. Because perception is filtered through prior models, conflicting evidence can be assimilated at low cost or can force accommodation, which demands uncertainty tolerance, motivation, and sometimes social support. Identity, group belonging, distance from personal risk, and reward structures can make preservation of the old model especially attractive.

[[hitler-s-olympics-part-1-the-blue-eyed-tornado-hitler-s-olympics-part-1-the-blue-eyed-tornado|Hitler’s Olympics, Part 1]] extends the mechanism from belief revision to moral action. Olympic participants could profess fair competition and international understanding while entering a Nazi-built propaganda spectacle. They could reduce dissonance either by changing behavior and refusing participation, as [[DorothyThompson]] urged, or by revising the meaning and consequences of participation until it seemed compatible with their ideals.

The combined synthesis is that accommodation has both epistemic and behavioral forms. A person can revise a model to fit evidence, revise conduct to fit values, or rationalize evidence, values, and consequences to preserve an identity or institution. Dissonance itself does not determine which response follows and does not prove conscious bad faith.

## Key Claims

- Dissonance begins when expectations, evidence, values, or behavior cannot all remain untouched.
- Assimilation conserves an existing model by reinterpreting the new fact, while accommodation changes the model itself.
- Moral dissonance can be reduced by changing conduct to fit values or by changing the interpretation of conduct and its consequences.
- Identity, group belonging, institutional loyalty, personal distance, and reward structures alter the cost of accommodation.
- Refusal can be an accommodation response when evidence shows that an intended good is serving a harmful system.
- Rationalization may preserve sincere ideals while making their practical violation easier to tolerate.

## Evidence

- Belief revision: [[132-zhenglun-yu-shuofu-women-weishenme-chaojia-zenme-cai-suan-ying-807583318]] distinguishes low-cost assimilation from model-changing accommodation when evidence conflicts with expectation.
- Social cost: [[132-zhenglun-yu-shuofu-women-weishenme-chaojia-zenme-cai-suan-ying-807583318]] connects belief persistence to identity, group belonging, information cocoons, motivation, and distance from personal consequences.
- Moral-behavior conflict: [[hitler-s-olympics-part-1-the-blue-eyed-tornado-hitler-s-olympics-part-1-the-blue-eyed-tornado]] frames participation in Berlin 1936 as a conflict between Olympic ideals and contribution to Nazi propaganda.
- Behavior-changing response: [[hitler-s-olympics-part-1-the-blue-eyed-tornado-hitler-s-olympics-part-1-the-blue-eyed-tornado]] presents Thompson's boycott position as resolving the contradiction by refusing the event rather than redefining participation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources offer explanatory frameworks, not controlled evidence that identifies every participant's internal state. Apparent inconsistency can reflect incomplete information, competing values, coercion, uncertainty about consequences, or a genuine counterargument rather than dissonance reduction. Accommodation is not automatically correct, and preserving a model is not automatically irrational. The Berlin application is an episode-level interpretation of collective moral compromise, not a psychological diagnosis of every athlete, official, voter, or government.
