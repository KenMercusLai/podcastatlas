## Definition

春秋决狱式意图裁判 / Spring-Autumn intent adjudication is the Han-era legal-interpretive pattern in which a judge reads a case through Confucian classic, ethical purpose, and presumed true intent rather than only through literal wording. The current wiki case is [[HeWuWesternHan|何武]]'s "断剑" inheritance judgment in [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-568-hewu-duanjian-gudai-zheqi-panjue-zhide-dangdai-xueyixue-ljd2ojjmsla46no-9m2q802syxlp|Hanji 568]].

## Current Synthesis

The concept is useful because He Wu's decision is neither simple will enforcement nor arbitrary sympathy. A wealthy father leaves wealth to daughters and only a sword to his young son, to be handed over when the son turns fifteen. He Wu treats the sword as a sign of decisional authority and infers that the father meant the adult son to take back the household estate after the daughters had enjoyed it during his minority.

The episode presents the ruling as humane and context-sensitive, but not unqualified. Its power comes from seeing beyond a literal document to family danger, symbolic language, and adult capacity. Its risk is that a judge's moral inference can override text, procedural equality, and the losing parties' claim to predictable rules. The concept should therefore be kept as a historically situated legal-ethics pattern, not a generic endorsement of ignoring written law.

## Key Claims

- The adjudication turns on interpreting a symbol: "sword" is read as authority to decide, not merely as an object.
- The ruling privileges inferred paternal intent over the will's literal allocation of wealth to daughters.
- The case shows a Confucian-ethical form of legal reasoning that looks for family order and moral meaning inside a dispute.
- The same reasoning is contestable from a modern legal perspective because it can weaken textual certainty and equal treatment.
- The concept links law, ritual language, inheritance, and judicial discretion rather than treating them as separate domains.

## Evidence

- Symbolic interpretation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-568-hewu-duanjian-gudai-zheqi-panjue-zhide-dangdai-xueyixue-ljd2ojjmsla46no-9m2q802syxlp|Hanji 568]] says He Wu explains the sword as a thing for cutting or deciding, and treats age fifteen as enough for intelligent control.
- Intent over literal text: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-568-hewu-duanjian-gudai-zheqi-panjue-zhide-dangdai-xueyixue-ljd2ojjmsla46no-9m2q802syxlp|Hanji 568]] says He Wu concludes the father meant the son to return and take charge of the family estate.
- Modern-law qualification: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-568-hewu-duanjian-gudai-zheqi-panjue-zhide-dangdai-xueyixue-ljd2ojjmsla46no-9m2q802syxlp|Hanji 568]] says the host admires the judgment's human feeling while noting that He Wu may look unfair from a contemporary judge's standpoint.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The current evidence is one podcast source's account of a transmitted anecdote, not a full Han legal file. The page does not prove the exact will text, the daughters' legal argument, the son's procedural position, or the statutory inheritance rule that would otherwise apply. The concept also should not be collapsed into modern purposivism: this case rests on Han-era Confucian classic and ethical reading, not a contemporary doctrine of statutory interpretation.
