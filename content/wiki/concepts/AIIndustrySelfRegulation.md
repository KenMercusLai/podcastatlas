## Definition
AI industry self-regulation is the use of industry-led standards, testing bodies, disclosure practices, ratings, or technical-review processes to govern AI systems before direct state rulemaking becomes the main control layer.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources distinguish standards-and-ratings, pre-release permissioning, plural independent auditors, company-created oversight, and the new accord's control-audit-board chain. Self-regulation looks strongest when it creates shared technical language, public evidence, incident learning, multiple contestable evaluators, and a path from findings to accountable directors or regulators. It looks weakest when firms grade themselves, one body becomes a gatekeeper, review lacks evidence access or consequences, or costly procedures exclude startups.

Webb's Facebook Oversight Board example sharpens the durability test: creating an ostensibly independent body does not establish adequate scale, authority, or permanence. The October accord adds speed and board responsibility but not proof of legal force or implementation. The Superintelligence Force adds a government-convened recommendation process expected to favor industry dialogue, yet its leaders' source-described sector ties make conflict rules and independent scrutiny part of the same credibility test.

Public consent is now an additional constraint. The cited Reuters/Ipsos poll reports cross-party majorities favoring stricter rules, while Sam Altman's harm-tolerance framing prompted criticism that executives should not decide which hacks or scams society must accept. Useful self-regulation therefore needs broad representation, narrow scope, independent access, concrete engineering controls, durable accountability, affordable participation, and public institutions capable of contesting both evidence and acceptable-risk judgments.

## Key Claims
- Shared standards and ratings have a different risk profile from pre-release permissioning and should not be treated as the same model.
- Broad startup and open-source representation is necessary because closed frontier labs can absorb heavier compliance costs.
- Plural independent evaluators reduce dependence on both company self-certification and a single captured gatekeeper.
- Review should be tied to concrete incidents, root-cause analysis, sandboxes, monitoring, regression testing, and release discipline.
- Company-created oversight lacks credibility when it has insufficient capacity, outside authority, evidence access, or institutional durability.
- Industry norms work only when incentives discourage defection and when evidence is public enough for meaningful outside critique.
- Board committees, existing-law exposure, conflict safeguards, and public contestability can strengthen a voluntary framework without turning it into prior government approval.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Governments may lack the technical staff and operating cadence needed to evaluate frontier systems, so industry expertise is not optional. Aviation, pharmaceuticals, financial audit, and biotechnology are analogies rather than directly transferable templates. The accord's primary text, signatories, audit standard, evidence rights, board charters, remedies, and implementation record are absent, while the task force has not yet issued recommendations. Professional auditors and government advisers can lack frontier-model expertise or independence; existing law reaching public statements does not necessarily make every voluntary duty enforceable; and one poll does not identify which specific regulatory design voters prefer.
