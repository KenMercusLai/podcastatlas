# Acquired-Trait Inheritance Boundary

## Definition
Acquired-trait inheritance boundary is the source's distinction between traits biologically transmitted through germ cells and traits or memories acquired during life that usually remain in the body or brain of the individual.

## Current Synthesis
The episode treats the boundary as the default starting point for any discussion of inherited memories. Eye color and other ordinary inherited traits make sense because sperm and egg carry genetic information into the next generation. Learned architecture, synaptic patterns, and gym-built muscle do not automatically enter germ cells, so they are not expected to appear biologically in children.

The boundary is not absolute in every organism or mechanism. The worm evidence shows that acquired molecular responses can cross generations under specific conditions, especially through small RNAs. The Ilardo episode adds human trauma and famine observations as suggestive epigenetic examples, but it does not identify a mechanism or design that separates direct exposure, parental effects, shared environment, and germline transmission. The current judgment therefore remains conditional: acquired-trait inheritance is real in some C. elegans experiments, while inherited human memories, learned skills, trauma responses, or acquired physical adaptations remain unestablished here.

## Key Claims
- Genetic inheritance and acquired experience are different evidence categories.
- Somatic changes such as learning or muscle growth normally do not become germline information.
- The [[WeismannBarrier]] and [[EpigeneticReprogrammingBoundary]] explain why acquired states usually stop within the individual.
- Narrow molecular exceptions can exist, especially in [[CElegansModelOrganism]] small-RNA systems.
- Human trauma and famine associations can motivate investigation but do not by themselves establish a transmitted causal mechanism.
- Worm evidence and human associations should not be generalized into claims that human memories or learned skills are biologically inherited.

## Evidence
- Ordinary boundary - [[essentials-genes-inheritance-memories-across-generations-dr-oded-rechavi-scim1624813352]] contrasts eye color inheritance with learned architecture and gym-built muscle.
- Somatic memory location - [[essentials-genes-inheritance-memories-across-generations-dr-oded-rechavi-scim1624813352]] places learned information in synapses and neural circuits rather than sperm or eggs.
- Barrier mechanisms - [[essentials-genes-inheritance-memories-across-generations-dr-oded-rechavi-scim1624813352]] names soma-germline separation and epigenetic reprogramming as two major obstacles.
- Narrow exception - [[essentials-genes-inheritance-memories-across-generations-dr-oded-rechavi-scim1624813352]] presents C. elegans small-RNA inheritance as a real acquired-response mechanism.
- Human association boundary - [[behaviors-that-alter-your-genes-to-improve-your-health-performance-dr-melissa-ilardo-scim2045300705]] cites refugee trauma and the Dutch famine as possible intergenerational epigenetic cases without supplying a mechanism that resolves germline transmission.
- Selection versus acquisition - [[behaviors-that-alter-your-genes-to-improve-your-health-performance-dr-melissa-ilardo-scim2045300705]] uses diving populations to distinguish selection on inherited variation from training acquired during life.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not deny every form of non-genetic inheritance. Their evidence differs in kind: controlled C. elegans experiments provide the clearest positive mechanism, while the human trauma and famine examples are broad interview-level associations. The page should not be used either to declare all human epigenetic transmission impossible or to claim that memories, skills, trauma responses, or parental exercise adaptations are biologically inherited.

- Clarified that diving adaptation can reflect selection on inherited variation without inheritance of acquired training.
