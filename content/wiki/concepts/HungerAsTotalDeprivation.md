# Hunger as Total Deprivation

## Definition

Hunger as total deprivation is the view that prolonged starvation removes not only calories but also bodily capacity, emotional stability, social connection, agency, and spiritual or moral orientation.

## Current Synthesis

[[LesterGlick]] entered the [[MinnesotaStarvationExperiment]] through a community-centered Mennonite commitment to feed strangers. As restriction deepened, his diaries recorded weakness, anger, food obsession, antisocial behavior, and withdrawal. Hunger therefore disrupted the very relational identity that had motivated him to participate.

The episode carries this insight into his later social work. Material provision and emotional or spiritual care are distinct but interacting forms of sustenance: arranging services without loving attention is incomplete, while empathy without food and practical support is also inadequate. Glick's family hunger ritual, gardening, and cinnamon-roll sharing attempt to keep those layers together, although induced scarcity is not automatically an effective or harmless way to teach empathy.

## Key Claims

- Severe hunger can impair movement, mood, attention, self-control, and social participation together.
- Food preoccupation and withdrawal are part of deprivation's psychological and relational burden, not merely side effects outside “real” hunger.
- Starvation can estrange a person from community-shaped values and identity.
- Effective care must join material sustenance to emotional and relational attention.
- Lived deprivation can motivate service without guaranteeing healing or moral clarity.
- Ritualized reminders of hunger may preserve empathy, but they can also transmit a survivor's unresolved relationship with food.

## Evidence

### Multidimensional loss

- [[the-mennonite-national-anthem-the-mennonite-national-anthem]] links Glick's weakness, anger, bread fixation, withdrawal, isolation, and disturbing food cravings during starvation.

### Vocation and care

- [[the-mennonite-national-anthem-the-mennonite-national-anthem]] reports that Glick taught social work as loving kindness rather than service arrangement alone.

### Practices of sustenance

- [[the-mennonite-national-anthem-the-mennonite-national-anthem]] describes weekly restricted rice meals, vegetables grown for neighbors, and cinnamon rolls shared through church and community.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The source offers one biographical and religious interpretation rather than a validated universal model of hunger or social-work practice.
- Physical nutrition, mental health, social belonging, and spiritual care should not be collapsed into interchangeable needs; each may require different expertise and intervention.
- Glick's rice ritual may have expressed empathy training, family authority, trauma repetition, or several motives at once. The summary does not establish its effects on his children.
- Meaning made after suffering does not prove that suffering was necessary or ethically justified.

## What Changed

- Created a framework connecting physiological deprivation to damaged social participation, identity, and care.
- Preserved the tension between turning hunger into service and carrying its unresolved effects into family ritual.
