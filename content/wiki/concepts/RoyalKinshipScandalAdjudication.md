## Definition

宗室亲属丑闻审理 / royal kinship scandal adjudication is the court pattern where an accusation against imperial kin forces public law, kinship taboo, royal-house dignity, evidence reliability, and central political interest into the same decision.

## Current Synthesis

The current sources supply opposite procedural outcomes. Hanji 545 grounds the restraint-and-review branch through [[LiuLiWesternHan|梁王刘立]]'s case. A report that begins with lawlessness and hostile speech against [[HanChengdi|汉成帝]]'s outer-relative family expands into an alleged relationship with [[LiuYuanziWesternHan|刘原子]], Liu Li's aunt. Because officials frame the accusation as “禽兽行” and request execution, the court must decide whether royal status makes punishment more necessary or procedure more careful.

[[GuYongWesternHan|谷永]] supplies the concept's key procedural counterweight. He does not say royal kin should be outside law; he argues that a politically explosive private accusation should not be treated as proved simply because the first investigation failed to confirm the original charge. His memorial uses ritual privacy, emotional plausibility, age difference, royal access to women, and Liu Yuanzi's likely shame to question whether coerced or confused testimony has turned an uncertain matter into public disgrace.

Hanji 284 supplies two punitive contrasts under [[HanWudi|汉武帝]]. An accusation routed through [[ZhuFuYan|主父偃]] ends with [[LiuDingguoYanKingWesternHan|燕王刘定国]]'s ordered suicide and the abolition of Yan. A second accusation against [[QiLiwangWesternHan|齐厉王]] leads to Zhufu Yan's appointment as Qi chancellor, torture-derived testimony, and the king's suicide. These cases show how scandal can become not only a difficult evidentiary problem but also a route for central control and alleged private revenge.

The concept therefore spans review and punitive escalation. It asks how a court can discipline royal kin without treating taboo as self-proving, allowing coercive testimony to replace verification, or hiding political and personal incentives behind moral language.

## Key Claims

- Royal kinship does not remove legal exposure, but it raises the political cost of public accusation and punishment.
- A case that expands from speech or political offense into private sexual scandal needs especially careful evidentiary review.
- Kinship taboo can make punishment seem urgent while also making false or coerced accusation more damaging.
- Royal scandal can serve central consolidation or private resentment even when the alleged conduct would be punishable if true.
- Coercive testimony weakens confidence precisely when the accusation carries irreversible consequences for a person and fief.
- Ritual privacy and royal-house dignity can function as procedural concerns rather than merely as coverups.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is grounded in two Western Han source notes and is not a complete doctrine of Han royal criminal procedure. Hanji 545 does not prove the Liu Li accusation false; Hanji 284 does not independently prove the allegations against Liu Dingguo or the Qi king. Nor do the sources reconstruct the full testimony, define “禽兽行” in statutory detail, or show how ordinary non-royal defendants would have been treated. The contrast supports a procedural judgment: accusation origin, coercion, political gain, and motive must remain visible even when the alleged act is morally explosive.
