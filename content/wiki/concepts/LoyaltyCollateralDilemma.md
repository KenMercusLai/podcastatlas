# Loyalty-Collateral Dilemma / 忠义牵连困境

Loyalty-collateral dilemma / 忠义牵连困境 is the ethical bind [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-72-shenme-yang-de-ren-cai-shi-zhongzhen-zhishi-logxegnqexgc7z-lodw6tr6-8f28]] draws from [[WangZhu|王蠋]] after [[YanState|燕国]] conquers most of [[QiState|齐国]]. Wang Zhu refuses to serve Yan because he sees service to an enemy conqueror as violating the loyal-minister principle, but Yan soldiers threaten to slaughter his city if he will not come.

The concept is narrower than ordinary honor-over-life. The pressure does not fall only on Wang Zhu's body or reputation; it is displaced onto surrounding people. That is why the episode treats his death as a way out of a coercive structure: accepting office would save the locality by breaking loyalty, while refusing office could preserve loyalty by making others pay the cost.

The source does not present the dilemma as easy moral instruction. Wang Zhu had already withdrawn from Qi service because [[QiMinWang|齐湣王]] would not listen, so his loyalty is not blind agreement with the ruler. His death instead becomes a severe claim that a person can reject a bad ruler, fail to save the state, and still refuse to let the conquering side convert him into a legitimating prize.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-910-shehui-shengcun-wo-quan-ni-duodian-chengfu-lp2fvuyb8ampzoeyr7znztnr0fgk|Hanji 910]] adds a late-Han frontier version through [[ZhaoBaoLateHan|赵苞 / 赵包]]. [[Xianbei|鲜卑]] forces under [[TanshihuaiXianbei|檀石槐]] capture Zhao Bao's mother and wife, place his mother in front of the army, and try to make his command choice depend on family rescue.

The source makes the bind severe because the collateral person does not remove herself from the structure before the decision. Zhao Bao's mother tells him not to lose righteousness for her sake, and Zhao Bao defeats the Xianbei, but both his mother and wife are killed. His later death from unresolved self-accusation keeps the case morally tragic even after military success and imperial recognition.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-148-2-peng-le-wangling-muqin-xiangyu-sharen-zhuxin-ljyv8bow4rj1l5ths3znx2evgvtm|Hanji 148-2]] adds a family-hostage version through [[WangLingMother|王陵母亲]]. [[XiangYu|项羽]] tries to make [[WangLingHanMinister|王陵]]'s political choice depend on his mother's survival, but she kills herself after instructing him to serve [[LiuBang|刘邦]]. The dilemma is therefore resolved by the collateral person removing herself from the coercive structure, which is morally extreme and politically effective in the source's reading.

In [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-163-liubang-shouxia-moushi-ruhe-bei-hanxin-neijuan-cansi-lubinu6hoihu59knmlixmaryhnq7|Hanji 163]], [[LiuTaigong|刘太公]] becomes the explicit collateral object at [[GuangwuChuHan|广武]] when Xiang Yu threatens to cook him. Liu Bang refuses to let paternal obligation dictate the war decision, while [[XiangBo|项伯]] argues that killing the hostage will not create strategic leverage. The episode shows hostage collateral failing when the target can publicly reframe kinship and when the coercer cannot connect violence to a useful political outcome.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-978-gushen-quantui-shuwan-dijun-ta-zenme-zuodao-de-lhgd8vsvuaf5oi9aefh0wxabmzwv|Hanji 978]] adds a late-Han officeholder version through [[JinYunLateHan|靳允]]. [[LyuBu|吕布]] has seized Jin Yun's family, but [[ChengYu|程昱]] persuades him to hold [[FanCountyLateHan|范县]] for [[CaoCao|曹操]], and Jin Yun kills [[FanYiLateHan|范仪]] after Fan Yi enters the county. [[XuZhongJin|许众]] later argues that Jin Yun should have prioritized his captured mother because his bond to Cao Cao was not yet a settled lord-subject relation; the host compares [[LiuBang|刘邦]] and [[LiuTaigong|刘太公]] to resist a single abstract rule.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-996-luanshi-zanghong-jiangyiqi-de-ren-xiachang-zui-biequ-lgpa-m6h6bvhyintnuj6zztopui76|Hanji 996]] adds a late-Han voluntary-collateral version through [[ZangHong|臧洪]]. Zang Hong's conflict with [[YuanShao|袁绍]] begins from loyalty to [[ZhangChaoLateHan|张超]], but once [[DongCommandery|东郡]] is under siege he tells officials, soldiers, and civilians that they may leave. They refuse to abandon him, so his private loyalty stance becomes shared starvation and death for thousands. The collateral pressure here is not a captor's hostage threat; it is group honor and attachment binding followers to a doomed leader.

## Key Claims
- Coercion can force moral cost onto third parties rather than only the target.
- Refusing an enemy ruler can be ethically complicated when the enemy threatens uninvolved local people.
- Death functions in this source as exit from a forced choice, not as a general solution to political conflict.
- Loyalty here is not simple obedience; Wang Zhu had previously remonstrated and withdrawn from court.
- The conquering side's respect for worthies becomes morally unstable once invitation turns into threat.
- Hanji 910 adds that a successful public-duty choice can still leave filial and family cost unresolved when the collateral people die during victory.
- Hanji 148-2 adds that a hostage can reverse coercion if the hostage refuses to remain usable leverage, but the act exposes the captor's political cruelty if the captor then desecrates the body.
- Hanji 978 adds that hostage pressure can fall on an officeholder whose political bond is still morally disputable, making the choice between family rescue and base defense open to later criticism.
- Hanji 996 adds that collateral suffering can also arise voluntarily when followers internalize a leader's loyalty stance and treat abandonment as dishonor.
