## Definition

强迁避敌反噬 / Coercive Frontier Relocation Backfire is the failure mode in which a state tries to deny rebels access to frontier civilians by forcing those civilians inland, but the coercive method destroys livelihood, weakens trust, and pushes some of the intended protected population into rebellion.

## Current Synthesis

The current evidence is source-bounded to [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-789-shanliang-meiyou-dixian-ni-jiang-yiwen-buzhi-ljv5hgmd0vxoa-teckj2zkigvcoi|Hanji 789]]. In the Qiang crisis, the Eastern Han court decides that frontier civilians should be moved inward so rebels cannot use them as labor, hostages, suppliers, or political cover. The measure has a strategic logic: separate population from insurgent space and reduce rebel support.

The implementation reverses that logic. Local authorities force movement in ways that cut crops, burn houses, disrupt food supply, and expose migrants to starvation before they reach safer ground. The policy therefore stops looking like protection to the people it claims to protect. [[DuJigongLateHan|Du Jigong]] and others become the source case: Han civilians under relocation pressure join Qiang forces, capture towns, and then reappear as organized enemies against Han commanders.

The concept is narrower than any criticism of relocation. It does not claim that population movement is always wrong in war. It says forced movement becomes strategically self-defeating when the state destroys the very livelihood, trust, and protection capacity that would make people accept relocation as safety rather than abandonment.

## Key Claims

- Population separation can be a plausible counterinsurgency aim, but it depends on credible protection and resettlement capacity.
- Coercive destruction of homes, crops, and local livelihood turns a security measure into visible state harm.
- Famine and humiliation can convert civilians from a passive protection problem into active rebel manpower.
- The backfire is political as well as military because rebels gain both recruits and a story that the court has abandoned its own people.
- Later battlefield victories do not erase the original policy failure if the state first created part of the enemy it then has to fight.

## Evidence

- Relocation logic: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-789-shanliang-meiyou-dixian-ni-jiang-yiwen-buzhi-ljv5hgmd0vxoa-teckj2zkigvcoi|Hanji 789]] says the court tries to move frontier civilians inward so Qiang forces cannot use them as attached population.
- Coercive implementation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-789-shanliang-meiyou-dixian-ni-jiang-yiwen-buzhi-ljv5hgmd0vxoa-teckj2zkigvcoi|Hanji 789]] reports destruction of crops and houses during movement, producing hunger and resentment.
- Rebel conversion: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-789-shanliang-meiyou-dixian-ni-jiang-yiwen-buzhi-ljv5hgmd0vxoa-teckj2zkigvcoi|Hanji 789]] uses Du Jigong as the case of a Han civilian who joins Qiang forces after the relocation pressure.
- Operational consequence: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-789-shanliang-meiyou-dixian-ni-jiang-yiwen-buzhi-ljv5hgmd0vxoa-teckj2zkigvcoi|Hanji 789]] shows Du Jigong defeating Pang Can's branch, retaking Dingxi after Sima Jun's brief success, and later requiring assassination through Yu Gui.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The page is currently based on one episode and should not be treated as a complete Eastern Han migration-policy history. The source also comes through a podcast transcript with some name and place normalization risk. The durable claim is therefore bounded: in this episode's Qiang-war narrative, coercive relocation is presented as a protection policy that creates a stronger enemy because livelihood and trust are destroyed during implementation.
