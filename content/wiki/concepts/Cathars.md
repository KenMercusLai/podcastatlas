# Cathars

## Definition

Cathars are a contested category applied to medieval dissenters, especially in Languedoc, whose local practices, hostile classification, later doctrinal consolidation, and modern reinvention do not map cleanly onto one ancient, unified, self-identifying church.

## Current Synthesis

[[302-the-mystery-of-the-cathars-glt9956144594]] and [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] distinguish the people targeted during the [[AlbigensianCrusade]] from the fully developed Cathar system often projected backward onto them. The earlier episode argues that “Cathar” was not the local self-description or ordinary regional clerical label: learned churchmen used ancient categories to interpret diverse dissent, and modern scholarship later normalized the name. Local communities instead revered *boni homines* and *bonae feminae* whose holiness could rival or exceed that of priests.

The category developed inside the [[GregorianReform]]'s sharper clerical boundaries, active search for heresy, political accusation, and escalating conflict. During and after the war, Cistercian polemic, accusations of dualism, refugee experience, surviving writings, Dominican preaching, and inquisitorial records made it more coherent. Later Protestant, anti-clerical, tourist, and New Age uses then reinvented the Cathars again. The synthesis therefore preserves real dissident practice and late dualist evidence without assuming that persecution merely discovered an already unified counter-church.

## Key Claims

- “Cathar” was neither a straightforward local self-description nor sufficient evidence for one uniform medieval church.
- Local good men and women challenged Roman clerical monopoly through recognized holiness and religious authority.
- Learned labels, reform institutions, and political accusation helped turn diverse local differences into a universal heretical enemy.
- Violence and forced recantation attacked local social identity as well as disputed doctrine.
- Polemic and persecution helped define, harden, and transmit the doctrinal category they claimed to suppress.
- Good women complicate male-only narratives but do not prove a modern feminist social order.
- Protestant, Enlightenment, and New Age afterlives are evidence of reception, not direct evidence of medieval belief.

## Evidence

- Naming and local authority: [[302-the-mystery-of-the-cathars-glt9956144594]] reports the absence of “Cathar” from the region's own normal vocabulary and describes good men and women; [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] contrasts their authority with the Roman clergy-laity distinction.
- Classification and doctrine formation: [[302-the-mystery-of-the-cathars-glt9956144594]] connects ancient labels, educated clerics, active investigation, and political accusation; [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] says systematic dualist claims became more prominent during the war.
- Survivor evidence: [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] discusses later dualist writings while preserving uncertainty about how accusation shaped self-understanding.
- Gender and reception: [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] qualifies feminist portrayals and traces Protestant, anti-clerical, and New Age reinvention.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Questioning a unified pre-war Cathar church is not evidence that every accused person was doctrinally conventional or that dualist writings are fabricated. The good men and women could reject parts of scripture, criticize clergy, or develop doctrine under pressure. Much evidence is hostile, late, or coercively produced, and both episodes rely strongly on the interpretations of R. I. Moore and Mark Gregory Pegg while only briefly acknowledging wider scholarly disagreement.

## What Changed

- Added the absence of a normal local “Cathar” label and the role of learned ancient categories in constructing a universal enemy.
- Located pre-crusade category formation within Gregorian reform, active heresy search, and regional political rivalry.
