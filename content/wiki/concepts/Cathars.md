# Cathars

## Definition

Cathars are the contested medieval people and beliefs classified by church authorities and later historians as a coherent heretical movement in Languedoc, although the episode emphasizes local good men and women, shifting accusations, persecution, and later reinvention over a single stable church.

## Current Synthesis

[[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] distinguishes the people targeted during the [[AlbigensianCrusade]] from the fully developed Cathar system often projected backward onto them. Local communities revered *boni homines* and *bonae feminae* whose holiness could rival or exceed that of priests. Rome treated that lay sacred authority as a challenge to the clergy-laity distinction, while the accused could understand the invaders rather than themselves as heretical.

During and after the war, Cistercian polemic, accusations of dualism, refugee experience, surviving writings, Dominican preaching, and inquisitorial records made the category more coherent. Later Protestants, anti-clerical writers, and New Age readers then reinvented the Cathars as predecessors suited to their own conflicts. The synthesis therefore preserves real dissident practice and late dualist evidence without assuming that persecution merely discovered an already unified counter-church.

## Key Claims

- “Cathar” should not automatically be treated as the self-description of one uniform medieval church.
- Local good men and women challenged Roman clerical monopoly through recognized holiness and religious authority.
- Accused communities could see crusaders and Roman clergy, rather than themselves, as religiously deviant.
- Violence and forced recantation attacked local social identity as well as disputed doctrine.
- Polemic and persecution helped define, harden, and transmit the doctrinal category they claimed to suppress.
- Good women complicate male-only narratives but do not prove a modern feminist social order.
- Protestant, Enlightenment, and New Age afterlives are evidence of reception, not direct evidence of medieval belief.

## Evidence

- Local authority and classification: [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] contrasts good men and women with the Roman clergy-laity distinction.
- Doctrine formation: [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] says accusations of systematic dualism and other doctrines became prominent during the war.
- Survivor evidence: [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] discusses later dualist writings while preserving uncertainty about how accusation shaped self-understanding.
- Gender and reception: [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] qualifies feminist portrayals and traces Protestant, anti-clerical, and New Age reinvention.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Questioning a unified pre-war Cathar church is not evidence that every accused person was doctrinally conventional or that dualist writings are fabricated. Most evidence is hostile, late, or produced under pressure, and the episode relies strongly on the interpretations of R. I. Moore and Mark Gregory Pegg while only briefly acknowledging wider scholarly disagreement.

## What Changed

- Established a synthesis separating local religious actors, hostile classification, persecution-driven consolidation, and modern reception.
