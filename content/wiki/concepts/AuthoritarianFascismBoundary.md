# Authoritarian-Fascism Boundary

## Definition

The authoritarian-fascism boundary distinguishes coercive conservative dictatorship from fascism's revolutionary style, mass mobilization, leader spectacle, and promise of national rebirth.

## Current Synthesis

The source uses [[AntonioDeOliveiraSalazar|Salazar]] and the [[EstadoNovoPortugal|Estado Novo]] to show why shared corporatism, symbols, anti-communism, censorship, and one-party rule do not settle classification by themselves. Salazar borrowed some fascist language and imagery but presented rule as sober, Catholic, technocratic, and preservative. He distrusted mass excitement, condemned Nazi paganism and state supremacy, suppressed Portuguese fascists, and sought to stop social transformation rather than unleash a permanent political revolution.

That distinction does not create a moral escape hatch. The same regime censored, imprisoned, tortured, disappeared, and killed opponents while defending empire through war. The useful boundary therefore separates political forms and mobilizing ambitions without treating personal austerity, lower comparative killing, stability, or administrative competence as democratic legitimacy.

## Key Claims

- Corporatism, a single ruling organization, anti-communism, and borrowed iconography can overlap with fascism without proving complete ideological identity.
- Fascist mass mobilization and revolutionary futurism differ from a dictatorship organized around Catholic hierarchy, restraint, and preservation.
- A ruler's austere personal style can strengthen authoritarian legitimacy while obscuring institutional coercion.
- Opposition to Nazism or domestic fascist rivals does not make an authoritarian regime liberal or nonviolent.
- Comparative restraint in repression does not erase censorship, torture, disappearance, political murder, or colonial war.

## Evidence

- Classification evidence: [[230-portugal-football-fado-and-fascism-part-4-glt9147423732]] says Salazar borrowed fascist language and imagery but avoided rallies, personality cult, mass mobilization, and revolutionary excitement.
- Ideological evidence: [[230-portugal-football-fado-and-fascism-part-4-glt9147423732]] describes his Catholic objection to Nazi paganism, state supremacy, youth exaltation, and direct-action politics.
- Coercive boundary: [[230-portugal-football-fado-and-fascism-part-4-glt9147423732]] records censorship, secret policing, prisons, torture, disappearance, killing, and the murder of [[HumbertoDelgado]].

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This is a classification framework grounded in one narrative episode, not a comprehensive comparative theory of fascism. The source relies partly on Robert Paxton's interpretation but does not systematically compare ideology, institutions, class coalitions, empire, race, gender, or political economy across regimes. Distinguishing Salazarism from Hitler's or Mussolini's projects should not flatten the Estado Novo's own violence or imply that all non-fascist dictatorships share one form.

- Added a bounded framework that separates authoritarian preservation from fascist revolutionary mobilization without minimizing coercion.
