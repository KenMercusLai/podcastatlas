# Authoritarian-Fascism Boundary

## Definition

The authoritarian-fascism boundary distinguishes coercive conservative dictatorship from fascism's revolutionary nationalism, mass mobilization, leader spectacle, violence, and promise of national and human rebirth.

## Current Synthesis

The sources use [[AntonioDeOliveiraSalazar|Salazar]] and [[FranciscoFranco|Franco]] to show why dictatorship, anti-communism, nationalism, corporatism, borrowed symbols, or relations with Hitler and Mussolini do not settle classification by themselves. Salazar's [[EstadoNovoPortugal|Estado Novo]] is the fuller case: it presented authority as sober, Catholic, technocratic, hierarchical, and preservative; distrusted mass excitement; condemned Nazi paganism and state supremacy; suppressed Portuguese fascists; and sought to arrest social transformation rather than sustain permanent political revolution.

Fascism, by contrast, is presented as a post-First World War project organized around humiliation, rebirth, anti-democracy, political violence, mass belonging, spectacle, technology, youth, and the creation of a new society and human type. The distinction is about political form and ambition, not a moral escape hatch. Salazar's regime censored, imprisoned, tortured, disappeared, and killed opponents, while Franco's dictatorship is only briefly classified rather than comprehensively assessed.

## Key Claims

- Dictatorship, anti-communism, nationalism, corporatism, and borrowed fascist imagery do not independently establish fascist identity.
- Fascist mass mobilization and revolutionary futurism differ from authoritarian orders organized around Catholic hierarchy, restraint, and social preservation.
- A ruler's sober or austere personal style can strengthen authoritarian legitimacy while obscuring institutional coercion.
- Opposition to Nazi paganism, mass politics, or domestic fascist rivals does not make an authoritarian regime liberal or nonviolent.
- Historical classification should compare goals, mobilizing methods, social vision, and relationship to modernity rather than use “fascist” as a synonym for evil.
- Comparative restraint in repression does not erase censorship, torture, disappearance, political murder, or colonial war.

## Evidence

- Classification criteria: [[17-fascism-glt5738760220]] defines fascism through postwar revolutionary rebirth, anti-democracy, anti-communism, violence, mass mobilization, spectacle, technology, youth, and a remade human type.
- Franco and Salazar comparison: [[17-fascism-glt5738760220]] presents both as conservative Catholic authoritarians seeking to preserve older social orders rather than build Mussolini's or Hitler's radically new society.
- Salazar's political form: [[230-portugal-football-fado-and-fascism-part-4-glt9147423732]] says he borrowed fascist language and imagery but avoided rallies, personality cult, mass mobilization, and revolutionary excitement.
- Ideological difference: [[230-portugal-football-fado-and-fascism-part-4-glt9147423732]] describes Salazar's Catholic objection to Nazi paganism, state supremacy, youth exaltation, and direct-action politics.
- Coercive boundary: [[230-portugal-football-fado-and-fascism-part-4-glt9147423732]] records censorship, secret policing, prisons, torture, disappearance, killing, and the murder of [[HumbertoDelgado]].

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This remains a source-bounded classification framework rather than a comprehensive comparative theory of fascism or authoritarianism. The sources do not systematically compare institutions, class coalitions, empire, race, gender, political economy, or the complete Franco regime. Movements and governments can borrow, hybridize, or change over time, so the boundary need not be perfectly binary. Distinguishing Salazarism or Francoism from Hitler's and Mussolini's projects must not flatten their violence or imply that all non-fascist dictatorships share one form.

## What Changed

- Generalized the boundary beyond Salazar by adding Franco as a second, more lightly evidenced reactionary-authoritarian comparison.
- Added historical specificity, revolutionary human remaking, technology, youth, and national rebirth to the classification criteria.
- Clarified that the boundary compares political projects rather than ranking their moral acceptability.
