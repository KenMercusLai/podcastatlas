## Definition

避讳 / Imperial Name Taboo is the rule-like avoidance of directly speaking or writing names belonging to emperors, senior officials, ancestors, or respected elders. In this source it includes changing wording, omitting strokes, avoiding homophones or paired characters, and treating ordinary textual mistakes as possible breaches of hierarchy.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 428 frames taboo through a contrast. [[EmperorXuanOfHan|汉宣帝]] changes his name from 刘病已 to 刘询 because "病" and "已" are common characters and could expose ordinary people to punishment when they write memorials or daily documents. That act presents taboo as a public burden that a ruler can reduce.

The same episode then shows the system's later expansion. A practice that begins as respect for names can become a legal and social pressure field: officials fear written mistakes, sons avoid ancestor names, candidates can lose exam routes, writers and compilers face political danger, and everyday reading habits absorb household hierarchy. The concept therefore sits between [[LiAsPoliticalOrder|礼制政治秩序]] and punishment politics: it makes hierarchy visible in language, but it can also convert ordinary speech and writing into risk.

## Key Claims

- Name taboo makes language part of political and familial hierarchy rather than a neutral medium.
- Xuan's name change is presented as a ruler-side mitigation of commoner risk when taboo characters are too ordinary.
- Later taboo practice expands across office, kinship, examinations, literature, dictionaries, and household reading.
- Legal codification and punitive enforcement can turn a respect practice into career loss, official fear, or lethal textual danger.
- The source treats taboo as historically variable: some rulers restrain it, while later regimes and households extend it into wider symbolic vocabularies and daily habits.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This page is bounded to one podcast source. It does not reconstruct the full legal history of taboo, adjudicate every anecdote, or distinguish all forms of national, official, familial, and literary avoidance. The episode's examples are sufficient to establish the concept's operating range in this wiki, but individual cases such as Li He's exam exclusion, Song imperial word bans, and Qing dictionary punishments need separate source notes before they become full standalone pages.
