## Definition

避讳 / Imperial Name Taboo is the rule-like avoidance of directly speaking or writing names belonging to emperors, senior officials, ancestors, or respected elders. In this source it includes changing wording, omitting strokes, avoiding homophones or paired characters, and treating ordinary textual mistakes as possible breaches of hierarchy.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 428 frames taboo through a contrast. [[EmperorXuanOfHan|汉宣帝]] changes his name from 刘病已 to 刘询 because "病" and "已" are common characters and could expose ordinary people to punishment when they write memorials or daily documents. That act presents taboo as a public burden that a ruler can reduce. [[12-dongxifang-quming-de-yishu-luwyjjavdsketsbzhtbgyqv5p_nm|Weird History episode 12]] places the same mechanism inside a wider naming system: considerate rulers may choose rare name characters or rename themselves, while less restrained practice can force relatives, officials, candidates, and writers to alter names and ordinary language.

Together the sources show the system's expansion. A practice framed as respect for names can become a legal and social pressure field: officials fear written mistakes, sons avoid ancestor names, candidates can lose exam routes, writers and compilers face political danger, and everyday reading habits absorb household hierarchy. The concept therefore sits between [[LiAsPoliticalOrder|礼制政治秩序]] and punishment politics: it makes hierarchy visible in language, but it can also convert ordinary speech and writing into risk.

## Key Claims

- Name taboo makes language part of political and familial hierarchy rather than a neutral medium.
- Xuan's name change is presented as a ruler-side mitigation of commoner risk when taboo characters are too ordinary.
- Later taboo practice expands across office, kinship, examinations, literature, dictionaries, and household reading.
- Legal codification and punitive enforcement can turn a respect practice into career loss, official fear, or lethal textual danger.
- The source treats taboo as historically variable: some rulers restrain it, while later regimes and households extend it into wider symbolic vocabularies and daily habits.
- The comparative naming source explicitly rejects taboo's claimed historical value and treats burden reduction by rulers as mitigation of a coercive institution rather than proof of its benefit.

## Evidence

Ruler-side mitigation:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-428-cong-hanxuandi-gaiming-liaoliao-gudai-de-bihui-lhjp6ztajzpxbo2mrlnr-n3mzatb|Hanji 428]] says Xuan changed 刘病已 to 刘询 because "病" and "已" were common characters that could expose people to punishment.
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-428-cong-hanxuandi-gaiming-liaoliao-gudai-de-bihui-lhjp6ztajzpxbo2mrlnr-n3mzatb|Hanji 428]] places the name change inside an edict concerned with judicial fairness, corrupt officials, abusive labor, and tax relief after epidemics.

Expansion of taboo risk:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-428-cong-hanxuandi-gaiming-liaoliao-gudai-de-bihui-lhjp6ztajzpxbo2mrlnr-n3mzatb|Hanji 428]] uses a Western Han writing-error anecdote to show official fear around written mistakes.
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-428-cong-hanxuandi-gaiming-liaoliao-gudai-de-bihui-lhjp6ztajzpxbo2mrlnr-n3mzatb|Hanji 428]] says Tang practice included legal punishment for accidental violation of official taboo, while Tang Taizong limited taboo around "世民" to the paired name.
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-428-cong-hanxuandi-gaiming-liaoliao-gudai-de-bihui-lhjp6ztajzpxbo2mrlnr-n3mzatb|Hanji 428]] says Song practice widened taboo toward imperial symbolic words, and Qing practice could connect taboo breach with literary-inquisition danger.
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-428-cong-hanxuandi-gaiming-liaoliao-gudai-de-bihui-lhjp6ztajzpxbo2mrlnr-n3mzatb|Hanji 428]] uses the Lin Daiyu / Jia Min reading habit in [[HongLouMeng|《红楼梦》]] to show taboo entering family ethics and daily literacy.
- [[12-dongxifang-quming-de-yishu-luwyjjavdsketsbzhtbgyqv5p_nm|Weird History episode 12]] adds the renaming of imperial brothers, homophone extension, the Li He examination story, and rulers' use of rare characters as a comparative naming-system sketch.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This page is bounded to two podcast sources and does not reconstruct the full legal history of taboo or distinguish every form of national, official, familial, and literary avoidance. The sources overlap in examples and are not independent verification. Individual cases such as Li He's exam exclusion, homophone avoidance, imperial-family renaming, Song restrictions, and Qing dictionary punishments remain episode-attributed pending direct textual and legal comparison.
