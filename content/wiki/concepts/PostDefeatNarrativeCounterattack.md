# Post-Defeat Narrative Counterattack / 败后叙事反击

## Definition
Post-defeat narrative counterattack / 败后叙事反击 is the attempt to limit a military loss's political effects by reallocating its causes, agency, and credit, then using the revised account to damage an opponent's reputation or expose tensions among the victors.

## Current Synthesis
[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1107-caocao-xiexin-mohei-zhouyu-zhezhao-lijianji-you-duohen-lq_9istnfzy56zjejuur2g9yefhr|Hanji 1107]] supplies the initial case after the [[RedCliffsBattle|赤壁之战]]. [[CaoCao|曹操]] tells [[SunQuan|孙权]] that disease forced the withdrawal and that he burned the ships himself, reducing [[ZhouYuLateHan|周瑜]]'s victory to undeserved fame. The message does not reverse the battlefield result; it tries to weaken the result's reputational ownership.

The episode also links that reputational move to a real alliance problem. Once the common battle ends, Sun Quan, Zhou Yu, and [[LiuBei|刘备]] have different interests in [[Jingzhou|荆州]]. Narrative attack becomes more plausible when it attaches to an existing distribution conflict rather than inventing one from nothing.

## Key Claims
- Battlefield defeat does not automatically settle public explanation, causal attribution, or ownership of credit.
- A losing actor can combine true elements such as disease or self-destruction of materiel into a selectively exculpatory account.
- Minimizing a commander's contribution can turn military fame into suspicion inside the victorious camp.
- Narrative counterattack is strongest when it attaches to genuine postwar disputes over territory, revenue, office, or future security.
- Information maneuver can limit political damage but cannot by itself erase lost forces, territory, or initiative.
- Modern “cognitive warfare” language may clarify the mechanism, but should not imply institutional equivalence across periods.

## Evidence
- Causal reallocation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1107-caocao-xiexin-mohei-zhouyu-zhezhao-lijianji-you-duohen-lq_9istnfzy56zjejuur2g9yefhr|Hanji 1107]] has Cao Cao emphasize disease and his own ship burning after Red Cliffs.
- Credit denial: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1107-caocao-xiexin-mohei-zhouyu-zhezhao-lijianji-you-duohen-lq_9istnfzy56zjejuur2g9yefhr|Hanji 1107]] preserves the “虚获此名” claim against Zhou Yu.
- Material follow-through: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1107-caocao-xiexin-mohei-zhouyu-zhezhao-lijianji-you-duohen-lq_9istnfzy56zjejuur2g9yefhr|Hanji 1107]] connects Cao Cao's destruction of remaining ships and deployment of Cao Ren and Xu Huang to practical damage control.
- Alliance surface: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1107-caocao-xiexin-mohei-zhouyu-zhezhao-lijianji-you-duohen-lq_9istnfzy56zjejuur2g9yefhr|Hanji 1107]] connects the message to the post-battle divergence between Sun Quan and Liu Bei over Jingzhou.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not establish how Sun Quan actually received the letter or whether it caused later Sun-Liu conflict. Disease, enemy fire, deliberate destruction during retreat, and political messaging can all coexist, so the concept concerns selective emphasis rather than proof that every factual element is false. The host's claims about Cao Cao's precise intention, Sun Quan's suspicion, and a planned Jiangling consumption trap remain interpretations. “Cognitive warfare” is retained only as a modern analogy.

## What Changed
- Established the concept from the Red Cliffs post-battle letter.
- Separated narrative damage control from denial of the underlying military loss.
- Added alliance-interest divergence as a necessary qualification on the claimed effect.
