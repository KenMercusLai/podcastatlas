# Homeric Hospitality Test

## Definition

Homeric hospitality test is the use of guest-host conduct in [[TheOdyssey]] to reveal whether a household, ruler, or society respects the social order around strangers, resources, and return.

## Current Synthesis

In [[688-the-odyssey-return-of-the-king-part-2-glt2882541084]], hospitality is diagnostic. The [[Phaeacians]] and [[Eumaeus]] receive exposed or disguised strangers in ways that restore Odysseus' path home, while the [[SuitorsOfPenelope]] consume another man's house and abuse the disguised guest, making their failure visible before Odysseus reveals himself.

## Key Claims

- Hospitality scenes test social order because strangers expose how a community treats vulnerability and uncertainty.
- The Phaeacians represent idealized hospitality through welcome, abundance, song, and transport home.
- Eumaeus shows a smaller household version of good hospitality through loyal care for a beggar he does not recognize as Odysseus.
- The suitors invert hospitality by acting as parasitic guests and violent hosts inside Odysseus' own palace.
- These tests prepare recognition because Odysseus can observe who honors the household order before he discloses his identity.

## Evidence

- Positive royal hospitality: [[688-the-odyssey-return-of-the-king-part-2-glt2882541084]] describes the Phaeacians as abundant, wise, technologically marvelous hosts who send Odysseus home.
- Positive servant hospitality: [[688-the-odyssey-return-of-the-king-part-2-glt2882541084]] has Eumaeus welcome the disguised Odysseus and remain loyal to the absent household.
- Negative hospitality: [[688-the-odyssey-return-of-the-king-part-2-glt2882541084]] condemns the suitors for consuming Odysseus' estate, abusing household women, and assaulting the beggar.
- Strategic observation: [[688-the-odyssey-return-of-the-king-part-2-glt2882541084]] has Odysseus enter the palace disguised so he can test loyalties before the bow contest and attack.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept is bounded to this episode's retelling. It should not be generalized into all Greek xenia practice without additional sources, and it does not make the episode's violent punishment sequence ethically transparent for modern readers.
