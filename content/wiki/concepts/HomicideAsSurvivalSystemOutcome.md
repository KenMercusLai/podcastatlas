# Homicide as a Survival-System Outcome

## Definition

Homicide as a survival-system outcome is the principle that fatal-violence statistics reflect both the incidence and severity of assault and the probability that wounded people survive emergency response, transport, and treatment.

## Current Synthesis

The sources separate two processes often collapsed into one number: whether a serious assault occurs and whether the victim dies. Ambulance response, field stabilization, travel time, destination capability, specialist availability, imaging, bleeding control, surgery, and treatment quality can convert similar injuries into different outcomes. Those differences can also alter criminal charges, imprisonment, and measured homicide rates.

The Kennedy reconstruction and reported Memphis trends extend the mechanism across time. Modern trauma capability may make a historically lethal injury survivable, while falling mortality can coexist with more admissions, greater severity, or multiple wounds. [[NonfatalShootingMeasurement]] is therefore needed alongside homicide to determine whether underlying violence changed.

This does not make medicine the cause of the original violence or imply that every death was preventable. It identifies survival infrastructure as a mediator whose unequal distribution can compound racial and geographic inequality after injury and then appear in both public-health and criminal-justice data.

## Key Claims

- Homicide counts combine violence incidence with medically mediated fatality.
- Time-sensitive bleeding makes dispatch, transport, and trauma capability part of the causal path from injury to death.
- Unequal survival opportunities can change both mortality statistics and the legal consequences imposed on assailants.
- Medical progress can make homicide and nonfatal-shooting trends diverge.
- Offender-based racial comparisons can reveal this mechanism but require careful control for injury, case, transport, and treatment differences.
- The framework complements primary violence prevention rather than replacing it.

## Evidence

### Clinical mechanism

- [[guns-part-5-the-footnote]] reports [[BabakSarani]]'s explanation that survival from severe bleeding can decline with every minute before definitive care.
- [[guns-part-4-moral-hazard]] describes advances in transport, imaging, vascular intervention, neurosurgery, bleeding control, and swelling management through the Kennedy counterfactual.

### Trend divergence

- [[guns-part-4-moral-hazard]] reports rising and more severe gunshot admissions alongside lower mortality at a Memphis trauma center and describes national estimates of deaths avoided through trauma improvement.

### Alabama comparison

- [[guns-part-5-the-footnote]] summarizes Hanke and Gundlach's finding that victims associated with Black women imprisoned for homicide more often died during transport or after admission than victims associated with white women.

### Individual case

- [[guns-part-5-the-footnote]] presents [[BrandonYoung]]'s reported death after a response of more than an hour for a six-mile ambulance trip as the footnote that embodied the mechanism.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources do not supply comparable injury-severity measures, full transport and treatment records, causal identification, uncertainty, or replication. Victim outcome can also reflect weapon, wound location, scene safety, bystander action, comorbidity, hospital capability, and chance. The Alabama imprisonment counterfactual, Kennedy survival reconstruction, trauma-improvement rate, national counterfactual homicide range, and Memphis trend therefore remain hypotheses or episode-reported estimates rather than proof about specific lives or a fully identified national effect.

## What Changed

- Added historical treatment comparison and city-level trend divergence to the survival-system mechanism.
- Made nonfatal-shooting measurement the necessary complement to homicide counts.
