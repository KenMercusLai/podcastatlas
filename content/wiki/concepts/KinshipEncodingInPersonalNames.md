## Definition

Kinship encoding in personal names is the use of name components, repeated elements, order, or inheritance rules to identify a person's parent, ancestor, lineage branch, marital connection, or generation. It includes but is not limited to hereditary surnames.

## Current Synthesis

[[12-dongxifang-quming-de-yishu-luwyjjavdsketsbzhtbgyqv5p_nm|Weird History episode 12]] compares several mechanisms. Chinese 字辈 and shared characters place relatives within a lineage sequence; compound surnames and modern combined parental surnames can preserve more than one family marker; Russian father-names specify descent while surnames change grammatical form; Icelandic patronymics or matronymics identify a parent without necessarily creating a hereditary family surname; Arabic ibn/bin/bint chains can extend across generations; and Spanish names commonly retain paternal and maternal surnames.

The main analytical guardrail is that a familiar-looking ending or final name element cannot automatically be read as a Western-style surname. Correct interpretation requires the language's inheritance rules, gender forms, customary abbreviation, and local ordering. Names preserve kinship information, but translation and shortening can discard or misclassify it.

## Key Claims

- Hereditary family surnames are only one way to encode descent in a personal name.
- Patronymics and matronymics can identify a parent afresh in each generation rather than reproduce one fixed family name.
- Double and compound surnames can preserve paternal, maternal, marital, territorial, or prestige-bearing connections, but their order and everyday abbreviation vary.
- Generation names and repeated dominant given names can mark family continuity without functioning as surnames.
- Translators and readers need system-specific rules before inferring kinship from name order, endings, or the component used in short reference.

## Evidence

Parent and ancestry markers:

Multiple-family and generational markers:
- [[12-dongxifang-quming-de-yishu-luwyjjavdsketsbzhtbgyqv5p_nm|Weird History episode 12]] uses Spanish paternal-maternal surnames, Western compound surnames, Chinese combined parental surnames, 字辈, and parent-child linked naming among several Chinese ethnic groups to show multiple ways of preserving family relationships.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The examples establish diversity, not one comprehensive typology. Language-specific morphology, legal rules, gender conventions, adoption, marriage, personal preference, and changing contemporary practice require specialist verification. A name suggests a recognized relationship under a convention; it does not independently prove biological descent.
