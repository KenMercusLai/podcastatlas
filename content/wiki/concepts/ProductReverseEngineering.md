# Product Reverse Engineering

## Definition

Product reverse engineering is the iterative reconstruction of a product's materials, process conditions, structure, sensory properties, and storage behavior from observable evidence rather than from a complete specification.

## Current Synthesis

[[the-formula-the-formula|The Formula]] shows why an ingredient list is only a starting point. Hydration, ratios, proofing, fermentation, refrigeration, handling, cooking surface, and age all change the muffin, so reconstruction requires controlled iteration across the whole production path.

The case also separates three outcomes that can be mistakenly collapsed: structural resemblance, perceptual indistinguishability, and user preference. [[RachelWyman]]'s team reaches the first, fails the second, and exceeds the original on the third for the tested students. Reverse engineering can therefore create useful causal knowledge and a better alternative without proving exact duplication.

## Key Claims

- Observable ingredients underdetermine a product whose process and storage conditions materially affect the outcome.
- Iteration should vary ingredients and process parameters while comparing structure, taste, smell, and handling.
- Visual similarity does not establish perceptual identity.
- Failure to duplicate exactly can still reveal mechanisms and support improvement.
- Preference testing answers a different question from an indistinguishability test.

## Evidence

### Process reconstruction

- [[the-formula-the-formula]] reports successive changes to hydration, overproofing, flour, griddle preparation, vinegar, sourdough, refrigeration, and staling.

### Distinct evaluation outcomes

- [[the-formula-the-formula]] reports a close visual match, 61 percent correct odd-sample identification, and nearly 80 percent preference for the sourdough alternative.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

One educational baking experiment cannot establish a general method for all products. The team lacked the full industrial process, did not achieve indistinguishability, and tested technically knowledgeable students rather than a representative consumer population. Preference for an alternative also does not establish manufacturability, shelf life, cost, or commercial success.

## What Changed

- Established process knowledge as distinct from ingredient disclosure.
- Separated resemblance, identity, and preference as three evaluation targets.
