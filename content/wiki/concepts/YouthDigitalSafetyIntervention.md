# Youth Digital Safety Intervention

## Definition
Youth digital safety intervention is an autonomy-preserving approach that combines staged access, practical risk education, platform safeguards, realistic practice, and timely support so young people can recognize and respond to online harm rather than depend only on bans or surveillance.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats safety as both protection and capability building. Adults and platforms should reduce avoidable exposure, but young people also need repeated opportunities to recognize manipulation, understand why information is sensitive, test decisions, and receive proportionate feedback. AI may assist through risk detection, moderation, mediation, and simulated practice, yet the same systems can introduce errors, surveillance, abuse channels, or overbearing interventions.

The resulting design rule is graduated agency with reachable support. Interventions should be realistic enough to transfer to ordinary online life, specific enough to act on, and restrained enough that teens retain meaningful choice. Independent testing and red-teaming remain necessary because a feature labeled as protective can fail to detect risk or can itself become an attack surface.

## Key Claims
- Restriction alone cannot teach self-regulation or situational judgment.
- Access should expand gradually with development, demonstrated capability, and reachable adult support.
- Safety advice is more usable when it names concrete information and explains the underlying risk.
- Simulated high-risk interactions can turn abstract warnings into recognition and decision practice.
- AI can detect, moderate, coach, or mediate, but should support rather than replace youth judgment.
- Effective prompts preserve agency and avoid tones that teens experience as parentalizing or controlling.
- Safeguards require independent evaluation, abuse testing, and attention to privacy and beneficial-use costs.

## Evidence
- Graduated access and adult guidance - [[tech-20261112-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261112-mp-tech-pod-128]] argues for intentional, staged technology access and adult honesty about shared screen-use difficulty.
- Realistic practice and feedback - [[tech-20261112-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261112-mp-tech-pod-128]] describes simulated grooming stages, risky social scenarios, and teen acceptance of non-coercive prompts.
- AI intervention and failure boundary - [[tech-20261112-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261112-mp-tech-pod-128]] presents real-time moderation and coaching alongside abuse, attack, and red-team requirements.
- Practical risk communication - [[tech-20261112-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261112-mp-tech-pod-128]] favors specific rules about names and addresses with explanations over blanket stranger-danger advice.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The bounded source does not provide intervention protocols, sample sizes, effect sizes, privacy assessments, longitudinal outcomes, or comparisons with non-AI education. Simulation willingness does not establish reduced real-world harm, and AI monitoring can create false positives, missed risk, surveillance, or new attack surfaces. Developmental stage, disability, family context, culture, platform, and severity of danger may require different levels of adult involvement or professional support.

## What Changed
- Established a framework joining staged access, realistic practice, AI assistance, and retained youth agency.
