# Focused Deterrence

## Definition

Focused deterrence is a violence-reduction strategy that identifies the small connected network driving a disproportionate share of serious harm, communicates directly with its members, offers credible support and community-backed alternatives, and applies clear, predictable consequences to continued violence.

## Current Synthesis

The episode's model is narrower than indiscriminate intensive policing and broader than enforcement alone. It depends on precise network knowledge, trusted communication, practical help, community legitimacy, and coordinated legal consequences. Removing the supportive side changes the intervention rather than merely making it tougher.

Boston supplies the reported proof case, while Baltimore supplies both the implementation lesson and a closer view of case-specific prevention. Part One shows weekly participants mapping grievances, grief, possible retaliation, protectors, and trusted intermediaries without making arrest the default response. Durable adoption requires local ownership, political authority, service capacity, repeated coordination, and willingness to intervene preventively around people assessed as at risk before a new offense or victimization occurs.

## Key Claims

- Serious violence may be concentrated in a small, identifiable, highly connected network.
- Narrow targeting can avoid treating an entire neighborhood as equally dangerous.
- Direct communication should pair credible consequences with practical assistance and a route away from violence.
- Community figures and service providers are constitutive parts of the strategy, not optional additions to policing.
- Implementation fidelity and durable local ownership matter because punishment-only or expert-dependent versions can collapse.
- Preventive intervention creates ethical duties around evidence quality, proportionality, privacy, coercion, and service access.
- Relationship-specific prevention can use protection, relocation, counseling, and trusted moral influence rather than enforcement alone.

## Evidence

### Concentration and design

- [[wolfgang-s-law-part-two]] says [[DavidKennedyCriminologist|David Kennedy]] and colleagues identified a small connected Boston group driving much of the violence and built [[OperationCeasefireBoston]] around communication, support, warnings, and enforcement.
- [[wolfgang-s-law-part-one]] connects [[MarvinWolfgang]]'s cohort finding to Baltimore's roughly 700-person Western District network and other concentration examples.

### Implementation and continuity

- [[wolfgang-s-law-part-two]] reports early gains in multiple cities but says programs weakened after outside experts left; Baltimore's later case illustrates the wider team and resources needed for continuity.
- [[wolfgang-s-law-part-one]] shows a weekly Baltimore coalition using personal relationships and [[TrustedCommunityViolenceInterruption]] to assess and interrupt retaliation pathways.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episodes report large declines but do not present a causal evaluation, comparison design, spillover analysis, false-positive rate, or participant perspective. Network labels and preventive intervention can themselves produce surveillance, coercion, stigma, or unequal service access. Local violence patterns, legal authority, institutional trust, and service capacity may limit transfer.

## What Changed

- Established focused deterrence as an integrated support, communication, community, and enforcement strategy rather than a punishment-only tactic.
- Made implementation fidelity and local institutional ownership part of the concept.
- Added expressive-retaliation case review and accumulated community trust as operational mechanisms within the integrated model.
