# Dual Executive Power Risk / 双首长权力风险

Dual executive power risk / 双首长权力风险 is the governance problem [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-44-yi-ge-gongsi-weishenme-buneng-you-liang-wei-ceo-lsz0xk6-0wwznkc62k-xkoxgz-r]] draws from [[HanXuanhuiwang|韩宣惠王]]'s plan to appoint [[GongzhongHan|公众]] and [[GongshuHan|公叔]] as joint chief ministers. The episode uses the modern "two CEOs" question to frame an older Warring States concern about two supreme executive officers under one ruler.

[[MiuLiu|谬刘]] gives the hard version of the warning. Two people with equal nominal authority will not necessarily remain balanced. If one is stronger, that person can gather a domestic faction and hollow out the ruler. If one is weaker, that person may seek outside support and trade away state interests to survive the internal contest.

The host adds the limiting condition: dual leadership is not the only danger. A single empowered minister can also monopolize power, become a usurper, or destroy the state if the person is not worthy. The practical lesson is not simply "one executive good, two executives bad"; it is that power-sharing, person selection, accountability, and faction incentives have to be designed together.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-984-liangzhouxi-junfa-neidou-ta-weihe-di-yige-daoxia-lurb2qfzsa7e-owh-dl-m-sccze5|Hanji 984]] extends the pattern from formal co-ministers to informal warlord co-control. [[LiJueLateHan|李傕]] and [[GuoSiLateHan|郭汜]] had jointly benefited from controlling Chang'an after Wang Yun, but the source says the same field cannot contain two strong military centers seeking court and army control. [[GuoSiWife|郭汜妻]]'s one-cage/two-roosters warning is presented as a proximate trigger, while the deeper problem is the absence of a stable authority boundary between armed co-rulers.

[[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-66-zhongguo-lishishang-naxie-diwang-shi-bei-huohuo-e-si-de-1-lvl1uxjadiedqwad-skxrwf-usnq]] extends the concept from two ministers to an abdicated ruler and a reigning successor. The host compares [[ZhaoWulingwang|赵武灵王]] and [[ZhaoHe|赵何]] to a chairman and general manager, but the source shows why the analogy breaks down: the old ruler's symbolic authority and the new ruler's formal court do not have clean jurisdictional boundaries. This is more precisely [[PartialAbdicationPowerSplit|退位留权式权力分裂]], but it belongs near dual-executive risk because rival centers can still form around each authority.

[[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-66-zhongguo-lishishang-naxie-diwang-shi-bei-huohuo-e-si-de-2-ltsw2eilkegs5yjtrp6gkhrmsxsg]] shows the failure mode after ambiguity becomes violence. Zhao Zhang's forged summons works because the old ruler's name still carries force, while Zhao He's court has enough practical authority to mobilize defense and later ignore Zhao Wuling Wang's confinement. The result is not stable co-governance but a winner-take-all settlement disguised by filial and political silence.

The episode's Song-era analogy extends the pattern beyond ancient Han. In [[SongFactionalPolitics|宋代党争]], attempts to keep new-party and old-party factions balanced often failed quickly, while choosing one camp created its own risks of retaliation, capture, and policy overcorrection.

## Key Claims
- Equal titles do not guarantee equal power.
- Dual executives can turn decision-making into faction competition if no authority boundary settles conflict.
- Informal armed co-control can reproduce dual-executive risk when no boundary allocates court access, troop command, and prize control.
- A predecessor-successor split can behave like a dual-executive design when symbolic authority, formal office, and command channels do not align.
- The stronger executive may build domestic partisans; the weaker executive may seek external backing.
- Concentrating authority in one executive can also be dangerous, so the risk is structural as well as numerical.
- Advice about power structure may itself be factional, as the episode speculates about Miu Liu's possible private interest.
- A viable design needs capable people, trusted boundaries, and correction mechanisms, not only a headcount rule.
- Once rival centers have mobilized force, later appeals to family hierarchy or nominal seniority cannot restore the intended balance.
