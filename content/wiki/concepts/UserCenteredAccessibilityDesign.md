# User-Centered Accessibility Design / 使用者中心无障碍设计

## Definition
User-centered accessibility design evaluates a facility or service by whether people with differing capabilities can complete a real journey or task, not merely by whether a prescribed accessibility object is present.

## Current Synthesis
The episode's strongest example is the blind path. A path can satisfy placement and clearance rules yet fail if it does not answer where the traveler needs to go, how to reach the lift, where the nearest train door is, or when the route ends. Accessibility therefore connects physical construction to orientation, destination, information, boarding, transfer, and recovery from uncertainty.

The same test applies beyond blindness. Larger train and platform labels help low-vision travelers and hurried passengers; lifts help wheelchair users, people with luggage, and caregivers with strollers; legible bus displays help people with myopia or astigmatism; orderly ride-hailing pickup and protected non-motorized space reduce cognitive and safety burdens for many users.

User participation is evidence, not a ceremonial consultation. Standards and enforcement still matter, but users reveal whether the full workflow functions. Low-cost process redesign can sometimes outperform conspicuous infrastructure when it removes matching, queueing, navigation, or information failure.

## Key Claims
- Accessibility is a journey-level outcome rather than a checklist of installed objects.
- Destination, orientation, legibility, boarding, transfer, and error recovery must be designed together.
- Direct user testimony is necessary to discover failures hidden by construction compliance.
- Redundant, prioritized, and readable information often benefits disabled and non-disabled users together.
- Process redesign can remove barriers without requiring only expensive physical reconstruction.
- Law, standards, enforcement, and everyday mutual assistance complement rather than replace usable design.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source offers lived examples rather than controlled usability studies, engineering specifications, or a legal compliance audit. A design effective in one transport system may not transfer without local testing. “Universal” benefit does not erase disability-specific needs, and informal help should not substitute for reliable infrastructure, service responsibility, or rights enforcement.

## What Changed
- Established a task-completion test for accessibility across infrastructure, information, and service workflows.
