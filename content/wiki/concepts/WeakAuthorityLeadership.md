## Definition
Weak Authority Leadership is the condition in which a nominal superior lacks the resolve, knowledge, or reward-punishment credibility needed to turn rank into effective command.

## Current Synthesis
Hanji 768 frames [[EmperorHeOfHan|Emperor He]] as a ruler who could sometimes identify self-interested obstruction yet failed to enforce a correct direction. The clearest case is [[CaoBaoEasternHan|Cao Bao's]] Han-rites project: Emperor He had used the rites and recognized that opposition by [[ZhangPuEasternHan|Zhang Pu]] and Zhang Min was not neutral, but he still abandoned the project. Hanji 569 adds a different mechanism under [[HanAidi|汉哀帝]]: when [[FuTaihouWesternHan|傅太后]] makes him reverse [[FuQianWesternHan|傅迁]]'s dismissal, formal rank remains with the emperor while credible personnel control shifts upward to the patron who can veto him. Together the sources show weak authority as an enforcement problem: subordinates follow the power center whose decisions hold.

## Key Claims
- Formal hierarchy does not produce authority unless the superior can back judgment with action.
- Knowing that a subordinate is right is insufficient if the leader cannot defend the subordinate against organized pressure.
- Reward and punishment are not decorative; they are the mechanism by which institutional expectations become credible.
- Weak leaders often allow skilled subordinates to be isolated while self-interested critics define the operational result.
- Reversed personnel decisions reveal a split between nominal hierarchy and effective authority, prompting observers to realign with the actor whose veto is credible.
- Leniency can be prudent in high-risk royal-house cases, but repeated under-enforcement can also reveal weak command.

## Evidence
- Cao Bao's abandoned work: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-768-lishi-gaosu-ni-jidu-shi-shaxin-lgk8qoxvs5ax8oikgecrxqvispna|Hanji 768]] says Emperor He stopped using Cao Bao's rites after repeated Zhang Pu and Zhang Min objections, despite recognizing self-interest in the opposition.
- Management rule: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-768-lishi-gaosu-ni-jidu-shi-shaxin-lgk8qoxvs5ax8oikgecrxqvispna|Hanji 768]] states the leadership standard that good work should be rewarded and errors punished.
- Royal-house leniency: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-768-lishi-gaosu-ni-jidu-shi-shaxin-lgk8qoxvs5ax8oikgecrxqvispna|Hanji 768]] shows Emperor He choosing limited punishment for Liu Chang, which can be read as containment but also fits his broader cautious governing style.
- Personnel-veto case: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-569-nianhui-buneng-ting-doushi-jiade-zhichang-zhandui-caishi-zhende-lvjg3qcsgkq4ltai8mqt6p26smk6|Hanji 569]] says Aidi dismisses Fu Qian but retains him after Fu Taihou intervenes, while Kong Guang and Shi Dan warn that the contradictory edicts damage command credibility.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Hanji 768 distinguishes weak enforcement from all forms of leniency. Emperor He's limited punishment of Liu Chang may have been prudent because royal-house usurpation accusations were explosive, while the Cao Bao case more directly displays failure to defend a work product the ruler had reason to value. Hanji 569's workplace analogy is interpretive and does not prove Fu Qian's private attitude or every court official's allegiance; its strongest evidence is the observable reversal and the ministers' explicit credibility warning.

## What Changed
- Added Hanji 569's personnel-veto mechanism: reversing a dismissal under higher pressure tells subordinates where effective authority sits.
- Created the concept from Hanji 768's leadership interpretation of Emperor He, Cao Bao, and Zhang Pu.
- Distinguished weak implementation from strategic restraint in politically dangerous cases.
