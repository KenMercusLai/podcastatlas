# Invented Tradition

## Definition

Invented tradition is the selective assembly, formalization, or recent creation of practices presented as continuous with an older past, often to support identity, legitimacy, tourism, or collective memory.

## Current Synthesis

The sources show that invention rarely means fabrication from nothing. In the Day of the Dead case, Catholic calendar and ritual, Indigenous flowers and language, colonial foodways, political cartoons, muralism, nationalism, and film are recombined into forms that can look timeless. The *Spectre* parade is the clearest recent case: a fictional procession borrowed recognizable symbols and then became a model for a real recurring civic event.

The bank-lion case adds decentralized commercial diffusion. An exceptional pair of [[HSBCLions|corporate lions]], older Chinese guardian-animal conventions, banknotes, feng-shui explanations, stone-carving catalogues, and branch procurement combine into a practice sometimes perceived as ancient banking custom. No state or single designer needs to standardize the result; repetition, visual familiarity, and auspicious sales language can do that work.

The concept is most useful when it distinguishes historical layers rather than mocking participants. A tradition can be recently standardized, politically promoted, or media-shaped and still acquire genuine social meaning.

## Key Claims

- Claims of uninterrupted antiquity require evidence for the specific practice, not only thematic resemblance.
- Modern traditions commonly reuse older materials rather than appearing ex nihilo.
- States, artists, markets, tourism, and media can all select which historical layers become publicly dominant.
- Origin stories can conceal political conflict, colonial exchange, or recent institutional promotion.
- Recent construction does not make collective attachment or lived meaning unreal.
- Commercial copying can strip a symbol of its original attribution while making it appear more traditional and general.

## Evidence

- Ritual layering: [[264-mexico-day-of-the-dead-glt9022174156]] separates Catholic-Iberian calendars and foods from later Indigenous-facing national presentation.
- State promotion: [[264-mexico-day-of-the-dead-glt9022174156]] links the festival's twentieth-century public form to post-revolutionary anti-clerical nationalism.
- Artistic reuse: [[264-mexico-day-of-the-dead-glt9022174156]] traces [[LaCatrina]] through Posada and Rivera rather than directly to ancient ritual.
- Media creation: [[264-mexico-day-of-the-dead-glt9022174156]] describes a film-invented parade becoming a real annual event.
- Corporate-to-commercial diffusion: [[gkwriaiorn5rawbazwtstr0k-gkwriaiorn5rawbazwtstr0k]] traces HSBC's reclining male lions into banknotes, global copies, stone catalogues, altered placement, and newly attached male-female or wealth-attraction meanings.
- Historical discontinuity: [[gkwriaiorn5rawbazwtstr0k-gkwriaiorn5rawbazwtstr0k]] reports that traditional money shops and most photographed Republican-era banks lacked freestanding entrance lions, weakening the claim of uninterrupted banking custom.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The label should not imply that every component is recent, centrally designed, or falsely experienced. The sources preserve older Catholic, colonial, Indigenous, local, artistic, Chinese guardian-animal, and geomantic inputs. Claims about the timing and extent of 1930s Mexican invention, ancient architectural restrictions, national bank-lion prevalence, workshop supply, and the meaning intended by any specific branch remain source-scoped. Similar outcomes can also arise independently rather than through one line of copying.

## What Changed

- Added decentralized commercial copying and auspicious reinterpretation alongside state, artistic, and media-led invention.
- Distinguished a corporate original from the later appearance of a general ancient banking custom.
