## Definition

年号制度 / Era-name system is the practice of dating years through a ruler- or court-proclaimed era name. In this wiki's current evidence, it is both a chronological convention and a claim that political authority can define the public frame within which time is recorded.

## Current Synthesis

The supplied episodes interpret era names as instruments of standardization, memory, and legitimacy. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-300-hanwudi-you-yi-weida-faming-yingxiang-zhongguo-shangqiannian-luwfqqrb1mi5zf0ufmtvnpbmkx7p|Hanji 300]] starts from coordination: imperial and vassal reign-year systems can produce competing local clocks, whereas one court-proclaimed era name supplies a shared reference. It also shows how [[AuspiciousOmenPolitics|祥瑞政治]] could wrap that administrative reform in a story of Heaven, sacrifice, and a hunted beast.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-301-tai-liqi-jiemi-nianhao-zui-da-wulong-shijian-lrsbd_9zfkcofnosolvrjvfcdfyg|Hanji 301]] extends the same institution into memory and legitimacy. A shared court chronology can make administration and commemoration more uniform, but it also competes with local or rival dating systems. The same symbolic power makes naming errors politically sensitive: [[ZhaoKuangyin|赵匡胤]]'s reported reuse of [[WangYanFormerShu|王衍]]'s 乾德 illustrates how an unexamined name can preserve the memory of an earlier regime inside a new dynasty's official time.

Era names also create comparison costs. Frequent changes and simultaneous regimes can make cross-polity chronology difficult, while Common Era dating supplies a shared external reference. The current evidence supports this political and historiographic interpretation, but it does not independently establish the institution's complete origin, diffusion timeline, or every numerical example used by the episode.

## Key Claims

- Era names combine chronological coordination with a visible claim to political authority.
- Auspicious signs can legitimate and narratively name a chronology without explaining the institution's entire administrative origin.
- A central dating system can marginalize rival local commemorative frames, although the episode does not quantify that effect.
- Reusing a predecessor regime's era name can create a legitimacy and historical-memory problem for a new court.
- Frequent changes and concurrent era names complicate comparison across rulers, regimes, and regions.
- Common Era dating improves cross-regional comparability without erasing the historical meaning of indigenous era names.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The current evidence is two adjacent podcast summaries, not a complete institutional history or a direct reading of primary chronological records.
- Hanji 300's wording calls the hunted-beast era “元朔,” although its own explanation points toward hunting; this likely transcription or chronology problem is preserved rather than silently repaired.
- Calling Han Wudi the straightforward inventor of era names compresses the episodes' own account of retrospective naming.
- The claim that era names were created partly to suppress local commemoration is an interpretive proposition; coordination, ritual, dynastic identity, and recordkeeping may also matter.
- The 乾德 mirror story demonstrates the episode's argument only if its inscription, ownership, discovery sequence, and causal link to 开宝 are reliable; all remain source-scoped here.
- The episode's adoption dates, counts of era names, early Han sequence, and claim about Japan as the only current user require independent verification.
