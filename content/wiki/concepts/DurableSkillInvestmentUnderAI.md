## Definition

Durable skill investment under AI is the practice of allocating learning effort according to whether a capability mainly performs a procedure that tools can cheaply reproduce or governs goals, evidence, standards, judgment, and adaptation across changing tools.

## Current Synthesis

The source's “disposable” and “fixed” skill labels are most useful as a portfolio heuristic, not a permanent taxonomy. Editing commands, office interfaces, formula syntax, image operations, and parts of programming can become cheaper to invoke through AI, reducing the return on memorizing every procedure. Yet the same operational knowledge can still be necessary for verification, unusual cases, high-consequence work, or understanding what the system is doing.

The more durable layer is meta-operational: decide what matters, locate trustworthy inputs, ask decision-relevant questions, communicate a specification, recognize quality, learn an unfamiliar domain, and accept responsibility for the result. Durability is therefore conditional. A skill merits deeper internalization when failure is costly, tacit judgment is dense, independent recovery matters, or the skill builds the mental model needed to supervise AI; it can be delegated more aggressively when the procedure is standardized, reversible, observable, and cheaply checked.

## Key Claims

- Automation changes the return on learning a procedure without making all procedural knowledge worthless.
- Goal definition, evidence judgment, communication, taste, analytical questioning, and self-directed learning transfer across tools more readily than interface memorization.
- Verification requires enough domain knowledge to recognize missing steps, unsafe assumptions, and false completion.
- Learning depth should rise with consequence, irreversibility, tacit complexity, and the need to recover when tools fail.
- AI can lower entry barriers to unfamiliar work, but useful delegation still requires task decomposition, tool choice, and acceptance criteria.
- Skill portfolios should be revisited as model capability, costs, job design, and personal goals change.

## Evidence

- Operational-skill repricing: [[ep391-dang-ai-wancheng-suoyou-gongzuo-yige-ren-yingdang-ruhe-zhengming-ziji-de-jiazhi-gkwridootojhakrdqqtwkv3q]] applies the “disposable” label to editing, image manipulation, office software, and parts of programming as examples of procedures increasingly callable through AI.
- Durable governing capacities: [[ep391-dang-ai-wancheng-suoyou-gongzuo-yige-ren-yingdang-ruhe-zhengming-ziji-de-jiazhi-gkwridootojhakrdqqtwkv3q]] identifies information acquisition and analysis, writing, taste, data analysis, communication, and self-study as capacities that shape what AI is asked to do and whether its work is accepted.
- Conditional delegation: [[ep391-dang-ai-wancheng-suoyou-gongzuo-yige-ren-yingdang-ruhe-zhengming-ziji-de-jiazhi-gkwridootojhakrdqqtwkv3q]] recommends stronger co-creation and checking in a person's core domain while using AI to enter lower-return unfamiliar domains.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The framework comes from practitioner discussion rather than longitudinal labor-market or learning-outcome evidence. Operational fluency can itself create speed, intuition, independence, and better verification; calling it disposable may encourage premature deskilling. Some apparently durable capacities can also be decomposed or partly learned by models, while regulation, liability, access, and workplace design may preserve procedural roles even when automation is technically possible. The relevant decision is how deeply to internalize a capability, not whether it belongs forever to a human or machine.

## What Changed

- Created a conditional skill-investment framework from the episode's “disposable” and “fixed” distinction.
- Replaced a binary human-versus-AI claim with consequence, reversibility, verification, and transfer criteria.
