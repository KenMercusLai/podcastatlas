# Grief Response Agency

## Definition
Grief response agency is the distinction between largely involuntary grief reactions and the limited but meaningful choices a bereaved person may have over attention, interpretation, action, and participation in daily life.

## Current Synthesis
The episode treats shock, pain, sweating, sleeplessness, numbness, emotional swings, and sudden “ambushes” as reactions that cannot simply be chosen away. Agency enters one layer later. A person may sometimes decide whether to continue a counterfactual loop, view a photograph, use social media late at night, attend a proceeding, check on family, seek awe, or take a restorative break.

The practical test is functional rather than moral: is this helping or harming me now? That question does not classify grief itself as bad, require positive thinking, or guarantee control. It creates a pause in which the bereaved person can experiment with a response while [[DualProcessBereavementOscillation]] preserves permission for both loss and restored activity. Agency therefore means participation in adaptation, not sole responsibility for its outcome.

## Key Claims
- Intense physical and emotional grief reactions can occur outside voluntary control.
- Thoughts, actions, attention, and environmental choices may retain partial flexibility even during grief.
- A helping-or-harming test can interrupt automatic continuation without judging the underlying emotion.
- Counterfactual limits can protect attention while leaving room for deliberate contact with loss.
- Restorative activity and positive emotion can coexist with sorrow and an enduring bond.
- Agency must remain optional, supported, and bounded by trauma, health, circumstance, and clinical need.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Agency is uneven and context-dependent. The source does not show that all thoughts or actions are voluntarily controllable, that its strategies prevent prolonged grief, or that a person who remains impaired has chosen poorly. Acute trauma, depression, suicidality, medical risk, family crisis, poverty, unsafe conditions, and culturally specific mourning may constrain available responses and require qualified care. One personal account and a structured episode summary cannot establish treatment effectiveness.
