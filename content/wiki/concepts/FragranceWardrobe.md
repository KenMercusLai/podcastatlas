# Fragrance Wardrobe / 香水衣橱

## Definition
Fragrance wardrobe / 香水衣橱 is the consumption pattern of owning and rotating multiple perfumes or scent products for different moods, seasons, occasions, identities, or layering effects instead of relying on one signature scent.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources connect category-making history to present product economics. [[social-insecurity-numbers-the-money-behind-the-manosphere-7eb4966be1685ee4f19adde69e240aaa]] presents [[AnnetteGreen]] as a mid-century advocate who encouraged women to move beyond a single, gift-selected bottle reserved for special occasions. Her wardrobe metaphor made fragrance an everyday emotional choice tied to mood, clothing, weather, and season.

[[meizhuang-xiaoyang-bianshen-duli-shangpin-pinpai-weihe-dou-zai-fenfen-buju-mini-chanpin-1011859226]] shows how that logic now supports small standalone perfume products. If consumers rotate several scents, 10-30 ml bottles reduce the cash needed to build a collection and make experimentation, layering, and carrying easier. [[FragranceProductStorytelling]] remains important because each scent needs a legible role within the set, while [[MiniBeautyStandaloneProduct]] supplies the format and merchandising layer.

## Key Claims
- Fragrance wardrobe shifts perfume demand from single-signature loyalty toward rotation and collection.
- Small bottles fit the pattern because they reduce the cash needed to own multiple scents.
- Mood, season, occasion, and layering become purchase logic, not just marketing copy.
- The wardrobe frame historically helped recast fragrance as everyday self-expression rather than a rare luxury selected by someone else.
- The pattern increases the importance of scent naming, story, and use-scene explanation.
- It can support standalone mini fragrance products even when consumers do not intend to buy the full-size version.

## Evidence
- Rotation evidence: [[meizhuang-xiaoyang-bianshen-duli-shangpin-pinpai-weihe-dou-zai-fenfen-buju-mini-chanpin-1011859226]] says more consumers change fragrance by mood, season, and occasion or layer multiple scents.
- Historical-language evidence: [[meizhuang-xiaoyang-bianshen-duli-shangpin-pinpai-weihe-dou-zai-fenfen-buju-mini-chanpin-1011859226]] notes that the wardrobe metaphor existed in the perfume industry as early as the 1920s.
- Product-format evidence: [[meizhuang-xiaoyang-bianshen-duli-shangpin-pinpai-weihe-dou-zai-fenfen-buju-mini-chanpin-1011859226]] says brands are offering 10-30 ml bottles that consumers can buy separately to build collections at lower total price.
- Category-making evidence: [[social-insecurity-numbers-the-money-behind-the-manosphere-7eb4966be1685ee4f19adde69e240aaa]] says [[AnnetteGreen]] promoted multiple fragrances chosen by mood, clothing, weather, or season and contrasted that practice with one special-occasion bottle.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources establish a historical and consumer frame, not a complete fragrance-market forecast. The earlier existence of wardrobe language means Green should be understood as an influential advocate rather than its proven inventor. The obituary's industry-growth figures do not isolate her causal contribution, while the product-trend source does not settle long-term repeat rates, brand loyalty, margin structure, or whether wardrobe behavior expands total demand rather than shifting spending among more small bottles.
