# Chronic Traumatic Encephalopathy Evidence Boundary

## Definition
The chronic traumatic encephalopathy evidence boundary separates evidence of postmortem tau pathology and association with repeated head impacts from representative prevalence estimates, prediction of symptoms in a living person, and proof that CTE caused an individual's depression or suicide.

## Current Synthesis
[[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof|Burden of Proof]] presents CTE as a serious reason for concern about repeated football impacts. [[OwenThomas|Owen Thomas's]] postmortem diagnosis and the reported brain-bank findings show that characteristic pathology can be found in exposed players, while reported cognitive, emotional, and behavioral symptoms make continued exposure consequential.

The same source states the principal limits. Donated brains are selected largely because families already suspect impairment, so a result such as 110 of 111 examined former NFL players cannot be generalized into the prevalence of CTE among all players. A diagnosis after death also cannot allocate causal weight among brain pathology, psychiatric illness, stress, personality, environment, and other contributors to one suicide. The evidence can therefore support precaution and further study without supporting a single-cause retrospective verdict.

## Key Claims
- Repeated head impacts are the episode's relevant exposure, not only formally diagnosed concussions.
- Postmortem tau pathology can support a CTE diagnosis without revealing the person's complete symptom history or cause of death.
- A donated-brain bank selected through family concern cannot estimate population prevalence directly.
- Reported cognitive, impulsive, depressive, memory, emotional, and suicidal symptoms are associations, not a unique clinical signature.
- Longitudinal comparison of similar exposed and unexposed groups would improve causal inference but delays timely certainty.
- Evidence can be strong enough to justify proportionate risk reduction while remaining too weak for individual causal certainty.

## Evidence
- Thomas diagnosis - [[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof]] reports that examination after [[OwenThomas|Thomas's]] death found CTE.
- Mechanism and phenotype - [[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof]] connects repeated impacts, tau accumulation, and a broad reported symptom range.
- Selected sample - [[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof]] reports 110 of 111 examined former NFL players while explicitly warning that donation followed suspected problems.
- Missing comparison - [[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof]] says definitive proof would require following comparable player and non-player groups through much of life.
- Unexamined parallel - [[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof]] treats Kyle Ambrose's decline as circumstantial because no CTE autopsy was conducted.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Selection bias limits prevalence inference but does not erase verified pathology in examined brains. Conversely, pathology in one person does not prove that every reported symptom arose from it. The source is a structured podcast summary rather than neuropathology criteria, autopsy documentation, a systematic review, or a prospective cohort. Its 2018 account should not be treated as current medical guidance, a method for diagnosing living people, or evidence that suicide has a single cause.

## What Changed
- Created a boundary between postmortem pathology, selected brain-bank findings, population prevalence, and individual causation.
- Preserved repeated-impact concern while preventing the 110-of-111 figure from becoming a population rate.
- Marked suicide attribution as multifactorial and distinct from the institutional case for precaution.
