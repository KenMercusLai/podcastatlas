## Definition
Internet political amplification is the source-scoped model that digital networks intensify, distribute, and organize pre-existing political impulses rather than creating populism, fascism, socialism, identity politics, or protest from nothing.

## Current Synthesis
The model separates origins from transmission. A political current may predate the internet while feeds, group formation, rapid communication, and public argument increase its visibility, emotional intensity, coordination capacity, and geographic reach. Amplification is not one-directional: online conflict may mobilize street action, substitute expressive argument for action, expose users to opponents, or provoke protest when authorities disable access.

## Key Claims
- Media systems can alter the scale and speed of political organization without originating the underlying ideology.
- Cross-cutting exposure may increase antagonism as well as understanding, so a leaky [[FilterBubble]] is not necessarily a calmer public sphere.
- Online argument can sometimes absorb political energy and sometimes become a bridge to offline mobilization.
- Internet shutdowns can be politically provocative because suppressing a routine coordination system imposes visible collective costs.
- Historical media analogies are useful only when differences in platform control, surveillance, reach, and feedback are kept explicit.

## Evidence
- Older ideas, new conduit: [[94-silicon-valley-part-2-glt3075342575]] records Andreessen's claim that contemporary technologies amplify and organize political traditions that long predate social media.
- Exposure and conflict: [[94-silicon-valley-part-2-glt3075342575]] contrasts affinity-based online grouping with frequent arguments among strangers.
- Mobilization and suppression: [[94-silicon-valley-part-2-glt3075342575]] uses the Arab Spring and internet shutdowns to argue that network control can move conflict between online and street settings.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source offers historical analogy and participant interpretation rather than comparative causal evidence. It does not isolate platform design from economic grievance, institutional weakness, political leadership, television, demographic change, or state repression. “Amplification” can also become too elastic unless a case specifies what increased—reach, frequency, intensity, coordination, recruitment, or conversion into action.

## What Changed
- Created a bounded distinction between the origins of political ideas and the network mechanisms that intensify them.
- Preserved both mobilization and substitution as possible offline effects.
