# De-Royalization Ritual

## Definition

De-royalization ritual is the coordinated use of names, clothing, posture, custody, space, procedure, bodily handling, burial, and attacks on dynastic memory to remove monarchy's sacred or exceptional status in public.

## Current Synthesis

The trials and deaths of [[LouisXVI|Louis XVI]] and [[MarieAntoinette|Marie Antoinette]] worked through repeated reversals of monarchy rather than one declaration alone. “Louis Capet,” “Widow Capet,” and “Louis-Charles Capet”; ordinary clothing, custody, binding, haircuts, transport, and burial; separation and political re-education of [[LouisCharles|the dynastic child]]; and destruction of royal remains at Saint-Denis asserted that republican authority could reduce a royal family to citizens, criminals, and historical debris. The exceptional security, propaganda, and spectacle around that reduction nevertheless exposed the political power that official normality tried to deny.

## Key Claims

- Political status can be dismantled through repeated embodied signs before formal punishment is complete.
- Naming can replace dynastic self-understanding with the category imposed by a new regime.
- Ordinary criminal procedure can communicate equality while also functioning as ideological theatre.
- Reused royal spaces make regime change visible by reversing their former meaning.
- Denial of exceptional burial and attacks on tombs or monuments attempt to prevent royal bodies and historical memory from sustaining dynasty.
- The scale of security and spectacle can expose the exceptional power that official normality tries to deny.
- Custody and forced re-education can target inherited identity when killing a dynastic child would be politically unacceptable.

## Evidence

### Courtroom reversal

- [[547-the-french-revolution-the-execution-of-the-king-part-4-glt2184509954]] groups the Capet name, civilian dress, seated and covered deputies, humble chair, and permission to sit as reversals of court hierarchy.

### Execution as ordinary and extraordinary

- [[547-the-french-revolution-the-execution-of-the-king-part-4-glt2184509954]] joins common-criminal restraint, haircut, and guillotine procedure to a controlled route, artillery, roughly 80,000 armed men, drums, and public display.

### Burial and memory

- [[547-the-french-revolution-the-execution-of-the-king-part-4-glt2184509954]] describes burial away from the royal dead, in quicklime and without a marker.

### Family, custody, and historical erasure

- [[709-the-terror-the-execution-of-marie-antoinette-part-3-glt8774364224]] groups imposed Capet names, separation and re-education of Louis-Charles, destruction of the Saint-Denis tombs, the queen's common-criminal treatment, and pauper burial as attacks on dynasty and memory.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The concept describes the episode's interpretation of coordinated symbolism; it does not prove that every participant shared one plan or meaning.
- Treating Louis as ordinary did not make the event socially ordinary or prevent royalist sacralization.
- Crowd, security, and ritual details remain source-scoped where the episode does not supply full documentary comparison.
- The institutional restoration of French monarchy after 1815 qualifies any claim that revolutionary de-royalization permanently eliminated monarchy, while leaving open whether its older sacred prestige recovered.
- Later royalist testimony about Louis-Charles requires source criticism even where multiple accounts support severe mistreatment.

## What Changed

- Extended the concept from one king's status destruction to the queen, dynastic child, royal tombs, and historical memory.
- Added custody and forced re-education as alternatives to publicly executing a politically dangerous child.
