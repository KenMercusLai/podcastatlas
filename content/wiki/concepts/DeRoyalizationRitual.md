# De-Royalization Ritual

## Definition

De-royalization ritual is the coordinated use of names, clothing, posture, space, procedure, bodily handling, and burial to remove a monarch's sacred or exceptional status in public.

## Current Synthesis

The trial and execution of [[LouisXVI|Louis XVI]] worked through repeated reversals of monarchy rather than one declaration alone. “Louis Capet,” seated deputies, the private citizen's clothing and chair, ordinary binding and haircut, the guillotine, the renamed square, and an unmarked criminal burial asserted that republican law could reduce a king to a citizen even while the exceptional military spectacle revealed how politically extraordinary that reduction remained.

## Key Claims

- Political status can be dismantled through repeated embodied signs before formal punishment is complete.
- Naming can replace dynastic self-understanding with the category imposed by a new regime.
- Ordinary criminal procedure can communicate equality while also functioning as ideological theatre.
- Reused royal spaces make regime change visible by reversing their former meaning.
- Denial of exceptional burial attempts to prevent the body from sustaining dynastic memory.
- The scale of security and spectacle can expose the exceptional power that official normality tries to deny.

## Evidence

### Courtroom reversal

- [[547-the-french-revolution-the-execution-of-the-king-part-4-glt2184509954]] groups the Capet name, civilian dress, seated and covered deputies, humble chair, and permission to sit as reversals of court hierarchy.

### Execution as ordinary and extraordinary

- [[547-the-french-revolution-the-execution-of-the-king-part-4-glt2184509954]] joins common-criminal restraint, haircut, and guillotine procedure to a controlled route, artillery, roughly 80,000 armed men, drums, and public display.

### Burial and memory

- [[547-the-french-revolution-the-execution-of-the-king-part-4-glt2184509954]] describes burial away from the royal dead, in quicklime and without a marker.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The concept describes the episode's interpretation of coordinated symbolism; it does not prove that every participant shared one plan or meaning.
- Treating Louis as ordinary did not make the event socially ordinary or prevent royalist sacralization.
- Crowd, security, and ritual details remain source-scoped where the episode does not supply full documentary comparison.

## What Changed

- A canonical concept now distinguishes the destruction of royal status from the later physical execution itself.
