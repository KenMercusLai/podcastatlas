# Bribery–Institutional Influence Boundary

## Definition

The Bribery–Institutional Influence Boundary separates an unauthorized private exchange for official action from institutionally accepted practices that let wealth shape access, attention, or selection.

## Current Synthesis

The Georgetown case makes the boundary morally unstable without making it legally meaningless. A direct cash payment to a coach can differ from donor cultivation, a paid camp, or wealth-aware recruitment in recipient, authorization, disclosure, timing, and institutional control. Yet when a university operationally tracks wealth and encourages relationships with affluent recruits, its claim that money and admission occupy wholly separate worlds becomes difficult to sustain.

## Key Claims

- Directness alone does not define bribery; intent, exchange, authority, authorization, disclosure, and benefit recipient also matter.
- Institutionally approved fundraising can give wealth practical admissions relevance without authorizing a private payment.
- Accepted routes such as camps, donations, and development cultivation may reproduce influence while changing its legal form and institutional beneficiary.
- Evidence of institutional hypocrisy can weaken moral contrast without disproving the elements of a criminal charge.
- A legally meaningful distinction can remain ethically contestable when both sides convert wealth into access or preference.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode does not show that Georgetown authorized the payment, that development staff caused Catherine's admission, or that donor cultivation and bribery satisfy the same legal elements. Katz's hypothetical is analytical rather than a judicial ruling, and the structured summary does not supply statutes, jury instructions, institutional policies, or the complete record.
