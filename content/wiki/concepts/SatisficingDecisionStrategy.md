# Satisficing Decision Strategy

## Definition
Satisficing is a decision strategy that selects an option once it meets meaningful requirements, rather than continuing to search for proof that it is the best among every possible alternative.

## Current Synthesis
The episode contrasts satisficing with maximizing. Maximizing keeps the comparison set open and makes unseen alternatives psychologically relevant; satisficing defines standards in advance and permits search to stop when an option clears them. In the wedding-dress example, love for the dress and fit with the budget formed sufficient criteria, while continued shopping threatened choice overload without making global optimality knowable.

Satisficing is not carelessness or settling for an unacceptable option. Its discipline lies in choosing the requirements that matter, screening for safety and irreversible downside, and accepting that further comparison has a cost. It is most useful when options are abundant, differences are hard to verify, and delay or continued attention can itself reduce well-being.

## Key Claims
- Maximizing seeks the best possible option, while satisficing seeks an option that meets defined standards.
- Continued search can increase comparison, uncertainty, and regret without establishing that a global optimum has been found.
- A stopping rule turns “good enough” into a deliberate threshold rather than an excuse for indifference.
- Search costs include time, attention, delayed commitment, and choice overload as well as money.
- Satisficing still requires standards proportionate to the decision's stakes and reversibility.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source provides an accessible personal example rather than a general decision trial. Satisficing can fail when standards are poorly chosen, information is dangerously incomplete, a decision has severe irreversible consequences, or powerful actors define “good enough” for someone else. Maximizing effort may be appropriate for high-stakes safety, legal, medical, fiduciary, or engineering decisions; the useful question is whether additional search has expected value, not whether careful comparison is inherently harmful.
