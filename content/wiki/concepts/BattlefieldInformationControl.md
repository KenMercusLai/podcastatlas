## Definition

Battlefield information control is the deliberate shaping of what opponents can observe, infer, and verify before or during military action. It includes concealment, staged visibility, controlled reconnaissance, feigned movement, rumor, command secrecy, and signals designed to make an opponent commit to the wrong model of the battlefield.

## Current Synthesis

Across the bounded source set, information control is not a decorative trick added to combat. It changes the enemy's allocation of troops, timing, route, morale, or willingness to engage. Zhao She feeds a spy an image of delay before force-marching; Bai Qi's identity stays hidden while Zhao's commander change is public; Xiang Yu preserves the appearance of a Qi campaign while returning secretly to Pengcheng; Han Xin repeatedly pairs visible crossing, retreat, flags, or weakness with a hidden route, cavalry unit, or engineered terrain.

The strongest cases combine three layers. First, the commander verifies the opponent's decision field through spies, route reading, or local intelligence. Second, a plausible visible signal fits the opponent's expectations. Third, concealed force or timing converts that mistaken interpretation into operational loss. Hanji 1078 adds a documentary-seasonal version: Cao Cao's notice about delaying the Wuhuan campaign until autumn or winter works because it accompanies a visible withdrawal and a route the opponent considers unusable, while Tian Chou's local knowledge enables the concealed movement through Lulong Road.

The concept also has narrower and adjacent forms. Xun Yu's composure can deter an uncertain army without a trap; Cao Cao's yellow-horse statement is immediate survival deception; Dong Zhuo's recycled troop entries manufacture political-military momentum around a capital. These cases share perception control but differ in scale, preparation, and objective.

## Key Claims

- Information control is operational when it changes enemy deployment, pursuit, route defense, morale, or political alignment rather than merely spreading falsehood.
- Effective deception usually fits an expectation the opponent already holds about weakness, season, terrain, authority, commander identity, or the obvious route.
- Hidden capability must complete the signal: ambush troops, a different crossing, a seized camp, an upstream dam, a secret commander, or a reopened road turns misreading into consequence.
- Reconnaissance and counter-reconnaissance are part of the same system; verified knowledge about enemy choices makes risky visible tactics usable.
- The category spans prepared campaign deception, deterrent demeanor, political force theater, and crisis misdirection, but those forms should not be treated as identical.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

These popular-history episodes often infer motive and tactical design from compressed narrative, and some preserve dramatic details that are not independently sourced. Hanji 975's informant and street rhyme, Hanji 177's fully designed Baideng trap, and Hanji 1078's account of Wuhuan documentary psychology should remain source-scoped.

Not every misleading appearance proves a planned information operation. Composure can deter without deception, a retreat can be genuine, and an opponent's error can arise from poor reconnaissance rather than the commander's deliberate signal. The “2018 U.S. military manual” and “47%” statistic in Hanji 1078 are unidentified and do not establish the episode's modern cognitive-warfare claims.
