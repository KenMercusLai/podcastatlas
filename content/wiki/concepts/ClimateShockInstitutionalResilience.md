# Climate Shock Institutional Resilience

## Definition

Climate shock institutional resilience is the capacity of political and social systems to maintain or adapt food, energy, health, infrastructure, and legitimate decision-making when environmental pressures intensify.

## Current Synthesis

[[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] treats environmental danger as a systems test. Climate shocks become more destructive when food supply, energy access, disease preparedness, insurance, supply chains, and political trust are already weak or unequally distributed.

Resilience is therefore neither denial of climate risk nor faith in one technology. It requires identifying transmission pathways, strengthening foundational systems, and recognizing that crisis responses can redistribute power upward as well as protect exposed populations.

## Key Claims

- Political structure helps explain why similar climate pressures produce different social outcomes.
- Food, energy, and resistance to disease environments are foundational resilience priorities.
- Supply shocks, inflation, and cost-of-living pressure can convert environmental stress into political crisis.
- Adaptation capacity is unequally distributed within and across societies.
- Crisis can be used to centralize power, so resilience must include legitimacy and distribution as well as continuity.
- Targeted technical interventions matter most when institutions can coordinate, measure, and scale them.

## Evidence

- Historical variation: [[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] contrasts the varied seventeenth-century outcomes associated with the Little Ice Age.
- Power redistribution: [[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] uses [[NaramSin]] to show crisis-linked centralization rather than uniform collapse.
- Present fragility: [[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] connects European energy dependence, supply disruption, inflation, biodiversity loss, sea-level rise, and insurance risk.
- Priority systems: [[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] closes by emphasizing food supply, energy, and preparedness for disease environments.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Resilience can conceal unequal sacrifice if system continuity is treated as the only goal. The episode identifies broad priorities but does not specify institutional designs, costs, or the political conflicts involved in implementing them.

- Initial concept created from the episode's comparison of environmental pressure, governance, and foundational systems.
