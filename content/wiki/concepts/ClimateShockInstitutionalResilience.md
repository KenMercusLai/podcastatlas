# Climate Shock Institutional Resilience

## Definition

Climate shock institutional resilience is the capacity of political and social systems to maintain or adapt food, energy, health, infrastructure, and legitimate decision-making when environmental pressures intensify.

## Current Synthesis

[[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] treats environmental danger as a systems test. Climate shocks become more destructive when food supply, energy access, disease preparedness, insurance, supply chains, and political trust are already weak or unequally distributed. [[89-climate-weather-glt6521438447]] supplies historical mechanisms: harvest dependence magnifies famine and unrest, broken transport intensifies scarcity, campaign supply failures turn cold into military defeat, and severe weather makes an already fragile political crisis visible.

Resilience is therefore neither denial of climate risk nor faith in one technology. It requires identifying transmission pathways, strengthening foundational systems, and recognizing that crisis responses can redistribute power upward as well as protect exposed populations. It also includes anticipatory knowledge: the [[JamesStagg]] case shows forecasting becoming useful only when experts can communicate uncertainty and decision-makers can change operational timing.

## Key Claims

- Political structure helps explain why similar climate pressures produce different social outcomes.
- Food, energy, and resistance to disease environments are foundational resilience priorities.
- Supply shocks, inflation, and cost-of-living pressure can convert environmental stress into political crisis.
- Adaptation capacity is unequally distributed within and across societies.
- Crisis can be used to centralize power, so resilience must include legitimacy and distribution as well as continuity.
- Targeted technical interventions matter most when institutions can coordinate, measure, and scale them.
- Forecasts reduce exposure only when institutions can interpret them, communicate them, and act within the available window.

## Evidence

- Historical variation: [[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] contrasts the varied seventeenth-century outcomes associated with the Little Ice Age.
- Power redistribution: [[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] uses [[NaramSin]] to show crisis-linked centralization rather than uniform collapse.
- Present fragility: [[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] connects European energy dependence, supply disruption, inflation, biodiversity loss, sea-level rise, and insurance risk.
- Priority systems: [[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] closes by emphasizing food supply, energy, and preparedness for disease environments.
- Historical transmission pathways: [[89-climate-weather-glt6521438447]] links environmental shocks to harvests, bread prices, transport, campaign supply, battlefield movement, strikes, and political image.
- Anticipatory capacity: [[89-climate-weather-glt6521438447]] presents [[JamesStagg]]'s D-Day forecast as specialist advice translated into a command decision.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Resilience can conceal unequal sacrifice if system continuity is treated as the only goal. The sources identify broad priorities and historical mechanisms but do not specify institutional designs, costs, or the political conflicts involved in implementing them. Earlier societies were not uniformly helpless, modern societies are not insulated from cascading failure, and the D-Day counterfactual cannot prove what would have happened under a longer delay.

## What Changed

- Added harvest, transport, campaign supply, battlefield mobility, and political legitimacy as historical transmission pathways.
- Added forecasting, expert communication, and command timing as anticipatory resilience capacities.
