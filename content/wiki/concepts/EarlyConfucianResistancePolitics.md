## Definition

Early Confucian Resistance Politics / 早期儒家的反抗政治 is the wiki's concept for early Confucian language that gives ministers and people moral standing against rulers, including remonstrance, dignity, people-first hierarchy, and the claim that tyrants can lose legitimate ruler status.

## Current Synthesis

Early Confucian resistance politics is a corrective to obedience-only imperial stereotypes. In the current evidence, [[Mengzi|孟子 / Mencius]] and Han-court debate material make early Confucianism a hard-edged political language: ministers are not slaves, the people matter more than the ruler, and a ruler who destroys renyi can lose the name of ruler.

The Western Han court-risk version makes the sharpness especially visible. Confucian "法先王" judges present rulers against ancient standards, while a more ruler-protective "法今王" posture protects whoever currently holds power. [[YuanGusheng|袁固生]]'s debate with [[HuangShengWesternHan|黄生]] before [[HanJingdi|汉景帝]] shows why anti-tyrant logic is politically dangerous: defending [[ShangTang|商汤]] and [[ZhouWuwang|周武王]] as legitimate replacers of tyrants can justify [[LiuBang|刘邦]]'s replacement of Qin, but it can also imply that later rulers may be replaced.

## Key Claims

- Early Confucian hierarchy is not identical with unconditional submission.
- Remonstrance depends on moral dignity and ancient standards, not merely on courtly permission.
- "暴君非君" and Tang-Wu revolution logic turn moral failure into a legitimacy failure.
- People-based mandate language can legitimate resistance but becomes dangerous inside an established monarchy.
- The concept challenges the idea that Chinese political vocabulary contains only ruler obedience and imported modern liberty.

## Evidence

Mencian resistance and dignity:
- [[30-mengzi-zhanguo-fenqing-de-chizi-zhixin-592494138]] uses "民为贵，社稷次之，君为轻," "君视臣如土芥，则臣视君如寇仇," and "闻诛一夫纣矣，未闻弑君也" to show that early Confucianism can deny legitimacy to an unrighteous ruler.

Han-court danger of anti-tyrant logic:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-392-3-daodi-zenme-zuo-caishi-yige-hao-diwang-lgrcpyv1mf14axsq6hnqxaymrpu0|Hanji 392-3]] has Yuan Gusheng defend Tang's overthrow of Jie and then ask whether Liu Bang's replacement of Qin would be illegitimate if ministerial overthrow were always forbidden.

Early Confucianism versus official domestication:
- [[30-mengzi-zhanguo-fenqing-de-chizi-zhixin-592494138]] explicitly separates Mencius from later obedience-centered imperial Confucianism.
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-392-3-daodi-zenme-zuo-caishi-yige-hao-diwang-lgrcpyv1mf14axsq6hnqxaymrpu0|Hanji 392-3]] uses the same distinction as the setup for Han Wudi's later "独尊儒术" problem.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not claim all Confucian practice was resistant or anti-monarchical. Both sources are interpretive podcast treatments emphasizing the sharp side of early Confucianism, and Hanji 392-3 specifically shows how court politics can restrict that sharpness once dynastic stability is at stake.
