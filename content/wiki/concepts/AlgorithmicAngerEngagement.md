# Algorithmic Anger Engagement

## Definition
Algorithmic anger engagement is the platform feedback pattern in which conflict, moral emotion, correction, and out-group attack generate interaction signals that increase visibility and train users and creators toward more antagonistic content.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources connect three levels. The creator conversation shows why familiar books and emotional controversy lower the cost of commenting and can outperform unfamiliar, slow material. The algorithm episode explains that even an angry rejection can become useful engagement data: comments, dwell time, and repeated interaction may train a feed toward more of what the user believes they are resisting. The Hidden Brain episode adds group direction, reporting that moral-emotional political language spreads more readily within groups and attacks on an out-group receive an especially strong sharing advantage.

The Marketplace Tech case adds an ethical asymmetry: users may condemn an influencer's exploitation of a vulnerable community, yet their comments, reaction videos, or corrective participation can still increase the original post's measurable activity and reach. The result is not a claim that platforms invent anger or that every ranking system explicitly optimizes hostility. Platforms can amplify preexisting conflict because outrage is legible as activity, creators observe what travels, and users receive social reward for identity-protective attack. Long-form context, reporting, blocking, deliberate non-engagement with rage bait, diverse feeds, and platform-level ranking choices may weaken the loop, but the supplied sources do not compare those interventions directly.

## Key Claims
- Familiar topics and clear conflict reduce the interpretive cost of reacting.
- Angry correction can function as positive engagement data even when the user experiences it as rejection.
- Visible performance feedback can move creators toward controversy and away from quiet or unfamiliar material.
- Moral-emotional language can spread through identity-aligned networks more readily than neutral phrasing.
- Out-group attacks may receive stronger sharing reward than in-group praise or general moral language.
- Corrective or condemnatory engagement can widen distribution of content the user is trying to oppose.
- Platform amplification interacts with prior identity and conflict rather than creating them from nothing; slower formats and deliberate feed practices may preserve context without removing the incentive.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not provide platform code, ranking weights, causal audits, effect heterogeneity, or evidence that all angry content receives amplification. The Hidden Brain percentages are episode-reported study summaries rather than current universal platform constants. The Marketplace Tech source does not measure whether correction's informational benefit outweighs its distribution effect. Users self-select, creators have independent motives, and offline institutions and political leaders also generate conflict. Long-form media can carry outrage, and unfamiliar or constructive material can still spread. The concept identifies an incentive loop, not a complete explanation of polarization.

## What Changed
- Added the critical-engagement paradox: condemnation and correction may still increase harmful content's measurable distribution signals.
- Added reporting, blocking, and non-engagement as distinct user responses while preserving uncertainty about their comparative effects.
