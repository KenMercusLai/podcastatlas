# Meritocratic Arrogance / 绩优主义傲慢

## Definition

Meritocratic arrogance is the conversion of measured achievement, credentials, or selected status into a belief in superior human worth and moral deservingness.

## Current Synthesis

The intelligence-testing episode grounds the concept in everyday status behavior: school labels, elite credentials, vocabulary, and high-IQ identity can turn ability into contempt. Its “check your privilege” response does not deny differences in effort or capacity; it asks advantaged people to convert those differences into responsibility and [[RationalHumility]].

The exams episode adds the institutional critique associated with [[MichaelYoung|Michael Young]]. A selection system can widen opportunity yet still teach winners that rank proves virtue. When preparation, family advantage, regional access, scarce positions, and institutional design disappear from view, achievement becomes a self-justifying dignity hierarchy.

## Key Claims

- Achievement can become a status weapon even when it began as study, discipline, or talent.
- Intellectual advantage should increase responsibility to explain, listen, and remain corrigible, not license contempt.
- "智力种姓" is dangerous because it makes a measured or inferred ability difference look like a stable human rank.
- The repair path is not pretending differences do not exist; it is refusing to convert difference into dignity hierarchy.
- Fairer selection does not guarantee humble winners or prevent elite self-reproduction.

## Evidence

- Score and credential status: [[37-zhishang-ceshi-qingwen-ni-shi-zhili-poluomen-ma-608020679]] connects intelligence labels, school sorting, elite education, jargon, and “智力婆罗门” identity to contempt.
- Meritocracy warning: [[84-exams-glt6507641896]] uses Michael Young to show how educated winners may interpret institutional success as moral desert.
- Structural qualification: [[84-exams-glt6507641896]] places winner confidence beside social closure, unequal access, and scarce elite roles.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Neither source treats achievement, expertise, or selection as unreal. The concept criticizes the moral inference from performance to superior dignity, not every use of tests or credentials. The sources do not establish how common this posture is across populations or which institutional designs reduce it.

## What Changed

- Added Michael Young's institutional warning that meritocratic selection can generate self-justifying winners.
- Distinguished opportunity expansion from the moral legitimacy people infer from rank.
