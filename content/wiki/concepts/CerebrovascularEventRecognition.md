# Cerebrovascular Event Recognition

## Definition
Cerebrovascular event recognition distinguishes blocked-vessel stroke, brain hemorrhage, transient ischemic attack, aneurysm-related bleeding, and less common vascular injury while treating sudden focal neurological symptoms as urgent even when the mechanism is unknown or symptoms improve.

## Current Synthesis
The source set organizes stroke around interrupted brain blood flow rather than one disease mechanism. Ischemic blockage and hemorrhage require different medical management, and risk context varies with blood pressure, smoking, lipids, stimulants, vessel fragility, anatomy, clotting treatment, and blood-thinning treatment. Transient improvement is not reassurance: modern imaging can reveal small infarcts in events once classified only by symptom duration.

For the public, recognition should precede mechanism guessing. Sudden facial asymmetry, arm weakness, speech change, visual change, imbalance, walking difficulty, cognitive change, severe headache, vomiting, or altered consciousness can require urgent assessment. FAST supplies a memorable subset—face, arm, speech, time—but does not exclude atypical stroke or tell a bystander whether bleeding or blockage is present. Imaging and specialist assessment make that distinction and determine whether thrombolysis, thrombectomy, hemorrhage management, aneurysm treatment, or another pathway applies.

## Key Claims
- Stroke can result from vascular blockage or hemorrhage, and the two mechanisms require different management.
- FAST-style facial, arm, and speech checks help the public recognize a possible stroke and act quickly.
- Sudden visual, balance, gait, cognitive, headache, vomiting, or consciousness changes can also be important and are not excluded by FAST.
- Ordinary people should not delay care while trying to distinguish ischemic stroke from brain hemorrhage.
- Symptom resolution does not make a suspected TIA harmless or remove the need for assessment.
- MRI has complicated the old time-based TIA distinction by revealing small infarcts in some transient episodes.
- Catheter-based clot removal and aneurysm treatment illustrate the increasingly less-invasive acute-care toolkit for selected patients.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
These sources provide public education, not a complete emergency protocol. FAST is useful but not exhaustive, symptoms can have mimics, and neither symptom pattern nor a podcast can determine clot versus hemorrhage. Eligibility windows, imaging pathways, thrombolysis, thrombectomy, blood-pressure management, and medication decisions require emergency clinical assessment; self-treatment with clotting or blood-thinning medication can be dangerous.

## What Changed
- Added FAST-style public recognition and made the “recognize first, differentiate clinically” boundary explicit.
- Added hemorrhage-associated headache, vomiting, consciousness, and rapid-deficit qualifications without treating them as a home diagnostic rule.
- Connected recognition more directly to time-sensitive imaging and condition-specific intervention.
