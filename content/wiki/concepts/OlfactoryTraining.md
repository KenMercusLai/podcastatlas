# Olfactory Training

## Definition
Olfactory training is the practice of repeatedly and attentively smelling safe, non-noxious odors to tune smell perception and support smell recovery or refinement.

## Current Synthesis
Across the two episodes, smell is treated as trainable because olfactory neurons turn over across life and smell-related tuning is activity-dependent. The recommended practice is deliberately simple: use safe odors such as lemon, rose, chocolate, coffee, or an orange; sniff repeatedly; inhale longer; close the eyes when possible; and direct attention toward the odor.

The newer Essentials episode adds anatomy and injury context. Olfactory fibers can be damaged where they pass through the cribriform plate after head impact, and smell recovery may provide one incomplete signal of broader recovery alongside balance, cognition, sleep, and clinical assessment. Training is therefore a plausible adjunct after some smell loss, not a substitute for evaluating illness or traumatic brain injury.

## Key Claims
- Smell ability is presented as improvable through repeated exposure to non-noxious odors.
- Attention is part of the training; the person should focus on the odor rather than sniff passively.
- Longer inhales and closing the eyes are framed as ways to increase sensory attention.
- Olfactory neurons turning over across life makes smell a plausible target for activity-dependent tuning.
- The practice may be especially relevant after respiratory illness or some smell-related injury when started early.
- After head injury, smell recovery may be informative but cannot independently establish neurological recovery.

## Evidence
- Training materials - [[your-top-health-questions-answered-scim2882548864]] recommends odors such as lemon, rose, chocolate, coffee, or similar safe smells.
- Practice method - [[your-top-health-questions-answered-scim2882548864]] recommends repeated sniffing, longer inhalation, eye closure when possible, and focused attention.
- Biological rationale - [[your-top-health-questions-answered-scim2882548864]] says olfactory neurons turn over across life and smell tuning is activity-dependent.
- Recovery context - [[your-top-health-questions-answered-scim2882548864]] says similar approaches may help after respiratory illness or some smell-related injury, especially when started early.
- Rapid perceptual practice - [[essentials-how-smell-taste-pheromones-shape-behavior-scim3853502826]] uses repeated attentive smelling of an orange to illustrate increased perceptual detail.
- Traumatic-injury context - [[essentials-how-smell-taste-pheromones-shape-behavior-scim3853502826]] links smell loss to shearing of olfactory fibers at the cribriform plate and describes olfactory training as promising rather than definitive.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Neither source makes olfactory training a universal cure for anosmia, neurological disease, toxic exposure, chronic sinus problems, or traumatic brain injury. Loss of smell after illness or injury can require medical evaluation, and the examples remain practical guidance rather than a complete treatment protocol. Exercise, blood flow, social interaction, and odor exposure are mentioned as possible supports for olfactory neurogenesis, but the condensed source does not establish their comparative effects.
