## Definition

Consumer food knowledge is the embodied and relational competence used to identify ingredients, judge condition and season, choose quantities, match food to cooking methods and taste memories, and question sellers about origin or use.

## Current Synthesis

The bounded source treats consumer knowledge as part of food-system capacity. Non-standard ingredients have little retail value unless buyers can recognize, prepare, and desire them. That competence grows through repeated market visits, sensory comparison, cooking, conversation with vendors and other shoppers, and memories of local taste.

Knowledge is unequally available. Long work hours, reliance on prepared food, migration away from production, and the convenience of preselected digital catalogs can reduce opportunities to learn. Yet market knowledge should not be romanticized: familiarity can coexist with misinformation, and buyers without insider relationships may face uncertainty or unequal treatment.

## Key Claims

- Ingredient diversity becomes usable demand only when consumers can identify and cook what is offered.
- Food judgment combines sight, touch, smell, timing, origin, variety, seller history, and intended preparation.
- Repeated interaction can transfer tacit knowledge that books or product listings do not fully convey.
- Time scarcity and distance from farming or fishing can interrupt intergenerational knowledge transmission.
- Consumer knowledge improves agency but does not replace food-safety controls, transparent labeling, or fair dealing.

## Evidence

- Experienced judgment: [[486d1fea-dd57-4ff1-8de9-cfea1f9de16a-486d1fea-dd57-4ff1-8de9-cfea1f9de16a]] describes Hainan shoppers judging pork through breed, feeding, fat, age, slaughter timing, seller, and cooking effect.
- Market learning: [[486d1fea-dd57-4ff1-8de9-cfea1f9de16a-486d1fea-dd57-4ff1-8de9-cfea1f9de16a]] recommends repeated visits, sensory attention, explicit quantity requests, and conversation rather than passive acceptance.
- Knowledge interruption: [[486d1fea-dd57-4ff1-8de9-cfea1f9de16a-486d1fea-dd57-4ff1-8de9-cfea1f9de16a]] connects younger consumers' work pace, migration, reduced cooking, and online ordering to weaker knowledge of season, origin, and production.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode does not measure knowledge by age, channel, city, class, gender, or cooking frequency. Younger consumers can acquire food expertise, older consumers can hold inaccurate beliefs, and digital tools can also teach seasonality, sourcing, or preparation. Sensory skill cannot detect every contaminant, adulterant, or cold-chain failure and should complement rather than substitute for institutional safeguards.

## What Changed

- Established food knowledge as a demand-side capability that helps sustain ingredient diversity.
- Preserved the distinction between practical agency and formal safety assurance.
