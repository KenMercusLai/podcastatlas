# 萧规曹随式制度连续 / Xiao-Gui-Cao-Sui Institutional Continuity

## Definition

萧规曹随式制度连续 / Xiao-Gui-Cao-Sui institutional continuity is the decision by a successor to preserve a capable predecessor's established rules because avoiding unnecessary error and disruption has greater value than displaying authorship through immediate reform.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-196-jiexi-xiaogui-caosui-da-zhihui-lic6c_-juuvxxrpdwglnlqlgi_8o|Hanji 196]] makes the pattern explicit through [[CaoShen|曹参]] after [[XiaoHe|萧何]]. Cao Shen's argument to [[LiuYing|汉惠帝刘盈]] is comparative and situational: because the founders were more capable and already created a serviceable order, their successors should preserve it carefully instead of assuming that succession itself proves the need for redesign.

This is not simple copyism. The successor still selects personnel, sets the enforcement temperature, distinguishes minor error from serious failure, and decides that recovery is the current objective. Continuity is therefore an active judgment about institutional quality and timing, with failure modes on both sides: restless reform can destroy stability, while excessive deference can preserve defects or miss changed conditions.

## Key Claims

- Leadership succession does not by itself justify policy change.
- Preserving an adequate system can be a positive governing achievement when society needs recovery and predictability.
- The successor remains responsible for personnel, implementation style, and the boundary between minor error and harmful neglect.
- Humility about comparative capacity can improve institutional judgment when it is grounded in evidence rather than ritual self-abasement.
- Visible busyness is not a reliable measure of administrative value.
- The pattern fails when inherited institutions are corrupt, conditions have materially changed, or tolerated errors impose serious public costs.

## Evidence

Succession judgment:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-196-jiexi-xiaogui-caosui-da-zhihui-lic6c_-juuvxxrpdwglnlqlgi_8o|Hanji 196]] has Cao Shen compare Liu Ying with Liu Bang and himself with Xiao He, then argue for careful adherence to the founders' settled rules.

Implementation through people and enforcement:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-196-jiexi-xiaogui-caosui-da-zhihui-lic6c_-juuvxxrpdwglnlqlgi_8o|Hanji 196]] says Cao Shen appoints cautious, substantial officials, rejects fame-seeking fault-finders, and declines to pursue minor errors aggressively.

Outcome frame:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-196-jiexi-xiaogui-caosui-da-zhihui-lic6c_-juuvxxrpdwglnlqlgi_8o|Hanji 196]] reports popular praise after Cao Shen's three-year chancellorship and interprets social stability as the relevant performance measure.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source argues the successful case and offers little direct evidence about the costs of Cao Shen's approach. Drinking, blocking remonstrance, and concealing errors can also be signs of negligent government; their reinterpretation as restraint depends on the episode's claimed context and outcomes. The concept should not be generalized into opposition to reform, protection of incumbents, or tolerance of misconduct. It requires a prior judgment that the inherited system is sound enough and the environment stable enough for continuity to outperform correction.

## What Changed

- Created the concept with explicit conditions separating prudent continuity from inertia or neglect.
