# Zoning Tax

## Definition
Zoning tax is [[EdGlaeser]]'s name for the portion of a home's market price above the physical and construction cost of producing it that is attributable to regulatory scarcity and constrained development rights.

## Current Synthesis
[[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-dde046734f50fc7c6a18ae9c19ada137-55355169-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-dde046734f50fc7c6a18ae9c19ada137-55355169]] uses the price-cost gap as a diagnostic rather than a literal government levy. When prices rise while housing production falls and buildable land remains available, zoning, permitting, parking, environmental review, minimum lots, lawsuits, and political opposition may prevent supply from responding.

The concept is cumulative. Removing one rule may do little if other rules or community vetoes remain binding, and legal upzoning may not create homes quickly when existing buildings, financing, litigation, and modest density gains leave redevelopment unattractive.

## Key Claims
- A large price-production-cost gap can signal that development permission is scarce.
- Housing constraints often arise from overlapping rules rather than one isolated prohibition.
- Rising prices combined with declining production are more diagnostic than prices alone.
- Nominal upzoning may produce little if the permitted density increase does not change project economics.
- Political incentives determine whether formal reform becomes actual construction.

- Implementation lag: [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-dde046734f50fc7c6a18ae9c19ada137-55355169-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-dde046734f50fc7c6a18ae9c19ada137-55355169]] reports limited ten-year buildout after upzoning and contrasts small permission changes with larger density shifts.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The price-cost gap can also reflect land value, location, finance, risk, infrastructure, taxes, labor, materials, market power, and expected future demand. The episode acknowledges that causal attribution to individual rules is statistically difficult. The concept does not establish that every regulation is inefficient or that development lacks infrastructure, environmental, displacement, or distributional costs.
