# Epigenetic Reprogramming Boundary

## Definition
Epigenetic reprogramming boundary is the source's explanation that many molecular marks on genetic material are erased in sperm, egg, or early embryos so development can restart from a relatively clean state.

## Current Synthesis
The episode presents epigenetic reprogramming as the second major barrier against routine inheritance of acquired states. Even if some molecular marks are present in germ cells, early development contains reset processes that remove many modifications before they can shape the next generation.

This boundary is especially important for interpreting public claims about inherited stress, memories, or parental experience. The Rechavi source does not say every epigenetic mark is erased or that mammals have no intergenerational effects; it requires an explanation of how a signal survives reprogramming and affects descendants. The Ilardo episode adds refugee-trauma and Dutch-famine examples as possible human intergenerational effects, but the summarized account does not establish that survival mechanism. In worms, small RNAs provide one clearer route; in mammals and humans, the mechanism remains much less settled.

## Key Claims
- Epigenetic marks are not equivalent to permanent inherited instructions.
- Many sperm, egg, and early-embryo modifications are erased before development proceeds.
- Reprogramming makes broad claims about inherited acquired experience harder to support.
- A credible exception must explain how the signal survives resetting and changes descendant development or behavior.
- Mammal and human claims remain less established than the C. elegans small-RNA cases.
- Human intergenerational associations remain suggestive until exposure pathways, shared environment, and germline transmission are distinguished.

## Evidence
- Reset mechanism - [[essentials-genes-inheritance-memories-across-generations-dr-oded-rechavi-scim1624813352]] describes epigenetic reprogramming as erasure of many chemical modifications so development can restart.
- Mammal estimate - [[essentials-genes-inheritance-memories-across-generations-dr-oded-rechavi-scim1624813352]] says that in mammals and humans most sperm and egg modifications are removed.
- Mechanism demand - [[essentials-genes-inheritance-memories-across-generations-dr-oded-rechavi-scim1624813352]] frames RNA as one candidate carrier precisely because ordinary marks face barrier and reset problems.
- Developmental route - [[essentials-genes-inheritance-memories-across-generations-dr-oded-rechavi-scim1624813352]] notes that inherited information in mammals may act early in development if it acts at all.
- Human examples - [[behaviors-that-alter-your-genes-to-improve-your-health-performance-dr-melissa-ilardo-scim2045300705]] cites refugee trauma and the Dutch famine as possible inherited epigenetic effects but does not specify how marks survive resetting.
- Timescale separation - [[behaviors-that-alter-your-genes-to-improve-your-health-performance-dr-melissa-ilardo-scim2045300705]] distinguishes rapid gene-expression response from the stronger claim of cross-generational inheritance.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Neither source provides a full current mammalian epigenetics review, and the "most modifications" estimate and human trauma/famine examples remain source-scoped public explanations. Intergenerational association can also reflect direct fetal exposure, parental physiology, behavior, or shared environment. The boundary should be read as a demand for mechanism and design, not as proof that all epigenetic inheritance is impossible.
