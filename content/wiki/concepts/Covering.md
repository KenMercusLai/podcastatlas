# Covering

## Definition
Covering is the behavioral downplaying of a known identity so that the person appears closer to a socially dominant or more comfortable norm. It differs from conversion, which attempts to change the identity, and passing, which prevents others from knowing it.

## Current Synthesis
[[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b9a08666432cea3e0a417d731ebdaf41-104957641-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b9a08666432cea3e0a417d731ebdaf41-104957641|Dropping the Mask]] presents covering as a widespread but non-uniform pressure affecting speech, appearance, family life, work, emotion, politics, disability, class, sexuality, race, and other dimensions of identity. Its harm arises when equal dignity or opportunity depends on performing a less disruptive version of oneself: the person may feel that acceptance attaches to an edited persona, and organizations may lose commitment from people who experience belonging as conditional.

Covering is not automatically wrong. Privacy, safety, civility, affection, cooperation, and empathy can justify some self-modulation, especially when expectations are reciprocal rather than selectively imposed on a less powerful group. The practical test is therefore contextual: identify the demand, assess harm, ask whether a legitimate competing value overrides that harm, and decide whether and how to challenge an unjustified demand.

Uncovering should expand agency rather than impose another performance. Stories, visible modeling, and allies can reduce stigma, while psychological safety requires making disclosure possible without declaring others inauthentic or requiring them to expose identities, trauma, health conditions, or vulnerability.

## Key Claims
- Conversion, passing, and covering distinguish changing, hiding, and behaviorally minimizing an identity.
- Covering demands can persist after formal inclusion because a person may be accepted only while muting identity-linked behavior or advocacy.
- Conditional acceptance can damage self-worth and belonging, and workplace expectations to cover can reduce organizational commitment.
- The framework is broadly applicable without making all identities, histories of subordination, or disclosure risks equivalent.
- Legitimate shared values can justify some covering, but selective conformity imposed for dominant-group comfort is suspect.
- Uncovering is safest when people retain control over whether, when, and how much they disclose.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- The cited surveys are summarized without instruments, sampling details, subgroup definitions, or full reports, so their percentages remain source-scoped.
- A single umbrella term can obscure differences in power, danger, and historical subordination; broad applicability is not equivalence.
- Observing self-modulation does not establish shame, coercion, or harm. The person's reasons and the surrounding demand matter.
- Political expression, workplace conduct, trauma, addiction, depression, and family disclosure carry distinct legal and safety considerations that the episode does not resolve comprehensively.

## What Changed
- Established a three-part distinction among conversion, passing, and covering.
- Added a harm-and-justification test that prevents authenticity from operating as an absolute rule.
- Connected voluntary disclosure and ally behavior to psychological safety rather than compulsory exposure.
