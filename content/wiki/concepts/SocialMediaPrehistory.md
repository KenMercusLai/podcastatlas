## Definition
Social media prehistory is the long development of multi-user messaging, profiles, status signals, games, pseudonyms, and social interaction before those mechanics were assembled into mass-market web platforms.

## Current Synthesis
The episode treats social media as diffusion and recombination rather than abrupt invention. Research systems, bulletin boards, and commercial online services demonstrated recurring social mechanics decades before Friendster, Myspace, and [[Facebook]] reached large consumer populations. Broadband, internet scale, interface design, and identity policy changed reach and social meaning; [[MarkZuckerberg]]'s real-name rule is one influential integration choice, not a complete explanation of social media's success or a universally beneficial norm.

## Key Claims
- Core social-media mechanics predate the modern web-platform category by decades.
- Mass adoption can make an old interaction pattern appear newly invented.
- Network scale and broadband changed the frequency, richness, and visibility of online social behavior.
- Identity design shapes whether online spaces feel separate from or continuous with offline life.
- Real-name systems can increase continuity and accountability while creating privacy, safety, and exclusion costs.

## Evidence
- Long technical lineage: [[93-silicon-valley-part-1-glt5734449332]] traces multi-user messaging, games, and status-like features from early research computing through bulletin boards and commercial online services.
- Mass-market sequence: [[93-silicon-valley-part-1-glt5734449332]] places Friendster, Myspace, and [[Facebook]] inside a mid-2000s consumer-internet and broadband inflection.
- Identity policy: [[93-silicon-valley-part-1-glt5734449332]] presents [[MarkZuckerberg]]'s real-name insistence as a break from handles and pseudonyms.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not provide a complete technical chronology or distinguish invention, independent reinvention, product integration, and mass diffusion with precision. The real-name claim is Andreessen's interpretation and underweights prior identity systems, platform-specific adoption factors, pseudonymity's protective value, and the risks created when legal identity, social graphs, and commercial surveillance converge.

## What Changed
- Created a long-duration account of social-media mechanics and mass diffusion.
- Qualified real-name identity as a consequential design choice rather than a singular invention or universal improvement.
