# Age-Adjusted Dementia Decline

Age-adjusted dementia decline is [[working-memory-the-surprising-decline-of-dementia-6a6b0eeca585d3be78cd9d1b]]'s distinction between total dementia burden and same-age risk. The episode says many people assume dementia is rising because age is the biggest risk factor and populations are ageing, but researchers found that older people in rich countries are less likely to have dementia than people of the same age several decades earlier.

The source says the decline across six North American and European countries was about 13% per decade between 1988 and 2015. It also says that people in their late 80s were once described as having roughly three-in-ten dementia prevalence, while the comparable rate is now closer to one-in-ten.

The concept changes the policy question. Ageing populations still create more absolute need for diagnosis, caregiving, and planning, but the cohort decline suggests prevention and environment matter enough to shift risk.

## Key Claims
- Total cases can rise while age-adjusted risk falls.
- Dementia risk rises steeply with age, so demographic ageing can hide prevention gains.
- The mechanism behind the decline remains uncertain in the source.
- The source treats the decline as strongest in richer countries, with poorer countries possibly bearing more future burden.
- The decline makes [[ModifiableDementiaRiskFactors]] more plausible as practical public-health targets.
