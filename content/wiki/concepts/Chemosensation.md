# Chemosensation

## Definition
Chemosensation is the detection of chemical cues through systems such as smell, taste, and other organism-specific receptors in air, water, food, bodies, or social environments.

## Current Synthesis
The current evidence spans human sensory neuroscience and ocean humanities. In humans, airborne odorants are trapped in nasal mucus and detected by olfactory neurons, while chemicals in the mouth activate gustatory pathways that help sample possible nutrients and hazards. Human-produced chemicals may also influence other people even though specific human pheromones and their mechanisms remain unsettled.

Underwater chemosensation widens the frame beyond noses and tongues. Abalone, brittle stars, microbes, fish, and kelp-forest ecologies encounter waterborne gradients through organism-specific and sometimes whole-body sensing. Chemical pollution and acidification can therefore damage not only bodies but also the sensory conditions for navigation, memory, anticipation, and survival.

## Key Claims
- Chemosensation includes human smell and taste but is not limited to those familiar sensory organs.
- Human olfaction combines innate responses, learned odor associations, and disputed accessory-pathway claims.
- Gustation samples chemicals at the digestive entrance and can bias approach or avoidance.
- Human chemical influence can exist without proof of a canonical pheromone system.
- Underwater sensing can be whole-body, spatial, and organized by chemical gradients.
- Changing environmental chemistry can disrupt how organisms sense and respond to their worlds.

- Multispecies sensing - [[melody-jue-ocean-memory-wxagwid1oeg]] uses abalone, brittle stars, microbes, fish, and kelp forests to show that chemical perception exceeds human sensory anatomy.
- Environmental vulnerability - [[melody-jue-ocean-memory-wxagwid1oeg]] links pollution and acidification to disrupted sensing, memory, navigation, and future response.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Chemosensation is an umbrella, not one mechanism shared unchanged across species or media. Animal accessory-olfactory effects cannot be directly generalized to humans; broad adaptive labels for tastes do not determine whether a particular food is beneficial or dangerous; and ocean-humanities translations from smell to sound are interpretive practices rather than direct reproductions of nonhuman experience.

## What Changed
- Expanded the concept from underwater sensing to a cross-media synthesis spanning human olfaction, gustation, social chemical cues, and multispecies sensing.
