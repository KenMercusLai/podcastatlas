# Noise Exposure Layering

## Definition
Noise exposure layering is the combined hearing risk created when environmental noise, headphone output, high intensity, long duration, sudden peaks, and closely spaced exposures stack before the auditory system has recovered.

## Current Synthesis
The sources treat sound safety as a dose-and-sequence problem. In quiet conditions, a user may listen at moderate volume. In subway, street, machinery, motorcycle, or other noisy settings, the same user may raise headphone volume to overcome background noise, turning environmental sound and earphone output into a layered stimulus. A construction week followed by a loud concert adds a temporal layer: closely spaced insults may interact before recovery rather than behaving as wholly independent events.

The evidence offers two compatible but nonidentical heuristics. The 60-60-60 rule limits continuous headphone use, device-volume fraction, and ambient noise. The Stankovic interview instead gives an 80 dB for eight hours rule with allowable time halving for each 3 dB increase. Both emphasize that level and duration trade off; neither is a personal guarantee or substitute for calibrated occupational guidance. Protection should be strong enough for the actual environment, while constant overprotection in ordinary sound can worsen sound intolerance for some people.

## Key Claims
- Hearing exposure is shaped by the total acoustic field around the listener, not only by the headphone setting, and persistent background noise can become a long-term health context rather than only an annoyance.
- Noisy settings can make safer-looking headphones riskier because users raise volume when speech or audio becomes hard to hear.
- Semi-in-ear, open-ear, and bone-conduction devices can preserve ventilation or environmental awareness but may invite higher volume in loud surroundings.
- The episode's 60-60-60 rule combines continuous-use time, percentage of maximum volume, and environmental noise as a practical safety heuristic.
- Sudden loud peaks matter because short high-energy events can produce different risk from steady moderate listening.
- Allowable exposure time falls rapidly as decibel level rises; closely spaced loud events may create more than a simple additive burden.
- Protection should match damaging exposure, but habitual earplug use in normal environments can contribute to sound sensitivity in susceptible people.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not provide a complete occupational-noise standard, calibrated personal-dose method, earplug fit test, or building-acoustics guide. Phone measurements and labeled attenuation are estimates, actual protection depends on fit, and the 60-60-60 and 80 dB/3 dB rules answer different practical questions. Genetic sensitivity, age, medicines, prior exposure, and recovery time can change individual risk.
