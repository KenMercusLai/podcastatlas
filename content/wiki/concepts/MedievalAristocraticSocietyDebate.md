## Definition
The Medieval Aristocratic Society Debate asks whether medieval Chinese elite power rested substantially on autonomous lineage interests and social capacity or primarily on offices and ranks granted by emperors.

## Current Synthesis
The episode maps the debate onto Kyoto- and Tokyo-school emphases and then uses Western Wei–Northern Zhou to complicate the binary. Senior military lineages could possess resources, collective interests, and enough standing to threaten a successor such as Yuwen Hu, yet their formal position was actively created and rearranged through ruler-directed titles, armies, surnames, and offices. The regime therefore shows aristocratic agency and bureaucratic dependence operating together rather than supplying a simple verdict for either school.

## Key Claims
- One interpretation emphasizes elite interests, power, and initiative not wholly derived from the throne.
- The opposing interpretation emphasizes bureaucratic office and imperial rank as the basis of status.
- Western Wei high commanders gained collective leverage but were also constituted through state organization.
- Succession crises reveal autonomous power more clearly than stable rule under a dominant organizer.
- Zhouli office reform and bestowed surnames show that elite identity could be politically reconstructed.
- The debate concerns degrees and mechanisms of dependence, not only the label “aristocracy.”

## Evidence
- School contrast: [[ltxaost4pgqhjdirwas15vizersj-ltxaost4pgqhjdirwas15vizersj]] contrasts Kyoto emphasis on autonomous aristocratic power with Tokyo emphasis on bureaucratic grant.
- Constructed status: [[ltxaost4pgqhjdirwas15vizersj-ltxaost4pgqhjdirwas15vizersj]] describes bestowed surnames, formal ranks, and a redesigned army hierarchy.
- Elite leverage: [[ltxaost4pgqhjdirwas15vizersj-ltxaost4pgqhjdirwas15vizersj]] presents senior pillars as threats after Yuwen Tai's death.
- Bureaucratic innovation: [[ltxaost4pgqhjdirwas15vizersj-ltxaost4pgqhjdirwas15vizersj]] treats Zhouli offices as an active restructuring of administration and status.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Kyoto and Tokyo “schools” are broad labels that can flatten internal diversity. One regime and one episode cannot settle the social character of the whole medieval period, and military-command autonomy is not identical to hereditary aristocratic independence.

## What Changed
- Established Western Wei–Northern Zhou as a mixed case of elite leverage and state-constructed status.
