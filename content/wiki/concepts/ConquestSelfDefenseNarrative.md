# Conquest Self-Defense Narrative / 兼并自卫叙事

Conquest self-defense narrative / 兼并自卫叙事 is the legitimacy move [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-120-1-shui-shi-huangdi-zhong-de-mabaonan-lhw8vsj6ta6rjdkugcvfohaqqxc0]] identifies after [[QinState|秦国]] destroys the six eastern states. Once military unification is complete, [[QinShiHuang|嬴政 / 秦始皇]] needs a public story that makes conquest look like reluctant punishment rather than naked expansion.

In the episode's reading, Qin does this by narrating each target as the offender. [[HanState|韩国]] first submits and then breaks faith; [[ZhaoState|赵国]] tears up agreements and later shelters rebellion in [[DaiState|代国]]; [[WeiState|魏国]] feigns submission while joining Han and Zhao; [[ChuState|楚国]] violates covenant and attacks [[NanCommandery|南郡]]; [[YanState|燕国]] sends [[JingKe|荆轲]] to assassinate the Qin king; [[QiState|齐国]] trusts [[HouSheng|后胜]] and is accused of severing relations and plotting rebellion.

The concept extends [[MoralizedWarPretext|道德化战争借口]] from a prewar coordination tool into a postwar imperial explanation. It also overlaps with [[VanquishedRulerReputationConstruction|亡国君污名建构]] because the conquered rulers' remembered faults become part of why the new empire claims it had to exist.

## Key Claims
- A conqueror still needs legitimacy after victory, especially when it must govern former enemies.
- Self-defense language can recode aggressive expansion as reluctant response.
- The narrative works state by state: each conquered polity receives a specific charge that makes Qin's attack appear deserved.
- The same story can calm subjects, instruct officials, and shape later memory of the fallen rulers.
- The concept is source-scoped to the episode's reading of Qin's post-unification explanation, not an independent verdict that every accusation was false.
