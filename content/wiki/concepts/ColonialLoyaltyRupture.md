# Colonial Loyalty Rupture

## Definition

Colonial loyalty rupture is the process by which an imperial subject who had identified with Britain comes to treat independence as necessary or unavoidable.

## Current Synthesis

The sources treat rupture as a cumulative and uneven process. Franklin spent much of his life in Britain, considered himself a loyal Englishman, admired George III, and initially tried to reduce tension; the Hutchinson letters, Cockpit humiliation, and Lexington fighting eventually made that allegiance unusable for him. The 1773-1775 sequence widens the model from one biography to intercolonial escalation: inherited-rights claims, the Boston Tea Party, punitive legislation, committees, arms, and fighting narrowed the middle while many delegates still professed loyalty to the king. The concept is therefore stronger than “becoming patriotic” and more contingent than inevitable nationalism.

## Key Claims

- Revolutionary identity can emerge from disappointed loyalty rather than from lifelong separatism.
- Warnings and parables can express a rupture before open commitment to independence.
- Public humiliation can change how an actor reads the legitimacy of imperial authority.
- Family division makes political rupture socially and emotionally concrete.
- Institutional entry, such as service in the Continental Congress, turns a private break into public founding work.
- Collective rupture can proceed through coordinated resistance and armed preparation before explicit independence.

## Evidence

- Loyal starting point: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says Franklin considered himself a loyal Englishman for much of his life, spent about a third of his life in Britain, and admired George III.
- Warning before rupture: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] describes Franklin's 1770 lion-cub parable as a warning that Britain should not assume America would remain weak.
- Hutchinson crisis: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says Franklin passed Hutchinson's letters to Boston radicals and was later publicly attacked before the Privy Council.
- War threshold: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says Franklin reached Philadelphia after Lexington and near-Boston fighting had begun.
- Family rupture: [[684-franklin-revenge-of-the-american-genius-part-2-glt6013835340]] says William Franklin tried to persuade his father to remain neutral, but Franklin favored independence.
- Intercolonial escalation: [[348-the-boston-tea-party-part-2-glt5189620267]] links the Coercive Acts to colonial coordination, collapsing royal authority, and formal weapons preparation.
- Residual allegiance: [[348-the-boston-tea-party-part-2-glt5189620267]] says the Second Continental Congress still spoke of loyalty to George III even after armed conflict began.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept should not imply all colonists moved through the same psychological path. William Franklin's loyalism and Quebec's different settlement show that imperial allegiance could persist. Nor should constitutional resistance be projected backward as a settled republican independence movement before 1775.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to track Franklin's movement from loyal Englishman to American founder.
- Generalized the concept through the 1773-1775 sequence while preserving continuing royal loyalty and regional variation.
