# Mawali Conversion Hierarchy

## Definition

Mawali conversion hierarchy is the unequal incorporation of non-Arab converts into early Muslim society through Arab patronage, fiscal pressure, and social distinctions that could persist after conversion.

## Current Synthesis

The episode rejects a rapid, universal conversion model. Arab conquerors remained a minority among Christians, Zoroastrians, Jews, and other communities, while taxes gave some non-Muslims material incentives to convert.

Umayyad authorities also had reasons to restrict that movement: tax revenue, agricultural labor, and Arab political identity could all be disrupted. Requiring an Arab patron and returning converted Iraqi peasants to estates made formal entry into Islam compatible with continuing social subordination. Converts could then use Quranic justice and equality as grounds for criticizing the dynasty.

## Key Claims

- Conquest did not produce immediate mass conversion.
- Fiscal rules could make conversion materially attractive.
- Arab patronage requirements preserved hierarchy inside the Muslim community.
- State and landholder interests could resist conversion or peasant mobility.
- Unequal incorporation gave non-Arab Muslims a religious language for political grievance.
- Freed enslaved people also expanded the non-Arab urban Muslim population.

## Evidence

- **Slow conversion:** [[376-baghdad-the-forging-of-islam-part-1-glt9529602805]] describes Arabs as a small ruling minority over largely non-Muslim populations.
- **Patronage and labor control:** [[376-baghdad-the-forging-of-islam-part-1-glt9529602805]] reports Arab-sponsor requirements and the forced return of converted Iraqi peasants.
- **Political consequence:** [[376-baghdad-the-forging-of-islam-part-1-glt9529602805]] connects convert literacy and Quranic egalitarian criticism to weakening Umayyad legitimacy.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode does not provide a systematic chronology of mawali status, taxation, regional variation, legal doctrine, or conversion rates. Fiscal incentive should not be treated as the sole motive for conversion, and one Iraqi example cannot establish uniform Umayyad policy.

## What Changed

- Established conversion inequality as both a social structure and a source of religiously framed anti-Umayyad criticism.
