# Sunningdale Power-Sharing Collapse

## Definition
Sunningdale Power-Sharing Collapse refers here to the May 1974 defeat of Northern Ireland's new power-sharing executive under pressure from the Ulster Workers Council strike, loyalist mobilization, paramilitary violence, and the shutdown of essential services.

## Current Synthesis
[[419-britain-in-1974-countdown-to-a-coup-part-3-glt2033018212]] treats Sunningdale's collapse as a concrete British precedent for the fear that organized disruption could defeat constitutional government. The fourteen-day strike shut much of Belfast, the UVF bombed Dublin and Monaghan, and the executive fell, returning Northern Ireland to direct rule. The episode uses this sequence to explain wider coup anxiety without equating the strike with a successful military coup.

## Key Claims
- Sunningdale attempted a new power-sharing order in Northern Ireland.
- Loyalist opposition combined workplace leverage, street coercion, and paramilitary force.
- Essential-service shutdown gave the UWC strike power beyond an ordinary workplace dispute.
- The Dublin and Monaghan bombings formed part of the violent crisis context.
- The executive's collapse restored direct rule and shaped mainland fears about state fragility.

## Evidence
- Strike and shutdown: [[419-britain-in-1974-countdown-to-a-coup-part-3-glt2033018212]] says the UWC strike lasted fourteen days and shut down much of Belfast.
- Paramilitary violence: [[419-britain-in-1974-countdown-to-a-coup-part-3-glt2033018212]] links the crisis to UVF car bombings in Dublin and Monaghan that killed 34 people.
- Political outcome: [[419-britain-in-1974-countdown-to-a-coup-part-3-glt2033018212]] says the power-sharing executive collapsed and direct rule returned.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The page reflects one episode's compressed treatment and is not a full account of Sunningdale's institutions, the Council of Ireland dispute, British government decisions, unionist opinion, security-force conduct, or responsibility for every action during the strike. The event is a precedent for crisis perception, not proof that an identical mainland coup was imminent.
