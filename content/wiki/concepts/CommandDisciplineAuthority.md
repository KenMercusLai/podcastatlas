## Definition

Command Discipline Authority / 军法威严 is the military-governance principle that command hierarchy must remain visibly enforceable, especially when high-status subordinates, famous commanders, or personally popular leaders make formal orders look optional. Enforceability is not identical to maximum severity: detention, referral, demotion, and other reviewable sanctions can preserve authority without turning execution into personal theater.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 297 supplies a Western Han case in which the disputed act is battlefield failure rather than open disobedience. After [[SuJianWesternHan|苏建]] returns alone from a destroyed force, one adviser urges [[WeiQingHanGeneral|卫青]] to execute him because killing a subordinate commander would display authority. [[RenAnWesternHan|任安]] and others answer that Su Jian fought to exhaustion and returned without defecting, and that automatic execution would make later defeated officers afraid to come back. Wei Qing preserves accountability by imprisoning Su Jian while refusing to convert delegated field power into an irreversible prestige display; final judgment returns to [[HanWudi|汉武帝]].

Hanji 684 gives the early Eastern Han version through [[WangLiangEasternHan|Wang Liang]]. [[LiuXiu|Liu Xiu]] appoints [[WuHanEasternHan|Wu Han]] commander over Wang Liang and other generals and orders all action to follow Wu Han's dispatch. Wang Liang resents the arrangement because both men have Yuyang-linked origins, Wang is locally rooted, and Wu Han can be read as an outsider. His unauthorized troop movement for merit forces Liu Xiu to authorize execution before the army.

The episode also shows why discipline is not identical to automatic severity. Zong Guang declines to execute Wang Liang on site, sends him back for Liu Xiu's personal judgment, and Liu Xiu converts the punishment into demotion. Command authority is preserved by making the breach visible and costly, while political room is preserved by avoiding an irreversible field execution of a high minister.

Hanji 936 supplies the late-Han command-law triangle. [[SunJianLateHan|Sun Jian]] urges [[ZhangWenLateHan|Zhang Wen]] to execute [[DongZhuo|Dong Zhuo]] under military law after Dong Zhuo returns without decisive victory, delays a summons, and behaves arrogantly toward his commander. Zhang Wen's hesitation is understandable because Dong Zhuo has western military prestige and no single uncontestable capital offense, but the command problem is that public contempt by a powerful subordinate teaches the army that hierarchy can be negotiated.

Hanji 1025 gives the internal-collapse endpoint through [[ZhangYangLateHan|Zhang Yang]]. The host's "慈不带兵" formula describes a commander who is generous and kind but cannot impose authority strongly enough to prevent subordinates from redirecting the force. When [[YangChouLateHan|Yang Chou]] kills Zhang Yang and [[SuiGuLateHan|Sui Gu]] then kills Yang Chou and turns toward [[YuanShao|Yuan Shao]], benevolence has stopped functioning as military control.

## Key Claims

- Command authority can be damaged before battle when high-status subordinates treat formal hierarchy as optional.
- Punishment can signal order, but detention and higher review may preserve accountability better than irreversible punishment used mainly to display personal authority.
- Maximum severity can weaken future compliance when officers expect that returning after defeat leads automatically to death.
- Unified command can require a superior whom local or senior subordinates resent; that resentment still cannot excuse unauthorized movement.
- Legal and political caution remain real constraints, as seen in both Zong Guang's refusal to execute Wang Liang immediately and Zhang Wen's hesitation over Dong Zhuo.
- Late-Han prestige and private troop attachment sharpen the problem because formal title may not match actual military leverage.
- Benevolent or humane command fails when subordinates can kill, redirect, or ignore the commander.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept is not a rule for automatic harsh punishment. Hanji 297 makes this explicit: Su Jian's imprisonment means restraint is not acquittal, while referral means field authority is not unlimited personal ownership of punishment. Its claim that execution would discourage future return is an organizational inference rather than proof of how every defeated officer would behave. Hanji 684 validates Zong Guang's effort to preserve imperial discretion, and Hanji 936 explains why Zhang Wen hesitates rather than treating caution as cowardice. The current synthesis names a pressure point: command credibility needs visible enforcement, but enforcement still operates inside legal, political, and personnel constraints.

## What Changed

- Added the Su Jian case as direct evidence that imprisonment and higher review can preserve discipline without execution for display.
- Added the return-incentive cost of automatic capital punishment after defeat.
- Sharpened the distinction between enforceable command and personal authority theater.
