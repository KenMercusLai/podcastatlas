# Merit-Based Reward and Punishment / 因功赏罚

Merit-based reward and punishment / 因功赏罚 is the governance rule [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-17-hanzhaohou-he-shenbuhai-lghjmjhq9qs4se96c5ofgz6caqof]] draws from [[HanZhaohou|韩昭侯]] and [[ShenBuhai|申不害]]. When Shen asks for an office for his cousin, Han Zhaohou refuses because the cousin has no matching merit; granting the request would turn office into private favor and undermine Shen's own teaching.

The worn-trousers story extends the principle from appointments to ordinary reward signals. The source says even a small item should not be given away casually if the ruler wants reward and punishment to remain credible. In this frame, ruler self-restraint is part of management: affection, irritation, thrift, and generosity all have to pass through the same standard.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-143-xiangyu-shi-ruhe-feijin-xinji-nongsi-chuhuaiwang-mixin-lstfzfvbd2h9i39f7y5dxrbxxwv2|Hanji 143]] adds a postwar territorial version. [[XiangYu|项羽]] rejects [[MiXin|芈心 / 楚怀王]]'s insistence on the [[HuaiwangAgreement|怀王之约]] by arguing that Mi Xin has no campaign merit and that the generals who fought for three years should divide the world. The source treats Xiang Yu's settlement as a military-merit reward order inside [[ZhouQinHybridOrder|周秦混合制]], even while showing that visible merit logic can still produce unstable politics when it overrides a public promise.

## Key Claims
- A reward loses governing force when it is detached from visible merit.
- A ruler must apply standards even against the minister who taught them.
- Small favors are politically meaningful because they teach the court what behavior is actually rewarded.
- The concept is adjacent to [[MilitaryMeritRankSystem|军功爵制]], but it is broader than battlefield rank because it covers office, goods, and ruler mood.
