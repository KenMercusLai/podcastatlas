## Definition
Superior credit conflict risk is the danger that a subordinate's real contribution becomes politically damaging when it is asserted in direct competition with a superior, patron, or superior-linked powerholder who controls recognition, protection, reporting channels, or punishment.

## Current Synthesis
The concept now has two variants. In [[RenShangLateHan|Ren Shang]]'s case, he and [[MaXianLateHan|Ma Xian]] had real battlefield merit against Langmo, but [[DengZunLateHan|Deng Zun]] received decisive court credit after arranging Langmo's assassination. Once Ren Shang competed for credit against a Deng-family actor, unrelated or adjacent vulnerabilities such as kill-count inflation, embezzlement, and gift-giving became instruments for punishment.

The sharper public-speech variant comes through [[XuYouLateHan|Xu You]]. Xu You's [[GuanduCampaign|Guandu]] contribution was real, but after [[CaoCao|Cao Cao]] takes [[JizhouLateHan|Jizhou]], Xu You publicly claims Cao could not have obtained the province without him and calls Cao by the familiar "曹阿瞒" name. The concept therefore covers both disputed allocation of credit and the status signal created when credit is asserted in front of an audience. It does not mean that subordinates should never seek recognition; it describes the exposure created when recognition claims collide with superior-linked political power or publicly diminish the authority that controls reward and punishment.

## Key Claims
- Credit risk depends on hierarchy as much as on factual contribution.
- Incomplete victories increase ambiguity, allowing another actor to claim decisive completion.
- Superior-linked credit competition can convert administrative irregularities into existential threats.
- Gifts, informal access, and household ties can widen a command dispute into a family-political scandal.
- Public boasting can turn real contribution into a visible authority challenge when the subordinate implies the superior's success depends on him.
- Modern workplace advice in the episode is a source-scoped analogy, not a timeless rule to surrender all credit.

## Evidence
- Hierarchical collision: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-790-lishi-gaosu-ni-ruhe-he-lingdao-da-jiaodao-lulf0r1sdkod3luzwru0-kkrijxk|Hanji 790]] makes Ren Shang's resentment toward Deng Zun central to the transition from military success to execution.
- Ambiguous completion: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-790-lishi-gaosu-ni-ruhe-he-lingdao-da-jiaodao-lulf0r1sdkod3luzwru0-kkrijxk|Hanji 790]] says Ren Shang and Ma Xian defeated Qiang forces but Langmo escaped, while Deng Zun later arranged Langmo's death.
- Punishment channel: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-790-lishi-gaosu-ni-ruhe-he-lingdao-da-jiaodao-lulf0r1sdkod3luzwru0-kkrijxk|Hanji 790]] records accusations of inflated kills and embezzlement after Ren Shang's credit competition.
- Gift exposure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-790-lishi-gaosu-ni-ruhe-he-lingdao-da-jiaodao-lulf0r1sdkod3luzwru0-kkrijxk|Hanji 790]] links Ren Shang's horse gift to Deng Feng with [[DengZhiLateHan|Deng Zhi]]'s visible punishment of his household.
- Workplace analogy: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-790-lishi-gaosu-ni-ruhe-he-lingdao-da-jiaodao-lulf0r1sdkod3luzwru0-kkrijxk|Hanji 790]] condenses the lesson as avoiding work and relationships that compete with superior-linked interests, credit, image, or factional standing.
- Public boast variant: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1064-xuyou-zhisi-gonggao-zhenzhu-yu-xingge-quexian-de-beige-lswmvj55p7ip97ts5e30xfdubwth|Hanji 1064]] says Xu You repeatedly implies that Cao Cao owes Jizhou to him and does so in public.
- Punitive outcome: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1064-xuyou-zhisi-gonggao-zhenzhu-yu-xingge-quexian-de-beige-lswmvj55p7ip97ts5e30xfdubwth|Hanji 1064]] says Cao Cao outwardly laughs but later kills Xu You after resentment forms.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not prove that Ren Shang's accusations were fabricated, nor do they establish that all superior-linked credit claims are illegitimate. Xu You's case also comes through a moralized host reading that emphasizes character and workplace boundary lessons; Cao Cao's own coercive power remains part of the structure. The concept should be used narrowly: it concerns recognition claims under asymmetric power, especially when a superior or patronage bloc can frame the subordinate's ordinary vulnerabilities, public words, or informal access as punishable offenses.

## What Changed
- Created the concept from Hanji 790.
- Connected the episode's historical Ren Shang-Deng Zun case to its modern workplace advice without treating the advice as universal.
- Added Hanji 1064's Xu You case as the public-boasting/status-insult variant of superior credit conflict.
