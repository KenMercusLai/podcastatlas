# Kleos Heroic Glory / 克莱奥斯英雄声名

## Definition

Kleos heroic glory is the Homeric value of gaining enduring fame through great deeds, especially martial and heroic action.

## Current Synthesis

The Nolan episode uses kleos to explain the distance between Homeric epic and a modern moral frame. In the hosts' reading, [[TheOdyssey]] already complicates Iliadic glory by showing [[Achilles]] in the underworld preferring ordinary mortal life to dead renown. Nolan pushes that suspicion further: his film rejects glory-seeking and treats heroic stories themselves as morally unstable after war.

The Alexander episode supplies a historical-reception case. [[AlexanderTheGreat]] reportedly treats Achilles as ancestor and model, performs conquest through Troy pilgrimage and spear-won possession, and fights visibly at the head of his cavalry. The desire for unsurpassed remembrance helps explain personal risk and expansion after the initial fiscal rationale weakens. It does not prove a transparent private motive: ancient stories and Alexander's own political theater can make heroic imitation appear more coherent than lived decision-making was.

## Key Claims

- Kleos makes heroic action durable by turning deeds into sung or remembered fame.
- The Odyssey can already be read as reflecting on the cost of Iliadic glory rather than simply repeating it.
- Nolan's adaptation is strongly anti-kleos because it treats fame, victory, and war success as morally compromised.
- Deconstructing kleos shifts the return story from heroic restoration toward repentance and survival after violence.
- Alexander's Achilles-centered performance shows how kleos can join command charisma, propaganda, personal exposure, and open-ended conquest.
- The concept remains ancient and source-specific; modern anti-war feeling should not be retrojected as Homer's own settled position.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The page follows one film-analysis episode and one popular-history reconstruction. It does not settle the full Homeric relationship between kleos, nostos, death, and social honor, or prove Alexander's private psychology. Troy ceremonies and Achilles stories can be sincere religion, self-fashioning, propaganda, later literary patterning, or several at once. The concept should remain separate from a general theory of all heroic fame.
