# Sadean Naturalism

## Definition

Sadean naturalism is the episode's nameable pattern in which [[MarquisDeSade|Sade]] rejects Christian moral authority, treats desire and struggle as natural facts, and then converts those facts into permission for the strong to dominate the weak.

## Current Synthesis

The source separates a materialist challenge to inherited morality from the further move that makes impulse morally sovereign. Cultural variation can weaken claims that every convention is universal, but it does not show that coercion is good; desire can be natural without becoming a right to use another person. Sade's provocative importance lies in collapsing those distinctions and exposing how appeals to nature, liberation, and authenticity can become anti-moral licenses when power and victimhood disappear from the test.

## Key Claims

- Rejecting divine command does not by itself supply an ethical rule for resolving conflicting desires.
- A descriptive claim about natural impulse or struggle cannot alone justify rape, murder, cruelty, or domination.
- Cultural variation in morality does not prove that every act is equally defensible.
- Strength-centered nature language can remove protection from vulnerable people while presenting violence as realism.
- The episode's Darwin, Nietzsche, Freud, fascist, Nazi, Stalinist, and Maoist comparisons identify perceived structural echoes, not one verified lineage.
- Sade's selected egalitarian positions demonstrate that anti-traditional argument can coexist with radically unequal power over victims.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The source does not provide a comprehensive reading of Sade's corpus or demonstrate that every text states one stable doctrine.
- “Natural” can mean descriptive, causal, typical, healthy, or morally justified; the episode's critique depends on not confusing these senses.
- Sade predates Darwin, Nietzsche, Freud, fascism, Nazism, Stalinism, and Maoism, so anticipation claims require caution about anachronism and direct influence.
- Opposition to Christianity or belief in material causes does not entail Sade's ethics.
- The source's treatment of ancient Rome and later totalitarian systems is selective and interpretive.

## What Changed

- Established a bounded concept for the move from natural desire to moral permission.
- Distinguished moral anti-foundationalism from the separate endorsement of domination.
- Preserved later-thinker and totalitarian comparisons as qualified structural analogies.
