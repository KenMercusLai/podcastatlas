# Usurpation Escalation Trap

## Definition

Usurpation escalation trap is the process by which an initially improvised intervention creates new enemies, evidentiary burdens, and survival risks that make further arrest, violence, delegitimation, and concealment increasingly useful to the would-be ruler.

## Current Synthesis

The episode reads Richard III's rise as a sequence rather than a single master plan. Seizing Edward V required justification; arresting the Woodvilles intensified sanctuary and council concern; Hastings's loyalty became dangerous; taking the second prince enabled joint delegitimation; coronation made the old heirs continuing threats; and their disappearance then deepened rebellion and legitimacy failure. Each step narrowed the possibility of returning to ordinary protectorship without political cost.

## Key Claims

- Escalation can emerge from short-term defensive choices even when the final usurpation was not planned at the outset.
- Each coercive act creates witnesses, opponents, legitimacy problems, and rollback costs that can make a stronger act appear necessary.
- Removing loyalists and controlling claimants changes the ruler's incentives by making restoration more dangerous.
- Legal and religious delegitimation can reduce the public cost of seizure, but it does not eliminate the displaced heirs' political value.
- Violence may secure immediate control while weakening coalition trust and generating rebellion.

## Evidence

- From interception to imprisonment: [[139-the-princes-in-the-tower-part-2-glt4685691538]] connects the Woodville arrests, council conflict, retinue mobilization, coronation delay, and Tower lodging.
- From obstacle removal to crown: [[139-the-princes-in-the-tower-part-2-glt4685691538]] treats Hastings's execution, the second prince's transfer, illegitimacy preaching, and coronation as a connected escalation.
- From crown to regime failure: [[139-the-princes-in-the-tower-part-2-glt4685691538]] connects the princes' disappearance to rescue attempts, Buckingham's rebellion, Henry Tudor's rise, and Richard's political isolation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Sequence does not prove inevitability or eliminate agency. Richard could have begun with more intention than the surviving evidence reveals, and different choices remained possible at several points. The concept explains accumulating incentives; it does not excuse murder or prove the exact decision path.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from the episode's interpretation of Richard's improvised but progressively coercive route to kingship.
