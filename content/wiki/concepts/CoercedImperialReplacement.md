# Coerced Imperial Replacement / 胁迫式废立

Coerced imperial replacement / 胁迫式废立 is the pattern [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-948-dongzhuo-feidi-quanchen-ruhe-gaibian-huangquan-youxi-ltekg1a-5b5u37aeekjm3dm2m6kk|Hanji 948]] builds from [[DongZhuo|董卓]]'s deposition of [[LiuBianHongnongWang|刘辩 / 弘农王]] and installation of [[EmperorXianOfHan|汉献帝]]. A military powerholder does not simply seize the throne himself; he forces the court to perform an imperial replacement that leaves the old symbolic machinery in place while redirecting control to himself.

The source case has several linked pieces. Dong Zhuo first tests [[YuanShao|袁绍]], then threatens the officials, invokes classical precedents, suppresses [[LuZhiLateHan|卢植]]'s objection, and makes [[YuanWeiLateHan|袁隗]] handle the seal transfer. [[EmpressDowagerHe|何太后]] is then removed and poisoned, while the wider He-family punishment shows that coercion backs the ceremony.

The concept differs from [[AlternativeSovereignLegitimacyTrap|另立君主合法性陷阱]]. In an alternative-sovereign trap, outsiders debate replacing a captured ruler and risk losing legitimacy by doing so. In coerced imperial replacement, the captor already controls the court and uses its forms to replace the ruler from inside.

## Key Claims
- Forced replacement needs procedure because the old title order still carries public force.
- Military threat can convert court deliberation into a ritual performance.
- Classical precedent becomes dangerous when a coercive actor uses it as cover rather than as a constraint.
- The replacement is not complete until the old ruler's family and supporters are neutralized.
- The new ruler's legitimacy is useful precisely because the coercive patron can govern through it.
