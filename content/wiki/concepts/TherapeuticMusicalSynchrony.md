# Therapeutic Musical Synchrony

## Definition
Therapeutic musical synchrony is the music-therapy pattern in which a therapist first matches or follows a client's movement, rhythm, sound, or attention, then uses that shared timing to build interaction, agency, and participation.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats synchrony as a way to enter the client's current state before asking for a new behavior. In the autism and severe intellectual-disability examples, the therapist does not start by demanding conversation or compliance. She follows small gestures, percussion hits, bell sounds, steps, or changes in speed and intensity so the client can perceive that their action changes the sound world and that another person is responding.

This makes the mechanism both musical and relational. Rhythm and timbre make otherwise small movements audible; repetition creates expectation; turn-taking makes interaction safer; and the therapist's following can gradually shift into invitation. The source connects this to behaviorist measurable goals, but it also leaves room for humanistic relationship work: the measurable change matters because a person notices, attends, reaches, rings, vocalizes, or participates differently.

## Key Claims
- Synchrony starts by joining the client's observable action rather than imposing the therapist's preferred musical structure.
- Musical feedback can make a person's movement or vocalization noticeable to themselves, creating a route from accidental action to intentional participation.
- Repetition, pause, and expectation can help autistic or nonverbal clients notice the therapist and the shared activity.
- The method can support observable goals such as sitting, looking, waiting, pressing a bell, stopping and going, changing intensity, or moving toward an instrument.
- Synchrony remains a clinical technique inside assessment and goal setting, not a guarantee that any rhythmic activity is therapeutic.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not prove that synchrony works for every autistic, nonverbal, hearing-impaired, or disabled client. It also does not replace speech therapy, occupational therapy, behavioral intervention, hearing care, or disability-specific support. The clinical claim depends on trained observation, adaptation, and goal tracking; casual mimicry or music play is not the same intervention.
