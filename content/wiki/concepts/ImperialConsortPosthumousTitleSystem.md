## Definition

皇后谥号制度 / imperial consort posthumous title system is the dynastic ritual practice of naming an empress after death through an evaluative title that joins personal moral memory to the reigning house's imperial title and ancestral hierarchy.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 737 grounds the concept through [[YinLihua|阴丽华]]'s posthumous title 光烈. The episode says the title combines the 光 from [[LiuXiu|刘秀]]'s 光武 imperial title with 烈 as Yin's own evaluative character, and treats this as the beginning of a durable pre-Tang empress-title pattern.

The Western Han Dingtao dispute shows the conflict side before the later [[FuTaihouWesternHan|傅太后]] settlement. Hanji 562 supplies the first title-pressure step: [[DongHongWesternHan|董宏]] cites Qin precedent to argue that Aidi can honor the Dingtao queen dowager as Di Taihou, while [[WangMang|王莽]] and [[ShiDanWesternHan|施丹]] object that Qin precedent cannot casually create a competing supreme title inside Han ritual order. Hanji 575 then shows the dispute deepening into attendants, vehicles, garments, and temple treatment for the Dingtao birth-parent line. Shi Dan resists because elevating Aidi's birth-line consorts and father risks making posthumous consort and parental rank compete with the inherited imperial hierarchy.

The concept therefore matters both positively and negatively. A title can convert consort biography into dynastic memory, as with Yin Lihua; it can also become a live status weapon when a ruler wants to honor birth parents whose public ritual position is constrained by adoption into the imperial line. The source's interpretations of Yin's restraint and Aidi's filial motive remain separate from the narrower title-system claim.

## Key Claims

- Empress posthumous titles can fuse personal evaluation with the emperor's dynastic title.
- The title gives a consort a formal place in imperial memory rather than leaving her identity only in family narrative.
- Yin Lihua's 光烈 title is treated by the source as a first major precedent for later empress posthumous naming.
- Consort or birth-parent title elevation can become politically dangerous when it creates a second status center beside the inherited imperial line.
- Precedent citation is not neutral in title disputes; an earlier dynasty's example can be treated as misleading when it threatens the receiving court's own hierarchy.
- Vehicle, garment, attendant, palace, and temple arrangements can matter as much as title wording because they make rank visible.
- The practice links gendered court biography, filial burial, and state ritual into one naming act.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The page does not independently verify the full institutional history of all later empress titles. It records Hanji 737's claim that Yin Lihua's 光烈 title is a decisive precedent and keeps the exact 600-year continuity as a source claim. Hanji 562 shows the opening precedent dispute around Fu Taihou's title but not Dong Hong's full memorial text or every title formula. Hanji 575 shows the political dispute around Dingtao-line elevation but does not provide the complete legal history of those titles or every later settlement step. Future sources should distinguish posthumous-title composition, burial ritual, temple placement, palace rank, and later Tang changes.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 562's Qin-precedent dispute as the opening Western Han title-pressure branch before Hanji 575's fuller Dingtao-line conflict.
- Preserved Hanji 737's Yin Lihua title as the positive precedent branch.
- Clarified that visible rank treatment, not only the written title, belongs to the political problem.
