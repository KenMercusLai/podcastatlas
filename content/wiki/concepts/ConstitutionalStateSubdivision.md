# Constitutional State Subdivision

## Definition

Constitutional state subdivision is the creation of one or more new U.S. states from the territory of an existing state under Article IV, Section 3 of the [[UnitedStatesConstitution]], subject to the consent requirements that govern the process.

## Current Synthesis

[[divide-and-conquer-divide-and-conquer|Divide and Conquer]] presents [[MichaelStokesPaulsen]] and [[VasanKesavan]]'s argument that Article IV does not absolutely forbid subdivision. Because the “but–nor” construction links the relevant clauses, the final consent language applies to both creating a state within an existing state and combining states or parts of states.

The Texas case adds advance consent. Congress's 1845 admission resolution allowed up to four additional states to be formed from Texas territory with Texas's consent, which the episode treats as continuing permission unless Congress revokes it before implementation. Legal authority still does not supply a complete process: boundaries, state approval, timing, federal response, litigation, administration, residents' interests, and public legitimacy remain unresolved.

## Key Claims

- Article IV's consent condition can be read to permit subdivision rather than impose a categorical ban.
- [[WestVirginia|West Virginia]] functions as a historical test of whether the consent language reaches state subdivision.
- Congressional consent may be given before a later subdivision proposal rather than only after a complete plan exists.
- Texas's 1845 admission resolution is the episode's example of possible continuing advance consent.
- Congress may retain a chance to revoke earlier permission before the state completes the act.
- Subdivision changes national representation because every resulting state receives its own senators.
- Legal possibility, operational procedure, democratic legitimacy, and partisan effect are separate questions.

## Evidence

### Text and historical application

- [[divide-and-conquer-divide-and-conquer]] attributes to Paulsen and Kesavan the linked-clause reading and their conclusion that West Virginia was constitutionally formed.

### Advance consent and revocation

- [[divide-and-conquer-divide-and-conquer]] presents the 1845 Texas resolution as advance congressional permission and reports Paulsen's view that Congress could withdraw it before completion.

### Representational consequences

- [[divide-and-conquer-divide-and-conquer]] explains that five successor states would have ten Senate seats and uses [[MichaelLiTexasDemographer|Michael Li]]'s map to show a contingent partisan outcome.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source summarizes one co-authored legal argument and an episode's political test rather than supplying a complete survey of doctrine or procedure. The 1845 permission may face disputes over continuing effect, revocation, required approvals, sequencing, judicial review, and democratic legitimacy. A power's textual availability does not establish that it can or should be exercised.
