## Definition

私恩任官侵蚀 / Private-Favor Appointment Erosion is the institutional failure in which titles, offices, and recommendation channels are used to repay intimacy, kinship, palace service, or near-attendant influence, weakening office and title as public trusts.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-813-gudai-de-gaoguan-jing-ye-hui-bei-ouda-ln-dw3xohnmpavkpu-bdvasvietv|Hanji 813]] gives the concept its first Shun-era title-boundary layer. [[ZuoXiongLateHan|左雄]] argues that [[SongELateHan|宋娥]] and [[LiangJiLateHan|梁冀]] should not receive marquisate through wet-nurse service or Liang-family intimacy. His boundary is not anti-gratitude: material support for Song E is acceptable, but landed title changes public authority and violates the meaning of Han title rules.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-814-dangguan-buwei-min-zuozhu-buru-huijia-mai-hongshu-lqlrfpuenzhr1tbrdd3urtolfw2p|Hanji 814]] then broadens the concept through [[LiGuLateHan|李固]]'s 133 CE disaster-policy answer to [[EmperorShunOfHan|汉顺帝]]. Li Gu likewise distinguishes money gifts from public appointment, then extends the warning from wet nurses and Liang relatives to attendants' children, Shangshu-adjacent families, and other near-court actors. Even a small office can harm the people when its holder enters through favor rather than tested fit.

Together, the two sources make the concept a boundary between private gratitude and public authority. [[PalaceProximityPower|宫廷近身权力]], wet-nurse service, empress kinship, and document-channel access become dangerous when they bypass the fitness tests that let office and title serve the public.

## Key Claims

- Public office and public title differ from private reward because they change land, authority, households, local administration, and legal order.
- Personal gratitude is institutionally safer when expressed as bounded gifts rather than as titles, salaries, or governing authority.
- Empress-kin, wet-nurse, eunuch, attendant, and Shangshu-family channels can pressure title and appointment systems even when no explicit order is issued.
- Small offices and narrow title exceptions still matter because precedent weakens standards for the next favor request.
- Appointment erosion is cumulative: one exception can become a rule-like precedent that makes later correction harder.
- The concept is a precondition for broader [[CourtOfficeCapture|court office capture]] rather than identical with full armed or monetary capture of offices.

## Evidence

- Title-boundary objection: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-813-gudai-de-gaoguan-jing-ye-hui-bei-ouda-ln-dw3xohnmpavkpu-bdvasvietv|Hanji 813]] says Zuo Xiong opposes enfeoffing Song E and Liang Shang's son because unmerited marquisates violate Han public-title rules.
- Gift-title distinction: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-813-gudai-de-gaoguan-jing-ye-hui-bei-ouda-ln-dw3xohnmpavkpu-bdvasvietv|Hanji 813]] says Zuo permits money support for Song E while warning against the more damaging grant of land and title.
- Partial correction: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-813-gudai-de-gaoguan-jing-ye-hui-bei-ouda-ln-dw3xohnmpavkpu-bdvasvietv|Hanji 813]] says Shun drops the Liang-family title attempt after Liang Shang refuses it but keeps Song E's title despite criticism.
- Gift-office distinction: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-814-dangguan-buwei-min-zuozhu-buru-huijia-mai-hongshu-lqlrfpuenzhr1tbrdd3urtolfw2p|Hanji 814]] says Li Gu allows that Shun may reward favored intimates with money while warning against granting titles and offices outside proper limits.
- Wet-nurse and Liang-kin privilege: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-814-dangguan-buwei-min-zuozhu-buru-huijia-mai-hongshu-lqlrfpuenzhr1tbrdd3urtolfw2p|Hanji 814]] identifies Song E and the Liang empress's relatives as examples of private closeness turning into public power.
- Near-attendant children: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-814-dangguan-buwei-min-zuozhu-buru-huijia-mai-hongshu-lqlrfpuenzhr1tbrdd3urtolfw2p|Hanji 814]] has Li Gu argue that children of attendants, Shangshu, and palace officials should not directly become officials or be pushed through Xiaolian recommendations.
- Cumulative erosion: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-814-dangguan-buwei-min-zuozhu-buru-huijia-mai-hongshu-lqlrfpuenzhr1tbrdd3urtolfw2p|Hanji 814]] says that apparently small appointment mistakes can gradually break the court's rules.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is grounded in two source notes and should remain narrower than a general theory of corruption. Hanji 813 and Hanji 814 both preserve the same boundary: not every private favor is equally damaging, and money gifts are less dangerous than title or office grants. The sources also do not independently prove the later performance records of Wuxuan, Yang Di, or every Liang-family appointee. The claim is institutional rather than biographical: the public meaning of title and office changes when grants follow private closeness rather than merit or tested fit.

## What Changed

- Moved the concept's starting point back from Li Gu's Hanji 814 memorial to Zuo Xiong's Hanji 813 title-boundary argument.
- Separated private gratitude as gift from private gratitude as marquisate, land, office, or recommendation pressure.
- Linked Song E, Liang Ji, wet-nurse influence, outer-relative favor, eunuch proximity, attendant families, and Shangshu-family privilege to one public-authority erosion pattern.
- Preserved source-scoped cautions around transcript forms and minor appointee identities.
