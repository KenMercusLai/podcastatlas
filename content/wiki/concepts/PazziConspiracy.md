## Definition

The Pazzi Conspiracy was the coordinated 1478 attempt to kill [[LorenzoDeMedici|Lorenzo]] and [[GiulianoDeMedici|Giuliano de' Medici]] and seize Florentine government, followed by civic resistance, retaliatory violence, papal sanctions, and war.

## Current Synthesis

The plot joined interests that cannot be reduced to one family feud. Papal territorial policy, banking competition, the Pisa archbishopric, Medici control inside a republic, mercenary force, and family survival all converged. The [[PazziFamily|Pazzi]] and papal allies concluded that both Medici brothers had to die together because leaving one alive would preserve the network.

The cathedral plan converted worship into tactical opportunity: both targets were expected at high mass, and the elevation of the host would lower heads and provide a signal. It succeeded against Giuliano but failed against Lorenzo, while an attempted seizure of the Palazzo della Signoria was defeated by officials and citizens.

Failure hardened rather than removed Medici power. Immediate public revenge included lynching and mutilation; formal reprisals included execution, banishment, confiscation, and erasure of Pazzi symbols. [[PopeSixtusIV|Sixtus IV]] then used excommunication, interdict, financial seizure, and allied military force, expanding an urban coup into interstate crisis.

## Key Claims

- Banking, ecclesiastical, territorial, and family rivalry jointly produced the plot.
- Simultaneous assassination was treated as necessary because Medici authority was networked rather than vested in one office.
- Sacred space and ritual timing supplied access, predictability, and tactical distraction.
- Survival of one principal target and resistance by civic officials caused the coup to collapse rapidly.
- Popular and official retaliation blurred justice with collective and extrajudicial violence.
- A failed internal coup can escalate into external religious, financial, and military conflict.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source is a narrative podcast rather than a primary-source study. It relies partly on Montesecco's testimony after torture, and it does not resolve whether Sixtus IV authorized murder. Exact participation, dialogue, crowd responsibility, victim counts, innocence, and the relationship between spontaneous revenge and directed repression remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Created a framework treating the conspiracy as a coup coalition rather than only an assassination.
- Separated tactical success against Giuliano from strategic failure against the Medici regime.
- Connected urban retaliation to the subsequent papal and Neapolitan war.
