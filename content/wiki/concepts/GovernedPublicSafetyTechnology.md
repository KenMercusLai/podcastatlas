# Governed Public-Safety Technology

## Definition

Governed public-safety technology is a design and institutional approach in which sensing, search, evidence, dispatch, or intervention capability is constrained by explicit purpose, authorization, audit, transparency, penalties, and independent review across its lifecycle.

## Current Synthesis

The Axon interview treats governance as part of the product rather than an external afterthought. Legitimate use begins with a defined public-safety purpose, limits data access to a real case, adds dual or supervisory authorization for sensitive action, records activity for audit, discloses deployment policy, imposes consequences for misuse, and preserves human responsibility for force.

These controls are complementary and incomplete. A case identifier can be false, two insiders can agree, logs can go unread, policies can be vague, advisers can lack veto power, and civilian review can occur only after harm. Governance therefore requires enforceable institutions and outcome evaluation in addition to interface controls. The framework does not settle the [[PublicSafetyPrivacyTradeoff]]; it specifies how safety claims should be tested and constrained.

## Key Claims

- Governance should be designed into collection, access, analysis, sharing, and intervention workflows.
- Purpose limitation requires more than a free-text justification; sensitive action may need independent authorization.
- Auditability matters only when review and consequences are credible.
- Publicly disclosed mission profiles make deployment scope contestable before routine use.
- Human control is necessary for force but does not guarantee good judgment or lawful outcomes.
- Independent civilian oversight should evaluate incidents, safeguards, and aggregate effectiveness.
- Advisory consultation is weaker than shared authority when a product creates contested public risk.

## Evidence

- **Access control:** [[allin-intv-ricksmith-ch-allin-intv-ricksmith-ch]] records Smith's support for valid case identifiers, dual authorization, auditability, and civil or criminal penalties for misuse.
- **Private-camera boundary:** [[allin-intv-ricksmith-ch-allin-intv-ricksmith-ch]] describes voluntary homeowner sharing through Ring rather than unrestricted police search of private feeds.
- **Drone policy:** [[allin-intv-ricksmith-ch-allin-intv-ricksmith-ch]] cites public mission profiles and flight limits tied to specific calls for service.
- **Force control:** [[allin-intv-ricksmith-ch-allin-intv-ricksmith-ch]] rejects autonomous force and proposes real-time supervision and multi-key authorization.
- **Oversight and authority gap:** [[allin-intv-ricksmith-ch-allin-intv-ricksmith-ch]] advocates civilian review while recording adviser resignations over continued Taser-equipped drone exploration.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source supplies proposed controls, not an independent audit of deployed systems. It does not establish the legal sufficiency, technical robustness, review independence, error rate, community acceptance, or deterrent effect of any safeguard. More governable surveillance can still expand surveillance, and a safer intervention relative to an armed entry can still be unacceptable under a rights-based or democratic-consent standard.

## What Changed

- Established a lifecycle governance framework joining technical controls to institutional authority and independent review.
