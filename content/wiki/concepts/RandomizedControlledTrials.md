# Randomized Controlled Trials

## Definition
Randomized controlled trials assign otherwise comparable participants or sites by chance to different interventions, making later outcome differences easier to attribute to the tested change.

## Current Synthesis
The sources present randomization as a practical challenge to stories that survive through intuition, ideology, selection bias, or vivid anecdote. Nigeria's entrepreneur grants and Busia's textbook, deworming, and HIV-risk studies show how assignment can make development interventions evaluable. Banerjee's teacher, vaccination, cash-transfer, and bed-net cases add a second use: a result can expose why an intervention succeeds or fails and thereby support [[InterventionMechanismLearning]].

Randomization does not make a study self-interpreting. Researchers still choose the population, comparison, treatment, outcomes, time horizon, and candidate explanations. A null result may expose an operating-model mismatch, as in syllabus-driven teaching, but it can also reflect measurement, implementation, statistical power, or setting. Trial evidence is strongest when joined to design transparency, mechanism testing, replication, external-validity judgment, and [[DevelopmentResearchEthics]].

## Key Claims
- Random assignment can reduce selection bias in comparisons between intervention and control groups.
- Null and mixed results can be useful when the implementation and measurement are interpretable.
- Trials can test policy effects and generate better theories about the mechanism behind those effects.
- Price, resources, and cultural stories should not be assumed causal when randomized comparisons permit direct tests.
- The validity of an answer remains bounded by the question, alternatives, outcomes, population, and time horizon built into the design.
- Evidence generation does not guarantee ethical participation, institutional adoption, financing, or durable local benefit.

## Evidence
- Assignment and policy effects - [[piles-of-cash-and-a-town-of-solutions-in-kenya-nigeria-summer-school]] describes randomized entrepreneur grants and education, health, and poverty trials in Nigeria and Busia.
- Informative failure - [[piles-of-cash-and-a-town-of-solutions-in-kenya-nigeria-summer-school]] reports that textbooks did not improve average scores, while [[putting-our-assumptions-to-the-test]] reports no gain from adding teachers or halving class size without changing instruction.
- Mechanism discovery - [[putting-our-assumptions-to-the-test]] links level-matched teaching, lentil incentives, cash transfers, and bed-net pricing to revised explanations of learning, procrastination, work, and use.
- Framing and translation limits - both [[putting-our-assumptions-to-the-test]] and [[piles-of-cash-and-a-town-of-solutions-in-kenya-nigeria-summer-school]] distinguish measured findings from complete theory, durable institutions, local benefit, and automatic policy uptake.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The supplied episodes summarize studies without complete protocols, sample sizes, attrition, outcome definitions, statistical uncertainty, replication history, or external-validity analysis. A randomized design can improve causal inference for the tested contrast without proving why the effect occurred or whether it transfers elsewhere. Poorly chosen outcomes, narrow candidate interventions, implementation failure, contamination, noncompliance, or underpowered samples can still produce limited or misleading answers.
