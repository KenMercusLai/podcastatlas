# Supplement Structure Function Claims

## Definition
Structure/function claims are the label and marketing phrases that imply bodily support without claiming to diagnose, prevent, cure, treat, or mitigate disease - the narrow band of meaning that supplement regulation allows across the wiki's sources.

## Current Synthesis
[[why-is-there-a-supplement-craze-if-they-dont-even-work]] establishes the U.S. version of the boundary. [[FrankCantone]] gives "supports metabolism" as acceptable phrasing, [[MelanieBenish]] says words such as "supports" and "promotes" should signal to consumers that the benefit is not proven the way a drug claim would be, and the FDA disclaimer on the label does not stop shoppers from treating the wording as authoritative. The concept is practical rather than academic: claim vocabulary can carry much of the product's value while remaining inside [[DietarySupplementRegulation]] and the [[DietarySupplementHealthAndEducationAct]].

The Australian counterpart comes from [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]], which shows that the listed AUSTL track permits only low-risk ingredients and low-level permitted claim wording - so claim scope is bounded in a way that makes the regime look stricter than the U.S. route - and that the categories competing for purchase are not argued on claim evidence at all. [[Swisse|Swisse]]'s gender-segmented multivitamins, natural-sounding ingredients, and lifestyle advertising sell a feeling of targeted effectiveness, and the hosts note that Australian officials periodically extend the permitted function descriptions, which then generate demand - the addition of a bone-and-joint-health claim is their example for a glucosamine boom. The synthesis is that permitted claim language sets the ceiling on what a product may say, while the competitive work happens in positioning, segmentation, and marketing.

## Key Claims
- Claim wording carries much of the product's value even when evidence for healthy consumers is weak.
- "Supports" and "promotes" language suggests benefit without becoming a disease-treatment claim.
- Label disclaimers may not change how consumers read health-oriented packaging.
- The Australian listed track bounds both ingredients and claim vocabulary, constraining language more visibly without adding proof of benefit.
- Adding a new permitted function description can create a new product category, which shows the claim list acting as demand infrastructure.
- Marketing can bypass the claim boundary by selling targeting, naturalness, and lifestyle rather than asserted benefit.

## Evidence
- U.S. claim vocabulary: [[why-is-there-a-supplement-craze-if-they-dont-even-work]] records [[FrankCantone]]'s "supports metabolism" example and [[MelanieBenish]]'s reading that supports-and-promotes phrasing signals an unproven claim.
- Disclaimer limits: [[why-is-there-a-supplement-craze-if-they-dont-even-work]] says the FDA disclaimer does not necessarily prevent consumers from treating claims as authoritative.
- Enforcement gap: [[why-is-there-a-supplement-craze-if-they-dont-even-work]] follows [[Prevagen]]'s claims into a [[FederalTradeCommission|FTC]] action that took years to resolve.
- Australian claim boundary: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] says the listed AUSTL track allows only permitted low-risk ingredients and low-level permitted claim expression.
- Permission as demand: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] describes a newly added bone-and-joint-health function description generating a wave of glucosamine products.
- Marketing beside claims: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] shows gendered multivitamins, natural ingredients, and lifestyle advertising carrying the sale while claim vocabulary stays minimal.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Claim rulebooks differ by jurisdiction and change over time, so this page records patterns rather than a compliance guide; permitted wording, disclaimer requirements, and enforcement posture must be checked against current regulator guidance. The Australian material is one podcast's summary, and the glucosamine example is a demand observation rather than an efficacy finding, while the U.S. material rests on reporting about one statute and a small set of cases. Nothing here establishes that permitted claims are meaningless - bounded vocabulary still excludes disease-treatment promises - only that the wording should be read as a legal ceiling rather than as evidence.
