# Coerced Consent Under Slavery

## Definition
Coerced consent under slavery is the analytical problem that choices made by an enslaved person in an intimate relationship with an owner cannot be treated as ordinary free consent.

## Current Synthesis
The episode applies this frame to [[SallyHemings|Sally Hemings]] and [[ThomasJefferson|Thomas Jefferson]]. Hemings may have had feelings, fears, family attachments, and strategic reasons for returning from France, but Jefferson owned her and was decades older. The central judgment is therefore structural: emotional uncertainty does not remove coercion when law, property, age, race, gender, and household dependence give one person overwhelming power over another.

## Key Claims
- Legal ownership fundamentally changes the meaning of consent in an intimate relationship.
- Unrecoverable private emotion should not be used to erase public power imbalance.
- Hemings's legal freedom in France makes her return to Virginia an important but still constrained decision.
- Promised privileges and freedom for children show negotiation under unequal conditions rather than free romance.
- Historical interpretation must separate possible affection from the coercive system that made refusal dangerous.

## Evidence
- Ownership: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] states that Jefferson owned Sally Hemings as property.
- Age and status: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] frames the relationship as involving a huge power imbalance between a man in his mid-forties and a teenage enslaved woman.
- France decision: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] says Hemings was legally free and pregnant in France before returning to America.
- Promised settlement: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] reports Madison Hemings's claim that Jefferson promised privileges and freedom for Hemings's children.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode leaves Hemings's motives unresolved and allows that homesickness, family ties, fear, or personal feeling may have combined. That uncertainty qualifies motive claims but not the structural claim that slavery made ordinary consent impossible.
