## Definition

居丧行为法律化 / mourning conduct legalization is the conversion of filial mourning expectations from private ritual and moral judgment into conduct that can affect punishment, status, succession, or office.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-342-2-gudai-fuqi-tongfang-jingyou-zhezhong-falv-xianzhi-lsnhz4q-oxjpefgkdfxkcal-37iv|Hanji 342-2]] grounds the concept in the case of [[LiuBoChangshanKingWesternHan|刘勃]]. Sickbed attendance, medicine tasting, residence in mourning quarters, abstention from drink, gaming, music, sexual conduct, conspicuous travel, and prison visits become evidence about whether a new king can inhabit filial and royal roles. The result is not a sentence for one isolated act: accusation, investigation, obstruction, household conflict, and succession fitness combine in deposition and exile.

The episode then generalizes beyond the case. It describes twenty-seven months as the practical “three-year” mourning term, lists prohibited pleasures and property division, and says false mourning claims and some mourning-period reproductive outcomes could receive corporal or penal sanctions. These general legal claims show the intended ritual-to-law bridge, but the source gives no named statute, dynasty-by-dynasty scope, or primary citation. They therefore define a research question and source-scoped claim, not a verified universal code.

## Key Claims

- Mourning can become public evidence of role fitness rather than remain a private emotional state.
- Sickbed care and post-death restraint form one continuity of filial accountability.
- Alcohol, entertainment, sex, mobility, property division, and public display can be interpreted as competing with the deceased parent's ritual priority.
- In a royal succession, mourning misconduct can affect title and territorial order even when execution is refused.
- Obstruction of inquiry must be distinguished from the underlying mourning allegation because it creates a separate challenge to central authority.
- Cross-dynastic penalty claims require statute, date, jurisdiction, and enforcement evidence before being treated as universal ancient law.

## Evidence

General penalty claims:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-342-2-gudai-fuqi-tongfang-jingyou-zhezhong-falv-xianzhi-lsnhz4q-oxjpefgkdfxkcal-37iv|Hanji 342-2]] describes corporal or penal consequences for mourning violations and claims one year of servitude for childbirth following conception during parental mourning.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source itself says marital intercourse was not directly prohibited in the categorical form implied by the title. Its reproductive-sanction claim, Qing abortion or adoption claim, exact mourning term, and list of punishments lack a named legal text and may combine periods with different rules. Liu Bo's case also includes central-investigation obstruction, so his deposition cannot be reduced to sex, drinking, or mourning ritual alone. The concept should therefore guide comparison among ritual norms, statutes, accusations, and actual enforcement rather than assume they were identical.

## What Changed

- Created a bounded concept separating Liu Bo's documented succession case from the episode's wider cross-dynastic legal generalizations.
- Made obstruction of investigation an independent aggravating layer rather than treating every consequence as punishment for mourning conduct.
