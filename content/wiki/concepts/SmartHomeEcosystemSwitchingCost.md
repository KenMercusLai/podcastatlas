# Smart Home Ecosystem Switching Cost

## Definition
Smart Home Ecosystem Switching Cost is the hardware, protocol, setup, learning, reliability, and replacement burden a household faces when moving connected devices and automations from one control ecosystem to another.

## Current Synthesis
A smart-home hub competes with an installed system, not with an empty house. Existing sensors, locks, cameras, speakers, appliances, routines, accounts, and household habits make compatibility and reliable control more important than the appeal of one new screen. Standards such as Matter can reduce some integration friction, but they do not automatically equalize device breadth, advanced features, local-market services, or the cost of replacing incompatible equipment.

The burden is cumulative and shared. A migration must work for multiple household members and preserve everyday automations; otherwise an attractive hub becomes an additional interface rather than a simplifying center.

## Key Claims
- Installed devices and working automations create path dependence before a new hub is evaluated.
- Protocol compatibility reduces friction only when required device functions remain available and reliable.
- Local ecosystem breadth can outweigh the industrial design or isolated capabilities of a new controller.
- Multi-user setup, accounts, permissions, and learned household habits add non-hardware switching costs.
- Reusing a tablet or phone as a control panel can be rational when a dedicated hub does not materially improve coordination.

## Evidence
### Installed-base advantage
- [[podcast177-v1-podcast177-v1]] says Chinese households may already rely on Mijia and brand-specific applications, making a move to Apple's home ecosystem a system replacement rather than a single-device purchase.

### Compatibility and control breadth
- [[podcast177-v1-podcast177-v1]] treats Matter support as useful but insufficient without broad device, Bluetooth, low-power, sensor, and automation compatibility.

### Replacement alternatives
- [[podcast177-v1-podcast177-v1]] proposes reusing an older iPad mini as a persistent home-control panel when multi-user identity and deeper integration do not justify a dedicated HomeView purchase.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- The source compares ecosystems through host experience rather than a complete device-compatibility audit.
- A new household or major renovation may face much lower switching cost than a mature installation.
- Strong multi-user identity, privacy, local processing, or reliability gains could justify migration even when hardware replacement is expensive.

## What Changed
- Created an installed-base and household-coordination frame for evaluating smart-home hubs.
