# Cultural Revolution

## Definition

The Cultural Revolution was the decade of upheaval beginning in 1966 in which Mao Zedong mobilized attacks on allegedly revisionist authority and the inherited social order, producing mass persecution, cultural destruction, factional conflict, military intervention, and enduring political trauma.

## Current Synthesis

The source explains the catastrophe through interaction rather than a single cause. [[MaoZedong]] sought to recover central authority after the Great Leap Forward, feared Soviet-style revisionism and posthumous repudiation, and believed revolution required recurring violent renewal. His signal activated youth resentment, class stigma, institutional grievance, xenophobia, anti-traditional ideology, and a personality cult built over decades.

The resulting movement exceeded a controlled purge. [[RedGuards]] attacked teachers, officials, people with stigmatized backgrounds, cultural sites, customs, and everyday objects; victims faced public denunciation, false confession, painful stress positions, torture, killing, and social ruin. Free travel and rallies created national solidarity, while factional conflict and access to weapons drove local escalation. Army intervention ended the classic Red Guard phase by 1969, but coercion, arrest, execution, and elite conflict continued until Mao's death in 1976.

## Key Claims

- Mao's struggle for authority and sincere anti-revisionist ideology reinforced one another.
- Long-built personal supremacy made an attack on party bureaucracy possible even when Mao lacked uncontested control of routine administration.
- Top-down authorization unlocked bottom-up youth rebellion, grievance, class conflict, and factional competition.
- Violence joined attacks on persons and institutions to [[RevolutionaryIconoclasm]] against historical continuity.
- [[MaoistPoliticalReligion]] helped transform political participation into ritualized devotion and moral purification.
- Military suppression changed the movement's form after 1969 but did not end political violence.
- The post-Mao party condemned the catastrophe while preserving Mao's foundational legitimacy.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode is a one-hour national overview and cannot represent every region, institution, faction, victim, or participant. Its casualty estimates remain attributed; Mao's initial intended scope is uncertain; evidence and interpretation concerning Guangxi cannibalism require care; and religious comparison illuminates political ritual without proving a single origin or equivalence. Explaining grievance and emotional appeal does not reduce responsibility for persecution.

## What Changed

- Established a multi-causal account joining elite struggle, ideology, personal cult, youth agency, grievance, and institutional breakdown.
- Distinguished the classic Red Guard phase from the longer decade of coercion.
- Integrated cultural destruction and ritualized devotion into the political explanation.
