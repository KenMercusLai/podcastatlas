# Sociometry

## Definition

Sociometry is the systematic measurement and visual representation of attraction, rejection, preference, and isolation within a group, using relational choices as data about social structure.

## Current Synthesis

[[acting-out]] traces sociometry through [[JacobLevyMoreno|J. L. Moreno]] and [[HelenHallJennings|Helen Hall Jennings's]] study at the [[NewYorkStateTrainingSchoolForGirls|New York State Training School for Girls]]. They asked residents whom they most and least wanted to live with and why, observed work settings, and condensed a claimed 10,000 pages of material into sociograms. The visual form made otherwise unmanageable relational patterns available for interpretation and intervention.

The method's value does not make its representation complete. Nodes and lines can show who chose, rejected, or connected to whom without showing why an institution segregated residents, imposed racialized labor, punished them, or made escape attractive. Sociometry is therefore strongest when relational structure is joined to institutional history, power, and alternative causal explanations.

## Key Claims

- Relational choices can be collected as structured data rather than treated only as anecdote.
- Sociograms make attraction, rejection, isolation, and subgroup structure visually inspectable.
- Whole-community coverage can reveal pathways and positions hidden by individual or aggregate analysis.
- Relational maps can guide organizational intervention, but reported before-and-after change does not by itself prove causal effect.
- A complete network map can remain an incomplete account of the institution producing the network.

## Evidence

### Measurement and visualization

- [[acting-out]] reports preference and rejection questionnaires, stated reasons, workplace observation, and cottage-level sociograms across roughly 500 residents.

### Interpretation and intervention

- [[acting-out]] describes the researchers' network-contagion reading of the escape cluster and the reported decline after relationship-informed cottage reorganization.

### Representational boundary

- [[acting-out]] contrasts the maps' interpersonal detail with their limited treatment of segregation, forced labor, punishment, and abuse.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The supplied episode summary does not reproduce the questionnaire, coding rules, missing-data treatment, sociograms, intervention design, or outcome records. Preference nominations may reflect coercion, cottage assignments, status, fear, opportunity, or institutional rules as well as affinity. The claimed post-reorganization decline is not a controlled estimate, and the Hudson study alone does not establish the later field's full history or current methods.

## What Changed

- Created a method-level synthesis linking relational measurement and visualization to explicit institutional-context limits.
- Distinguished network visibility from causal sufficiency and intervention proof.
