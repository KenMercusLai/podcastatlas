# Tokugawa Foreign-Trade Ambivalence

## Definition

Tokugawa foreign-trade ambivalence is the governing tension between seeking foreign commerce, navigation, shipbuilding, knowledge, and diplomatic options while restricting missionary allegiance and external political leverage.

## Current Synthesis

The William Adams source shows early Tokugawa openness to useful Protestant English and Dutch expertise alongside growing fear of Catholic Iberian missionary networks. Ieyasu's use of Adams and later expulsion of Jesuits belong to the same state-building calculus.

[[277-japan-samurai-and-shoguns-glt3851919583]] strengthens the conclusion that Tokugawa Japan was not simply sealed. European access narrowed sharply, but Dutch trade at Dejima and exchange with China, Korea, and Southeast Asia continued. Restriction was selective and politically governed.

## Key Claims

- Early Tokugawa policy includes genuine openness to trade and technique.
- Mission networks turn commercial contact into a loyalty and governance problem.
- Adams is useful partly because he offers knowledge without Jesuit alignment.
- European restriction coexists with controlled Dutch and continuing Asian exchange.
- Selective contact is more accurate than a complete-isolation model.

## Evidence

- Utility and threat: [[14-wushi-weilian-dahanghai-shidai-de-riben-he-xifang-555771285]] connects Adams's technical role with Ieyasu's assessment of confessional rivalry and later Jesuit expulsion.
- Controlled European channel: [[277-japan-samurai-and-shoguns-glt3851919583]] identifies Dejima as the Dutch exception.
- Wider exchange: [[277-japan-samurai-and-shoguns-glt3851919583]] explicitly retains trade with China, Korea, and Southeast Asia.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

“Risk management” describes the regime's logic but does not validate persecution or minimize coercion. The sources compress changing policy across rulers and do not quantify trade, smuggling, regional variation, or the experience of Christian communities.
