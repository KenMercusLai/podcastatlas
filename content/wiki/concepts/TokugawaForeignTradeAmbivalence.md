# Tokugawa Foreign-Trade Ambivalence

Tokugawa foreign-trade ambivalence is the source's pattern where the early [[TokugawaShogunate]] wants foreign trade, navigation knowledge, shipbuilding, and diplomatic options while also fearing Christianity and foreign political leverage. In [[14-wushi-weilian-dahanghai-shidai-de-riben-he-xifang-555771285]], [[TokugawaIeyasu|Tokugawa Ieyasu]] is open enough to use [[WilliamAdams|William Adams / 三浦按针]], build ships, and discuss trade, yet concerned enough to expel [[Jesuits]] in 1614.

The concept is not simple isolationism. The episode presents restriction as a political choice made after rulers judge that missionary networks and foreign pressure can threaten rule. Adams's career shows the upside of foreign connection; the later withdrawal of English traders and survival of a narrow Dutch channel show the downside calculus winning.

## Key Claims
- Early Tokugawa policy includes genuine openness to trade and technique.
- Christian mission networks turn commercial contact into a loyalty and governance problem.
- Adams is protected because he is useful and not aligned with Jesuit missionary pressure.
- The later [[SakokuDutchWindow]] reflects selective risk management rather than a complete absence of exchange.
