# 江湖生存焦虑 / Jianghu Survival Anxiety

## Definition
Jianghu survival anxiety is the episode's explanation for why Jianghu culture prizes force, money, and tight dependency: people who feel unprotected seek security through stronger violence, larger resources, and control over potentially threatening companions.

## Current Synthesis
[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-483-3-lishi-jiemi-hun-zhichang-de-jianghu-guiju-lg6vr0avzvtqireys3hhp7trwrsl]] treats Jianghu as a survival culture and parasitic culture formed by weak individual security. Its people do not merely romanticize loyalty; they try to reduce fear through circles, leaders, wealth, and violence. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-483-2-lishi-jiemi-hun-zhichang-de-jianghu-guiju-ltukp3q2cg7p5egxqte7nuseenaw]] adds the material underside: people without stable work, kinship incorporation, or ordinary economic standing may be pushed by hunger into craft, deception, intimidation, or violence. Together, the sources make Jianghu order simultaneously protective and dangerous.

The episode extends the logic upward into imperial politics by briefly invoking [[LiuBang|刘邦]] and Zhu Yuanzhang. The point is not a detailed biography of either ruler, but a structural analogy: even after gaining supreme power, a leader shaped by insecurity may still view old companions and founding brothers as unresolved threats.

## Key Claims
- Survival anxiety turns Jianghu organization into a search for protection, resources, and backing.
- Violence and wealth are treated as security instruments because weak actors fear being consumed by stronger ones.
- Brotherhood language does not remove insecurity; it can intensify the danger when former companions become rivals or witnesses.
- Hunger and livelihood instability can strip Jianghu of romance by making survival more urgent than etiquette or moral consistency.
- The episode reads post-victory purges as a continuation of Jianghu insecurity at the top of the hierarchy.
- This concept should remain source-scoped because both episodes are short thematic commentaries with brief historical examples.

## Evidence
### Force And Wealth As Security
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-483-3-lishi-jiemi-hun-zhichang-de-jianghu-guiju-lg6vr0avzvtqireys3hhp7trwrsl]] says Jianghu people develop violence and pursue wealth from inner insecurity, believing survival requires sufficient force and resources.

### Group Dependence
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-483-3-lishi-jiemi-hun-zhichang-de-jianghu-guiju-lg6vr0avzvtqireys3hhp7trwrsl]] describes Jianghu circles as built around common interest, private desire, exchange, brotherhood rhetoric, and opportunistic personal dependency.

### Hunger And Marginality
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-483-2-lishi-jiemi-hun-zhichang-de-jianghu-guiju-ltukp3q2cg7p5egxqte7nuseenaw]] describes marginal people outside stable livelihood and social order as vulnerable to hunger-driven violence, predation, and improvisational survival.

### Ruler Analogy
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-483-3-lishi-jiemi-hun-zhichang-de-jianghu-guiju-lg6vr0avzvtqireys3hhp7trwrsl]] uses Liu Bang and Zhu Yuanzhang as short examples of rulers who, after taking power, still need to eliminate former brothers before feeling secure.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is drawn from two short interpretive episodes. It should not flatten all Jianghu association into predation, since related pages show craft, mutual aid, mediation, and reputation constraints as well. The Zhu Yuanzhang comparison is not yet integrated into an entity page and should stay source-scoped.

## What Changed
- Created the concept from Hanji 483-3 to capture the episode's anxiety-based explanation of Jianghu violence, money, and post-victory threat perception.
- Added Hanji 483-2's hunger-and-livelihood layer for marginal Jianghu survival.
