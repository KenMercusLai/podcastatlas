# Property–Equity Wealth Shift

## Definition
Property–equity wealth shift is a change in household wealth formation and young-adult expectations from owner-occupied or speculative real estate toward renting, financial accounts, and equity exposure.

## Current Synthesis
The episode argues that high purchase prices and post-2022 interest rates have weakened the automatic financial case for home ownership just as recent equity gains and easier investment access have made stocks more salient. The useful judgment is comparative rather than triumphalist: a home supplies shelter and control but concentrates capital and often adds leverage and carrying cost; equities are divisible and liquid but can be highly valued and fall quickly. Government tax, renter-protection, foreign-purchase, and landlord rules can also change the relative return after a purchase.

## Key Claims
- High mortgage rates can reduce affordability and leveraged returns even when headline house prices remain elevated.
- Slower expected house-price growth weakens the assumption that ownership will reproduce older generations' wealth path.
- Younger adults' growing use of investment accounts creates a practical alternative to saving only for a deposit.
- Retreat by small landlords and flippers can reflect narrower margins, policy change, financing cost, and weak resale demand rather than one universal housing cycle.
- A primary home should not be evaluated only as an investment because it also provides consumption, autonomy, stability, and location value.
- Stocks are not a riskless replacement: high valuations and faster drawdowns can make recent relative performance a poor forecast.

- Wealth composition and participation: [[80e710d10d18ebe74145f9a83b5ea1e8-80e710d10d18ebe74145f9a83b5ea1e8mp3aid6ae852a798a5c8b2e347e723chidd556eb54-6160-4]] reports a larger recent contribution from stocks than property to global household wealth, U.S. equities surpassing real estate as a wealth share, and higher investment-account use among 25-year-olds.
- Housing economics: [[80e710d10d18ebe74145f9a83b5ea1e8-80e710d10d18ebe74145f9a83b5ea1e8mp3aid6ae852a798a5c8b2e347e723chidd556eb54-6160-4]] links expensive homes, higher rates, and slower expected appreciation to weaker purchase arithmetic.
- Landlord and policy pressure: [[80e710d10d18ebe74145f9a83b5ea1e8-80e710d10d18ebe74145f9a83b5ea1e8mp3aid6ae852a798a5c8b2e347e723chidd556eb54-6160-4]] reports declining flipping margins, landlord sales, foreign-purchase restrictions, renter protections, and weaker Toronto condominium sales.
- Two-sided risk: [[80e710d10d18ebe74145f9a83b5ea1e8-80e710d10d18ebe74145f9a83b5ea1e8mp3aid6ae852a798a5c8b2e347e723chidd556eb54-6160-4]] preserves the use value of homes while noting expensive equity valuations and faster stock-market crashes.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode uses cross-country aggregates and selected markets, so it does not establish that renting and buying have the same relative value everywhere. Taxes, rent, mortgage structure, leverage, maintenance, transaction costs, local supply, tenure horizon, portfolio behavior, and non-financial preferences can reverse an individual decision. Reported wealth shares, price histories, valuation ratios, participation changes, forecasts, and landlord trends require primary-data verification before quantitative reuse.

## What Changed
- Established a comparative frame for the weakening default status of home ownership among younger adults.
- Preserved housing's use value while separating it from expected financial return.
- Added equity valuation and crash speed as qualifications to recent stock-market outperformance.
