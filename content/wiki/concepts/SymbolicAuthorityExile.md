## Definition

象征权威的政治流放 / Political exile of symbolic authority is the removal of a defeated figure from the social and geographic networks where title, ritual, memory, or reputation can still mobilize people after direct governing or military power has disappeared.

## Current Synthesis

Episode 41 supplies two contrasting cases. [[YeMingchen|Ye Mingchen]] had exercised substantial provincial authority but lost it with Guangzhou; British officials nevertheless treated his local prestige as a threat to occupation and avoided nearby Chinese-majority destinations. [[BahadurShahII|Bahadur Shah II]] had possessed little operational authority before or during the 1857 uprising, but rebels used his Mughal title and Britain treated that same prestige as politically dangerous.

Exile therefore does not measure prior control. It manages future coordination and commemoration by severing a person from audiences, retainers, sacred places, and claims of continuity. The afterlives also show the limit of the tactic: Ye's captivity generated loyalist and satirical stories, while attempted suppression of Bahadur Shah's grave did not prevent later veneration.

## Key Claims

- Practical power and symbolic authority can diverge sharply.
- Defeated officials or sovereigns may remain politically dangerous because others can organize around title, dignity, or remembered legitimacy.
- Distant confinement weakens access to local networks while avoiding the immediate risks of execution.
- Choice of destination can reflect demographic and political calculations, not merely prison logistics.
- Removal can suppress coordination more effectively than memory; exile narratives and graves may acquire new symbolic power.
- The mechanism explains a colonial tactic but does not establish the captive's innocence, popularity, or actual capacity to lead resistance.

## Evidence

- Ye's removal: [[41-tongbei-yingguo-fulu-tong-kesi-yixiang-liangguang-zongdu-yemingchen-yu-mowoer-modai-huangdi-lt2jwc0wbce-shs0q2zdzvygqxu3|episode 41]] says British officials feared Ye's continuing Guangzhou prestige and rejected Singapore or Malaya partly because of their Chinese populations before sending him to Calcutta.
- Bahadur Shah's deportation: [[41-tongbei-yingguo-fulu-tong-kesi-yixiang-liangguang-zongdu-yemingchen-yu-mowoer-modai-huangdi-lt2jwc0wbce-shs0q2zdzvygqxu3|episode 41]] says Britain chose exile over execution because the last Mughal emperor retained popular standing after Delhi's recapture.
- Divergent prior authority: [[41-tongbei-yingguo-fulu-tong-kesi-yixiang-liangguang-zongdu-yemingchen-yu-mowoer-modai-huangdi-lt2jwc0wbce-shs0q2zdzvygqxu3|episode 41]] joins an active provincial governor to an already powerless emperor, showing that exile responds to symbolic risk rather than one level of former control.
- Memory limits: [[41-tongbei-yingguo-fulu-tong-kesi-yixiang-liangguang-zongdu-yemingchen-yu-mowoer-modai-huangdi-lt2jwc0wbce-shs0q2zdzvygqxu3|episode 41]] contrasts disputed “Sea-borne Su Wu” stories around Ye with secret burial and later shrine veneration around Bahadur Shah.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept currently rests on one comparative podcast account and two British colonial cases. Security, diplomacy, punishment, humanitarian restraint, and administrative convenience may also shape exile decisions. Claims about popularity and official motives are especially difficult to infer, and later commemoration does not prove that either captive could have organized effective resistance at the time.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from the shared exile mechanism while preserving the protagonists' different degrees of agency and former power.
