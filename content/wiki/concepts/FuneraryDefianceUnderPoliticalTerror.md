## Definition

Funerary defiance under political terror is the pattern where mourning, guarding, collecting, or returning a body becomes public resistance because a powerholder has turned death rites or corpse exposure into intimidation.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-830-donghan-ligu-wo-sui-si-yourong-lt-j7n9gotiw-negqxojoacf6ji|Hanji 830]] supplies the institutional case. [[LiangJiLateHan|梁冀]] displays the bodies of [[LiGuLateHan|李固]] and [[DuQiaoLateHan|杜乔]] and forbids mourning. [[GuoLiangLiGuStudent|郭亮]], [[DongBanNanyangLateHan|董班]], and [[YangKuangDuQiaoRetainer|杨匡]] mourn or guard the bodies despite threats; [[EmpressDowagerLiangNaLateHan|梁妠 / 梁太后]] then pardons them and permits return burial. Their acts recover ritual dignity and force a surviving authority to acknowledge a boundary.

A smaller friendship case appears in [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1090-ta-ceng-maosi-cangxiong-wei-mu-kuxue-zhen-shi-buxiao-zhi-ren-ma-lg-z9lyx_y6dxbopsfrqxoyymdjj|Hanji 1090]]. After [[KongRong|孔融]] and his household are executed and others fear approaching, [[ZhiXiLateHan|脂习]] publicly mourns Kong Rong's exposed body. [[CaoCao|曹操]] spares him. The result is narrower than return burial, but it supports the same judgment: terror can isolate a body temporarily without fully controlling the dead person's reputation or the survivor's ritual loyalty.

## Key Claims

- A mourning ban or fear around an exposed body turns ordinary grief into a political test.
- Public corpse exposure seeks to control memory as well as physical remains.
- Defiant mourners protect the dead person's reputation by refusing to let intimidation define the final public image.
- Mourning can remain symbolically effective even when it does not reverse a sentence or weaken the regime materially.
- Pardon or non-punishment by a powerholder marks a practical limit on how far symbolic dissent will be pursued.
- Friendship, student duty, and retainer loyalty can all supply the relationship that makes funerary risk worth taking.

## Evidence

- Intimidation and mourning ban: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-830-donghan-ligu-wo-sui-si-yourong-lt-j7n9gotiw-negqxojoacf6ji|Hanji 830]] says Liang Ji displays Li Gu and Du Qiao's bodies and threatens mourners.
- Collective ritual resistance: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-830-donghan-ligu-wo-sui-si-yourong-lt-j7n9gotiw-negqxojoacf6ji|Hanji 830]] says Guo Liang and Dong Ban mourn Li Gu, while Yang Kuang guards Du Qiao's body for twelve days.
- Ritual repair: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-830-donghan-ligu-wo-sui-si-yourong-lt-j7n9gotiw-negqxojoacf6ji|Hanji 830]] says Liang Na pardons the mourners and permits the bodies to return home for burial.
- Friendship mourning: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1090-ta-ceng-maosi-cangxiong-wei-mu-kuxue-zhen-shi-buxiao-zhi-ren-ma-lg-z9lyx_y6dxbopsfrqxoyymdjj|Hanji 1090]] says Zhi Xi approaches Kong Rong's exposed body and cries when others are afraid.
- Bounded tolerance: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1090-ta-ceng-maosi-cangxiong-wei-mu-kuxue-zhen-shi-buxiao-zhi-ren-ma-lg-z9lyx_y6dxbopsfrqxoyymdjj|Hanji 1090]] says Cao Cao spares Zhi Xi, which the host interprets as toleration of grief that does not constitute a power threat.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The acts do not overturn Liang Ji or Cao Cao, reverse the executions, or establish institutional reform. Hanji 830 provides an explicit mourning ban and later return burial; Hanji 1090 provides fear, exposed remains, mourning, and survival but does not establish the same formal ban or burial recovery. Exact dialogue, exclusivity claims about who mourned, and powerholder motives remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Hanji 1090 adds Zhi Xi's friendship-based mourning of Kong Rong and Cao Cao's decision to spare him.
- The synthesis now distinguishes full ritual recovery in Hanji 830 from narrower symbolic mourning in Hanji 1090.
- The concept's relationship base expands from students and retainers to old friendship.
