# Iran Sanctions

## Definition
Iran sanctions are the long-running U.S. and international restrictions on Iran's trade, assets, oil revenue, and financial access. The wiki treats them as a policy system that combines dollar banking pressure, oil-export limits, secondary sanctions, relief credibility, evasion channels, and domestic economic pain.

## Current Synthesis
The strongest current synthesis is that sanctions can create real leverage over [[Iran]] only when the coercive mechanism is operationally tight and politically credible. Earlier source notes show the 2010-era campaign gaining force through [[DollarFinancialSanctions]], oil-import pressure, and global bank risk, then helping produce the 2015 nuclear deal inside [[USIranNuclearDiplomacy]]. They also show why rollback is fragile: [[SanctionsOvercompliance]] persisted after relief was promised, and the 2018 U.S. withdrawal damaged the credibility of future concessions.

[[even-more-maximum-pressure-sanctions-on-iran-6a8d6a9b66c4a1e81a4c86a9|Even-more-maximum pressure: sanctions on Iran]] updates the current branch by returning sanctions to the center of U.S.-Iran policy after renewed conflict. [[ScottBessent|Scott Bessent / 贝森特]] presents "Operation Economic Outcast" as a major financial offensive, but [[FraserMcIlwraith]] reads the concrete announcement as narrower than the rhetoric. The episode says the coercive channel is still serious because U.S. blockade enforcement has crushed reported oil income, the rial has weakened sharply, and third-country compliance pressure now runs through [[China]] and the [[UnitedArabEmirates]]. Its qualification is equally important: Washington may avoid the harshest moves against large Chinese banks because escalation with China would be costly, and Iran can respond through regional bases, Gulf energy infrastructure, or the [[StraitOfHormuz]].

## Key Claims
- Sanctions have the most bargaining force when they join clear demands, dollar-bank access, oil-export pressure, and credible future relief.
- The 2015 nuclear deal remains the wiki's clearest Iran case where sanctions contributed to a diplomatic result, while later U.S. reversal weakened the value of promised relief.
- The civilian burden is not incidental: the sanctions mechanism works partly through inflation, currency collapse, reduced purchasing power, and reduced access to ordinary goods.
- Iran's long exposure to sanctions limits the political shock value of new threats, but concentrated oil-revenue and banking pressure can still hurt state capacity.
- The current "maximum pressure" branch depends heavily on third-country enforcement, especially Chinese oil purchasing and UAE-based trade and financial channels.
- Sanctions pressure can create escalation risk if Iran retaliates against cooperating states, U.S. bases, energy infrastructure, or shipping confidence in the Gulf.

## Evidence
- Historical leverage and relief credibility: [[iran-protests-and-sanctions]] traces sanctions from the 1979 asset freeze through the 2010-era oil and dollar-bank campaign, the 2015 nuclear deal, and the later credibility damage after U.S. withdrawal.
- Civilian harm and insider effects: [[iran-protests-and-sanctions]] links sanctions to food-price pressure, currency collapse, ordinary hardship, and gains for politically connected insiders such as the [[IslamicRevolutionaryGuardCorps]].
- Evasion and payment workarounds: [[how-to-get-through-the-strait-of-hormuz]] adds an alleged crypto-paid [[StraitOfHormuz]] toll, showing how maritime control and [[SanctionsEvasionCryptoPayments]] can appear when ordinary payment rails are constrained.
- Regime legitimacy and escalation context: [[ep251-yilang-kunju-sishinian-zhanlue-xuanze-de-daijia-yu-xianshi-lp8vyonyugtd2ozymgof-mim9zkd]] argues that sanctions, fiscal collapse, and failed proxy investments make it harder for Iranian leadership to admit strategic failure.
- Current maximum-pressure mechanics: [[even-more-maximum-pressure-sanctions-on-iran-6a8d6a9b66c4a1e81a4c86a9]] adds "Operation Economic Outcast", oil-income collapse under blockade enforcement, UAE compliance pressure, Chinese-bank escalation limits, and Iran's possible retaliation options.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Sanctions are not a clean substitute for war. The wiki's sources repeatedly stress that they can impoverish civilians, strengthen insiders, and make relief hard to deliver even when policy formally changes. The latest source also distinguishes rhetoric from implementation: Bessent's "Economic D-Day" language suggested a sweeping escalation, but the concrete package was closer to designations, threats, and pressure on enablers. The China constraint matters because sanctioning major Chinese financial institutions could disrupt U.S.-China diplomacy and global banking. Iran may also read a pivot back to sanctions as evidence that the United States has less appetite for further military pressure.

## What Changed
- Added "Operation Economic Outcast" as the latest sanctions-pressure frame after renewed U.S.-Iran conflict.
- Added third-country enforcement through Chinese oil purchasing and UAE trade-finance channels as the current operational bottleneck.
- Qualified the idea that maximum-pressure rhetoric alone changes Iranian behavior; implementation and escalation constraints now carry more weight.
- Strengthened the link between sanctions pressure and Gulf escalation risk through bases, energy facilities, and the Strait of Hormuz.
