## Definition

时绌举赢 / untimely extravagance is the political-ethics pattern where visible pleasure, palace maintenance, prestige building, ritual display, or luxury acceptance becomes wrong because the surrounding condition of war, disaster, public burden, or fragile legitimacy cannot bear it.

## Current Synthesis

The concept does not condemn all building, ritual, beauty, or court display. Its stable test is circumstance. Zhouji 28 supplies the phrase through [[HanZhaohou|韩昭侯]] building a high gate after Qin attack and drought; Hanji 178 part 2 keeps the ambiguity through [[XiaoHe|萧何]]'s defense of [[WeiyangPalace|未央宫]] as imperial authority while [[SimaGuang|司马光]] criticizes the timing against poverty and war. Zhouji 42 extends the rule to diplomatic luxury: the ivory bed offered to [[Mengchangjun|孟尝君 / 田文]] is dangerous because accepting it would reset reputation and reciprocity expectations.

The late-Han sources make the concept sharper because extravagance is repeatedly financed through public burden. Hanji 870 adds the earliest current Huan-era variant: [[XunShuangLateHan|荀爽]] criticizes thousands of palace women as a drain on commoners after a solar eclipse, while [[EmperorHuanOfHan|汉桓帝]]'s Buddhist-Laozi ritual display sits beside famine, office churn, and frontier crisis. Hanji 917 through Hanji 936 then show Emperor Ling turning palace women, gardens, palace-market play, private stores, towers, hunting, palace repair, bronze statues, mechanical devices, land purchases, and eunuch-household storage into visible signs that ruler desire is consuming state capacity during public stress.

## Key Claims

- Timing means political circumstance, not calendar date.
- Luxury becomes politically damaging when war, disaster, famine, epidemic, rebellion, frontier pressure, or household hardship should dominate attention.
- Harem, palace, garden, and ritual spending are especially exposed when commoners finance the display through taxes, corvee, requisitions, or office-entry fees.
- Court extravagance often travels with feedback failure: advisers name the burden, but rulers and favorites recode, ignore, or only temporarily repair the criticism.
- The pattern covers display and protocol as well as construction; a gift can be untimely if accepting it damages reputation or sets an excessive standard.
- Extravagance can still have a public-order argument, as Xiao He's Weiyang Palace defense shows, but that argument remains contestable when state capacity is thin.
- The late-Han variant is cumulative: repeated displays make private desire visible as governance failure before wider collapse.

## Evidence

- Circumstance test: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-28-hanzhaohou-de-kuaile-ni-xiangxiang-budao-ln2tbxniz4kvwogbwp32exmdokz8|Zhouji 28]] says Han Zhaohou's high gate becomes wrong because Qin attack and drought define the moment, and [[QuYijiu|屈宜臼]] treats "time" as circumstance.
- Contestable palace grandeur: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-178-maodun-weihe-yao-fangguo-liubang-zhe-cai-shi-zhenxiang-2-loluh53iyrzo4ucf3ttzmn3-otkv|Hanji 178 part 2]] contrasts Xiao He's authority-and-future-cost defense of Weiyang Palace with Sima Guang's criticism of construction during poverty and war.
- Luxury protocol: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-42-gei-mengchangjun-ti-yijian-de-houguo-lr1jp0tpiaboaca3j5-yvyhyn4vf|Zhouji 42]] says Mengchang Jun refuses the Chu ivory bed after advice that accepting it would harm his reputation for righteousness and create an excessive gift standard.
- Huan-era harem and ritual display: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-870-gudai-tizhinei-buganle-meiyou-tuilu-lpwbniu9ifpt6cp4ip9czqwsh5xd|Hanji 870]] says Xun Shuang criticizes thousands of palace women and commoner burden after an eclipse, while the same episode records Huan's elaborate Buddha-Laozi sacrifice amid famine and frontier pressure.
- Palace women and commoner burden: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-917-ruhe-xunsu-zhaodao-shengzhi-jiaxin-de-pojudian-lk2zt5c3onvui9a4ppbowshy66tg|Hanji 917]] says [[LyuQiangLateHan|吕强]] contrasts thousands of palace women and daily provisions with households forced by taxes and labor to sell grain cheaply and suffer hunger and cold.
- Garden project and classicized flattery: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-919-xiang-huode-hao-xian-tikai-shanliang-de-ziji-lrqxfzir4bqxt0v3ccvfef8faade|Hanji 919]] says Emperor Ling's new gardens would destroy fields and displace households, while attendants try to justify the project through public-pleasure precedents.
- Palace-market play: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-920-mingwu-zhege-daoli-caineng-zhuan-daqian-lqmabg3bpyz6ct2v4arx-iun57ws|Hanji 920]] says palace shops, palace women trading and fighting, official-costumed dogs, and donkey-cart play appear beside rebellion, frontier pressure, hail, eclipse, palace fire, and office churn.
- Private-store extraction: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-921-jinqian-ru-shui-que-ze-ke-si-tan-ze-yan-si-lj1dk4qdgcago9elr7ctuvre3zic|Hanji 921]] says Emperor Ling's private-treasury demand turns all-under-heaven rhetoric into added local requisition, transport, and fee burdens.
- Tower and travel during disorder: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-922-ruhe-zai-fanchen-zhong-xiulian-qingshang-lsxiqneyyeod6aeeqxuhs517pn1x|Hanji 922]] places Ling's 400-chi tower, hunting, and travel beside epidemic, drought, palace fire, flood, landslide, and the Bandun Man rebellion.
- Palace repair finance: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-932-weishenme-ren-yue-hao-yue-nan-dang-daguan-lvwzel782wcxs1lpt3rg2vmkdy29|Hanji 932]] says palace repair and bronze statues are funded through added per-mu money, material requisitions, inspection underpricing, provincial add-ons, and office-entry charges.
- Hoarding and mechanical display: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-936-luanshi-xian-sha-shengmu-xin-zhehua-dui-ma-lml-kuyc7yys2ijh2kt46pwnugar|Hanji 936]] says Emperor Ling stores money and silk in West Garden and eunuch households, buys land and builds in his old fief, and orders palace construction and devices while rebellion, frontier danger, and hunger remain active.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept is a timing and burden test, not an anti-luxury rule. Xiao He's Weiyang Palace argument shows that grandeur can be defended as authority-building or future cost control, and ritual display may have public legitimacy functions. The concept marks the point where those arguments weaken because visible consumption sits against scarcity, coercive financing, ignored remonstrance, or a public/private split in which ruler pleasure receives priority over state repair.
