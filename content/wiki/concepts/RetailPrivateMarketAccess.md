# Retail Private-Market Access

## Definition
Retail private-market access is the widening set of broker, secondary, fund, special-purpose vehicle, and IPO-allocation channels through which ordinary investors can gain exposure to high-growth companies before or near their transition to broad public trading.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources treat access as a continuum rather than an unconditional democratization good. Private-company secondaries and regulated products can give employees, venture funds, and retail investors liquidity or earlier exposure, while direct IPO allocations through brokers can narrow the gap between accredited buyers and ordinary investors. The newest [[Anthropic]] episode reports that [[Robinhood]] and [[Fidelity]] helped more retail investors obtain IPO shares and that product recognition, prestige, and fear of missing out increased demand.

Broader access changes who can participate; it does not improve the underlying price. Famous names, scarcity, thin floats, layered fees, lockups, opaque marks, and first-day excitement can transfer risk from insiders and earlier investors to buyers with less information and weaker liquidity. The relevant test is therefore whether access arrives with comprehensible valuation, fee, concentration, transfer, and holding-period constraints.

## Key Claims
- Retail access now spans private secondaries, SPVs, diversified private-company products, direct IPO allocations, and broad public-index ownership.
- Earlier access can broaden ownership and provide liquidity, but it also exposes more households to opaque marks, long lockups, thin floats, and valuation resets.
- Brokerage distribution can make a famous IPO easier to buy without making it suitably priced or diversified.
- Product recognition, prestige, and FOMO can amplify first-day demand independently of audited long-run economics.
- Diversified baskets may be more suitable than concentrated single-company exposure for many investors, though fees and underlying marks still matter.
- Useful democratization requires disclosure of valuation method, fee layers, transfer limits, lockups, concentration, liquidity, and loss capacity.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are investor and technology commentary rather than brokerage allocation records, prospectuses, customer-level suitability studies, fee audits, or realized-return datasets. Reported SpaceX and Anthropic offering chronology, allocation breadth, valuation, float, and demand remain source-attributed. “Access” can mean the legal ability to buy, a small allocation, indirect fund exposure, or meaningful ownership; the sources do not measure these consistently. Accreditation reform, lower minimums, and broker distribution may improve opportunity, but they do not remove adverse selection, illiquidity, fee stacking, concentration, promotional framing, or the possibility that insiders are selling into retail enthusiasm.
