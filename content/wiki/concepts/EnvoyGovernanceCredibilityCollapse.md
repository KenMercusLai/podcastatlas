## Definition

使团治理失控与外交信用崩塌 / envoy governance credibility collapse is the pattern in which rapid mission expansion outruns personnel screening, financial accountability, provisioning, and security, causing repeated low-quality contact to destroy the trust earlier diplomacy created.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-357-1-zhangqian-sihou-xihan-waijiao-luanxiang-lingren-zhazhe-lrjdmvyvoswgfduajch9om9r-b6b|Hanji 357-1]] grounds the concept in the contrast between [[ZhangQianWesternHan|张骞]] and the missions following him. Zhang Qian's name and Bowang Hou title carry trust, but broad authorization lets poorly screened applicants recruit large parties for long journeys with weak oversight. Some reportedly appropriate gifts, sell goods, make unreliable claims, and arrive without sustainable supply arrangements.

The receiving states' incentive structure compounds the sending court's management failure. Western Regions polities provision nearby [[Xiongnu|匈奴]] envoys because retaliation appears quickly deliverable, whereas distant Han missions must often pay their own way. Hanji 357-2 also argues that profit opportunities attract unsuitable participants and that Wudi favors mission scale and spectacular reception without applying comparable quality control.

The combined damage is cumulative. Local communities move from welcoming exchange to doubting envoys and refusing to feed large recurring parties; scarcity then produces conflict among the visitors themselves. Gifts and visible abundance may raise expectations of extraction rather than trust. At corridor chokepoints, distrust combines with Xiongnu pressure and turns into obstruction and raiding. Han's later cavalry sorties and commandery expansion address security and logistics, but neither source shows that force or spectacle alone repairs credibility already lost.

## Key Claims

- A trusted predecessor's reputation can create access, but borrowed reputation decays when later representatives do not meet the same standard.
- Mission volume is a governance variable: more delegations can reduce influence when screening, oversight, and host capacity do not scale with them.
- Diplomatic gifts and trade goods are credibility instruments, so appropriation, resale, and inferior quality damage both the mission and the sending state.
- Provisioning failure externalizes mission costs onto host communities and can turn hospitality into resistance.
- Profit opportunity changes the applicant pool, so mission expansion requires screening and incentive design as well as authorization.
- Repeated misconduct changes the meaning of later contact: envoys who arrive under a trusted banner can eventually be read as extractive or deceptive.
- Military reach shapes whether hosts fear retaliation, but protection and corridor infrastructure do not by themselves restore relational trust.

Accountability and supply failure:

Escalation and state response:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-357-1-zhangqian-sihou-xihan-waijiao-luanxiang-lingren-zhazhe-lrjdmvyvoswgfduajch9om9r-b6b|Hanji 357-1]] says Loulan and Gushi/Cheshi obstruct or raid missions, after which Han sends cavalry and expands commandery-settlement support in the northwest.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The two sources are compact popular-history accounts and do not isolate misconduct from other causes. Local resource limits, large mission sizes, Xiongnu pressure, corridor geography, Han expansion, and small-state survival incentives all contribute to resistance. Hanji 357-2's claim that lavish displays taught foreign states to seek material gain is a host inference rather than direct testimony. The concept therefore identifies a credibility mechanism, not a complete explanation for Han-Western Regions conflict. It also does not imply that every later envoy was corrupt or that military protection is irrelevant; the narrower claim is that security cannot substitute for trustworthy personnel, sustainable logistics, and accountable exchange.
