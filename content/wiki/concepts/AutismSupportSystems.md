# Autism Support Systems / 自闭症支持体系

## Definition
Autism support systems are the episode's institutional and community supports around autistic people and their families, including early intervention, inclusive education, therapy services, transition planning, healthcare funding, employment, housing, translation access, and rights advocacy.

## Current Synthesis
The source argues that autism support cannot remain a private family project. Families still do daily care, but schools, public programs, therapists, disability law, healthcare financing, community organizations, and employers shape whether autistic people can communicate, study, work, live, and participate with dignity. The U.S. examples are not presented as perfect, but they show what system layers can exist beyond cure-seeking and parental endurance.

The episode also stresses unequal access. Chinese American families may value services and fight for children, but language barriers, special-education paperwork, fragmented funding stages, and reluctance to assert rights can reduce what support they actually receive.

## Key Claims
- Support systems should begin before school through early-intervention services and continue through special education, transition planning, adult care, employment, and housing.
- Inclusive education matters because disability support should happen in the least isolating appropriate setting.
- Funding and jurisdiction change across age stages, so families need planning before services shift or end.
- Language access is a rights issue when families cannot read plans, request translation, or advocate inside school systems.
- Community employment and integrated housing examples show support as social participation, not only treatment or supervision.

## Evidence
- Early and school services - [[cong-jiyin-bianji-nvtong-zhisi-shuoqi-zibizheng-bushi-xuyao-bei-xiufu-de-cuowu-lg7dofyeqv-7noq9w74oh6hsyk4b]] says U.S. 0-to-3 early intervention can include speech and occupational therapy, and New York can provide behavioral intervention and public special education.
- Legal and educational principle - [[cong-jiyin-bianji-nvtong-zhisi-shuoqi-zibizheng-bushi-xuyao-bei-xiufu-de-cuowu-lg7dofyeqv-7noq9w74oh6hsyk4b]] names IDEA and least restrictive environment as protections for disabled children in U.S. public education.
- Transition and adult support - [[cong-jiyin-bianji-nvtong-zhisi-shuoqi-zibizheng-bushi-xuyao-bei-xiufu-de-cuowu-lg7dofyeqv-7noq9w74oh6hsyk4b]] describes post-school planning, Medicaid-related funding, group living, and shared-support possibilities after families can no longer provide all care.
- Community inclusion - [[cong-jiyin-bianji-nvtong-zhisi-shuoqi-zibizheng-bushi-xuyao-bei-xiufu-de-cuowu-lg7dofyeqv-7noq9w74oh6hsyk4b]] mentions cafes employing people with intellectual disabilities and integrated residential communities with gardens and small businesses.
- Access barriers - [[cong-jiyin-bianji-nvtong-zhisi-shuoqi-zibizheng-bushi-xuyao-bei-xiufu-de-cuowu-lg7dofyeqv-7noq9w74oh6hsyk4b]] says Chinese American families can face language barriers, translation gaps, fragmented age-based funding, and under-assertion of rights.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source gives comparative examples rather than a full policy map. U.S. supports vary by state and locality, and the episode itself notes that New York and California may be stronger than many other places. China-facing support-system details are raised as a gap rather than fully mapped.
