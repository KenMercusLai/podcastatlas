# Sacral Queenship and Regency

## Definition

Sacral queenship and regency is a framework for female political authority grounded in royal marriage, dynastic maternity, consecration, intercession, and the right to govern for an absent or underage ruler.

## Current Synthesis

The sixth- and tenth-century cases show elite women acting through recognizable offices and expectations rather than only exceptional personality. In the Ottonian setting, anointing gave queens a public religious status, while intercession, mercy, care, charity, and access to the ruler created routine authority. In the Merovingian setting, royal marriage and maternity let [[Fredegund]] and [[Brunhilde]] protect minor heirs, negotiate with kings and nobles, and exercise regency across rival kingdoms.

Regency converted that standing into government during succession crisis, but it did not create independent or secure sovereignty. Fredegund's appeal to Guntram, Brunhilde's repeated reliance on descendants, and both women's need for aristocratic and military backing show authority operating through a male dynastic line. At the same time, Fredegund's battlefield leadership and Brunhilde's treaty-making show that the office could extend beyond pious intercession.

[[Theophano]] and [[AdelaideOfItaly]] show that multiple legitimate female roles could compete over who directed the court and protected succession. The Fredegund-Brunhilde feud extends that rivalry across kingdoms and generations while also demonstrating that two women could govern simultaneously.

## Key Claims

- Consecration made queenship a public and sacral office.
- Intercession and mercy were political functions, not merely private virtues.
- Royal marriage and dynastic motherhood could support formal government during a minority.
- Regency could include diplomacy, coercion, military command, and territorial government as well as court intercession.
- Female authority was institutional enough to recur across kingdoms, generations, and the death of an individual queen.
- Rivalry among elite women does not negate the wider legitimacy of their offices.
- Dependence on heirs and elite backing made regency powerful but structurally precarious.

## Evidence

- Public office - [[460-the-empress-of-the-apocalypse-glt9103397168]] describes anointing, intercession, charity, piety, and care as expected queenly duties.
- Dynastic regency - [[460-the-empress-of-the-apocalypse-glt9103397168]] credits Theophano with preserving Otto III and Adelaide with taking over after 991; [[521-warlords-of-the-west-killer-queens-part-2-glt5666966864|Part 2]] presents Fredegund and Brunhilde governing for sons and later descendants.
- Governmental range - [[521-warlords-of-the-west-killer-queens-part-2-glt5666966864|Part 2]] connects regency to treaty, alliance, revolt, punishment, and battlefield command.
- Contest - [[460-the-empress-of-the-apocalypse-glt9103397168]] presents Theophano and Adelaide competing for court influence, while [[521-warlords-of-the-west-killer-queens-part-2-glt5666966864|Part 2]] follows rival queens ruling separate Frankish territories.
- Structural vulnerability - [[521-warlords-of-the-west-killer-queens-part-2-glt5666966864|Part 2]] shows child death, male guardianship, noble defection, and military defeat repeatedly constraining female rule.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources focus on exceptional elite women and do not establish the reach of female authority below royal courts. Formal status did not eliminate misogyny, xenophobia, faction, or dependence on dynastic proximity. The Ottonian “century of women” label is an interpretive generalization, while the Merovingian account is filtered through [[GregoryOfTours|Gregory of Tours]] and a dramatic feud narrative. These cases demonstrate institutional possibilities, not a uniform medieval system or unconstrained female sovereignty.

## What Changed

- Extended the framework from consecrated Ottonian office to Merovingian marriage, maternity, and simultaneous regency.
- Added diplomacy, coercion, and military command to the demonstrated range of queen-regent government.
- Made dependence on heirs, guardians, nobles, and military survival a central qualification.
