## Definition

口才替代能力式识人偏差 / eloquence bias in official selection is the error of treating a fluent, confident, or detailed answer as sufficient evidence that a person deserves a broader office, without separately testing role fit, judgment, record, and the quality of information reaching the ruler.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-214-tiemian-zhangshizhi-dui-huangdi-lan-taizi-1-lqlbul2drksmqgmligaelawflsvq8|Hanji 214 part 1]] supplies a compact Western Han case. An animal-park supervisor cannot answer [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]]'s questions, while a lower tiger-pen keeper answers fluently; Liu Heng therefore wants the speaker promoted. [[ZhangShizhiWesternHan|张释之]] interrupts the inference rather than denying that the answer was useful.

Zhang's counterargument separates communication performance from office fitness. He invokes [[ZhouBo|周勃]] and Zhang Xiangru as respected but slow-speaking officials, then uses Qin as a warning that polished reporting can leave superiors unable to hear error. The durable claim is not that eloquence is bad: verbal clarity is one signal, but it becomes dangerous when it collapses the rest of the appointment process and controls the ruler's picture of reality.

## Key Claims

- A strong answer to one narrow question does not by itself establish fitness for a broader office.
- Fluency can be useful evidence while still being an unreliable proxy for judgment, integrity, or execution.
- Slow or awkward speech does not negate competence, as the episode's Zhou Bo comparison is intended to show.
- Promotion bias becomes a governance risk when polished subordinates can filter out mistakes and unpleasant information.
- Correcting the bias requires widening the evidence considered, not simply preferring inarticulate candidates.

## Evidence

Narrow performance and promotion leap:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-214-tiemian-zhangshizhi-dui-huangdi-lan-taizi-1-lqlbul2drksmqgmligaelawflsvq8|Hanji 214 part 1]] says the tiger-pen keeper's fluent answers lead Liu Heng to propose immediate appointment as Shanglin director.

Counterexamples to the proxy:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-214-tiemian-zhangshizhi-dui-huangdi-lan-taizi-1-lqlbul2drksmqgmligaelawflsvq8|Hanji 214 part 1]] says Zhang contrasts the keeper with the slow speech of Zhou Bo and Zhang Xiangru, whom Liu Heng regards as substantial elders.

Information-system risk:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-214-tiemian-zhangshizhi-dui-huangdi-lan-taizi-1-lqlbul2drksmqgmligaelawflsvq8|Hanji 214 part 1]] reports Zhang's Qin analogy: orderly, persuasive presentation can help keep a ruler from hearing institutional faults.

Decision correction:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-214-tiemian-zhangshizhi-dui-huangdi-lan-taizi-1-lqlbul2drksmqgmligaelawflsvq8|Hanji 214 part 1]] says Liu Heng accepts the objection and abandons the proposed promotion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept rests on one popular-history episode and does not establish whether the keeper was actually unqualified, whether the existing Shanglin official was competent, or which formal appointment checks would otherwise have applied. Clear communication can be relevant to office performance; the problem is sufficiency and over-weighting, not eloquence itself. The Qin comparison is a cautionary historical analogy rather than proof that this single appointment would reproduce Qin's information failures.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Zhang Shizhi's objection to promoting a fluent tiger-pen keeper on one performance signal.
- Distinguished useful communication from the error of treating fluency as a complete appointment test.
