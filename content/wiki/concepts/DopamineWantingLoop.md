# Dopamine Wanting Loop / 多巴胺渴爱循环

## Definition
Dopamine wanting loop is E46's synthesis of reinforcement learning, prediction error, and Buddhist 渴爱. The source distinguishes wanting from liking: a system can keep pursuing what updates expectation or promises reward without producing lasting satisfaction.

## Current Synthesis
The episode places dopamine inside the second breakthrough in evolved intelligence: animals learn from outcomes and adjust future action. Meng Yan stresses that dopamine is not simply a pleasure molecule; it encodes a gap between expectation and result. The practical cost is a wanting loop that can keep the organism moving even when the wanted object does not actually satisfy. In the episode's broader argument, this loop is one layer of [[EvolutionaryMisrecognition]] because it makes the world appear through attraction, pursuit, and dissatisfaction.

## Key Claims
- Learning from outcomes depends on feedback loops that compare expectation with result.
- Wanting and liking can separate, so pursuit may persist without corresponding satisfaction.
- The loop is adaptive because it motivates exploration and correction.
- The same loop can become suffering when desire treats the next object as the answer.
- In E46, 渴爱 is interpreted as an ancient algorithmic tendency rather than only a moral weakness.

## Evidence
- Neural framing: [[e46-women-congwei-kanjian-bici-lmliksaiwxakmjbn-p2voaaoenxh]] explains basal ganglia, hypothalamus, dopamine, and prediction error as a learning system.
- Wanting/liking distinction: [[e46-women-congwei-kanjian-bici-lmliksaiwxakmjbn-p2voaaoenxh]] uses Berridge-style research to say dopamine drives wanting more than liking.
- Buddhist connection: [[e46-women-congwei-kanjian-bici-lmliksaiwxakmjbn-p2voaaoenxh]] links this mechanism to 渴爱 as an evolved source of dissatisfaction.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The page records the source's explanatory frame and should not overstate dopamine as the only basis of desire, addiction, or suffering. The source uses neuroscience as a bridge for reflection, not as clinical advice.
