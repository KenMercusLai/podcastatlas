# Employee Retention Economics

## Definition

Employee retention economics is the operating logic that spending on pay, benefits, ownership, promotion, communication, and physical work conditions can create business value when it lowers avoidable turnover and protects execution quality.

## Current Synthesis

The current evidence spans three mechanisms. [[Costco]] links higher wages, benefits, internal promotion, and long tenure to lower attrition, shrinkage, training loss, and operating mistakes. [[GSI]] adds broad worker ownership, where visible upside and trusted communication reportedly coincided with a large decline in quits, though results across firms were uneven. [[WorkplaceEnvironmentInvestment|办公环境投资]] adds the physical layer: durable and comfortable workstations may protect health, energy, productivity, and continued employment at a monthly cost that is small relative to salary and rent.

Together, the sources treat retention as an output of an operating system rather than a single perk. Employees must be able to see, trust, and use the investment; ownership hidden until exit cannot motivate earlier behavior, and a premium chair cannot compensate for unsafe workloads or weak management.

## Key Claims

- Higher employee spending can be economically rational when reduced turnover, shrinkage, training loss, and mistakes exceed the added cost.
- Internal promotion and long tenure preserve operating memory and execution quality.
- Broad ownership can strengthen retention only when the stake is understood and leadership builds trust.
- Physical work conditions belong in retention economics when they affect health, energy, and sustained performance.
- Employee investment is systemic: no isolated benefit proves retention or substitutes for sound management.

## Evidence

- **Pay, promotion, and operating memory:** [[costco-1]] presents Costco's wages, benefits, internal promotion, and low attrition as mutually reinforcing parts of the warehouse-club model.
- **Ownership and communication:** [[a-pro-worker-experiment-in-private-equity]] contrasts a surprise Capital Safety payout with GSI's communicated ownership program and reports that GSI's quit rate fell from roughly half the workforce to around 15% over five years.
- **Physical environment and lifecycle cost:** [[shangye-xiaoyang-51-wei-shenme-bangongyi-yue-gui-dui-laoban-faner-yue-hesuan-gkwrimaoqq8naeo0kgtrbnkp]] argues that chairs and office fit-outs should be amortized across use and treated as possible protection for employee health, productivity, and tenure.
- **Trust as mechanism:** [[costco-1]] ties employee treatment to member-facing execution, while [[a-pro-worker-experiment-in-private-equity]] says empathetic leadership helps convert ownership into engagement rather than mechanical extraction.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources do not establish one universal retention formula. KKR's worker-ownership outcomes vary across companies, GSI's quit-rate change cannot be attributed to equity alone, and Costco's culture and operating model have compounded over decades in ways competitors may not reproduce. The office-chair source provides approximate accounting rather than controlled health, productivity, or turnover evidence. Higher retention can also preserve poor fit or reduce useful renewal if management treats tenure as an end rather than part of effective, voluntary employment.
