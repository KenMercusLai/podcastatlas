# Smoking Cessation Support / 戒烟支持

## Definition
Smoking cessation support combines a defined quit attempt with behavioral preparation, trigger management, clinical assessment, appropriate medication, follow-up, family or social support, and recovery after lapses.

## Current Synthesis
The current sources treat quitting as a process rather than a character test. Cutting down, tea, gum, exercise, healthy snacks, water, entertainment, and attention shifts can help prepare or manage cravings, but a defined quit attempt still needs a plan and continuing support. People with stronger dependence may need cessation clinics, medication or nicotine replacement, repeated visits, family help, and psychological support; a lapse is information about triggers, not proof that future cessation is impossible.

Adolescents add a social-environment problem. Withdrawal may be joined by “social withdrawal”: temporarily avoiding friends, parties, devices, or settings that cue vaping. Nicotine replacement for people under 18 is described as off-label and difficult to dose when exposure is high, so the sources support clinician involvement rather than self-directed extrapolation from adult regimens.

## Key Claims
- A quit date turns indefinite reduction into a defined behavior change.
- Removing cigarettes, ashtrays, and visible cues reduces immediate triggers.
- Exercise and attention substitution may help people with lower dependence manage cravings.
- Medication and nicotine replacement require indication, contraindication, adverse-effect, and clinician assessment.
- Early withdrawal and adjustment can be difficult, so follow-up and expectation-setting matter.
- Grief, stress, and low mood can drive relapse, but one lapse need not become a full return; psychological support and prompt restarting can preserve the quit trajectory.
- Adolescent quitting may require temporary distance from peers and settings strongly linked to vaping cues.

## Evidence
- Behavioral preparation and clinical support: [[vol-164-shijie-wuyanri-dianzishi-yande-weihai-yuan-chao-xiangxiang-yisheng-qinshu-nianqing-huanzhe-qidao-bi-laoyanmin-gengzao-ljg7oqxs6d_n1-u7fzcyl7xdbxn]] describes quit dates, cue removal, attention substitution, exercise, cessation clinics, medication, nicotine patches, and follow-up.
- Youth cessation: [[vaping-alcohol-use-other-risky-youth-behaviors-dr-bonnie-halpern-felsher-scim9155116429]] adds withdrawal, use after waking, family support, exercise, food and water, therapy, peer-cue avoidance, and the under-18 nicotine-replacement boundary.
- Relapse recovery: [[vol-164-shijie-wuyanri-dianzishi-yande-weihai-yuan-chao-xiangxiang-yisheng-qinshu-nianqing-huanzhe-qidao-bi-laoyanmin-gengzao-ljg7oqxs6d_n1-u7fzcyl7xdbxn]] uses a grief-related relapse case to frame renewed cessation and mental-health support as part of the process.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not name specific medicines, doses, validated dependence measures, or individualized protocols. Under-18 nicotine replacement is described as not FDA-approved but sometimes used off label; that observation is not a recommendation for unsupervised use. Timelines, patch-use descriptions, traditional-medicine references, dose equivalence, and mechanism explanations are broad public education and should not replace qualified assessment.

## What Changed
- Added adolescent peer-cue management, family support, and off-label nicotine-replacement boundaries.
- Extended the cessation model from cigarettes to high-dependence youth vaping contexts.
