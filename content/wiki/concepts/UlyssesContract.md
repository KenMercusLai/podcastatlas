# Ulysses Contract

## Definition
A Ulysses contract is a commitment device through which a present self changes future options, incentives, or friction before a predictable temptation or low-control state arrives.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats self-control as a coordination problem among temporally different versions of the same person. A present intention is often weakest at the moment of temptation, so precommitment can remove access to a phone, alcohol, or cash; add social or financial consequences; or place desired tools such as running shoes where action becomes easier. The useful principle is environmental design, not proof that every commitment device is safe, fair, or effective.

## Key Claims
- Present intentions do not guarantee future behavior under craving, fatigue, distraction, or changed state.
- Restricting access before temptation can outperform relying on willpower at the point of choice.
- Social and financial stakes can make commitments more durable.
- Lowering friction for a desired action is the constructive counterpart to blocking an undesired action.
- Commitment devices work best when the target, constraint, and foreseeable side effects are explicit.

## Evidence
- Access restriction - [[science-tools-of-learning-memory-dr-david-eagleman-scim5564931565]] gives locked phones, frozen cash, removed alcohol, and limited cash as examples.
- Social stakes - [[science-tools-of-learning-memory-dr-david-eagleman-scim5564931565]] uses meeting someone at the gym and putting money or reputation on the line.
- Positive friction design - [[science-tools-of-learning-memory-dr-david-eagleman-scim5564931565]] describes placing running shoes near the door before sleep.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source offers illustrative examples rather than comparative trials or a universal protocol. Restricting money, communication, medication, or movement can create safety, coercion, or access problems; recovery and clinical contexts require qualified support rather than improvised deprivation.
