## Definition

汉代王侯厚葬与玉衣不朽信仰 / Han royal thick burial and jade-suit immortality belief is the conjunction of abundant elite grave goods, rank-marked jade burial clothing, and the expectation that jade could preserve bodily or vital continuity after death.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-310-2-gumu-zhong-de-jinlvyuyi-zhen-neng-qisihuisheng-lj_f2c88afcbjraslzodaisgkqbq|Hanji 310-2]] presents thick burial as materially enabled by the territorial resources and treasure collecting of Western Han vassal kings. Its comparisons are archaeological rather than merely proverbial: the tombs associated with [[LiuShengZhongshanKingWesternHan|中山王刘胜]], the Shizishan Chu king, and [[LiuHeHaihunHou|刘贺 / 海昏侯]] are said to contain very large assemblages of valuable objects.

The gold-thread jade suit compresses three meanings into one object. Its jade plaques and precious wire display concentrated wealth; different wire materials mark hierarchy; and the garment gives bodily form to claims that jade could keep essence from dispersing and the dead from decaying. The excavated object establishes an elite burial practice, not the efficacy of resurrection or preservation belief.

## Key Claims

- Royal political economy could be converted into tomb construction and concentrated grave wealth.
- Excavated royal assemblages make unusually rich Western Han vassal burial plausible without validating every treasure legend.
- Jade suits materialize status through costly plaques, labor, and rank-differentiated connecting wire.
- The garment links funerary display to a belief in bodily preservation, retained essence, and possible posthumous return.
- Archaeological evidence for an object and textual or popular claims about its efficacy have different evidentiary strength.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The current synthesis rests on one compact podcast summary rather than excavation publications, excavated inscriptions, transmitted ritual regulations, or a comparative study of jade-suit chronology. Counts, object classifications, rank rules, and the uniformity of belief across the Han remain source-scoped. The presence of a jade suit proves that such a burial object existed in the cited setting; it does not prove that all Western Han nobles shared one doctrine, that wire material always mapped cleanly to rank, or that jade caused physical preservation or resurrection.

## What Changed

- Created the concept by separating the archaeological reality of rich royal burial and jade garments from claims about their supernatural efficacy.
