# Capetian Succession Crisis

## Definition

Capetian succession crisis names the episode's account of how the failure of direct male heirs after [[PhilipIVOfFrance|Philip IV]] ended the stable father-to-son [[CapetianDynasty|Capetian]] sequence and made [[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]]'s rejected French claim a cause of the [[HundredYearsWar|Hundred Years' War]].

## Current Synthesis

The concept is currently bounded to the 1328 French succession dispute. The episode emphasizes that the issue was not a simple application of already-settled law. Edward III had a claim through [[IsabellaOfFrance|Isabella]], daughter of Philip IV, while French elites rejected transmission through a woman and selected [[PhilipVIOfFrance|Philip of Valois]].

The crisis matters because it fuses legality, gender, dynasty, and geopolitical risk. A French acceptance of Edward would put the English king on the French throne; rejection protects French continuity but leaves Edward with a claim he can later politicize alongside Gascony, Scotland, and Aquitaine.

## Key Claims

- Succession stability made the crisis sharper because fourteen Capetian father-to-son successions had preceded it.
- Philip IV's three sons dying without surviving sons ended the direct male Capetian line.
- Edward III's claim through Isabella was rejected because it passed through a woman.
- The episode says this exclusion was not yet fully settled law, so the French decision remained politically contestable.
- Philip VI's selection solved the immediate French succession problem while helping create the legitimacy field for war.
- The succession claim becomes powerful only when combined with Gascony, Aquitaine, Scotland, and English mobilization.

## Evidence

- Stable baseline: [[318-hundred-years-war-a-game-of-thrones-part-1-glt5584156008]] says fourteen Capetian kings succeeded father to son after Hugh Capet.
- Dynastic failure: [[318-hundred-years-war-a-game-of-thrones-part-1-glt5584156008]] says Philip IV's three sons succeed rapidly and none leaves a surviving son.
- Edward's claim: [[318-hundred-years-war-a-game-of-thrones-part-1-glt5584156008]] says Edward III claims through Isabella, Philip IV's daughter.
- Legal uncertainty: [[318-hundred-years-war-a-game-of-thrones-part-1-glt5584156008]] says rejection of a claim through a woman was not yet settled law.
- War relevance: [[318-hundred-years-war-a-game-of-thrones-part-1-glt5584156008]] places the rejected claim beside Gascony and Scotland rather than treating it as the only cause.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source does not give a technical legal history of Salic law or French succession doctrine. It also says Edward initially accepts Philip VI and does not immediately go to war, so the claim should be treated as one enabling dispute rather than the sole or immediate cause.
