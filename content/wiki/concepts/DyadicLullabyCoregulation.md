# Dyadic Lullaby Coregulation

## Definition
Dyadic lullaby coregulation is the bidirectional adjustment of caregiver and infant arousal during soothing singing.

## Current Synthesis
In the episode's experiment, mothers sang playful and soothing versions of the same song while infants sat separately in high chairs. Playful singing kept arousal relatively stable, while soothing singing reduced measured sweat-gland arousal in both mother and baby as the song continued.

The design suggests that vocal and musical interaction can contribute to calming even without the holding and rocking that usually accompany lullabies. Because both partners changed, the result fits a reciprocal rather than sender-receiver model of care.

## Key Claims
- Soothing vocal interaction can affect infant arousal without simultaneous tactile soothing.
- Caregiver arousal can change during the same interaction.
- Playful and soothing singing can organize arousal differently.
- Lullaby regulation is relational rather than a one-way intervention.

## Evidence
- Singing comparison - [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-63970084fa0b4353e8e3de25a37c3714-52798272-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-63970084fa0b4353e8e3de25a37c3714-52798272]] describes 30 mothers singing playful and soothing versions of “Twinkle, Twinkle, Little Star.”
- Bidirectional arousal - [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-63970084fa0b4353e8e3de25a37c3714-52798272-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-63970084fa0b4353e8e3de25a37c3714-52798272]] reports reduced sweat-gland activity in mothers and babies during soothing singing.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
One small laboratory design does not establish clinical benefit, sleep effects, developmental outcomes, or universality across songs, cultures, infants, and caregivers. Sweat-gland activity is one arousal measure and should not be equated with the full emotional experience of either participant.
