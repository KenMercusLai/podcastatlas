## Definition

纵容式政治清除 is the source's hypothesis that a ruler can repeatedly excuse or enable a potential rival's violations until the rival commits an offense serious enough to justify removal through ordinary-looking legal or political procedure.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-217-kan-fuhei-hanwendi-ruhe-wansi-yimudi-liuchang-1-lglv6qnnmxlvr9qnles6zeoytmjx|Hanji 217 part 1]] applies the pattern to [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] and [[LiuChangHuainanKing|淮南王刘长]]. The episode says Liu Heng permits Liu Chang to displace centrally appointed officers and repeatedly avoids final punishment; Liu Chang then moves from unlawful royal autonomy to an exposed rebellion plan. The host reads this escalation as “宠杀 / 捧杀”: indulgence supplies both behavioral momentum and eventual justification for elimination.

The evidence supports a narrower conclusion than the host's intent claim. Selective leniency and weak supervision can increase later enforcement costs, and [[YuanAngWesternHan|袁盎]]'s warning makes fatal exile risk foreseeable. Yet sequence, opportunity, and political benefit do not prove prior design. The concept is therefore best used as an intent hypothesis that requires direct evidence, not as an automatic label for every case in which indulgence precedes punishment.

## Key Claims

- Selective non-enforcement can let a privileged actor normalize progressively larger boundary violations.
- Escalation can convert an ambiguous rival into an actor removable under a clearer legal or security justification.
- The ruler may benefit from the sequence even when direct elimination would have carried higher kinship or legitimacy costs.
- Foreseeability of the final harm is weaker evidence than proof that the ruler designed or desired that harm.
- Alternative explanations include affection, indecision, weak supervision, mistaken confidence in reform, and loss of control over implementation.

## Evidence

Indulgence and escalation:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-217-kan-fuhei-hanwendi-ruhe-wansi-yimudi-liuchang-1-lglv6qnnmxlvr9qnles6zeoytmjx|Hanji 217 part 1]] says Liu Heng permits Liu Chang to appoint senior officials and had already excused serious misconduct before Liu Chang joins a rebellion plan.

Removal and foreseeable risk:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-217-kan-fuhei-hanwendi-ruhe-wansi-yimudi-liuchang-1-lglv6qnnmxlvr9qnles6zeoytmjx|Hanji 217 part 1]] says the court converts rebellion into loss of title and exile, while Yuan Ang explicitly predicts that the punishment may kill Liu Chang and burden Liu Heng with a fratricide reputation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The only current source is a popular-history episode whose strongest conclusion is explicitly interpretive. It provides no direct plan, instruction, private admission, or contemporaneous evidence that Liu Heng intended to provoke rebellion or death. Liu Heng's commutation of a death sentence, stated intention to recall a reformed brother, grief, burial arrangements, and grants to Liu Chang's sons can be read as either concealment and repair or genuine leniency followed by an implementation disaster. The concept must keep those alternatives live.

## What Changed

- Created the concept as a source-scoped intent hypothesis rather than a settled description of Liu Heng's conduct.
- Separated evidence of indulgence, escalation, benefit, and foreseeable harm from proof of deliberate design.
