## Definition

The Noche Triste, or Night of Tears, was the June 30-July 1, 1520 attempt by [[HernanCortes|Hernan Cortes]] and his Spanish and Indigenous allies to escape Tenochtitlan, ending in a catastrophic Mexica attack along the western causeway.

## Current Synthesis

The episodes present the retreat as the outcome of a political rupture as well as a battlefield disaster. The [[ToxcatlMassacre|Toxcatl massacre]] converted an unstable hostage occupation into open war; [[Moctezuma]] could no longer pacify the city, [[Cuitlahuac]] mobilized resistance after release, and Mexica forces blocked or broke the causeways. A column divided into vanguard, center, and rearguard then left at midnight carrying portable treasure and a bridge improvised from palace materials. After an alarm, warriors attacked from canoes; the bridge collapsed; and people, horses, cannon, and gold filled the gaps in the causeway. Armor, crowding, water, and the column's stretched formation turned withdrawal into mass death, especially in the rearguard.

The disaster did not decide the war. Survivors reached [[Tlaxcala]], where political leaders chose to preserve the alliance. That choice, more than any inevitable Spanish recovery, connects the retreat to the later [[SiegeOfTenochtitlan|siege of Tenochtitlan]]. The event also became a memory problem because Cortes survived to narrate it while many eyewitnesses did not.

## Key Claims

- The retreat failed through alarm, constrained causeway geography, canoe attack, bridge collapse, crowding, and the burden of armor and treasure.
- The Toxcatl massacre, failed hostage mediation, and Cuitlahuac's mobilization created the siege conditions that forced escape.
- Losses in people, horses, cannon, and gold brought the Spanish-led campaign close to collapse.
- Mexica tactical success was real but did not prevent survivors from reaching Tlaxcala and rebuilding the coalition.
- Tlaxcalan political choice was the hinge between battlefield disaster and renewed invasion.
- Cortes's survival and the deaths of other eyewitnesses shaped the event's later narrative record.

## Evidence

- Escape mechanics: [[389-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-night-of-tears-part-6-glt2744313815]] describes the midnight column, makeshift bridge, alarm, canoe attack, collapse, and causeway deaths.
- Material and human loss: [[389-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-night-of-tears-part-6-glt2744313815]] reports the loss of artillery, most horses and treasure, and many Spanish and Tlaxcalan lives.
- Strategic aftermath: [[389-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-night-of-tears-part-6-glt2744313815]] follows the survivors to Tlaxcala and the renewed alliance.
- Historical memory: [[389-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-night-of-tears-part-6-glt2744313815]] argues that lost witnesses increased Cortes's ability to center his own account.
- Immediate prehistory: [[388-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-festival-of-blood-part-5-glt8784071073]] connects the Toxcatl massacre, Moctezuma's failed mediation and death, Cuitlahuac's release, and causeway obstruction to the need for flight.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The alleged festival plot, responsibility for Moctezuma's death, roughly six-hundred-Spanish-death estimate, identity and role of the woman who raised the alarm, claim that the Mexica anticipated the move, famous weeping-tree scene, speeches, and dialogue remain source-scoped. Cortes's concern for the gold may have worsened the retreat, but his private motives cannot be established from these episodes. “Night of Tears” preserves Spanish memory and should not obscure that the event was a Mexica military victory with heavy Indigenous allied losses.

## What Changed

- Established the Noche Triste as a combined tactical, coalition, and historical-memory turning point.
- Made post-defeat Tlaxcalan choice central to explaining why the disaster did not end the campaign.
- Added the Toxcatl massacre and failed hostage strategy as the retreat's immediate political causes.
