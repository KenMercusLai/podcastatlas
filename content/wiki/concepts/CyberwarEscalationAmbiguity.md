## Definition
Cyberwar escalation ambiguity is the uncertainty over when hacking, ransomware, election interference, data theft, or attacks on civilian infrastructure should count as armed conflict and justify conventional retaliation.

## Current Synthesis
Digital attacks blur espionage, crime, sabotage, coercion, and war. The ambiguity is strategic because attribution can be delayed or disputed, effects vary from inconvenience to physical harm, state and non-state actors overlap, and public retaliation thresholds may invite both opportunism and escalation. A credible doctrine therefore needs more than a list of targets: it needs standards for attribution, severity, proportionality, collective defense, civilian protection, and de-escalation.

## Key Claims
- Cyber operations cross legal and strategic categories that are more distinct in conventional conflict.
- Critical-infrastructure attacks can create physical consequences without using conventional weapons.
- Unclear retaliation thresholds may weaken deterrence while preserving flexibility.
- Attribution confidence and actor identity are central to proportional response.
- Public doctrine must balance signaling with the risk of locking leaders into escalation.

## Evidence
- Attack surface: [[94-silicon-valley-part-2-glt3075342575]] names ransomware, tax records, voting systems, power plants, and hospitals as plausible targets.
- Doctrine gap: [[94-silicon-valley-part-2-glt3075342575]] records Andreessen's view that Washington lacked a clear threshold for converting an online attack into real-world military retaliation.
- Strategic framing: [[94-silicon-valley-part-2-glt3075342575]] describes network-centric and irregular conflict as difficult to fit into inherited concepts of war.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode is not a survey of international law, military doctrine, or government policy, and its statement about Washington is explicitly limited to Andreessen's knowledge in 2021. It does not distinguish existing classified doctrine from public signaling, nor does it assess particular attribution methods, alliance commitments, or response options short of force.

## What Changed
- Created a framework separating cyber harm from the unresolved threshold for conventional retaliation.
- Added attribution, proportionality, and de-escalation as necessary doctrine dimensions.
