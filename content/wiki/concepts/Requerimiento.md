## Definition

The Requerimiento was a Spanish declaration that narrated Christian and royal authority, demanded Indigenous submission and conversion, and threatened coercive consequences for refusal.

## Current Synthesis

The Mexico and Peru episodes use the Requerimiento to show that Spanish conquest pursued legal and theological legitimacy even when its demands could not produce meaningful consent. Reading an alien legal-religious history to people who might not understand it did not turn threatened subjugation into voluntary agreement; contemporary critic Bartolome de las Casas condemned the instrument as irrational and unjust.

[[645-the-fall-of-the-incas-massacre-in-the-andes-part-2-glt2938007438]] sharpens the distinction between procedure and cause. Valverde demands that [[Atahualpa]] accept Christianity and Charles V's authority, and the disputed handling of a prayer book is treated as the immediate pretext for attack. Yet [[FranciscoPizarro]] has already hidden men and prepared the [[CajamarcaMassacre|Cajamarca ambush]], so the exchange legitimates planned violence rather than recording a neutral offer followed by an unforeseen refusal.

The form nevertheless exposes a tension inside empire. By constructing a procedure for demanding submission, it implicitly confronted existing people and land claims. Later Indigenous delegations could use the empire's own agreements and courts to press claims, sometimes successfully. That later leverage does not redeem the original coercion.

## Key Claims

- The declaration joined Christian universal history, papal or royal authority, conversion, and political submission.
- Its procedure served Spanish legal justification at least as much as Indigenous understanding.
- Formal reading under threat could not establish meaningful consent.
- Las Casas's criticism shows that the instrument's injustice was visible within the Spanish world itself.
- At Cajamarca, the declaration-like demand and book incident supplied a pretext for violence planned before the encounter.
- Imperial legalism could later supply limited tools for Indigenous petition without becoming an equal system.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episodes summarize rather than reproduce a particular reading or legal text, and it is not certain whether Valverde recited the formal Requerimiento at Cajamarca. They do not establish how consistently captains complied or courts later honored Indigenous claims. Recognition of a claim to land is not recognition of equal sovereignty, and procedural form is not substantive justice. Accounts disagree over whether Atahualpa threw or dropped the book.
