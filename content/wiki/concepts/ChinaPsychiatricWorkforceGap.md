# China Psychiatric Workforce Gap / 中国精神卫生人才缺口

## Definition
China psychiatric workforce gap / 中国精神卫生人才缺口 is the mismatch, in Jiang Tao's source-scoped account, between large mental-health demand and the limited supply of psychiatrists, reliable psychotherapists, and community support workers.

## Current Synthesis
The source frames the access problem through numbers and queues. [[JiangTaoPsychiatrist|姜涛]] says China has roughly 5 million doctors but only about 50,000 psychiatrists, while mental-illness demand reaches very large patient populations. He also says reliable psychotherapists are scarce enough that some appointments may be booked years out.

The gap is not only headcount. It shows up as rushed outpatient experience, weak tiered care, patients preferring familiar senior doctors for reassurance, scarce psychotherapy referral capacity, and the need to train community hospitals, nurses, pharmacists, social workers, hotline staff, and grid workers. The concept therefore links clinical workforce, patient trust, community outreach, and crisis infrastructure.

## Key Claims
- Psychiatric doctors make up a small share of China's total doctor workforce in the source's account.
- Large patient demand makes outpatient access and physician time structurally tight.
- Tiered diagnosis and follow-up can fail when patients need reassurance from familiar or authoritative doctors.
- Psychotherapy capacity is also scarce, so referral from psychiatry to therapy is not simply a matter of advice.
- Community and hotline systems can extend reach, but they require training, supervision, and trust.
- Workforce shortage amplifies the temptation to seek unqualified substitutes.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The figures are source-scoped podcast claims and should not be treated as audited workforce statistics without outside data. The page does not evaluate regional distribution, professional licensing rules, hospital budgets, or treatment outcomes. A larger workforce would not automatically solve stigma, family cooperation, education pressure, service regulation, or crisis-response quality.
