# Unrestricted Submarine Warfare

## Definition

Unrestricted submarine warfare is the First World War naval practice, presented in [[673-the-first-world-war-the-submarine-strikes-part-3-glt3632974949]], of attacking enemy merchant shipping in a declared war zone without consistently following older cruiser-rule procedures of warning, search, and passenger evacuation.

## Current Synthesis

The source frames unrestricted submarine warfare as a strategic adaptation with high diplomatic risk. Germany wanted U-boats to pressure Britain's trade lifelines and possibly starve Britain into submission. Yet submarines were poorly suited to traditional prize rules: surfacing to inspect and evacuate a merchant ship exposed the submarine to danger, especially when British vessels could be armed or disguised.

The Lusitania case shows the central contradiction. Submarine warfare promised strategic leverage against maritime trade, but applying it against passenger liners made civilian death, neutral rights, propaganda, and legal ambiguity inseparable from the military campaign.

## Key Claims

- Germany's U-boat strategy translated economic warfare into a maritime attack on British imports and trade links.
- Cruiser rules became difficult to reconcile with submarine vulnerability and wartime deception.
- The February 1915 war-zone warning turned waters around Britain and Ireland into a declared danger area for enemy and neutral ships.
- Passenger liners created the highest-risk version of the policy because civilian deaths could outweigh military-economic gains.
- The sinking of [[RMSLusitania|Lusitania]] temporarily strengthened civilian-casualty and neutral-rights constraints on Germany's submarine campaign.
- The 1917 return to unrestricted submarine warfare made earlier Lusitania memory politically useful to American intervention arguments.

## Evidence

- Strategic logic: [[673-the-first-world-war-the-submarine-strikes-part-3-glt3632974949]] says German commanders believed U-boats could target British merchant shipping and pressure Britain's food and trade dependence.
- Rule conflict: [[673-the-first-world-war-the-submarine-strikes-part-3-glt3632974949]] explains that traditional rules required warning and evacuation, but submarines faced acute danger when surfacing near merchant ships.
- War-zone declaration: [[673-the-first-world-war-the-submarine-strikes-part-3-glt3632974949]] describes Germany's February 1915 warning around Britain and Ireland and the risk to neutral vessels.
- Diplomatic check: [[673-the-first-world-war-the-submarine-strikes-part-3-glt3632974949]] links Lusitania and the later Arabic sinking to Germany's September 1915 recall of U-boats from the Atlantic and English Channel.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source does not present unrestricted submarine warfare as irrational from Germany's perspective; it was a response to Britain's blockade and economic vulnerability. The qualification is that strategic logic did not remove legal, moral, and diplomatic costs, especially where passenger ships and neutral citizens were involved.
