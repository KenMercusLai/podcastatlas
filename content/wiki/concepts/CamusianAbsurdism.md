# Camusian Absurdism

## Definition

Camusian absurdism is the source's label for [[AlbertCamus]]'s examination of the conflict between human demands for meaning, order, and justice and a world that offers no guaranteed answer.

## Current Synthesis

[[12-days-solomon-northup-and-albert-camus-glt6039583450]] uses absurdism to correct the loose popular classification of Camus as an existentialist. *The Myth of Sisyphus* supplies the explicit philosophical reference, while *The Plague*, *Caligula*, *The Just Assassins*, and *Exile and the Kingdom* show the problem in narrative, dramatic, political, and Algerian forms.

The episode does not present absurdity as mere despair. Camus's Resistance record, anti-Stalinism, and criticism of revolutionary violence suggest a demand to act without converting history, ideology, or justice into permission for unlimited killing. His Algerian position makes that moral stance harder rather than cleaner: attachment to his mother and pied-noir community prevented abstract justice from functioning as a complete answer to colonial conflict.

## Key Claims

- Camus is popularly associated with existentialism but did not accept the label as his own.
- Absurdity names a conflict between the human demand for intelligibility and a world without guaranteed meaning.
- Recognition of absurdity need not collapse into nihilism or political passivity.
- Resistance and anti-Stalinism connect philosophical seriousness to refusal of ideological excuse.
- Criticism of revolutionary violence places limits on action undertaken in the name of justice.
- Personal loyalty can expose the incompleteness of abstract political principle without automatically justifying neutrality.

## Evidence

- Philosophical naming: [[12-days-solomon-northup-and-albert-camus-glt6039583450]] explicitly distinguishes Camus's absurdism from the existentialist label and names *The Myth of Sisyphus*.
- Literary expression: [[12-days-solomon-northup-and-albert-camus-glt6039583450]] discusses *The Plague*, *Caligula*, *The Just Assassins*, *Exile and the Kingdom*, and the unfinished *The First Man*.
- Political limit: [[12-days-solomon-northup-and-albert-camus-glt6039583450]] links Camus's Resistance work, anti-Stalinism, movement away from the left establishment, and criticism of revolutionary violence.
- Algerian qualification: [[12-days-solomon-northup-and-albert-camus-glt6039583450]] frames justice and maternal loyalty as competing commitments during the Algerian War.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source is a short literary-biographical discussion rather than a close reading of Camus's philosophical corpus. It does not establish a complete distinction among absurdism, existentialism, humanism, and later political positions. Its treatment of Algeria is especially compressed: personal loyalty can explain Camus's conflict without resolving the colonial injustice or proving that neutrality was adequate.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to distinguish Camus's accepted philosophical frame from his popular existentialist classification.
- Connected absurdity to Resistance, anti-Stalinism, limits on revolutionary violence, and unresolved Algerian loyalty.
