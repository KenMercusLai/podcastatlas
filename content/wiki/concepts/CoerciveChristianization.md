# Coercive Christianization

## Definition

Coercive Christianization is the use of military, legal, fiscal, or custodial power to impose Christian identity or practice on a population rather than relying on voluntary belief and persuasion.

## Current Synthesis

The Saxon case joins conquest and conversion so tightly that baptism becomes a test of political submission. [[Charlemagne]] destroys a shrine, requires baptism by treaty, treats reversion as apostasy, and later attaches death penalties to refusal of baptism and selected pagan practices. Raiding, hostage-taking, massacre, and deportation make religious policy inseparable from the coercive machinery of frontier rule.

[[Alcuin]] supplies an internal Christian objection rather than an external modern standard. He argues that force can produce the sacrament's outward form without faith and that newly converted people require gradual teaching. The reported easing of forced baptism and a less punitive 797 charter show counsel altering policy, but continued military pressure and monastic expansion prevent that change from being read as a clean abandonment of coercion.

## Key Claims

- Coercive conversion can make visible compliance serve simultaneously as religious profession and political submission.
- Compulsory baptism does not establish inward belief, even within the theology used to justify Christian expansion.
- Capital law, collective punishment, deportation, and instruction occupy different positions on a coercion spectrum but can operate inside one conversion system.
- Internal criticism can narrow a coercive program without dissolving the conquest structure that sustains it.

## Evidence

- Political-religious fusion - [[524-charlemagne-pagan-killer-part-2-glt1992648453|Part 2]] links shrine destruction, compulsory baptism, apostasy language, and anti-pagan capital laws to the Saxon war.
- Coercive enforcement - [[524-charlemagne-pagan-killer-part-2-glt1992648453|Part 2]] records devastation, hostages, execution, and deportation alongside the conversion program.
- Doctrinal objection and partial change - [[524-charlemagne-pagan-killer-part-2-glt1992648453|Part 2]] attributes to Alcuin a voluntary-faith argument and connects it to policy easing in 796-797.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source is a podcast synthesis based largely on Frankish and clerical evidence; it does not recover Saxon religious experience directly or independently establish every number, date, law, or motive. A less punitive charter is evidence of modification, not proof of voluntary conversion or the end of domination. The concept also should not collapse all missionary work, monastic foundations, parish teaching, or later Christian adherence into the same degree of coercion.

## What Changed

- Created the concept around the Saxon case's fusion of conquest, law, and baptism.
- Preserved Alcuin's voluntary-faith objection as an internal doctrinal limit.
- Distinguished partial policy moderation from the end of military Christianization.
