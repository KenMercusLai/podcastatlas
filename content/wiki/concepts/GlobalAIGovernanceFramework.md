# Global AI Governance Framework

## Definition
A global AI governance framework is an international, multi-stakeholder system for setting shared expectations, oversight, and accountability around AI capabilities and cross-border effects.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources agree that AI is persistent, general-purpose, and transnational, so purely national rules leave gaps around autonomous action, infrastructure, deployment, information concentration, and spillovers. One proposes a United Nations-level, multi-stakeholder framework; another supplies an incremental route through information sharing and common evaluations; the India summit source adds a geographic legitimacy test by asking whether developing regions help set priorities and share benefits; and the Brown interview makes values, concentrated user information, material resource demands, and government automation part of the scope. The combined judgment is that international coordination can begin without a world regulator, but expertise, funding announcements, and summits remain insufficient unless representation, evidence access, authority, enforcement, dispute resolution, and responsibility for embedded objectives become concrete.

## Key Claims
- AI should be governed as a lasting tool rather than approached through fear or denial.
- The central problem is autonomy without accountable institutional boundaries.
- Cross-border scope makes international coordination necessary even when national rules remain important.
- Companies must participate because they build and operate much of the relevant capability and infrastructure, but a credible framework must specify enforcement, responsibility, and representation rather than stopping at principles.
- Shared evaluations and information exchange can create practical coordination before states agree on binding rules.
- Geographic inclusion matters because a framework dominated by early frontier-model states and firms may miss development priorities and lack legitimacy.
- Governance must reach the objectives, thresholds, and values encoded in automated decisions rather than treating automation as politically neutral.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- [[JDVance]]'s source-scoped position rejects world-scale AI governance in favor of national defensive capability and targeted cyber controls.
- The SDG analogy does not establish that states would delegate meaningful AI authority or comply under strategic competition.
- Corporate participation can improve feasibility while also creating capture, representation, and accountability risks.
- The source does not define institutional powers, thresholds, inspections, sanctions, or dispute resolution.
- The newer source's national-regulation comparison is broad and source-time specific; shared standards could also become lowest-common-denominator commitments or reflect the interests of the most powerful states and firms.
- India's proposed bridge role and the summit's equitable-benefit framing are interpretations without evidence here of delegated authority, durable representation, or distributional outcomes.
- Brown's critique of older multilateral institutions creates an internal design warning: putting AI governance at a global level does not itself guarantee contemporary representation, legitimacy, implementation, or enforcement.

## What Changed
- Added concentrated user information, embedded values, and automated public decisions to the framework's scope.
- Added institutional legitimacy and implementation as explicit tests rather than assuming that global scale produces accountability.
