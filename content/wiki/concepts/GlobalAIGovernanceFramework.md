# Global AI Governance Framework

## Definition
A global AI governance framework is an international, multi-stakeholder system for setting shared expectations, oversight, and accountability around AI capabilities and cross-border effects.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources agree that AI is persistent, general-purpose, and transnational, so purely national rules leave gaps around autonomous action, infrastructure, deployment, and spillovers. One proposes a United Nations-level, multi-stakeholder framework; another supplies an incremental route through information sharing and common evaluations; the India summit source adds a geographic legitimacy test by asking whether developing regions help set priorities and share benefits. The combined judgment is that international coordination can begin without a world regulator, but expertise, funding announcements, and summits remain insufficient unless representation, evidence access, authority, enforcement, and dispute resolution become concrete.

## Key Claims
- AI should be governed as a lasting tool rather than approached through fear or denial.
- The central problem is autonomy without accountable institutional boundaries.
- Cross-border scope makes international coordination necessary even when national rules remain important.
- Companies must participate because they build and operate much of the relevant capability and infrastructure.
- A framework is credible only if it specifies enforcement, responsibility, and representation rather than stopping at principles.
- Shared evaluations and information exchange can create practical coordination before states agree on binding rules.
- Geographic inclusion matters because a framework dominated by early frontier-model states and firms may miss development priorities and lack legitimacy.

- Geographic legitimacy: [[ep-31-googles-30m-bet-the-ai-impact-summit-india-and-the-global-south]] presents [[India]] and [[GlobalSouthAIParticipation]] as a challenge to governance centered only in established Western institutions and frontier-model companies.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- [[JDVance]]'s source-scoped position rejects world-scale AI governance in favor of national defensive capability and targeted cyber controls.
- The SDG analogy does not establish that states would delegate meaningful AI authority or comply under strategic competition.
- Corporate participation can improve feasibility while also creating capture, representation, and accountability risks.
- The source does not define institutional powers, thresholds, inspections, sanctions, or dispute resolution.
- The newer source's national-regulation comparison is broad and source-time specific; shared standards could also become lowest-common-denominator commitments or reflect the interests of the most powerful states and firms.
- India's proposed bridge role and the summit's equitable-benefit framing are interpretations without evidence here of delegated authority, durable representation, or distributional outcomes.

## What Changed
- Added information sharing and shared evaluation standards as a pre-treaty coordination path.
- Strengthened the distinction between technical capacity and enforceable international authority.
- Added geographic participation and benefit distribution as tests of international legitimacy.
