## Definition

Principled office refusal is the choice by capable scholars or officials to decline office when service would require trust in a court or patronage structure they no longer regard as morally credible.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 849 creates the concept from a late-Han contrast. [[FanPangLateHan|范滂]] enters office and tries to clean up corruption as Qingzhao envoy, while [[XuZhiLateHan|徐稚]], [[JiangGongLateHan|姜公]], [[YuanHongLateHan|袁闳]], [[WeiZhuLateHan|韦著]], and [[LiTanLateHan|李昙]] refuse summons despite imperial gifts. The refusal is not presented as inability, laziness, or hatred of all social ties. Xu Zhi farms and mourns carefully; Jiang Gong sustains family solidarity; Yuan Hong cultivates integrity; Wei Zhu teaches texts; Li Tan practices filial service.

The concept therefore narrows reclusion into a political judgment. A court can offer honors and polite recruitment, but if its surrounding order is distrusted, worthy people may preserve moral agency by withholding service. The serving and non-serving branches stay related: Fan Pang shows what principled office can look like when one accepts duty, and the five recluses show why the same moral seriousness may lead others to refuse.

## Key Claims

- Office refusal can be an ethical judgment about institutional credibility, not a sign that the person lacks ability.
- Respectful recruitment and gifts do not guarantee legitimacy when the court's broader conduct is distrusted.
- Reclusion can coexist with work, teaching, mourning courtesy, filial service, and family duty.
- The concept is distinct from total retreat: the recluses preserve selective obligations while withholding court service.
- Fan Pang's serving branch clarifies the contrast because principled office and principled refusal answer the same corruption problem differently.

## Evidence

- Recruitment failure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-849-sushi-ziyou-du-de-fanpangzhuan-jiujing-shuo-de-shui-ltquglqzvezeyp0mqxwgwnlp12lf|Hanji 849]] says Chen Fan recommends five scholars, Huan sends formal gifts, and all five refuse office.
- Capable non-service: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-849-sushi-ziyou-du-de-fanpangzhuan-jiujing-shuo-de-shui-ltquglqzvezeyp0mqxwgwnlp12lf|Hanji 849]] presents Xu Zhi, Jiang Gong, Yuan Hong, Wei Zhu, and Li Tan as morally respected figures rather than as incapable outsiders.
- Selective obligation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-849-sushi-ziyou-du-de-fanpangzhuan-jiujing-shuo-de-shui-ltquglqzvezeyp0mqxwgwnlp12lf|Hanji 849]] shows Xu Zhi farming and mourning, Jiang Gong protecting and forgiving, Wei Zhu teaching, and Li Tan honoring his stepmother.
- Court-confidence diagnosis: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-849-sushi-ziyou-du-de-fanpangzhuan-jiujing-shuo-de-shui-ltquglqzvezeyp0mqxwgwnlp12lf|Hanji 849]] explicitly reads the five refusals as loss of confidence in the court.
- Serving contrast: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-849-sushi-ziyou-du-de-fanpangzhuan-jiujing-shuo-de-shui-ltquglqzvezeyp0mqxwgwnlp12lf|Hanji 849]] places Fan Pang's accountable impeachment work before the recluse cluster, making office integrity and office refusal parallel responses.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept should not be generalized into a rule that all officeholding under disorder is compromised. Hanji 849 itself gives Fan Pang as a serving integrity case, and later wiki pages show officials who accept office to protect people, enforce law, or rescue others. The source supports a narrower claim: under a distrusted court, refusal can be principled when paired with disciplined obligations outside office.
