# Deterrence Failure Against Expansion

## Definition

Deterrence failure against expansion is the process by which an aggressor’s limited, reversible probe goes unopposed and therefore changes both the strategic balance and the aggressor’s expectations about future resistance.

## Current Synthesis

The episode distinguishes constraint from retrospective inevitability. [[AdolfHitler]] backed down after Austrian Nazis murdered [[EngelbertDollfuss]] in 1934 because [[BenitoMussolini]] moved forces toward Austria and Germany was not ready to challenge him. The case shows that Hitler’s objectives could remain radical while his action changed under credible opposition.

By 1935–36, collective resistance had weakened. Britain’s separate naval agreement with Germany undermined the Stresa Front, Italy’s Abyssinian war divided the former Allies, France was politically constrained, and fear of another general war combined with sympathy for some German grievances. Hitler used those conditions to enter the demilitarized Rhineland with orders to retreat if France intervened.

Non-intervention did more than permit one treaty breach. It protected Germany’s western position, raised Hitler’s prestige, and reinforced his belief that opponents would not resist bold faits accomplis. Deterrence failure therefore altered capability, domestic authority, and risk perception at the same time.

## Key Claims

- Ideological commitment does not make the timing or success of aggression inevitable.
- Credible external action constrained Hitler in Austria in 1934 while Germany remained weak.
- Separate bargains and alliance disputes reduced the credibility of collective resistance.
- Fear of war and belief in legitimate grievances can make a limited violation appear tolerable in isolation.
- A reversible probe becomes durable when opponents do not impose the anticipated cost.
- Successful risk-taking changes later expectations and can encourage larger gambles.

## Evidence

- Constraint case: [[406-the-nazis-in-power-hitlers-road-to-war-part-3-glt6776521114]] says Mussolini’s 1934 mobilization forced Hitler to retreat after the Dollfuss assassination and failed Austrian Nazi coup.
- Coalition erosion: [[406-the-nazis-in-power-hitlers-road-to-war-part-3-glt6776521114]] connects the Anglo-German Naval Agreement and Abyssinian crisis to the rapid weakening of the Stresa Front.
- Reversible probe: [[406-the-nazis-in-power-hitlers-road-to-war-part-3-glt6776521114]] says German troops had orders to withdraw if France intervened during the [[RhinelandRemilitarization|Rhineland remilitarization]].
- Inaction conditions: [[406-the-nazis-in-power-hitlers-road-to-war-part-3-glt6776521114]] emphasizes French division, British reluctance, fear of another war, and sympathy for German grievances.
- Feedback effect: [[406-the-nazis-in-power-hitlers-road-to-war-part-3-glt6776521114]] connects success to increased popularity, strategic protection, and Hitler’s stronger belief in destiny and continued audacity.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not establish that military confrontation was costless, politically easy, or guaranteed to prevent later war. It also does not treat every compromise, treaty revision, or negotiated settlement as appeasement. Counterfactual claims about French intervention, public support, coalition cohesion, and Hitler’s later choices remain source-scoped; deterrence is one mechanism in a larger ideological, military, economic, and political sequence.

## What Changed

- Established the 1934 Austrian retreat and 1936 Rhineland success as a paired constraint-versus-non-intervention comparison.
- Added alliance fragmentation and separate bargaining as mechanisms that weakened deterrence.
- Added success-driven confidence as a bridge from one limited gamble to later escalation.
