# Deterrence Failure Against Expansion

## Definition

Deterrence failure against expansion is the process by which an aggressor’s limited, reversible probe goes unopposed and therefore changes both the strategic balance and the aggressor’s expectations about future resistance.

## Current Synthesis

The episode distinguishes constraint from retrospective inevitability. [[AdolfHitler]] backed down after Austrian Nazis murdered [[EngelbertDollfuss]] in 1934 because [[BenitoMussolini]] moved forces toward Austria and Germany was not ready to challenge him. The case shows that Hitler’s objectives could remain radical while his action changed under credible opposition.

By 1935–36, collective resistance had weakened. Britain’s separate naval agreement with Germany undermined the Stresa Front, Italy’s Abyssinian war divided the former Allies, France was politically constrained, and fear of another general war combined with sympathy for some German grievances. Hitler used those conditions to enter the demilitarized Rhineland with orders to retreat if France intervened.

Non-intervention did more than permit one treaty breach. It protected Germany’s western position, raised Hitler’s prestige, and reinforced his belief that opponents would not resist bold faits accomplis. Deterrence failure therefore altered capability, domestic authority, and risk perception at the same time.

The [[MunichAgreement1938|Munich crisis]] adds a more complex failure than simple inaction. Britain and France actively negotiated to avoid immediate war, but they pressured [[Czechoslovakia]] to cede the Sudetenland without Czech participation. The transfer removed frontier defenses and productive capacity, weakened the remaining state, and dissolved the immediate German military conspiracy. A settlement could therefore succeed at short-run war avoidance while worsening the strategic balance and the aggressor's estimate of future opposition.

The episode also prevents a purely passive account. Czech mobilization, Anglo-French warnings, German public reluctance, concern inside the high command, and intervention by Göring and Mussolini changed Hitler's immediate path. He accepted a conference despite wanting war. Constraints still mattered, but because the final settlement delivered the territory, he could interpret tactical restraint as confirmation that escalation worked.

The March 1939 sequence shows both escalation and attempted correction. After the Anschluss, Sudetenland settlement, and Munich assurances, Hitler destroyed the remaining Czech state through separatist pressure, threat, and occupation. Because Prague involved ruling a non-German population, it broke the earlier self-determination rationale and moved Britain and France toward the [[BritishFrenchGuaranteeToPoland|guarantee of Poland]]. The correction came late: the guarantee changed the declared threshold but lacked a prompt mechanism for relieving Poland, while Hitler interpreted earlier success as evidence that the commitment would collapse.

## Key Claims

- Ideological commitment does not make the timing or success of aggression inevitable.
- Credible external action constrained Hitler in Austria in 1934 while Germany remained weak.
- Separate bargains and alliance disputes reduced the credibility of collective resistance.
- Fear of war and belief in legitimate grievances can make a limited violation appear tolerable in isolation.
- A reversible probe becomes durable when opponents do not impose the anticipated cost, and successful risk-taking can encourage larger gambles.
- A negotiated concession can avoid immediate war while transferring capability and weakening later deterrence.
- A blatant breach can restore political resolve without immediately repairing the military credibility lost through earlier failures.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not establish that military confrontation was costless, politically easy, or guaranteed to prevent later war. It also does not treat every compromise, treaty revision, or negotiated settlement as appeasement. Munich genuinely avoided immediate general war, and Germany's public and military anxiety shows that Hitler did not face a frictionless path; neither fact makes Czech consent free or the strategic transfer harmless. The guarantee demonstrates that political resolve and operational capacity can diverge: Britain and France could credibly widen a war without being able to save Poland quickly. Counterfactual claims about Czech resistance, French intervention, public support, coalition cohesion, alternative guarantees, conspiracy success, and Hitler’s later choices remain source-scoped; deterrence is one mechanism in a larger ideological, military, economic, and political sequence.
