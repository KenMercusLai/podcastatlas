# Surrender Guarantee Integrity

## Definition

Surrender guarantee integrity is the requirement that negotiated protections remain reliable once defenders have relied on them by leaving fortifications, disarming, or entering custody, even when a superior later disputes the negotiator's authority.

## Current Synthesis

The Naples capitulation placed formal command and good-faith reliance in conflict. [[CardinalRuffo|Cardinal Ruffo]] promised republican defenders passage to Toulon in exchange for surrender of their castles and weapons. [[HoratioNelson|Horatio Nelson]] and the restored court treated Ruffo as lacking authority, but British officers reportedly argued that the prisoners could not honorably be detained after acting on the agreement: the alternatives were to fulfill the passage or restore them to the castles and resume hostilities.

The principle matters because military surrender depends on credible commitments under uncertainty. If a superior can retain the disarmament while repudiating the protection, tactical advantage destroys the trust needed for later capitulations. That does not settle whether every subordinate may bind a sovereign; it identifies reliance, reversibility, custody, and the treatment of prisoners as separate moral constraints on correcting an unauthorized bargain.

## Key Claims

- Authority to negotiate and integrity after reliance are related but distinct questions.
- Disarmament creates an asymmetry that makes later repudiation especially coercive.
- A disputed agreement can be unwound more honorably by restoring the prior military position than by keeping only its advantage.
- Obedience to a sovereign does not erase an officer's responsibility for the use of custody and national force.
- Reliable surrender terms reduce later bloodshed; opportunistic repudiation damages both immediate legitimacy and future bargaining.

## Evidence

### Reliance on negotiated terms

- [[608-nelson-slaughter-in-naples-part-1-glt8594694484]] describes republican defenders leaving the castles, surrendering weapons, and boarding ships under Ruffo's promise of passage to Toulon.

### Officer objections and repudiation

- [[608-nelson-slaughter-in-naples-part-1-glt8594694484]] reports British officers arguing for passage or restoration to the castles, while Nelson invoked orders and used British ships to detain prisoners.

### Punitive aftermath

- [[608-nelson-slaughter-in-naples-part-1-glt8594694484]] connects repudiation to trials, imprisonment, executions, and enduring criticism of Nelson's honor.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The source does not resolve Ruffo's formal authority, Nelson's precise orders, the applicable law, or whether all prisoners relied on identical guarantees.
- A commander may need to correct terms beyond a negotiator's mandate, especially where fraud or coercion is involved; integrity does not mean every purported promise is automatically valid.
- The principle is synthesized from one contested case and should not substitute for case-specific legal analysis of armistices, capitulations, prisoner status, and command responsibility.

## What Changed

- Established a reusable distinction between disputed negotiating authority and obligations created by irreversible reliance.
