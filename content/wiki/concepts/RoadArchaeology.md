## Definition
道路考古 / Road Archaeology is the reconstruction of long, often partly vanished routes by combining material traces, documents, landscape analysis, geospatial tools, oral memory, and embodied survey.

## Current Synthesis
Unlike excavation centered on a bounded buried site, road archaeology must connect dispersed evidence across valleys, ridges, settlements, bridges, cliff faces, and later infrastructure. A road may survive as sockets rather than decking, a bridge without its adjoining path, a field boundary, an old market itinerary, a place name, or a modern road laid over an older line.

The strongest reconstruction comes from convergence. Gazetteers and maps propose a corridor; waterways and passes test geographic plausibility; bridges and cuttings provide material anchors; GPS and aerial imagery connect points; residents preserve recent route memory; and walking reveals details hidden by vehicle speed.

## Key Claims
- Roads require landscape-scale inquiry because their evidence is linear, dispersed, and repeatedly altered.
- No single source type is sufficient; documents, surface remains, terrain, technology, and oral testimony must corroborate one another.
- Bridges, sockets, inscriptions, and place names can preserve alignment after the road surface disappears.
- Walking is an evidentiary method because it reveals gradients, junctions, visibility, and small traces missed from vehicles.
- Scarce remains increase the need to distinguish plausible reconstruction from tourism legend or retrospective certainty.

- Mixed method: [[ep285-tafang-gushudao-ganwen-lu-zai-hefang-li20senvn3oelioproznnykdajvt|EP285]] lists archaeology, historical geography, texts, ground survey, oral history, GPS, aerial imagery, and walking as complementary tools.
- Vanished alignments: [[ep285-tafang-gushudao-ganwen-lu-zai-hefang-li20senvn3oelioproznnykdajvt|EP285]] describes Liangping farmland where gazetteers, maps, waterways, and old bridges are used to recover a route.
- Distributed remains: [[ep285-tafang-gushudao-ganwen-lu-zai-hefang-li20senvn3oelioproznnykdajvt|EP285]] shows how Baoxie trestle sockets and later cliff roads connect otherwise discontinuous segments.
- Evidence discipline: [[ep285-tafang-gushudao-ganwen-lu-zai-hefang-li20senvn3oelioproznnykdajvt|EP285]] tests the alleged Wanyuan inscription against provenance, gazetteers, surviving object access, and handwriting.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Oral memory may preserve only relatively recent market roads, later bridges may sit on altered alignments, and physical traces can be reused or misdated. A plausible corridor is not automatically proof of one exact ancient itinerary.

## What Changed
- Created the road-archaeology concept.
- Defined convergence across documents, terrain, material traces, memory, and walking as the evidentiary standard.
- Added an explicit boundary against filling sparse evidence with attractive local stories.
