# Social Media Age Bans

## Definition
Social media age bans are laws or proposals that block children below a threshold age from using social-media platform accounts or covered social services.

## Current Synthesis
The current wiki record treats youth social-media age bans as a child-safety policy with three separable questions: what is banned, how the ban is enforced, and who is responsible when young users evade it. The international policy version in [[tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128]] runs through [[France]], [[Australia]], and related [[UnitedKingdom|UK]], [[Canada]], and [[EuropeanUnion|EU]] debates. [[400-shejiao-meiti-de-dayancao-shike-shui-gai-wei-qingshaonian-de-chengyin-fuze-32ada8c4]] sharpens the definition by distinguishing social-media account restrictions from a general internet ban: education sites, email, web access, forums, and some point-to-point communication tools can remain available even when algorithmic, account-based feeds are restricted.

The concept sits next to [[OnlineAgeVerification]] because access bans require enforcement. If platforms must prove a user is old enough, the system can shift from self-declared birthdays toward identity checks, age-estimation systems, app-store/device controls, or login controls. That enforcement layer creates [[SocialMediaAgeGateSpeechBurden]] and [[CivilLibertiesSurveillanceRisk]] even when the policy goal is child safety. The newest source contributes a legal-default argument: a ban can shift social norms around when a child should first have a social-media account even if evasion remains possible.

## Key Claims
- Child-safety concerns are real, but a categorical age ban does not automatically solve platform harms.
- The scope should distinguish account-based, data-accumulating, algorithmic social-media services from general internet access, education resources, email, and direct communication.
- Young users may route around bans through fake accounts, alternate login methods, VPNs, or borrowed devices.
- Age bans can push platforms toward stronger identity verification, affecting anonymity and privacy for adults as well as children.
- Law can still set a social default even when enforcement is incomplete, similar to youth alcohol or tobacco rules.
- The policy becomes harder when platforms serve social, educational, political, family, entertainment, and creator functions at once.
- Defining the covered service is unstable when short-video, streaming, creator, and communication products converge.

## Evidence
- International policy spread: [[tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128]] describes France's under-15 ban, the Australia comparison, and related debates in the UK, Canada, and other EU countries.
- Account-ban distinction and legal default: [[400-shejiao-meiti-de-dayancao-shike-shui-gai-wei-qingshaonian-de-chengyin-fuze-32ada8c4]] argues that youth social-media restrictions target accounts, data accumulation, and algorithmic feeds rather than the entire internet, and that imperfect enforcement can still move social norms.
- Enforcement and evasion: [[tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128]] and [[400-shejiao-meiti-de-dayancao-shike-shui-gai-wei-qingshaonian-de-chengyin-fuze-32ada8c4]] both preserve fake-account, VPN, parental-control, and age-check concerns.
- Responsibility allocation: [[400-shejiao-meiti-de-dayancao-shike-shui-gai-wei-qingshaonian-de-chengyin-fuze-32ada8c4]] says many legal designs punish platforms rather than minors or parents when youth users evade restrictions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Age bans can suppress lawful youth speech and community access, especially where social media is used for religion, politics, family connection, identity support, or peer community. Stronger age verification can create privacy and surveillance burdens for adults as well as children. The France timeline remains source-scoped because existing sources differ on whether and how the policy was proceeding at specific moments. The source's claim that Australia's ban may still leave many teenagers able to access platforms should be treated as an implementation warning, not a final effectiveness measurement.
