# Social Media Age Bans

## Definition
Social media age bans are laws or proposals that block children below a threshold age from using social-media platform accounts or covered social services.

## Current Synthesis
The current wiki record treats youth social-media age bans as a child-safety policy with four separable questions: what is banned, how the ban is enforced, who is responsible when young users evade it, and whether the law targets platform accounts rather than public information access. The international policy version in [[tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128]] runs through [[France]], [[Australia]], and related [[UnitedKingdom|UK]], [[Canada]], and [[EuropeanUnion|EU]] debates. [[400-shejiao-meiti-de-dayancao-shike-shui-gai-wei-qingshaonian-de-chengyin-fuze-32ada8c4]] and [[351-xifang-zhengfu-jinzhi-qingshaonian-shiyong-shejiao-meiti-zoudao-nabu-le-luvc8m2kuqkyv2hddfcse-6ogxym]] sharpen the definition by distinguishing social-media account restrictions from a general internet ban: education sites, email, public web access, open videos, search, and some point-to-point communication tools can remain available even when account-based, data-accumulating, algorithmic feeds are restricted.

The concept sits next to [[OnlineAgeVerification]] because access bans require enforcement. If platforms must prove a user is old enough, the system can shift from self-declared birthdays toward identity checks, age-estimation systems, app-store/device controls, operating-system eligibility signals, or login controls. That enforcement layer creates [[SocialMediaAgeGateSpeechBurden]] and [[CivilLibertiesSurveillanceRisk]] even when the policy goal is child safety. The strongest policy argument in the current evidence is not perfect exclusion, but norm-setting and friction: a ban can change the expected age of first social-media account, lower constant exposure, and help families coordinate out of peer-pressure defaults even when evasion remains possible.

## Key Claims
- Child-safety concerns are real, but a categorical age ban does not automatically solve platform harms.
- The scope should distinguish account-based, data-accumulating, algorithmic social-media services from general internet access, education resources, open videos, search, email, and direct communication.
- Young users may route around bans through fake accounts, alternate login methods, VPNs, or borrowed devices.
- Age bans can push platforms toward stronger age assurance, affecting anonymity and privacy for adults as well as children unless verification follows a minimum-information design.
- Law can still set a social default even when enforcement is incomplete, similar to youth alcohol or tobacco rules.
- Shared legal defaults may help parents solve a collective-action problem when peer networks make individual family opt-outs socially costly.
- The policy becomes harder when platforms serve social, educational, political, family, entertainment, and creator functions at once.

## Evidence
- International policy spread: [[tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128]] describes France's under-15 ban, the Australia comparison, and related debates in the UK, Canada, and other EU countries.
- Account-ban distinction and legal default: [[400-shejiao-meiti-de-dayancao-shike-shui-gai-wei-qingshaonian-de-chengyin-fuze-32ada8c4]] and [[351-xifang-zhengfu-jinzhi-qingshaonian-shiyong-shejiao-meiti-zoudao-nabu-le-luvc8m2kuqkyv2hddfcse-6ogxym]] argue that youth social-media restrictions target accounts, data accumulation, platform contracts, and algorithmic feeds rather than the entire internet.
- Enforcement and evasion: [[tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260724-0724-mp-tech-pod-128]], [[400-shejiao-meiti-de-dayancao-shike-shui-gai-wei-qingshaonian-de-chengyin-fuze-32ada8c4]], and [[351-xifang-zhengfu-jinzhi-qingshaonian-shiyong-shejiao-meiti-zoudao-nabu-le-luvc8m2kuqkyv2hddfcse-6ogxym]] preserve fake-account, VPN, borrowed-device, parental-control, and age-check concerns while treating incomplete enforcement as expected.
- Responsibility allocation and family coordination: [[400-shejiao-meiti-de-dayancao-shike-shui-gai-wei-qingshaonian-de-chengyin-fuze-32ada8c4]] says many legal designs punish platforms rather than minors or parents when youth users evade restrictions; [[351-xifang-zhengfu-jinzhi-qingshaonian-shiyong-shejiao-meiti-zoudao-nabu-le-luvc8m2kuqkyv2hddfcse-6ogxym]] adds the collective-action argument that shared rules can reduce the social cost of opting out.
- Minimum-information age assurance: [[351-xifang-zhengfu-jinzhi-qingshaonian-shiyong-shejiao-meiti-zoudao-nabu-le-luvc8m2kuqkyv2hddfcse-6ogxym]] argues that device or operating-system checks should ideally send only an age-threshold yes/no signal, not broader identity data.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Age bans can suppress lawful youth speech and community access, especially where social media is used for religion, politics, family connection, identity support, or peer community. Stronger age verification can create privacy and surveillance burdens for adults as well as children, even if minimum-information systems reduce some exposure. The France timeline remains source-scoped because existing sources differ on whether and how the policy was proceeding at specific moments. Claims about Australia's early effectiveness, including reported usage declines or evasion, should be treated as provisional implementation signals rather than final measurements.
