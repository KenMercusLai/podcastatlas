# Nutrition Label Sugar Traps / 营养标签糖陷阱

## Definition
Nutrition label sugar traps are wording, ingredient, and nutrition-panel patterns that make a food or drink appear sugar-safe even though naturally occurring sugar, other added sugars, refined carbohydrates, energy density, serving patterns, or health halos still matter.

## Current Synthesis
The sources converge on one consumer-literacy rule: a prominent front label is incomplete. “Zero sucrose” does not mean sugar-free, low energy, or suitable for unrestricted sugar control, because a product may still contain starch, honey, syrup, maltose, concentrated juice, fruit ingredients, or other sugar sources. “Zero sugar” can also be a threshold claim rather than literal absence.

The functional-food source extends the same reasoning to meal replacements, energy bars, protein products, sleep drinks, electrolyte drinks, and supplement-adjacent products. The newest Starbucks dispute adds another distinct boundary: no added sugar does not mean the finished drink contains no naturally occurring sugar. Ingredient lists, carbohydrate and sugar values, serving size, product category, and exact claim wording must therefore be interpreted together.

## Key Claims
- “Zero sucrose” does not establish absence of all sugars or low total carbohydrate.
- Naturally occurring sugar can remain even when no sugar is added during preparation.
- Refined starch can affect blood glucose without appearing as sucrose.
- Honey, syrups, maltose, concentrated juice, fruit, and cereal add-ins can supply sugar.
- Health-coded and functional products still require ingredient, energy, carbohydrate, sodium, and serving checks.
- Regulatory thresholds and exact wording matter when interpreting “sugar-free” or “zero sugar.”

## Evidence
- **Sucrose and carbohydrate boundary:** [[vol-215-di-gi-fengmi-ling-zhetang-kangtangwan-zhejie-kongtangren-daodi-caile-duoshao-keng-ft-dashihua-ltb5lyaycaxoijiktbo4pxne2a5z]] explains why zero sucrose does not mean no sugar, low energy, or unrestricted suitability.
- **Hidden sources and health halos:** [[vol-215-di-gi-fengmi-ling-zhetang-kangtangwan-zhejie-kongtangren-daodi-caile-duoshao-keng-ft-dashihua-ltb5lyaycaxoijiktbo4pxne2a5z]] names honey, syrups, juices, maltose, fruit, cereals, yogurt, kombucha, and sour drinks as cues requiring back-label checks.
- **Functional-product extension:** [[vol-209-bie-wei-gongnengxing-shangtou-ni-yiwei-de-jingzhun-yangsheng-qishi-yue-bu-yue-zao-liao-liao-daicanfen-dianjiezhi-dengdeng-ljlwqq2yhtcsa4r3nvnzaa6p5i9o]] extends label scrutiny to meal replacements, energy bars, protein and electrolyte products, and other health-coded goods.
- **No-added-sugar distinction:** [[guoqing-jiaqi-duocheng-chuanyou-redu-pansheng-xingbake-yin-wutang-yinliao-zhong-hantang-bei-qisu-1021649123]] records allegations about Starbucks “sugar-free” protein drinks and Starbucks' response that the sugar came from milk while no sugar was added.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The nutrition sources are China-facing public education, while the Starbucks dispute arises in the United States and is unresolved litigation. Label definitions, thresholds, required disclosures, menu wording, and consumer interpretation vary by jurisdiction and product. The concept is a screening heuristic, not a legal conclusion or individualized nutrition prescription.

## What Changed
- Added naturally occurring milk sugar as distinct from added sugar.
- Added litigation as an example of how shorthand product claims can become a consumer-trust and legal dispute.
