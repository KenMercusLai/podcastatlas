# Nutrition Label Sugar Traps / 营养标签糖陷阱

## Definition
Nutrition label sugar traps are packaging, ingredient, and nutrition-panel signals that make a food seem sugar-safe while other sugars, refined carbohydrates, serving patterns, or health halos still create meaningful sugar exposure.

## Current Synthesis
The episode's label-literacy claim is straightforward: the front of the package is not enough. "Zero sucrose" is not the same as sugar-free, low energy, or safe for sugar control, because a product may still contain starch, fructose-glucose syrup, maltose, concentrated fruit juice, honey, syrup, sweet cereal clusters, or fruit ingredients. Similarly, sour or healthy-feeling drinks may need sugar to taste good.

The practical response is to read the ingredient list and nutrition panel together. Ingredient names identify likely sugar sources, while the carbohydrate line helps estimate the broader sugar and starch burden. The source also notes that "zero sugar" or "sugar-free" claims can legally mean below a threshold rather than literal absence, so label claims need threshold and serving-size awareness.

## Key Claims
- "Zero sucrose" does not mean sugar-free, low-energy, or automatically suitable for unrestricted sugar control.
- Refined starch products can raise blood sugar even when they contain no sucrose as an added ingredient.
- Sugar may appear through honey, syrups, maltose, fructose-glucose syrup, concentrated juice, fruit ingredients, or sweetened cereal add-ins.
- Sour, fermented, fruit, yogurt, and "healthy" cues can hide sugar added for palatability.
- Ingredient lists and nutrition panels should be read together because marketing copy highlights only selected claims.
- "Sugar-free" and "zero sugar" labels can reflect regulatory thresholds rather than absolute absence.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is China-facing podcast nutrition education and does not provide a full regulatory comparison across jurisdictions. The concept should therefore be used as a consumer-literacy heuristic: investigate claims, ingredients, carbohydrate content, serving size, and frequency before accepting front-package promises.
