# Legacy Privacy Statute Internet Spillover

## Definition
Legacy privacy statute internet spillover is the expansion of a law written for an older, narrower technology or transaction into routine modern website practices whose actors, data flows, and user relationships do not map cleanly to the original categories.

## Current Synthesis
The [[VideoPrivacyProtectionAct]] illustrates both sides of the problem. Older statutes can supply enforceable privacy remedies when Congress has not enacted comprehensive federal rules, but broad translation can turn ordinary analytics, advertising, and video-bearing websites into defendants under terms designed for videotape rentals. The resulting policy choice is not simply privacy versus no privacy: courts must weigh statutory text, technological continuity, remedy design, consent friction, and the risk that technical violations generate litigation beyond the original harm.

## Key Claims
- A federal privacy-policy gap increases pressure to repurpose older sectoral, computer-crime, or eavesdropping laws.
- Technological analogy is unstable when modern sites combine video, analytics, advertising, identity, and many unrelated services.
- Statutory damages can magnify uncertain category boundaries into large litigation exposure.
- Consent prompts may reduce exposure while shifting costs into access friction and repeated user decisions.
- Legislative amendment can reset an obsolete boundary, but courts still must resolve live claims under existing text.

## Evidence
- Policy-gap pressure - [[tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128]] says plaintiffs have also turned to older computer-crime and eavesdropping statutes in the absence of comprehensive federal privacy law.
- Scale risk - [[tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128]] reports Goldman's warning that almost every site with video could face claims under a broad reading.
- Institutional response - [[tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128]] says California amended one older law after repeated online applications.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Calling a statute old does not show that its protected interest disappeared or that new technology should escape its terms. Conversely, functional similarity does not prove that Congress covered every modern data flow. The source does not provide a comparative survey of cases or the text of the California amendment, so frequency and doctrinal generalization remain source-scoped.

## What Changed
- Established the concept as a tradeoff between enforceable privacy continuity and category mismatch, litigation scale, and consent friction.
