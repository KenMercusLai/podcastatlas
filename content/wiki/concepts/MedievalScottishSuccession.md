# Medieval Scottish Competitive Succession

## Definition

Medieval Scottish competitive succession is the episode's model of kingship in which several adult men within a wider royal kindred could hold plausible claims, making selection, regional power, marriage, and warfare more important than simple father-to-eldest-son primogeniture.

## Current Synthesis

[[104-macbeth-glt3313769671]] situates succession inside the incomplete consolidation of Alba and the continuing power of Moray. Royal descent created a field of eligible competitors rather than one automatic heir. Malcolm II's preference for Duncan, Macbeth's Moray base, and [[Gruoch]]'s rival-line ancestry therefore generated competing claims inside the political order.

This frame changes the meaning of Macbeth's accession. Duncan attacks Moray and dies in battle; Macbeth's kingship is violent, but not simply an inexplicable seizure by an outsider. Later histories serving a restored line can retrospectively narrow legitimacy and turn a former competitor into a usurper.

## Key Claims

- Wider royal kin eligibility produced multiple plausible adult claimants.
- Regional power in Moray remained relevant to kingship in Alba.
- Marriage could consolidate genealogical and territorial claims.
- Preemptive violence became structurally likely when rivals could not be excluded by a settled rule.
- Later primogeniture-shaped narratives can misread competitive succession as simple usurpation.

## Evidence

- Political geography: [[104-macbeth-glt3313769671]] describes Alba, Moray, and neighboring polities as an unsettled landscape rather than a completed modern Scotland.
- Claim structure: [[104-macbeth-glt3313769671]] contrasts Malcolm II's chosen grandson Duncan with Macbeth's ancestry, Moray kingship, and Gruoch's lineage.
- Conflict and memory: [[104-macbeth-glt3313769671]] presents Duncan's attack, Macbeth's battlefield victory, and the later restoration of Duncan's line as the sequence behind the usurper narrative.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode simplifies a sparse and disputed early-medieval record. It does not fully specify the legal rules, terminology, eligibility boundaries, regional institutions, or how selection worked in each reign. Competitive succession explains recurrent conflict but does not make every claimant equally legitimate or every killing politically accepted.

## What Changed

- Created a framework for reading Macbeth's accession inside royal-kin competition rather than simple primogeniture.
