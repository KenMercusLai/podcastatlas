# Colonial War as Military Incubator

## Definition

Colonial war as military incubator is the process by which an overseas conflict develops officers, specialized forces, coercive practices, operational methods, and legitimating myths that later move into domestic or larger international war.

## Current Synthesis

The [[RifWar]] case links institutional failure to institutional rebuilding. Annual discredited Spain's weak conscript army but strengthened Africanist arguments for professional colonial formations. Moroccan regulares and the Foreign Legion created the [[ArmyOfAfrica]], while commanders such as [[FranciscoFranco]] accumulated battlefield authority in an environment where racialized contempt, massacre, and extreme violence were normalized.

The incubation was technical as well as political. Chemical weapons, aerial bombing, tanks, landing craft, naval fire, and air support appeared in the same conflict, culminating at Alhucemas. Transfer should not be overstated: later wars had independent causes and very different scales, but Morocco supplied trained people, organizational capacity, practices, and narratives that could be reused.

## Key Claims

- Colonial defeat can strengthen military factions that blame civilian politics and demand professional autonomy.
- Specialized colonial forces concentrate combat experience unavailable to much of the metropolitan army.
- Racialized distance can lower restraints on coercive methods and normalize extreme violence.
- Operational experimentation in peripheral war can precede later combined-arms practice.
- Battlefield survival and success can become political mythology around particular commanders.
- Transfer creates enabling capacity and repertoire, not a complete causal explanation of later conflict.

## Evidence

- Institutional rebuilding: [[282-morocco-the-rif-war-glt7389759975]] contrasts the army defeated at Annual with regulares, the Foreign Legion, and the later Army of Africa.
- Officer culture: [[282-morocco-the-rif-war-glt7389759975]] connects imperial humiliation, contempt for politicians, colonial brutality, and the careers of Franco, Mola, and Sanjurjo.
- Technical repertoire: [[282-morocco-the-rif-war-glt7389759975]] describes chemical weapons, volunteer air raids, amphibious landing craft, armor, artillery, naval bombardment, and air support.
- Political reuse: [[282-morocco-the-rif-war-glt7389759975]] treats the Army of Africa and Franco's Moroccan reputation as important resources in the Spanish Civil War.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Similarity does not prove direct transfer, and comparisons to Guernica, D-Day, Vietnam, or Afghanistan can become rhetorical shortcuts. The Spanish Civil War arose from many political, social, economic, and international causes outside Morocco. The concept is strongest when it identifies concrete personnel, units, practices, and narratives rather than claiming that one colonial conflict caused later wars.

## What Changed

- Added an institutional-transfer framework linking colonial campaigns to later domestic military power.
- Built a causal boundary between enabling inheritance and single-cause determinism.
