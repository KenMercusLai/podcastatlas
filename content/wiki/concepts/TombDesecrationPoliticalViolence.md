## Definition

Tomb-desecration political violence names grave robbery, mausoleum destruction, corpse removal, forced reburial, or corpse-directed violation when it functions as an attack on family continuity, dynastic memory, ritual order, and political legitimacy rather than only as theft. The pattern can appear in private revenge, rebel collapse, warlord extraction, or state-directed posthumous punishment, and it overlaps with but is not identical to [[PublicCorpseHumiliation|public corpse humiliation]].

## Current Synthesis

The concept now spans four bounded cases. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-626-waren-fenmu-wangmang-jing-ruci-sangxinbingkuang-lotwk6rbtl5pvbrnlfrysu5ol6rz|Hanji 626]] gives the state-directed posthumous-punishment version: [[WangMang|王莽]] uses rank and burial propriety to open, demote, strip, relocate, and level the tombs of [[FuTaihouWesternHan|傅太后]] and [[DingJiWesternHan|丁姬]]. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-903-weishenme-quan-ni-yuanyuan-xiangbao-heshi-le-lokpt93muc8kasvn44vgsjki8bom|Hanji 903]] gives the private-feud version: [[SuBuweiLateHan|苏不韦]] fails to kill [[LiHaoLateHan|李昊]] directly, then digs up Li Hao's father's grave and decapitates the corpse, extending revenge through ancestry and public shame. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-687-si-hou-200-nian-luzhi-jing-zao-qi-chi-da-ru-lhakzy89w1l4vhazjuyenbjrw9iz|Hanji 687]] gives the rebel-collapse version: the [[ChimeiRebellion|赤眉]] are remembered as looting Western Han imperial mausoleums and allegedly violating [[LuZhi|吕雉]]'s corpse, while the host explicitly marks the exact Lu Hou report uncertain. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-952-danxing-haoshi-weishenme-mowen-qiancheng-li9vvaeaues9-fxy4e4yp-f4pugj|Hanji 952]] gives the warlord-capital version: [[DongZhuo|董卓]] loots imperial mausoleums and ministerial graves around [[LuoyangLateHan|洛阳]], turning relocation and plunder into ancestral and cultural rupture.

Across the cases, tomb desecration becomes politically legible because burial places carry identity beyond the dead individual. A grave or imperial mausoleum stores lineage status, ritual continuity, memory, and public claims about order. Violating it can therefore punish living descendants, mark a dynasty as unprotected, extract wealth from a collapsing order, or advertise that ordinary restraints have failed.

The concept remains narrower than general atrocity. It requires burial-space or corpse-continuity harm, not merely killing or looting. It remains broader than public corpse humiliation because it includes attacks on tombs, coffins, grave goods, ancestors, and mausoleum landscapes even when the body itself is not displayed.

## Key Claims

- Tomb violation in political crisis can attack public memory and ritual legitimacy, not just seize wealth.
- State power can frame tomb violation as ritual correction when burial rank and posthumous honor are declared improper.
- Private feud can use a dead ancestor as a proxy target when direct revenge against the living fails.
- Rebel collapse can make mausoleum violation a sign that dynastic protection and ritual boundaries have broken down.
- Warlord capital plunder can make urban destruction ancestral and cultural by adding imperial and ministerial graves to ordinary material seizure.
- Corpse-directed humiliation is an overlapping subtype, but tomb-desecration political violence also covers burial space, lineage memory, and regime continuity.

## Evidence

- State-directed posthumous punishment: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-626-waren-fenmu-wangmang-jing-ruci-sangxinbingkuang-lotwk6rbtl5pvbrnlfrysu5ol6rz|Hanji 626]] says Wang Mang pushes to open Fu Taihou and Ding Ji's tombs, recover seals and ribbons, replace or demote burial materials, and relocate or lower their posthumous burial status.
- Mass tomb leveling: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-626-waren-fenmu-wangmang-jing-ruci-sangxinbingkuang-lotwk6rbtl5pvbrnlfrysu5ol6rz|Hanji 626]] says more than 100,000 participants level the two large tombs in about twenty days and enclose the sites with thorns as public warning.
- Private-revenge claim: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-903-weishenme-quan-ni-yuanyuan-xiangbao-heshi-le-lokpt93muc8kasvn44vgsjki8bom|Hanji 903]] says Su Buwei digs up Li Hao's father's grave and decapitates the corpse after failing to kill Li Hao.
- Ancestor-as-proxy claim: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-903-weishenme-quan-ni-yuanyuan-xiangbao-heshi-le-lokpt93muc8kasvn44vgsjki8bom|Hanji 903]] makes the violated grave an indirect attack on Li Hao's family line and public standing.
- Rebel-collapse claim: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-687-si-hou-200-nian-luzhi-jing-zao-qi-chi-da-ru-lhakzy89w1l4vhazjuyenbjrw9iz|Hanji 687]] records the historical tradition that Chimei looted Western Han imperial mausoleums while Guanzhong and Sanfu were collapsing under war and famine.
- Corpse-humiliation overlap: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-687-si-hou-200-nian-luzhi-jing-zao-qi-chi-da-ru-lhakzy89w1l4vhazjuyenbjrw9iz|Hanji 687]] reports the alleged Lu Hou corpse violation but keeps its factual status uncertain, making it evidence for memory handling rather than settled event reconstruction.
- Warlord-plunder claim: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-952-danxing-haoshi-weishenme-mowen-qiancheng-li9vvaeaues9-fxy4e4yp-f4pugj|Hanji 952]] says Dong Zhuo's forces loot imperial mausoleums and ministerial graves around Luoyang.
- Cultural-rupture claim: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-952-danxing-haoshi-weishenme-mowen-qiancheng-li9vvaeaues9-fxy4e4yp-f4pugj|Hanji 952]] places tomb looting beside capital ruin, coercive relocation, and cultural inheritance loss.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The wiki should not treat every grave robbery as political violence. The concept applies when the source connects grave violation to revenge, regime collapse, dynastic memory, ritual order, or public humiliation. Hanji 626 is a state-policy case, but the source still leaves exact tomb geography, participant counts, and Wang Mang's inner motive source-scoped. Hanji 687's Lu Zhi corpse report is explicitly uncertain in the episode, so it should support the category as a remembered or recorded atrocity tradition, not as an independently verified fact. Hanji 903 is a private-feud case, not a state policy case. Hanji 952 is stronger for warlord extraction and ritual-cultural damage, but it still does not require that every looter had an ideological motive.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 626 as the state-directed posthumous-punishment version through Fu Taihou and Ding Ji.
- Expanded the definition from robbery and destruction to include forced reburial and official demotion of tomb status.
- Preserved the earlier distinction between burial-space violation and narrower public corpse humiliation.
- Reframed the concept across state punishment, private revenge, rebel collapse, and warlord capital plunder.
