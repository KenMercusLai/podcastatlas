## Definition
Fragmented foreign security intervention is a deployment into a crisis where several domestic, international, public, and private armed actors operate under overlapping or unclear mandates, making coordination, attribution, accountability, and exit harder.

## Current Synthesis
The Haiti case in [[08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5-08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5mp3aid6af5967413c30781a320173dchidd556eb54-6160-4]] combines an understrength UN-backed gang-suppression mission, Haitian police and army, a separate Salvadoran deployment, personnel associated with a private military entrepreneur, and entrenched gangs. Formal consent from Haiti's interim government permits outside action but does not create a unified command, shared rules, clear responsibility for abuses, or a common end state.

Fragmentation changes the risk of force. An independently aggressive contingent can escalate conflict, interfere with a cautious mission, make abuses difficult to attribute, and turn an urgent rescue or strike into open-ended war. The transfer of a security model is therefore constrained not only by tactics but by institutional fit, local legitimacy, detention capacity, intelligence, coordination, and an exit strategy.

## Key Claims
- Host-state authorization is not equivalent to operational unity or accountable command.
- Multiple armed actors increase risks of deconfliction failure, mistaken attribution, rights abuses, and mission competition.
- A force optimized for visible aggression may undermine a parallel mission designed around restraint or limited mandate.
- Domestic anti-gang success does not transfer automatically when institutions, territory, intelligence, and gang organization differ.
- Rescue, political signaling, gang suppression, and state stabilization require different success criteria and exit conditions.
- An intervention without a defined end point can convert a bounded trigger into a prolonged foreign commitment.

## Evidence
- Force fragmentation - [[08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5-08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5mp3aid6af5967413c30781a320173dchidd556eb54-6160-4]] identifies Haitian security services, a roughly 2,000-person UN-backed mission, Salvadoran troops outside that mission, gang forces, and personnel linked to Erik Prince.
- Mandate mismatch - [[08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5-08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5mp3aid6af5967413c30781a320173dchidd556eb54-6160-4]] reports that Salvadoran forces were not expected to join the UN-backed mission and might operate more aggressively.
- Transfer and exit limits - [[08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5-08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5mp3aid6af5967413c30781a320173dchidd556eb54-6160-4]] records skepticism that El Salvador's domestic model fits Haiti and notes unclear scope and end point.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source describes a fast-moving operation with incomplete public detail, so command relationships and mission design may change. A successful strike or rescue could still produce a bounded gain, and fragmentation does not prove inevitable conflict. Death, displacement, hunger, personnel, and territorial-control figures remain source-reported, while the episode offers limited Haitian perspectives and no complete operational documents.

## What Changed
- Created a framework separating legal invitation from unified, accountable operations.
- Added mandate competition, attribution risk, transfer limits, and exit conditions to the evaluation of foreign anti-gang deployments.
