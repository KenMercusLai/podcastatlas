# Campaign Logistics and Battlefield Selection

## Definition

Campaign logistics and battlefield selection is the process by which time, disease, provisions, transport, weather, routes, and crossings constrain where and whether armies can fight.

## Current Synthesis

The 1346 and 1415 campaigns show battlefield selection beginning long before opposing lines deploy. In 1346, [[EdwardIIIOfEngland|Edward III]] repaired a broken Seine bridge at Poissy, forced the Blanchetaque ford across the Somme before the tide returned, and then stopped near Crécy on ground suited to his prepared defense. Movement, engineering, water, pursuit, and timing produced the opportunity to choose rather than merely accept a battlefield.

In 1415, a late landing reduced the campaigning window; the prolonged siege of [[Harfleur]] consumed men and time; an eight-day food plan assumed a direct march; and Henry's refusal to authorize general plunder reduced local resupply. French control of the Somme crossings then worked as logistical warfare, pushing the English inland while hunger, rain, mud, and fatigue accumulated.

The English discovery and repair of two unguarded fords solved the immediate river problem but released French forces from guarding a wide barrier. They could now concentrate beyond the Somme and block the Calais road. Agincourt therefore emerged from competing route, supply, concentration, and reputational decisions rather than from either side freely choosing an isolated battlefield.

## Key Claims

- Campaign time is a consumable resource: late arrival and siege delay can defeat a wider plan without defeating the army.
- Disease and sanitation are operational forces because they reduce manpower and command capacity before battle.
- A fixed provision plan becomes dangerous when route length or tempo assumptions fail.
- Rivers, guarded fords, destroyed bridges, and damaged causeways can redirect an enemy without requiring decisive combat.
- Solving one chokepoint can enable enemy concentration by ending the need to defend many possible crossings.
- Honor, political reputation, and refusal to retreat can keep an army moving after the safest military option has narrowed.

## Evidence

- Time and attrition: [[488-hundred-years-war-the-road-to-agincourt-part-2-glt2110427983]] connects the late landing and Harfleur siege to disease, delayed movement, and reduced English strength.
- Provision assumptions: [[488-hundred-years-war-the-road-to-agincourt-part-2-glt2110427983]] records eight days of food for an expected direct march and growing hunger once the route failed.
- Barrier strategy: [[488-hundred-years-war-the-road-to-agincourt-part-2-glt2110427983]] describes guarded fords, destroyed crossings, and the English turn inland along the Somme.
- Crossing and concentration: [[488-hundred-years-war-the-road-to-agincourt-part-2-glt2110427983]] shows the English crossing at unguarded fords and the French then concentrating to block Calais.
- Engineering and crossing in 1346: [[319-hundred-years-war-triumph-of-the-longbow-part-2-glt3100937638]] connects repair at Poissy and the tide-limited Blanchetaque crossing to Edward's escape from river barriers.
- Selection after movement: [[319-hundred-years-war-triumph-of-the-longbow-part-2-glt3100937638]] shows Edward halting near Crécy and preparing ground suited to the battle he wanted.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept does not make logistics mechanically decisive: surprise, reconnaissance, engineering, discipline, morale, politics, and tactical choices also shaped both campaigns. The sources supply detailed narratives but do not independently verify every distance, ration assumption, troop count, crossing sequence, or commander motive.

## What Changed

- Added the 1346 Seine and Somme crossings as an earlier case where engineering and timing enabled Edward to choose prepared ground at Crécy.
- Distinguished Edward III's successful battlefield selection from Henry V's later forced approach to Agincourt.
