# Chocolate Label Standards

Chocolate label standards are the legal ingredient rules that make a candy package's wording economically meaningful in [[reeses-heir-vs-chocolate-skimpflation]]. The source says U.S. milk chocolate must contain at least 10% chocolate liquor and use cocoa butter rather than vegetable oil as the relevant fat. It also says peanut butter must contain enough peanuts to use that name, while peanut butter cream falls below that threshold.

The concept turns labels from marketing copy into a consumer-protection signal. A product can still be a familiar [[Reeses|Reese's]] shape, but if the package says [[ChocolateCompound|chocolate compound]], chocolate candy, or peanut butter cream, the legal label indicates that the product is not the same as milk chocolate plus peanut butter.

## Key Claims
- Food labels can preserve technical distinctions that ordinary package design blurs.
- A legally defined term such as milk chocolate creates a boundary between ingredient quality and chocolate-like substitutes.
- Ingredient lists matter: finding vegetable oil near the top of a label can support a [[Skimpflation]] interpretation.
- [[FoodAndDrugAdministration|FDA]] standards can shape [[ConsumerBrandMoat]] because the package either confirms or weakens the customer's belief in the brand promise.
