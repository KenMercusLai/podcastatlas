# 历史报道中的AI幻觉 / Historical Reporting AI Hallucination

Historical reporting AI hallucination is the source's warning that AI assistants can be useful for organizing historical-cultural reporting while still being unsafe as factual authorities. In [[ep264-tafang-tangshi-zhilu-shengtang-qixiang-shaonianxin-loo2dfuxffizpbl4si5nwcixnngi]], the reporters say tools such as Claude, ChatGPT, DeepSeek, Gemini, and Qwen helped with route planning, material sorting, and logic discussion, but also produced invented classical prose, book titles, scholar names, and unreliable chronology.

The concept is sharper than generic AI inaccuracy because classical literature and ancient history create high-confidence-looking traps: many claims depend on variant chronologies, poem dating, place-name change, lost sites, archaeological finds, and specialist scholarship. The source therefore treats AI as a draft assistant inside [[AIJournalismTrust|AI journalism trust]], not as a substitute for [[FieldworkAsKnowledgeMethod|fieldwork]], primary texts, expert interviews, and [[ObservationBeforeInference|evidence discipline]].

## Key Claims
- AI can help reporters explore structure, route options, and large bodies of secondary material.
- Historical and classical-literary questions are vulnerable to hallucinated citations, people, books, archaic phrasing, and overconfident timelines.
- A useful AI-assisted workflow still requires checking primary texts, chronologies, archaeological material, and expert judgment.
- Site visits and material evidence become more valuable, not less, when AI can generate plausible but false historical synthesis.
- The source's caution also applies to cultural travel writing: a coherent itinerary from an AI tool may hide disputed dating, lost locations, or false local correspondences.
