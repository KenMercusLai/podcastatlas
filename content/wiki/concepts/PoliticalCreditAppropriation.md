## Definition

Political credit appropriation / 善行君恩化 is the court practice of treating a subordinate's beneficial act as politically safe only when its public credit is attributed to the ruler. The concept includes both successful absorption of credit and punitive failure when the ruler sees independent ministerial virtue as a threat.

## Current Synthesis

The Qi restoration case supplies the successful form. [[TianDan|田单]] gives a robe to a freezing man; [[QiXiangWang|齐襄王]] fears the popular reputation created by that act; an unnamed artisan advises the king to praise Tian Dan as carrying out royal benevolence and to extend relief. The action remains beneficial, but its authorship is repackaged as ruler virtue.

Hanji 190 supplies the failed and punitive form. [[XiaoHe|萧何]] proposes opening unused imperial-park land to cultivation, but [[LiuBang|刘邦]] interprets public benefit as merchant bribery and an attempt to acquire popular support. No protective credit-transfer mechanism operates before imprisonment. [[WangWeiweiWesternHan|王卫尉]] wins Xiao He's release by defending the chancellor's duty and past loyalty, yet Xiao He's barefoot apology shows that reversal does not restore equal ownership of judgment.

Together, the cases show that public benefit and political safety are separable. A policy may help people while still threatening a ruler who experiences ministerial reputation as competing legitimacy. Credit appropriation can preserve the action and hierarchy at once, but it leaves the underlying insecurity intact.

## Key Claims

- Beneficial action can become dangerous when public gratitude attaches to a subordinate rather than the ruler.
- Reframing ministerial virtue as ruler instruction can preserve hierarchy without cancelling the public benefit.
- When credit is not absorbed, suspicion can convert policy disagreement into accusation and punishment.
- Past loyalty and formal office may rebut an accusation without eliminating the structural insecurity behind it.
- The pattern is about ownership of reputation, not proof that the beneficial actor intended political competition.

## Evidence

Successful absorption of ministerial credit:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-81-lishi-zaoqi-pua-jishi-lu-1-ljxbn4gznuohegzyee0-tudi9z-y|Zhouji 81 part 1]] says the Qi king publicly treats Tian Dan's kindness as matching royal instruction, then widens relief for cold and hungry people.

Punitive suspicion before credit can be absorbed:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-190-xiaohe-ruyu-lggvu7f-6lodqnb1eqnmdmq5c3gm|Hanji 190]] says Liu Bang reads Xiao He's cultivation proposal as bribery and popular-credit seeking, imprisons him, and releases him only after Wang's remonstrance.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Neither source proves that public gratitude always threatens rulers or that every ruler response is primarily about credit. In Hanji 190, merchant bribery is an explicit accusation while popular-credit anxiety is partly interpretive; the episode rejects the bribery claim but cannot directly establish Liu Bang's full inner motive. In the Qi case, appropriation also produces wider relief, so a hierarchy-preserving tactic can still generate real benefit. The concept should not erase differences between restored-state insecurity, imperial court discipline, land policy, and personal suspicion.

## What Changed

- Extended the concept from successful rhetorical absorption to punitive failure when ministerial credit remains independent.
- Added a land-policy case in which alleged motive displaces evaluation of the proposed benefit.
- Clarified that release or policy benefit does not necessarily resolve ruler insecurity.
