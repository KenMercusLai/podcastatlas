# Political Credit Appropriation / 善行君恩化

Political credit appropriation / 善行君恩化 is the court tactic [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-81-lishi-zaoqi-pua-jishi-lu-1-ljxbn4gznuohegzyee0-tudi9z-y]] attributes to the unnamed pearl-stringing artisan around [[QiXiangWang|齐襄王]]. When [[TianDan|田单]] gives his fur robe to a freezing old man, the king sees an independent act of popular kindness as a threat. The artisan's solution is to make Tian Dan's action appear to flow from royal concern for the people.

The tactic does not deny that Tian Dan acted well. It changes the ownership of the credit. The king publicly rewards Tian Dan, says his conduct matches royal instruction, and then orders broader care for cold and hungry people. In the episode's reading, the move temporarily converts a minister's reputation into ruler virtue and lowers the perceived threat.

This concept is narrower than [[PoliticalImageMaintenance|political image maintenance]]. It specifically describes taking someone else's virtuous act and reframing it as evidence of the ruler's benevolent will. It also exposes a limit in [[GoodIntentionsPoliticalLimits|good intentions political limits]]: in a court ruled by insecurity, doing good can require political packaging before it is survivable.

## Key Claims
- Public virtue can become dangerous when credit attaches to the wrong political actor.
- Reframing a subordinate's act as ruler instruction can preserve hierarchy without openly condemning the act.
- Credit appropriation may produce real public benefit, but its immediate function is managing ruler insecurity.
- The tactic works only provisionally if the underlying suspicion remains.
