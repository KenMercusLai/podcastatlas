# Peter Principle

## Definition

The Peter Principle is the tendency for hierarchies to promote people on the strength of performance in one role until they enter a role whose different demands they cannot meet.

## Current Synthesis

The principle identifies a prediction error, not a law that every promotion ends badly. Strong performance as a seller, craft worker, or specialist can be real while remaining weak evidence of management, training, coordination, or political skill. [[StephanieByrne]]'s self-demotion in [[the-laws-of-the-office-revisited]] shows that restoring role fit can be rational even when organizational status falls.

The organizational problem extends beyond choosing the stronger manager. In [[AlanBenson]]'s sales-organization research, top sellers were promoted more often but their teams reportedly performed worse than teams led by collaborative sellers; yet overlooking the stars reduced their subsequent sales performance. Organizations therefore cannot solve the mismatch while leaving compensation and status incentives unchanged. [[hamlet-was-wrong-hamlet-was-wrong|Hamlet Was Wrong]]

## Key Claims

- Performance in one role does not automatically predict performance in a role with different tasks and relationships.
- Promotion can remove an excellent individual contributor while creating an ineffective manager.
- Role fit should be evaluated separately from title, pay, recognition, and career progress.
- Passing over a top performer can damage motivation when promotion is the main visible reward for excellence.
- Better promotion design may require parallel advancement, higher specialist pay, reversible moves, or explicit management assessment.
- Self-demotion can be rational when it restores fit, autonomy, and useful performance.

## Evidence

- Individual role mismatch - [[the-laws-of-the-office-revisited]] uses [[StephanieByrne]]'s return from management to her former work as a concrete escape from the promotion trap.
- Sales-organization pattern - [[hamlet-was-wrong-hamlet-was-wrong]] reports that star sellers were promoted disproportionately even though their teams later underperformed those led by collaborative sellers.
- Motivation trade-off - [[hamlet-was-wrong-hamlet-was-wrong]] reports declining sales among stars who were passed over, linking role allocation to incentive design.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Neither source shows that promotion is generally harmful or that current-role performance contains no useful signal. The sales results are supplied through an episode summary rather than the underlying paper and may depend on firm, task, measurement, and selection context. Collaboration may predict management in some sales settings without becoming a universal management test. Lotteries, boss elimination, or deliberate promotion of weak candidates are provocations, not validated default remedies.

## What Changed

- Added sales-organization evidence for the cross-role prediction failure.
- Added the motivation cost of passing over top individual contributors.
- Reframed remedies around separating specialist reward from management allocation.
