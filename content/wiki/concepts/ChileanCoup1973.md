# 1973 Chilean Coup

## Definition
The 1973 Chilean Coup was the September 11 military overthrow of [[SalvadorAllende]]'s elected government and the opening rupture that enabled [[AugustoPinochet]]'s dictatorship.

## Current Synthesis
The two-part account explains the coup through interacting causes rather than a single mastermind. [[370-the-1973-chilean-coup-allende-nixon-and-the-cia-part-1-glt6405070034]] adds Allende's long electoral formation, the reform program, falling copper prices, inflation and shortages, institutional conflict, U.S. campaign and opposition funding, economic pressure, plans to block his accession, and the fatal attack on constitutionalist commander [[ReneSchneider]]. [[371-the-1973-chilean-coup-general-pinochet-seizes-power-part-2-glt3740766328]] carries those pressures through the Tanquetazo, [[CarlosPrats]]'s removal, military planning, the September 11 operation, and state terror. The United States helped make a coup more likely, but Chilean officers chose, organized, and executed it.

## Key Claims
- Allende's reforms, their social support, economic crisis, and institutional resistance were causal forces, not scenery around a purely foreign operation.
- U.S. intervention moved from electoral influence and accession-blocking plans into economic pressure and opposition support without proving operational authorship of the September 11 plan.
- The failed attack on Schneider shows that constitutional command could block an early coup route and that coercion could initially strengthen legal succession.
- The failed Tanquetazo served as both warning and rehearsal for later plotters.
- Prats's resignation and Pinochet's appointment removed a key command-level restraint.
- The coup's consequence was not only a change of government but the creation of conditions for personal dictatorship and state terror.

## Evidence
- Reform, crisis, and foreign pressure: [[370-the-1973-chilean-coup-allende-nixon-and-the-cia-part-1-glt6405070034]] combines reform support, inflation, copper-price decline, constitutional conflict, U.S. hostility, and opposition agency.
- Early coup obstruction: [[370-the-1973-chilean-coup-allende-nixon-and-the-cia-part-1-glt6405070034]] connects congressional and military plans to Schneider's constitutional stance, kidnapping attempt, and death.
- Command succession: [[371-the-1973-chilean-coup-general-pinochet-seizes-power-part-2-glt3740766328]] links the Tanquetazo, Prats's fall, and Pinochet's appointment.
- September 11 operation: [[371-the-1973-chilean-coup-general-pinochet-seizes-power-part-2-glt3740766328]] traces the seizure of Valparaiso, isolation and attack of La Moneda, and Allende's refusal to leave.
- Regime consequence: [[371-the-1973-chilean-coup-general-pinochet-seizes-power-part-2-glt3740766328]] follows early killings and detention into the Caravan of Death, DINA, and Pinochet's personal rule.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources dispute the simplified claim that Washington masterminded the September 11 operation, not the documented claim that the United States tried to prevent Allende's accession, destabilized his government, encouraged military alternatives, and welcomed his removal. The relative weight of government policy, copper prices, global crisis, domestic opposition, U.S. pressure, and military choice remains interpretive. Allende's possible referendum plan and the timing of Pinochet's commitment remain uncertain. Explanations for Chilean support do not establish legal or moral equivalence between political-economic crisis and military repression.

## What Changed
- Extended the causal timeline back through Allende's election, reforms, the Schneider attack, and failed constitutional obstruction.
- Specified electoral, economic, informational, and military channels of U.S. pressure while retaining Chilean agency.
- Added the March 1973 election as a failed opposition route before military overthrow.
