# Magnitude Bias

## Definition
Magnitude bias is the tendency to assume that a large, dramatic, or morally consequential outcome must have a correspondingly large, intentional, or narratively satisfying cause.

## Current Synthesis
In [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-0a3c8347ab08972a4dce8dba455358cf-53039697-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-0a3c8347ab08972a4dce8dba455358cf-53039697|Fluke: How Chance and Chaos Shape Our Lives]], magnitude bias helps explain resistance to ordinary or distributed causation. Conspiracy accounts of Princess Diana's death feel more proportionate to the cultural scale of the event than a car crash, even when the grand accounts conflict. The same expectation makes weather, fabric, timing, personal travel, and other small inputs seem unworthy of historical explanation.

The corrective is not to substitute a tiny-cause story automatically. It is to separate emotional proportionality from evidence, keep causal chains plural, and admit when the available record cannot support one complete account. Pattern detection and post-event commentary can manufacture a coherent explanation for almost any observed result; causal humility asks whether the same framework would have confidently predicted the opposite.

## Key Claims
- People often expect cause size, intentionality, and narrative drama to match outcome magnitude.
- Dramatic explanations can feel satisfying even when they are mutually incompatible or weakly evidenced.
- Ordinary accidents and small contextual details can contribute to major outcomes without becoming the only cause.
- Hindsight makes flexible narratives appear more predictive than they were before the event.
- A proportional story should be tested against evidence, alternatives, and its ability to explain opposite outcomes.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Large outcomes can have large structural or intentional causes, so rejecting magnitude bias does not justify minimizing power, policy, ideology, planning, or institutional responsibility. A small contributing event is not necessarily the most explanatory cause. The Princess Diana and punditry examples remain episode-scoped, and the source does not provide experiments measuring the bias or its prevalence.

- Created an evidence-calibration frame separating felt causal proportionality from demonstrated explanation.
