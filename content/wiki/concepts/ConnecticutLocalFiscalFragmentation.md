## Definition

Connecticut local fiscal fragmentation is the episode's account of how 169 largely independent municipalities, mostly separate school systems, duplicated services, and limited state redistribution concentrate public-service finance in local property taxes.

## Current Synthesis

The source presents high property-tax bills not as a single-rate problem but as an institutional financing structure. Towns and cities fund schools, police, water, transport, food programs, special education, and other services from uneven local tax bases, while duplicated municipal and school functions can raise costs. Reform therefore requires choices among state revenue, local spending, service consolidation, redistribution, tolls, fees, and taxes on high earners or large nonprofits.

## Key Claims

- Heavy local service responsibility makes property tax a central balancing mechanism for municipal budgets.
- Limited statewide redistribution leaves municipalities with unequal capacity and pressure to raise local revenue.
- Separate school and municipal systems can duplicate transportation, food, special-education, and administrative functions.
- Consolidation can reduce duplication but also redistributes authority, service design, employment, and local control.
- Tax reform is politically difficult because every revenue or spending alternative creates visible constituencies that may lose.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The source gives selected household bills and broad comparisons but no complete distribution, municipal budget dataset, effective-rate analysis, or interstate benchmark.
- Duplication is not automatically waste: separate systems can reflect geography, service variation, accountability, or local preferences.
- The source does not quantify savings from consolidation or behavioral effects from taxes and tolls.
- Recommendations are policy proposals rather than evaluated reforms.
