## Definition

禁令遵从不对称 / prohibition compliance asymmetry is the risk that a restriction removes a capability first from people willing to obey while actors already prepared to violate more serious rules retain or replace it, shifting immediate exposure without eliminating the underlying harmful intent.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-289-2-buyi-chengxiang-gongsunhong-yanyi-jingdian-zhichang-shengcunfa-lndfcv7sgoiz_xshfimuqkf6t_bx|Hanji 289-2]] supplies one ancient source-bounded case. [[GongsunHong|公孙弘]] proposes banning ordinary people from carrying bows because armed robbers deter officials. The opposing memorial argues that determined offenders already facing severe punishment may continue carrying weapons, while law-abiding people can lose a means of self-protection or become offenders merely by retaining it.

The mechanism is narrower than a general anti-regulation claim. It asks whether compliance, enforcement, substitution, legitimate use, and underlying motive are distributed evenly. A restriction may still work if enforcement reaches high-risk actors, replacement is difficult, legitimate need is protected, and the targeted instrument materially changes harm. The episode does not test those conditions or provide outcome data because the proposal is rejected at debate stage.

## Key Claims

- Formal prohibition and practical removal are different; actors willing to violate the ban may be the least responsive to it.
- A restricted tool can have both harmful and legitimate uses, so policy assessment must include the cost imposed on compliant users.
- Severe background penalties do not automatically prove deterrence, but neither does criminal persistence prove that every narrower restriction is useless.
- Historical precedent and ritual meaning can widen a policy debate, but enforcement behavior and substitution are the concept's central causal tests.

## Evidence

Unequal compliance:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-289-2-buyi-chengxiang-gongsunhong-yanyi-jingdian-zhichang-shengcunfa-lndfcv7sgoiz_xshfimuqkf6t_bx|Hanji 289-2]] says the rebuttal expects determined robbers to retain bows while compliant people lose them or incur liability.

Instrument versus intent:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-289-2-buyi-chengxiang-gongsunhong-yanyi-jingdian-zhichang-shengcunfa-lndfcv7sgoiz_xshfimuqkf6t_bx|Hanji 289-2]] invokes Qin-era destruction of weapons followed by violence with agricultural tools and sticks to argue that removing one instrument does not by itself remove violent purpose.

Legitimate-use cost:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-289-2-buyi-chengxiang-gongsunhong-yanyi-jingdian-zhichang-shengcunfa-lndfcv7sgoiz_xshfimuqkf6t_bx|Hanji 289-2]] presents bows as tools of ritual and lawful self-protection as well as violence, making foregone legitimate capacity part of the proposed ban's cost.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept currently rests on one short popular-history episode and a reported court memorial, not a comparative study of prohibition outcomes. The Qin analogy does not control for institutions, enforcement, weapon lethality, availability of substitutes, or changes in violence. “Law-abiding” and “criminal” are not fixed populations, self-defense claims can be abused, and regulation can reduce availability or harm even when compliance is incomplete. Nothing here establishes a position on modern firearms, public safety law, or any other technology with different risk and enforcement conditions.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from the episode's distinction between legal compliance and practical disarmament.
- Limited it to an enforcement test rather than adopting the source's argument as a universal rejection of prohibition.
