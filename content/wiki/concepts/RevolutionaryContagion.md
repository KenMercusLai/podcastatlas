# Revolutionary Contagion

## Definition

Revolutionary contagion is the process by which news, symbols, expectations, and repeated interactions among crowds and authorities make upheaval in one place intelligible and actionable elsewhere without requiring a single coordinating organization.

## Current Synthesis

In 1848, European newspapers were already reporting Palermo, Naples, and Switzerland before the Paris revolution. This shared information horizon allowed political actors to recognize local tension as part of a broader sequence. Contagion therefore meant more than copying a program: it changed expectations about what crowds might attempt, what governments might fear, and how quickly authority could dissolve.

The source also treats contagion as interactive and contingent. In Palermo, a poster announced a revolution before a real revolutionary committee existed; the expected gathering drew crowds and troops, and clashes helped produce the event. Paris likewise confounded authorities prepared for underground conspiracy when society broadly ceased to fear the forces of order. Anticipation, policing, crowd formation, and violence could thus generate the crisis they were responding to.

## Key Claims

- Cross-border reporting can make separate disturbances appear part of one repeatable political sequence.
- Contagion spreads expectations and repertoires, not necessarily one ideology or command structure.
- Government fear and preventive deployment can become part of the escalation mechanism.
- Announced action can coordinate crowds even when the organization claimed behind it does not exist.
- Accidental shots or clashes can transform gathering into uprising.
- Diffusion does not make every local revolution causally identical.

## Evidence

### European information horizon

- [[326-the-year-of-revolutions-1848-glt9746323554]] says news from Palermo, Naples, and Switzerland circulated before Tocqueville's warning and the Paris revolution.

### Self-generating escalation

- [[326-the-year-of-revolutions-1848-glt9746323554]] recounts Bagnasco's Palermo poster, the nonexistent committee, crowd assembly, troop deployment, and clashes.

### Beyond conspiracy

- [[326-the-year-of-revolutions-1848-glt9746323554]] contrasts French preparation for underground conspiracy with a broader collapse of fear and respect for the regime.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The contagion metaphor can obscure local causes, languages, social structures, and political goals.
- Temporal sequence and media awareness do not by themselves prove direct causation.
- State action is not the sole cause of upheaval; prior distress and political organization remain necessary parts of the account.
- The exact role of newspapers, rumor, and interpersonal networks remains source-scoped rather than quantitatively established.

## What Changed

- Established contagion as shared recognition plus crowd-state interaction, not simple imitation or centralized conspiracy.
