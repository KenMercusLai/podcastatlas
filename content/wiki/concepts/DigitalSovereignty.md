# Digital Sovereignty

## Definition

Digital sovereignty is the claim that a country, institution, or organization needs meaningful control over its data, AI infrastructure, legal jurisdiction, technology suppliers, and operational dependencies.

## Current Synthesis

The concept extends individual [[LocalPrivateAI]] into an institutional and national frame. A local product such as [[KindPrivateAI]] can keep one user's data off the internet, but sovereignty also depends on where infrastructure runs, which contracts govern it, who can access it, and whether policy can be enforced inside the relevant jurisdiction.

The Palantir-in-Britain branch makes the dependency problem more concrete. Even when a U.S. platform supplier solves real public-service data problems, reliance on American technology inside the [[NHS]], armed forces, or police can raise questions about procurement leverage, lock-in, public legitimacy, and whether the state can control critical digital infrastructure on its own terms.

## Key Claims

- Digital sovereignty is not only data residency; it includes governance over AI tooling, infrastructure, legal agreements, supplier power, and operational dependency.
- Cloud AI dependence can conflict with privacy, procurement, compliance, and national strategy when critical data is processed outside a trusted boundary.
- Legal obligations are only enforceable when the deployment path is clear enough to audit, control, and exit.
- The concept links individual privacy tools to institutional procurement because both turn on who controls sensitive data and compute.
- A foreign vendor can be genuinely useful and still deepen sovereignty concerns when it becomes hard to replace inside critical public services.

## Evidence

- Local-control frame: [[ep-47-the-ai-pioneer-who-decided-privacy-matters-more-than-hype]] has [[JonathanSchaeffer]] use Canada and Europe to argue that reliance on mostly U.S. AI companies creates sovereignty problems for sensitive data, models, and compute.
- Individual-to-institutional bridge: [[ep-47-the-ai-pioneer-who-decided-privacy-matters-more-than-hype]] connects [[KindPrivateAI]] and [[LocalPrivateAI]] to the broader need for local control over data and infrastructure.
- Public-service dependency: [[right-in-front-afd-could-win-german-state-6a97f0a5a4a22c581728b85f]] says [[Palantir]] is deeply embedded in British public services and describes worries about reliance on American tech firms.
- Exit and legitimacy risk: [[right-in-front-afd-could-win-german-state-6a97f0a5a4a22c581728b85f]] pairs Palantir's utility with concerns over politics, surveillance associations, lobbying, vendor lock-in, and the absence of strong British alternatives.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Digital sovereignty should not be treated as automatic rejection of foreign technology. The Palantir branch shows the opposite pressure: public agencies may depend on foreign suppliers because domestic alternatives are weak and the operational problem is real.
