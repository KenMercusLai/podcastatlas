# Spine-Load Tipping Point

## Definition
The spine-load tipping point is the source's model for the threshold beyond which otherwise adaptive physical stress becomes cumulative tissue trauma, pain sensitization, or unrecoverable training exposure.

## Current Synthesis
The source does not treat load as inherently harmful. Bodies require stress to maintain and develop capacity, but the effect depends on total exposure: force, repetition, range, speed, technique, fatigue, age, injury history, and recovery. A movement can be tolerable for several repetitions or short walks and painful only after accumulated volume crosses the person's current threshold.

This makes restraint an active training skill. Shorter walks, reduced end range, fewer swings, slower progression, or alternate exercises can keep useful activity below the pain or tissue threshold while capacity is rebuilt. Because the threshold changes with context, yesterday's tolerance is not a permanent prescription.

## Key Claims
- Adaptive stress and damaging stress differ by dose and recovery, not merely by exercise name.
- Repetition and total exposure can matter as much as peak intensity.
- Pain may appear only after a person crosses a cumulative threshold.
- Small reductions in range, duration, volume, or load can preserve practice below the trigger.
- Age, tissue history, sensitization, technique, and goals change the current threshold.
- Progression should expand tolerable capacity without repeatedly provoking setbacks.

## Evidence
- General stress model: [[build-a-strong-pain-proof-back-dr-stuart-mcgill-scim9911539455]] says body systems need stress but can accumulate trauma after a tipping point.
- Walking dose: [[build-a-strong-pain-proof-back-dr-stuart-mcgill-scim9911539455]] uses three pain-free 20-minute walks versus one painful 60-minute walk to show volume partitioning.
- Sport volume: [[build-a-strong-pain-proof-back-dr-stuart-mcgill-scim9911539455]] distinguishes golf motion from the number and intensity of repeated swings.
- Load progression: [[build-a-strong-pain-proof-back-dr-stuart-mcgill-scim9911539455]] connects overly fast deadlift progression and repeated bending with disc and collagen damage risk.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
A tipping point is a conceptual load-management model, not a measurable universal number. Pain is not a perfect tissue-damage meter, and staying below a symptom threshold is not sufficient when neurological deficits, systemic symptoms, acute trauma, or other red flags require clinical assessment.
