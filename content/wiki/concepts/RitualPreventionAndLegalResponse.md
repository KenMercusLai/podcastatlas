## Definition

礼教预防与法令处置 / ritual prevention and legal response is the source-scoped distinction between forming intention and habit before harmful conduct occurs and using public rules, rewards, and punishments once conduct has become visible. It is a claim about timing and function, not a simple choice between having ritual or having law.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-219-jiayi-de-zhian-ce-cangzhe-guojia-xingshuai-de-mimi-1-likaommqtr0c2luijqr-fum2uhjt|Hanji 219 part 1]] attributes the distinction to [[JiaYi|贾谊]]'s [[ZhianCe|《治安策》]]. The episode says law is easy to observe because it responds after an event, while ritual education is harder to measure because success may consist in a harmful intention never becoming action.

The episode still assigns positive functions to legal governance. Rewards can encourage approved conduct, punishment can restrain wrongdoing, predictable rules can create public expectations, and impartial enforcement can support trust. Ritual is ranked higher only in the sense that it aims to shape desire, shame, habit, and ordinary conduct before adjudication becomes necessary.

The crown-prince branch makes the mechanism concrete. Repeated exposure to upright tutors, companions, speech, and conduct is supposed to become second nature, while Hu Hai's instruction in interrogation and severe punishment under [[ZhaoGao|赵高]] is presented as the negative case. Prevention therefore operates through environment and habituation, not only through ceremonial performance.

The source evaluates the model by contrasting long-lived Xia, Shang, and Zhou rule with Qin's short duration. That comparison supplies a political warning for Han, but it cannot isolate ritual-law balance from succession, war, extraction, institutions, elite conflict, or other causes of dynastic survival.

## Key Claims

- Ritual education is presented as working before harmful conduct by shaping intention, habit, and ordinary expectations.
- Law is presented as most visible after conduct occurs through judgment, reward, and punishment.
- Preventive priority does not eliminate law; predictable and impartial enforcement retains positive governing value.
- The effects of successful prevention can be hard to measure because the avoided event never becomes visible.
- Heir education illustrates the theory by treating repeated social environment as political character formation.
- A coercive curriculum can normalize violent rule just as an upright environment is expected to normalize restraint.
- Dynastic longevity is used as a source-level test of the theory, not as controlled proof of a single causal variable.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The evidence inventory currently contains one popular-history episode rather than a primary-text comparison or a cross-dynastic study. The precise meanings of 礼, 教, 法, reward, punishment, and the quoted formulations require checking against 《治安策》 and transmitted histories. Avoided harms are genuinely difficult to observe, but that difficulty cannot itself prove that a preventive institution worked. The Three Dynasties–Qin comparison also bundles many political differences, so it cannot establish that ritual priority caused longevity or that law-centered government alone caused Qin's fall. Finally, prevention through hierarchy and habituation can suppress independent judgment as well as harmful intention; this episode does not test that risk.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Hanji 219 part 1's explicit distinction between pre-action ritual formation and post-action legal response.
- Preserved the source's positive account of predictable, impartial rewards and punishments.
- Connected the abstract distinction to crown-prince environment and habituation.
- Limited the dynasty-duration comparison to source-scoped causal evidence.
