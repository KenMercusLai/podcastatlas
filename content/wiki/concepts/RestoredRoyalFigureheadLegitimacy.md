# Restored Royal Figurehead Legitimacy / 复国王室名义合法性

Restored royal figurehead legitimacy / 复国王室名义合法性 is the mechanism [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-130-3-weihe-li-mutong-wei-chuwang-limian-dayou-wenzhang-lmcuvrxer-cou6gmlppqwhb3n4vz|Qinji 130-3]] uses to explain why [[XiangLiang|项梁]] installs [[MiXin|芈心]] as a new Chu Huai Wang. Mi Xin is politically useful not because he commands troops or institutions, but because his descent from [[ChuHuaiwang|楚怀王]] lets Xiang Liang attach the anti-Qin campaign to old Chu royal memory.

The concept is a more specific case inside [[SixStateRestorationAntiQinStrategy|六国复国式反秦策略]]. Six-state restoration can create many state centers against [[QinState|秦国]]; figurehead legitimacy names the narrower act of putting a low-power royal descendant in a high-symbolic position so that a military leader can inherit emotional and moral capital without taking the royal title directly.

The source also makes the mechanism different from [[RebelMandateManufacture|起义天命制造]]. Chen Sheng and Wu Guang fabricate signs and borrow living-or-dead names to make uprising look destiny-backed. Xiang Liang instead uses a real old-house descendant and a remembered royal grievance, making the legitimacy claim genealogical and reputational rather than omen-based.

It is also different from [[ClaimRestraintThroughAttachment|有所归属式名位节制]]. Chen Ying refuses a top title to reduce exposure; Xiang Liang refuses the royal title because someone else's name is more mobilizing than his own. In both cases title restraint is useful, but the underlying logic differs.

[[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-132-5wan-wanbao-40wan-xiangyu-cizhan-fengshen-lk87nb3fskg5zh6fcgptypixhzuw|Qinji 132]] adds an important sequel: a figurehead can become a real allocator when the military patron dies. After [[ZhangHanQin|章邯]] kills Xiang Liang at [[Dingtao|定陶]], [[XiangYu|项羽]], [[LiuBang|刘邦]], and [[LuChenQinRebel|吕臣]] move Mi Xin to [[Xuzhou|彭城]], but Mi Xin then merges armies, grants offices, recognizes [[WeiBao|魏豹]] as Wei king, and issues [[HuaiwangAgreement|怀王之约]].

This does not erase the original figurehead logic. Mi Xin's authority still depends on old Chu royal memory, but the episode shows that symbolic legitimacy can become institutional leverage when the surrounding military hierarchy is disrupted.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-149-xichu-bawang-xiangyu-weihe-shijun-1-lsqxs2hzu0ngnrpmzsklkqhtyuvw|Hanji 149]] adds the lethal sequel. Once [[XiangYu|项羽]] no longer wants to use [[MiXin|芈心 / 楚怀王]] as a figurehead, Mi Xin's residual title still matters because another actor might use him. The episode reads Yi Emperor's murder as Xiang Yu's attempt to prevent Liu Bang or other lords from turning Mi Xin into a political banner.

This makes the concept double-edged. A restored royal figurehead can help gather legitimacy while useful, become an allocator when the patron dies, and later become dangerous to a military overlord precisely because the symbol has not lost public meaning.

## Key Claims
- A powerless royal descendant can still supply legitimacy if the old house retains public sympathy or historical grievance.
- The figurehead lets a military organizer claim continuity without exposing himself as a bare usurper.
- The mechanism works best when the old title carries emotional memory, as with Chu Huai Wang's death in Qin captivity and [[ChuThreeHouseholdsAntiQinMemory|楚虽三户式反秦记忆]].
- It can stabilize one rebel camp while still failing to solve broader coalition problems among other restored states.
- The concept should be kept source-grounded, because the figurehead's symbolic value does not prove real command authority.
- Qinji 132 adds that figurehead legitimacy can become command authority after a patron's death, especially when the figurehead controls appointments and reward rules.
- Hanji 149 adds that symbolic legitimacy can remain threatening after practical power is stripped away, because others may still use the figurehead as a banner.
