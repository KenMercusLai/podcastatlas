## Definition
Financial stress cognitive tax is the mental load created when a household must continually calculate, compare, and justify small cost-saving choices because prices and income leave too little slack.

## Current Synthesis
The concept is grounded in [[LindsayCole]]'s [[PlanetMoney]] story. Her household's choices about showers, hot water, snacks, electricity, baking, car replacement, and children's activities show that budget pressure is not only a lower consumption level. It also takes attention, creates guilt, and can reduce capacity for work and family life.

## Key Claims
- The tax appears when ordinary routines become repeated price calculations.
- Small savings can be rational and still impose a large mental cost because the decisions recur.
- Guilt is part of the mechanism when a parent feels obliged to pursue every possible household saving.
- The concept extends [[AggregateIndicatorsLivedExperienceGap]] because headline inflation and wage data do not measure attention drain.
- It overlaps with [[KShapedConsumerSpending]] when some households spend freely while others must optimize basics.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept should not imply that every act of frugality is harmful. Some budgeting is useful, voluntary, or identity-forming. The cognitive-tax claim applies when financial slack is low enough that small recurring choices become compulsory and emotionally loaded.
