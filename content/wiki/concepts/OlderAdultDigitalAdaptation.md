# Older-Adult Digital Adaptation / 中老年数字适应

## Definition
Older-adult digital adaptation is the acquisition and selective use of new devices, applications, and online practices through practical need, accessible design, patient support, recoverable experimentation, and peer learning rather than an assumption that age fixes technical ability.

## Current Synthesis
EP109 presents older users as selective rather than uniformly resistant. Interviewees use payment, shopping, video, news, travel, teaching, reading, and work applications according to concrete needs. Short-video and audio platforms can be entertainment, but also routes into language learning, household skills, gardening, literature, and continued participation when reading becomes physically harder.

Learning style and confidence matter. Some parents prefer manuals and hesitate because an error may feel irreversible, while younger users are described as learning through direct trial. Appropriate guidance and visible recovery paths can narrow that gap. The pandemic teaching case adds a social mechanism: colleagues learning online-teaching tools together can make an imposed transition less intimidating. Adaptation is therefore not measured by using every platform, but by gaining enough control to meet chosen goals without avoidable exclusion.

## Key Claims
- Chronological age alone does not determine whether someone can learn a new digital tool.
- Practical goals make adoption more meaningful than pressure to use every available platform.
- Fear of irreversible error can inhibit experimentation even when interfaces are technically simple.
- Patient guidance, clear recovery paths, and peer learning can reduce adoption anxiety.
- Audio, short video, and online teaching can support access and learning while still carrying quality and distraction tradeoffs.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source provides a small, self-selected set of anecdotes and no usability testing or population measure of digital skill. Device cost, disability, literacy, connectivity, privacy, scams, platform design, work requirements, and access to helpers can all alter adaptation. Forced adoption during a crisis may produce functional use without comfort or durable mastery. Short-form platforms can teach useful skills while also fragmenting attention or exposing users to unreliable information.

## What Changed
- Created a needs-led model connecting older-adult technology use to error recovery, support, peers, and accessible formats.
- Distinguished selective competence from indiscriminate platform adoption.
