# Deindividuation / 去个体化

## Definition
Deindividuation is a context-dependent weakening of self-awareness, personal accountability, or perception of another person as a concrete individual under anonymity, group identity, arousal, distance, or responsibility diffusion.

## Current Synthesis
The current sources treat deindividuation as a risk condition rather than a personality type or automatic cause of cruelty. Darkness, masks, crowds, group language, shared righteousness, online handles, and fast collective judgment can make individual responsibility less salient and another person's pain less visible. The result can be lowered restraint, but context changes probability rather than erasing agency.

Status comparison supplies one route into the mechanism. When envy or contempt is shared inside a group, a target can become an enemy category, joke object, or status symbol rather than a specific person. Group approval then makes an [[InternetMoralTrial|online pile-on]] easier to join while empathy and counterevidence recede.

VOL.189 extends the frame outside crowds and the internet. A closed car can partially conceal the driver and replace ordinary face-to-face feedback with horns, lights, vehicle position, and maneuvers. This may loosen restraint or make other drivers feel like obstacles, but it does not prove that a person who rages in traffic behaves the same way everywhere, and it is a looser application than classic group-based deindividuation.

## Key Claims
- Anonymity, group identity, arousal, and responsibility diffusion can change behavior without establishing a fixed character trait.
- Deindividuation can weaken both self-monitoring and recognition of a target's individuality.
- Online handles, swarm language, speed, and visible group approval reproduce several classic risk conditions.
- Envy, contempt, and moral righteousness can make deindividuated punishment feel deserved.
- Vehicle enclosure and reduced face-to-face feedback may create a partial, non-group form of the mechanism.
- Reduced accountability raises risk but does not remove personal responsibility or make antisocial behavior inevitable.

## Evidence
- Classic and experimental frame - [[123-renxing-shiyan-dangxin-ni-ziji-779479569]] connects darkness, masks, group membership, anonymity, and responsibility diffusion to weakened self-control and antisocial behavior.
- Status-comparison route - [[145-jidu-yu-bishi-wo-gai-ruhe-duobi-zhe-shuangxiang-de-dujian-882378647]] links shared envy and contempt to group hostility, online attack, dehumanization, and reduced target-specific empathy.
- Enclosed-car extension - [[vol-189-lunuzheng-zhenxiang-ni-renzhule-shenghuo-de-qingxu-que-zai-kaiche-shi-shikong-lrhaufg_zvbv4wn86-vjh3q3n4ws]] compares the closed car's mediated communication and reduced interpersonal visibility with online interaction when explaining road-rage behavior.
- Repair direction - [[145-jidu-yu-bishi-wo-gai-ruhe-duobi-zhe-shuangxiang-de-dujian-882378647]] argues that individuating information, empathy, changed comparison frames, cooperation, and shared-unit design can make targets concrete again.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source notes summarize public-facing discussions rather than a systematic review of the construct or its replication history. Deindividuation should not be used to claim that anonymity always causes harm, that group participation removes agency, or that every angry driver has a stable personality problem. The enclosed-car application is a source-scoped analogy with classic deindividuation, not proof that all traffic conflict follows the same mechanism.
