## Definition

任命式间接报复 / appointment as indirect retaliation is the use of a formally prestigious or defensible personnel placement to expose a rival to danger, distance them from a decision center, or reduce their practical influence while preserving the appearance of recommendation or promotion.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-289-2-buyi-chengxiang-gongsunhong-yanyi-jingdian-zhichang-shengcunfa-lndfcv7sgoiz_xshfimuqkf6t_bx|Hanji 289-2]] attributes two examples to [[GongsunHong|公孙弘]]. He recommends [[DongZhongshu|董仲舒]] to serve [[LiuDuanWesternHan|胶西王刘端]], whose treatment of senior officials makes the post dangerous, and recommends [[JiAnWesternHan|汲黯]] for a metropolitan administrative office that the episode says removes a persistent critic from the central court. The first interpretation is also present in Hanji 367-1, but only Hanji 289-2 is currently in this concept's canonical evidence inventory.

The mechanism depends on a gap between nominal status and practical consequence. A role can look like recognition while increasing personal exposure, geographic distance, workload, or loss of access. Because ordinary appointment also matches the visible act, retaliatory intent requires more than an adverse outcome: timing, prior conflict, option selection, private evidence, repeated pattern, or implausibility of the stated personnel rationale must carry the inference.

## Key Claims

- Personnel authority can impose costs without demotion when rank, danger, location, access, and practical influence move in different directions.
- Formal praise or recommendation gives indirect retaliation plausible deniability and makes refusal costly for the target.
- An adverse placement outcome does not by itself establish revenge; legitimate staffing reasons and mixed motives remain live alternatives.
- Targets can sometimes limit exposure through refusal, illness, withdrawal, or by building authority in the assigned role, so the mechanism does not guarantee removal.

## Evidence

Dangerous placement:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-289-2-buyi-chengxiang-gongsunhong-yanyi-jingdian-zhichang-shengcunfa-lndfcv7sgoiz_xshfimuqkf6t_bx|Hanji 289-2]] says Gongsun Hong remembers Dong Zhongshu's criticism and recommends him to a vassal king known for killing senior officials; Dong later leaves through illness.

Prestigious displacement:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-289-2-buyi-chengxiang-gongsunhong-yanyi-jingdian-zhichang-shengcunfa-lndfcv7sgoiz_xshfimuqkf6t_bx|Hanji 289-2]] says Gongsun Hong frames Ji An as sufficiently prestigious to govern a difficult metropolitan district, while interpreting the approved appointment as moving him away from central decision-making.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept currently rests on one popular-history episode's motive reconstruction. Dong Zhongshu's and Ji An's appointments can have legitimate administrative rationales, and neither prior conflict nor beneficiary effect alone proves concealed intent. The source does not independently establish private deliberation, exact institutional distance, counterfactual candidates, or whether Han Wudi shared the alleged retaliatory purpose. The concept should therefore guide questions about personnel power, not turn every transfer, difficult assignment, or promotion into presumed punishment.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from two episode-attributed appointments whose formal prestige diverges from their alleged practical purpose.
- Made proof of concealed intent an explicit qualification rather than inferring retaliation from outcome alone.
