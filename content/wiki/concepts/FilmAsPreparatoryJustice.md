# Film as Preparatory Justice / 电影作为预备性正义

## Definition

Film as preparatory justice is the preservation and organization of testimony, everyday experience, images, absences, and interpretive language before formal political transition or accountability becomes possible.

## Current Synthesis

[[20-shiji-zhuanzhi-shi-dianying-xian-qingsuan-yi-bufen-gkwriaiosoyxcck-awttzvx8]] argues that cinema participates in justice without becoming a court. A film cannot determine criminal liability under judicial standards, but it can preserve how state power enters homes, bodies, work, childhood, surveillance, shame, silence, and memory. It can also record what official archives omit or suppress.

Preparation is active rather than merely preservational. Testimony gains meaning when joined to documents, propaganda, places, objects, reenactment, or deliberately visible gaps. Fiction can supply an experiential language for histories that remain censored or publicly unprocessed, while documentary can expose how witnesses and perpetrators remember, perform, deny, or revise the past. These materials may later support investigation and education, but their immediate value also lies in preventing complete erasure.

## Key Claims

- Film can support justice without possessing a court's authority to convict or impose punishment.
- Everyday scenes make institutional violence legible at the scale where coercion is lived.
- Missing images are evidence problems that can be addressed through transparent montage, testimony, models, text, and reenactment rather than concealed invention.
- Preserving material before political transition can widen future options for truth finding and accountability.
- Archival value depends on provenance, context, ethical construction, and relation to other evidence, not on footage alone.
- Fiction, allegory, fantasy, and slowness can preserve historical pressure when direct representation is unavailable or politically blocked.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Archival ambition does not guarantee factual accuracy, consent, representativeness, legal admissibility, or political effect. Reenactment can clarify memory or manipulate it; perpetrator access can reveal impunity or reproduce spectacle; and fiction can preserve complexity while also creating false recollection. The source offers a critical framework, not evidence that any particular film caused transition, prosecution, or reconciliation.

## What Changed

- Created the concept for film's evidence-preserving role before formal transitional justice is available.
- Distinguished a film's archival contribution from judicial proof and legal judgment.
