## Definition

Elective authoritarianism is a pattern in which a government wins meaningful electoral legitimacy but then uses that mandate to weaken judicial independence, opposition media, legal restraint, and rules limiting executive continuation.

## Current Synthesis

The source uses [[JuanPeron|Juan Perón]]'s first government to distinguish authoritarian drift from both a normal competitive democracy and an immediate full dictatorship. Perón won a fair 1946 election and held a congressional majority amid real economic and welfare gains, yet his government later purged the Supreme Court, took over opposition newspapers, punished disrespect toward public figures, and changed the constitution to permit consecutive reelection.

This sequence matters because popular support and material improvement can coexist with institutional erosion. Elections explain how power is acquired but do not guarantee that courts, media, opposition, or succession rules remain meaningfully independent afterward.

## Key Claims

- Electoral victory can supply authentic legitimacy without preventing later authoritarian consolidation.
- Welfare gains and favorable economic conditions can deepen mass support while institutional checks weaken.
- Court purges, opposition-media control, speech restrictions, and self-serving constitutional change are cumulative warning signs.
- The category describes a trajectory and hybrid condition, not necessarily a completed dictatorship.

## Evidence

- Electoral legitimacy and performance: [[496-evita-the-worlds-most-powerful-woman-part-3-glt3440260328]] describes Perón's fair 1946 victory, congressional majority, export income, and improved conditions for many Argentines.
- Institutional erosion: [[496-evita-the-worlds-most-powerful-woman-part-3-glt3440260328]] identifies the Supreme Court purge, opposition-newspaper takeovers, laws against disrespect, and constitutional change enabling consecutive reelection.
- Charismatic reinforcement: [[496-evita-the-worlds-most-powerful-woman-part-3-glt3440260328]] links these developments to welfare delivery, spectacle, labor loyalty, and Evita's emotional authority.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The current evidence is one narrative-history episode and uses "elective authoritarianism" as an interpretive label rather than a fully specified comparative measure. The source explicitly stops short of calling the late-1940s regime a complete dictatorship. The category should therefore preserve differences among electoral unfairness, executive aggrandizement, competitive authoritarianism, and closed autocracy rather than treating them as interchangeable.

## What Changed

- Established an initial synthesis separating electoral legitimacy from the continued health of democratic constraints.
