# TCM Spring Self-Care Boundary / 中医春季养生边界

## Definition
TCM spring self-care boundary is the separation between low-risk, adaptable seasonal routines and source-scoped liver, wind, dampness, phlegm, qi, yang, constitution, food-therapy, herbal, tea, and acupoint interpretations from [[MaoZheTCMDoctor|毛哲]] and [[DongRheumatologyDoctor|董飞]].

## Current Synthesis
The episode's most defensible practical layer is moderation. Spring temperatures can fluctuate, so clothing should follow actual conditions; gentle outdoor movement can be useful without turning “春捂” into overheating; ordinary seasonal food can take priority over premium ingredients; and sleep, mood, exercise, and diet slogans need personal limits rather than literal or extreme application.

The later episode adds fatigue, work stress, coffee, short rest, low-intensity movement, sleep, lipids, uric acid, and gout. Its strongest layer is again behavioral and triage-oriented: pronounced or persistent sleepiness, cognitive slowing, swelling, cold intolerance, loud snoring, unrefreshing sleep, or impaired function should not be explained away as “spring fatigue.” Coffee, strong tea, energy drinks, sugar, flower teas, and acupoints do not replace sleep or cause-based assessment.

The clinical boundary is equally important. The sources organize spring through the liver, growth, wind, cold, dampness, phlegm, qi, blood, yin, yang, and constitutional patterning, then connect those categories to foods, teas, herbs, moxibustion, and acupoints. These are claims from two TCM podcast guests, not validated diagnostic rules. Severe allergy, prolonged amenorrhea, pallor, persistent gastrointestinal symptoms, recurrent illness, marked cold intolerance, excessive sleepiness, breathing disturbance, substantial mood change, or other significant impairment requires qualified assessment rather than seasonal self-treatment.

## Key Claims
- Seasonal self-care should adapt to weather, tolerance, symptoms, place, and activity rather than follow one fixed calendar rule.
- Clothing, comfortable movement, regular sleep, ordinary food, and known-allergen avoidance form a lower-risk behavioral layer.
- The source's liver-and-growth model and its wind, dampness, qi, blood, yin, yang, and “发物” categories remain TCM interpretations rather than established diagnoses.
- Food, tea, herbs, patent medicines, moxibustion, and acupoints require indication, dose, interaction, contraindication, and evidence checks that the episode does not supply.
- Dangerous, prolonged, or unexplained symptoms override seasonal-wellness experimentation and need appropriate medical care.
- Stimulants and sugar can change alertness temporarily, but neither validate a TCM pattern nor replace sleep and cause-based fatigue assessment.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources provide no clinical validation that seasonal signs identify a liver, wind, dampness, phlegm, qi, blood, yin, yang, or constitutional pattern, and they do not establish the efficacy or safety of their foods, teas, herbs, patent medicines, moxibustion schedules, or acupoints. The proposed warming-and-brain-blood-flow explanation for spring sleepiness is not established here. Weather-appropriate clothing, gentle activity, short rest, or reduced stimulant use are not treatments for allergy, amenorrhea, anemia, endocrine disease, infection, gastrointestinal disease, sleep apnea, dyslipidemia, gout, or mood disorders. “Late sleep,” exact organ-clock timing, and an eight-hour ceiling remain source-specific claims rather than universal sleep guidance.

## What Changed
- Added fatigue, sleep, stimulant, office-rest, gentle-movement, and metabolic-health branches.
- Extended the boundary from one guest's seasonal model to two distinct source-scoped TCM accounts.
- Added excessive sleepiness, respiratory clues, swelling, cold intolerance, and functional impairment as reasons for cause-based assessment.
