## Definition

名位—实权错位 / nominal office–practical power mismatch is the condition in which an office's name or formal rank does not match its practical decision rights, administrative command, resource control, or accountability.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-2-ta-ai-da-huangdi-de-lian-weihe-neng-shanzhong-lhsygwstd8wysfxfgdbfkkyokscl|Hanji 333-2]] grounds the pattern through Chancellor [[ZhuangQingdiWesternHan|庄青翟]] and Censor-in-Chief [[ZhangTangWesternHan|张汤]]. Zhuang is formally Zhang's superior, but [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] repeatedly bypasses the chancellor to discuss state affairs with Zhang. The title remains with Zhuang while decision access and practical influence move toward Zhang.

The mismatch is more than a prestige insult. It obscures responsibility, weakens the formal superior's ability to perform the office, and gives both actors reasons to monitor each other. The episode treats this as a fragile equilibrium preceding their later conflict, but the source does not prove that mismatch alone causes the Zhang Tang case.

[[16-dongxifang-de-zaixiang-lpor4-tu5o02vkbwd58e2tgl3rp|Weird History 16]] broadens the pattern across Chinese and comparative government. It proposes a functional test for chief ministers: participation in the highest decisions plus leadership or supervision of officials, supplemented in comparative cases by control of usable administrative resources and the office's line of accountability. On this account, palace secretariats can acquire governing substance while older high offices become honorific, and a translated title such as prime minister, premier, or chancellor can conceal very different constitutional positions.

The broader evidence also prevents one-directional reasoning. Practical power can migrate upward to a ruler, inward to trusted secretaries, sideways across several ministers, downward to an administratively stronger component government, or outward toward a legislature and party coalition. The concept therefore diagnoses a gap to investigate; it does not by itself identify who is legitimate, competent, or constitutionally supreme.

## Key Claims

- Formal rank and practical authority can diverge when the ruler bypasses the office hierarchy.
- Direct access, trusted interpretation, and policy execution can outweigh nominal supervisory status.
- A title without corresponding decision rights creates ambiguity about responsibility and accountability.
- The practically stronger subordinate and nominally senior superior can both perceive the arrangement as unstable.
- Chief-ministerial functions can be divided among drafting, review, execution, supervision, resource control, and legislative support rather than concentrated in one title.
- Translation convention cannot substitute for reconstructing the office's institutional setting and actual powers.
- Power mismatch is a risk condition, not sufficient proof of conspiracy or an inevitable later rupture.

## Evidence

Formal hierarchy:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-2-ta-ai-da-huangdi-de-lian-weihe-neng-shanzhong-lhsygwstd8wysfxfgdbfkkyokscl|Hanji 333-2]] presents Zhuang Qingdi as chancellor and Zhang Tang as the formally subordinate censor-in-chief.

Practical bypass:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-2-ta-ai-da-huangdi-de-lian-weihe-neng-shanzhong-lhsygwstd8wysfxfgdbfkkyokscl|Hanji 333-2]] says Han Wudi frequently consults Zhang directly, leaving Zhuang's chancellorship practically hollow.

Instability:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-333-2-ta-ai-da-huangdi-de-lian-weihe-neng-shanzhong-lhsygwstd8wysfxfgdbfkkyokscl|Hanji 333-2]] frames the arrangement as a tacitly maintained balance that a later case will break.

Comparative functional test:
- [[16-dongxifang-de-zaixiang-lpor4-tu5o02vkbwd58e2tgl3rp|Weird History 16]] distinguishes formal or translated titles from participation in sovereign decisions, supervision of officials, command of resources, and accountability to monarch, president, or legislature.
- [[16-dongxifang-de-zaixiang-lpor4-tu5o02vkbwd58e2tgl3rp|Weird History 16]] uses changing Chinese secretariats, the Ming cabinet, the Qing Grand Council, British cabinet leadership, and Bismarck's dual offices to show several directions in which title and governing substance can separate.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept rests on two popular-history episodes, not a comprehensive constitutional comparison. The Han source does not reconstruct the statutory jurisdictions of chancellor and censor-in-chief, quantify their decision rights, establish every channel Han Wudi used, prove Zhuang lacked all practical authority, or demonstrate that the mismatch caused the later investigation and suicides. The comparative source's two-power definition of 宰相 is an attributed analytical rule, and its translations, etymologies, dates, and office summaries require specialist verification. Differences in competence, delegation, emergency context, collegial government, federal structure, and ruler or legislative preference can produce uneven influence without nullifying an office.

## What Changed

- Expanded the concept from a Western Han hierarchy case into a comparative test using decisions, administration, resources, and accountability.
- Added divided functions and translation convention as reasons not to infer practical power from one title.
