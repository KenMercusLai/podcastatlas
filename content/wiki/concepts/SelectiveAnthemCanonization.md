# Selective Anthem Canonization

## Definition

Selective anthem canonization is the settlement in which a state retains an anthem tradition but makes only part of it publicly authoritative, usually to preserve continuity while containing a politically tainted layer.

## Current Synthesis

The German case makes the mechanism clear. Modern [[Germany]] keeps the melody and song lineage of the [[GermanNationalAnthem]], but the official public anthem is effectively the third verse, "Einigkeit und Recht und Freiheit." The first verse is not treated in the episode as simply illegal or erased; it is conventionally excluded because [[NaziGermany]]'s use made "Deutschland über alles" politically toxic in postwar public ritual.

This differs from replacing a symbol and from fusing rival symbols. It uses continuity, but narrows the canon so public ceremony can attach to a safer internal portion of the inherited song.

## Key Claims

- Selective canonization keeps an inherited anthem usable by privileging one verse, lyric, or performance convention.
- The mechanism works when abandonment would sever too much national continuity but full restoration would carry unacceptable memory burdens.
- Convention can matter as much as formal law; the German source stresses that the first two verses were not legally banned but became excluded from public singing.
- Selective use does not erase the tainted material; public controversy can return when the excluded part is sung or invoked.
- The strategy contrasts with anthem fusion, where multiple traditions are combined, and with outright replacement, where the old symbol is discarded.

## Evidence

- Third-verse settlement: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] says modern Germany sings the third verse of *Das Lied der Deutschen* rather than the first verse.
- Postwar compromise: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] says [[WestGermany]] restored the anthem through a compromise in which only the third verse would be sung.
- Convention boundary: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] says the first two verses were not legally banned but became excluded by convention.
- Flashpoint risk: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] says 1954 World Cup reports and later first-verse controversies show the excluded layer can still reopen memory conflict.
- Replacement alternatives: [[679-germany-the-song-hitler-stole-part-3-glt6217148052]] compares the settlement with [[AuferstandenAusRuinen]] and [[Kinderhymne]], both of which were not adopted by reunified Germany.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Selective canonization depends on public compliance and shared memory discipline. It can make ceremony possible without resolving why the excluded material remains meaningful to fringe actors, critics, or older constituencies. The German case also shows that narrowing the official anthem may leave rival alternatives aesthetically or morally attractive to some listeners.
