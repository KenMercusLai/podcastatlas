# China Divorce Restrictions

## Definition
China divorce restrictions are the legal, administrative, evidentiary, economic, and cultural constraints that can make leaving a marriage slower or less practically available in China.

## Current Synthesis
The sources converge on a distinction between reducing recorded divorce and improving family wellbeing. A 30-day administrative cooling-off period may interrupt some impulsive decisions, but the practitioner account shows that it also requires continued cooperation from couples who may already have negotiated property and child arrangements. Where one spouse withdraws consent, the alternative can involve slow litigation and difficult proof of violence, separation, cohabitation, or relationship breakdown. The policy sources place this machinery within a broader demographic context, but only one presents population management as an explicit framing and the practitioner source labels that explanation speculative. The current judgment is therefore procedural rather than motivational: exit restrictions distribute time, proof, safety, and bargaining costs unevenly, and their social effect cannot be inferred from a lower official divorce count alone.

## Key Claims
- Administrative cooling-off can affect considered divorces as well as impulsive ones because completion requires both spouses' continuing cooperation.
- Formal access to litigation or protective measures does not guarantee timely relief when evidence, time, money, and institutional support are scarce.
- Apparently neutral rules can impose greater costs on spouses facing violence, coercion, unpaid care, income dependence, or poor knowledge of family assets.
- Lower recorded divorce numbers do not by themselves show fewer harmful marriages or better household relationships.
- Harder exit may discourage marriage entry, although the scale and causal strength of that response are not established by these sources.
- Demographic policy is a plausible context for divorce restriction, not a verified explanation of every rule or case outcome.

## Evidence
### Cooling-off and repeated consent
- [[ep158-cong-lihun-lengjingqi-kan-hunyin-ziyou-ckwrijed9jaoabaaaace9iqg]] describes the 30-day cooling-off period, subsequent completion window, and the ability of either spouse to stop the administrative route before registration.

### Litigation, proof, and unequal exposure
- [[ep158-cong-lihun-lengjingqi-kan-hunyin-ziyou-ckwrijed9jaoabaaaace9iqg]] connects administrative delay to difficult proof, lengthy litigation, domestic-violence risk, asset opacity, and unequal bargaining power.
- [[fear-jerker-americas-ai-backlash-6a3cf783d760508ebaecd9fd]] adds contested-divorce standards, property allocation, domestic-labor compensation, and cultural censorship to the restriction set.

### Demography and marriage entry
- [[fear-jerker-americas-ai-backlash-6a3cf783d760508ebaecd9fd]] frames stricter exit as a response to falling fertility and argues that it can make marriage less attractive.
- [[building-things-and-breaking-things-in-china-summer-school-world-tour]] places divorce restrictions beside intrusive fertility planning, weak household support, and declining confidence in the boom-era marriage-and-children pathway.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The practitioner accepts that cooling-off may help some couples reconsider and does not claim that all delay is harmful. The three sources are podcast accounts rather than a legal-impact evaluation; they do not estimate how many divorces are prevented, delayed, diverted to litigation, or followed by reconciliation. The practitioner source's “flash divorce” figure, litigation timelines, foreign comparison, and population-policy inference are unverified or explicitly speculative. Legal rules and enforcement can also change after the 2021 episode, so this synthesis is not current legal advice.
