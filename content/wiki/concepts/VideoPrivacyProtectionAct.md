# Video Privacy Protection Act

## Definition
The Video Privacy Protection Act is a 1988 U.S. privacy statute restricting disclosure of identifiable customer information by videotape service providers, enacted after publication of [[RobertBork]]'s video-rental history.

## Current Synthesis
The statute now sits at a category boundary. Its original setting involved an identifiable customer, a video service, and viewing records; contemporary claims involve website analytics, advertising identifiers, and sites where video may be only one feature. The Supreme Court dispute described by [[tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128]] could either extend a concrete statutory privacy remedy into common internet data flows or limit a law whose transactional assumptions no longer map cleanly to the web.

## Key Claims
- The statute arose from disclosure of a nominee's video-rental history, making identifiable viewing records its historical core.
- Modern claims test who counts as a consumer, what counts as a video service, and whether analytics or advertising disclosures fall within the law.
- Consent can provide a compliance path but may add friction and does not by itself resolve every category question.
- Statutory damages can make technical compliance disputes economically significant even without individualized proof of loss.
- The law's current reach remains unresolved because courts have translated its older terms differently.

## Evidence
- Historical purpose - [[tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128]] connects passage to publication of Bork's rental history.
- Modern application - [[tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128]] says plaintiffs use the law against analytics and targeted advertising on sites with video.
- Remedy and scale - [[tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128]] reports Goldman's concern that a broad interpretation and statutory damages could sustain extensive litigation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode does not supply the case caption, briefs, statutory text, oral-argument transcript, lower-court holdings, or competing doctrinal tests. Its account is grounded mainly in Goldman's interpretation. A website's inclusion of video does not establish that every visitor or data flow is covered, and the Supreme Court's procedural or substantive disposition remains open.

## What Changed
- Established the statute as a modern website-analytics and advertising dispute rather than only a videotape-era privacy rule.
