# Family Rejection and Minority Stress / 家庭拒斥与少数压力

## Definition
Family rejection and minority stress is the pathway by which concealment, attempted correction, compulsory marriage or childbirth expectations, discrimination, and fear of lost support produce distress around a non-pathological identity.

## Current Synthesis
VOL.144's clinical and personal accounts converge on a relational source of harm. Some people accept their own orientation but encounter parents seeking medical confirmation or reversal; others fear disclosure because housing, education, livelihood, or emotional belonging may depend on family acceptance. Forced heterosexual marriage can also displace harm into a spouse relationship and present to medicine as sexual dysfunction.

EP109 adds an anticipatory, pre-disclosure layer. Parents discussing a hypothetical gay or lesbian child combine unfamiliarity, mistaken role assumptions, traditional expectations, concern about social treatment, and family reputation. Some distinguish private discomfort from a right to interfere, and some become willing to learn after an adult child explains that intimacy is not reducible to reproduction. This suggests that family resistance can change through knowledge and repeated dialogue, without making the child's legitimacy depend on successful persuasion.

Support therefore cannot be reduced to individual resilience. Family psychoeducation, carefully chosen disclosure, family therapy when appropriate, material independence, and a less punitive social environment may all matter. The phrase that a child comes out while parents “go into the closet” captures family adjustment, but it does not give relatives authority to redefine or cure the person.

## Key Claims
- Self-acceptance does not remove pressure created by family rejection or dependence.
- Marriage and childbirth expectations can turn identity conflict into medical, relational, and reproductive problems.
- Anticipated rejection can be built from knowledge gaps, role misconceptions, social evaluation, and concern about a child's future before any disclosure occurs.
- Attempts at correction can intensify concealment, anxiety, depression, insomnia, and withdrawal.
- Family members may need support and education without making their acceptance a precondition for identity legitimacy.
- Material independence can widen disclosure choices but is not a substitute for safer families and institutions.
- A parent's willingness to learn can improve the relationship, but private discomfort does not justify overriding an adult child's partner choice.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources supply clinical anecdotes, two personal accounts, and six parent interviews rather than prevalence estimates or causal identification. EP109 records hypothetical answers, not observed responses to an actual family disclosure, and stated tolerance may not predict conduct. Families differ in dependence, safety, culture, and change over time. Disclosure can carry material or physical risk, so encouragement of self-strengthening or dialogue should not be read as a universal instruction to come out.

## What Changed
- Added anticipatory rejection and acceptance before disclosure, separating knowledge gaps, social pressure, and claimed non-interference.
- Added willingness to learn as a possible family-change pathway without making identity legitimacy depend on persuasion.
- Preserved hypothetical tolerance as weaker evidence than conduct during an actual disclosure.
