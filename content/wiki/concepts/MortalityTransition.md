# Mortality Transition

## Definition

The mortality transition is the historically recent and uneven shift in which infectious diseases cease to be the dominant causes of death and chronic or degenerative conditions become more prominent.

## Current Synthesis

[[146-disease-vs-the-rise-of-civilisation-glt4238916512]] presents this transition as one of the deepest changes in human existence. It first became visible in Britain, the United States, and Western Europe in the late nineteenth century and spread unevenly, changing not only life expectancy but the expected shape of family and social life.

Lower childhood and infectious mortality reduces the number of births needed for population replacement, makes long investment in education more feasible, and can expand women's autonomy. It also resets moral expectations: deaths from smallpox, tuberculosis, plague, and similar infections move from ordinary features of life toward anomalous and politically scandalous events.

## Key Claims

- The dominant causes of death can change even when mortality itself remains universal.
- Reduced infectious mortality transforms childhood survival, family formation, and population replacement.
- Longer expected lives support extended education and other long-horizon investments.
- The transition can contribute to women's empowerment by changing reproductive and family constraints.
- Public expectations change with epidemiological success, making once-routine infectious deaths appear exceptional.
- The transition is geographically uneven rather than a single universal date or completed global event.

## Evidence

- Historical threshold - [[146-disease-vs-the-rise-of-civilisation-glt4238916512]] places the first crossover toward non-infectious mortality in late-nineteenth-century Britain and the United States.
- Demographic pathway - [[146-disease-vs-the-rise-of-civilisation-glt4238916512]] connects child survival to lower replacement fertility and longer educational investment.
- Normative pathway - [[146-disease-vs-the-rise-of-civilisation-glt4238916512]] describes infectious death as increasingly anomalous in developed societies by the mid-twentieth century.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode supplies a large historical synthesis rather than country-by-country mortality series. Timing, causal weights, disease classification, and the relationship between health change and women's empowerment require more granular evidence. Uneven progress and renewed outbreaks mean transition does not imply the disappearance of infectious disease.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to distinguish a change in the structure and expectation of mortality from a simple increase in average lifespan.
