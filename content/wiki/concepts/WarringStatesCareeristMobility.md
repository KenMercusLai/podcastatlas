## Definition

Warring States careerist mobility / 乱世职业经理人式流动 is the movement of persuaders, reformers, commanders, and scholars among competing courts in pursuit of office, recognition, safety, achievement, or a usable patron. It supplements state-interest explanations by treating portable skill, personal reputation, exit options, and patron succession as causal political forces.

## Current Synthesis

The complete evidence shows a political labor market with both constructive and dangerous sides. [[ZhangYiStrategist|张仪]], [[GongsunYan|公孙衍]], [[ShangYang|商鞅]], [[FanJu|范雎]], [[CaiZe|蔡泽]], [[LiSi|李斯]], and [[YaoJia|姚贾]] move because courts need skill but gate access unevenly. Movement can be self-planned, patron-enabled, forced by suspicion, or triggered by succession. A receiving state can convert an overlooked or expelled outsider into reform, diplomacy, or strategic advantage; the losing court can export talent to a rival through neglect or violence.

Mobility is not identical to opportunism. [[LeYi|乐毅]] changes courts yet refuses to use Zhao patronage for revenge against Yan, while Zhang Yi sometimes wraps self-preservation in state strategy. The same portability that creates bargaining power also creates doubt about loyalty, dependence on access channels, and exposure when a ruler dies. [[WarringStatesRetainerPatronage|战国养士]] supplies a social base by feeding and recognizing wandering scholars before their ability becomes office.

Zhouji 05 part 3 adds the everyday bargaining logic behind this elite pattern. [[TianZifang|田子方]] tells the crown prince that a poor scholar whose advice is rejected can simply leave, whereas a powerful ruler or great officer risks losing state or household by treating people contemptuously. Interstate competition and rulers' need for a reputation of honoring talent make exit credible. The episode's contrast with Qin unification suggests that centralization reduces outside options and shifts advancement toward state-controlled channels, though it does not prove mobility disappeared.

## Key Claims

- Interstate competition gives portable talent exit options and makes a ruler's reputation for honoring people strategically valuable.
- Mobility can be chosen for advancement, forced by danger, or used as an exit strategy after patron succession or factional defeat.
- Receiving courts gain when they convert outsider knowledge into reform, diplomacy, command, or access; losing courts may strengthen rivals by neglecting or expelling talent.
- Portable skill creates loyalty and information risks because access, networks, and expertise can cross borders.
- Patronage and private access channels mediate the market: talent alone does not guarantee an audience or appointment.
- Mobility can coexist with ethical limits, as when a minister leaves an unsafe court but refuses revenge against the former state.
- Political centralization can narrow bargaining power by reducing alternative courts and formalizing advancement channels.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

“Careerist” is an analogy, not a claim that Warring States actors operated in a modern labor market or lacked political loyalty. The sources often reconstruct motive from speeches and outcomes, and several preserve chronology or name problems, especially the Zhang Yi-to-Wei sequence. Mobility remained constrained by wealth, birth, patronage, travel, private access, violence, and ruler whim. The Qin-unification contrast in Zhouji 05 part 3 is particularly broad: centralized rule may narrow court competition without eliminating recommendation, informal access, coercion, or elite movement. Later examination systems also did not produce complete opportunity equality.
