# Buridan Indecision Effect / 布利丹效应

## Definition

Buridan Indecision Effect / 布利丹效应 is the decision-failure pattern where a person or organization freezes between options because goals, available resources, or choice criteria are unclear, causing delay to become its own cost.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 482 introduces the concept through the traditional "Buridan's donkey" story and applies it to management. It argues that when a leader cannot identify the goal or the chips in hand, the organization may spend scarce resources while competitors act, employees carry the risk, and the moment for action passes. Waiting for perfect clarity may feel responsible, but in competitive settings clear information is often public information.

Hanji 375-1 adds the simpler equal-choice mechanism. Its donkey starves between two indistinguishable piles of fodder because it keeps searching for a reason to prefer one. The source pairs that fable with [[ZhaoDiWesternHan|赵弟]]'s rapid action during a prisoner escort and argues that careful thought needs a stopping point. Together the episodes define Buridan indecision as unbounded comparison rather than caution itself: delay becomes pathological when additional thought no longer changes the ranking of options but continues consuming time, energy, or opportunity.

The combined evidence still does not justify “always decide quickly.” High error costs, reversible information gathering, and decisions lacking trained judgment can make delay rational. The practical distinction is whether waiting is expected to produce decision-relevant evidence under an explicit deadline, or merely postpones ownership of the choice.

## Key Claims

- Indecision is often caused by unclear goals and unclear assessment of available chips.
- Near-equivalent options can produce paralysis when the chooser demands a uniquely optimal answer that the evidence cannot supply.
- Organizational indecision externalizes risk to teams, employees, and families rather than staying inside the leader's private psychology.
- Waiting for complete information can destroy opportunity because rivals receive the same clarity.
- Too many voices and too many plans can paralyze decision systems when authority and criteria are weak.
- Good decision-making requires information triage, a stopping rule, and ownership of the action that follows.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Both sources strongly favor decisiveness, but neither supplies a complete protocol for error cost, reversibility, evidence value, or conditions where waiting is prudent. The concept should therefore not be flattened into "always choose quickly." Its stronger reading is that choosers need explicit criteria, resource awareness, a deadline, and a reason to believe further waiting will improve the decision.

The AI-assisted decision point is only a brief direction-of-travel claim. Hanji 482 does not specify a model, workflow, accountability boundary, or evidence standard for AI decision support.

Hanji 375-1's prisoner killing is an irreversible and violent case. It can illustrate action ownership under perceived time pressure without proving that Zhao Di's action was necessary, lawful, or generally exemplary. The episode's meditation and “sixth sense” suggestions also remain source-scoped; stillness may support attention, but this source does not validate intuition as an independent accuracy mechanism.

## What Changed

- Added the equal-options mechanism and a stopping-rule test: further delay is suspect when it no longer promises decision-relevant evidence.
- Qualified the Zhao Di analogy so decisiveness is not confused with proof that an irreversible action is correct.
