# Famine Entitlement Failure

## Definition
Famine entitlement failure occurs when people lose effective routes to food—through production, wages, exchange, credit, protection, mobility, or relief—even where some food remains in circulation. It can coincide with, rather than replace, an absolute fall in available food.

## Current Synthesis
The 1931 flood account makes the combination clearest: destroyed crops and livestock cut production, while lost earnings and credit erode purchasing and recovery capacity. Historical episodes from the same *Ruiqi Jiangtou Zizhi Tongjian* series offer distinct illustrations of access breakdown under war: looted households, diverted relief, flight from an abandoned capital, and grain held by some while others face extreme prices. They concern different periods and places, and the five historical episodes are one interpretive source cluster, not five independent corroborations of a single famine.

## Key Claims
- Flood can remove food-producing assets while simultaneously destroying wages, credit, and the means to buy or exchange for food.
- Relief can fail at the distribution stage when issued supplies are diverted or diluted, even if rulers recognize hunger.
- Prolonged military occupation and plunder can strip households of grain and protection before the wider political order collapses.
- In a capital's breakdown, people unable to flee or access supply channels are especially exposed.
- Extreme prices and unequal access to stored grain can coexist with aggregate production failure; the existence of stores alone does not establish their owners' political protection.

## Evidence
- **Production and purchasing channels:** [[65-longwang-zhinu-1931-nian-de-changjiang-hongshui-658100922]] recounts the 1931 Yangtze flood's lost harvests, livestock, wages, and credit. Livestock meant savings, labor, transport, fertilizer, and recovery capacity, not just food. Some survivors still had markets, loans, or stores while others ate husks, roots, bark, leaves, and lotus products; relief-for-work could misread people too hungry to work. The discussion places [[NationalistGovernment|government]] and [[ChinaInternationalFamineReliefCommission|charitable relief]] within this uneven response; debt could restart production yet burden later recovery. This is a podcast discussion of a history book, not an independent estimate of flood mortality or famine prevalence.
- **Relief delivery:** [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-979-zaoyu-tongliao-beici-ta-ruhe-wei-caocao-liwan-kuanglan-luu58a2ww11g-radh-5g4l7fcis]] recounts [[EmperorXianOfHan|Emperor Xian]] testing a rice-and-bean gruel allocation in drought-stricken [[ChangAnHanCapital|Chang'an]] and exposing [[HouWenLateHan|Hou Wen]]'s diversion. Food issued from a store need not reach the intended recipients; an emperor's audit of one channel did not restore a captured court's general capacity.
- **Armed extraction:** [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-969-yuanshi-xiongdi-neidou-shui-neng-jianlou-lsgj6sewo35jpsgfm-koivijtr6r]] connects the long [[TianKaiLateHan|Tian Kai]]–[[YuanTan|Yuan Tan]] standoff in [[QingzhouLateHan|Qingzhou]] to exhausted armies seizing grain from civilians. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-984-liangzhouxi-junfa-neidou-ta-weihe-di-yige-daoxia-lurb2qfzsa7e-owh-dl-m-sccze5]] places Sanfu famine beside armed plunder amid the split of the [[LiangzhouWarlordClique|Liangzhou military bloc]] around [[LiJueLateHan|Li Jue]] and [[GuoSiLateHan|Guo Si]], including [[FanChouLateHan|Fan Chou]]'s downfall. These are different late-Han contexts presented by the same narrator; the latter episode predates the capital-flight collapse.
- **Loss of exit and order:** [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-992-luanshi-you-duo-kepa-jiemi-changan-de-xuese-mengyan-ltuxzrb-1x5y2ctwo4drggxn5stz]] reports strong residents scattering after [[EmperorXianOfHan|Emperor Xian]]'s departure from [[ChangAnHanCapital|Chang'an]] while weaker people remain amid disorder and hunger. Lost mobility and protection, not just market purchasing power, matter here. The phrase about the weak eating one another is attributed to the historical narrative, not independently verified here.
- **Price and stores:** [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-155-1-lishi-jizai-shenme-qingkuang-hui-fasheng-ren-chi-ren-canju-lrwz-prnxd8hzchagyrbywkux7lh]] reports Chu-Han-era [[GuanzhongRegion|Guanzhong]] grain at an extreme stated price of ten thousand coins per unit, depopulated fields, migration permitted toward Ba-Shu and Hanzhong, and [[RenShiQinMerchant|Ren Shi]]'s profitable grain stores while ordinary households faced hunger. The host's suggestion that protection enabled his hoarding is an inference, not a documented transaction in this note.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Neither the flood nor the war cases can be reduced to distribution alone: crop destruction and wartime loss of agricultural labor also shrink supply. The historical episodes span Chu-Han and late Han and draw on one retelling series; their quoted prices and cannibalism reports remain source-attributed. The 1931 account likewise does not independently verify quantitative death or disease figures. Ren Shi's access to protection is conjectural, and surviving stores do not by themselves prove a functioning food market for the poor.

## What Changed
- Reframed famine as an interaction of supply loss and broken routes to food rather than a choice between the two.
- Grouped historical cases by relief, armed extraction, flight, and unequal exchange instead of episode order.
- Kept the shared narrator, historical attribution, and speculative protection claim visible.
