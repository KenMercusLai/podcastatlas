## Definition

Mexica sacrificial practice is interpreted here through overlapping religious obligations to sustain or repay divine powers and political uses of captive death as imperial intimidation.

## Current Synthesis

The episode rejects two flattening responses: denying sacrifice because colonial accounts can sensationalize it, and treating sacrifice as the essence of Mexica culture. It places blood offering within a wider reciprocal theology in which humans owed gratitude and costly return to the gods; human life, especially that of a strong captured warrior, could be understood as the greatest offering.

Imperial growth altered that practice. Captives taken from subordinate or hostile regions became both offerings and instruments of rule, and selected witnesses could carry fear back to communities beyond the capital. Priestly institutions and ritual embodiment, including the wearing of flayed skins associated with [[XipeTotec]], helped sustain the system. Poetry emphasizing life's beauty and violence's tragedy shows that ritual participation did not eliminate moral complexity.

## Key Claims

- Religious reciprocity explains how sacrifice could be intelligible to participants without making it morally harmless.
- Captive sacrifice connected ritual practice to warfare and imperial expansion.
- Public spectacle could serve deterrence and political intimidation as well as worship.
- Bloodletting and other bodily offerings place human sacrifice within a broader sacrificial field.
- Poetry, mourning, and reverence for life complicate claims that Mexica society simply celebrated killing.

## Evidence

- Sacred obligation: [[51-aztecs-glt9590586130]] connects gratitude to the gods with escalating value in offerings and human life as the greatest gift.
- Imperial intimidation: [[51-aztecs-glt9590586130]] describes captive sacrifice and the return of witnesses as instruments for projecting fear across a far-flung empire.
- Ritual range and institution: [[51-aztecs-glt9590586130]] discusses bloodletting, child sacrifice in Tlaloc ceremonies, flayed-skin embodiment, and a professional priestly cohort.
- Moral complexity: [[51-aztecs-glt9590586130]] connects songs and poems more often to tragedy, flowers, and the preciousness of life than to killing as an intrinsic good.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This synthesis relies on one interview and does not establish numbers, frequency, change over time, regional variation, captive demographics, children's origins, drug use, witnesses' selection, priestly motives, or the exact relation between theology and state strategy. Spanish sources can exaggerate for polemical ends, while that bias does not justify denying practices supported by other evidence. Explanation of religious and political function is not exculpation.

## What Changed

- Established a dual religious-and-imperial model instead of treating sacrifice as either pure devotion or pure terror.
- Added bloodletting, ritual embodiment, poetry, and institutional priesthood as context for human sacrifice.
