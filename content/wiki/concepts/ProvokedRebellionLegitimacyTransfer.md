## Definition

Provoked-rebellion legitimacy transfer / 诱发反叛式名分转移 is the episode's strategic pattern in which an actor applies coercive pressure without formally declaring war, induces the target to initiate resistance, and then uses that first move to recast aggression as legitimate suppression.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 1120 applies the pattern to the political opening of the 211 CE [[WeinanBattleLateHan|渭南之战]]. [[CaoCao|曹操]] publicly targets [[ZhangLuFivePecks|张鲁]] in Hanzhong, but the route crosses the power sphere of [[MaChao|马超]], [[HanSui|韩遂]], and other Guanzhong commanders who nominally submit to the Han court. The movement thus creates a credible threat without first naming them as enemies.

The episode argues that Cao Cao anticipates their defensive coalition and preserves the sequencing by ordering [[CaoRen|曹仁]] not to attack first. Once the coalition initiates hostilities, the public roles change: actors who feared preemptive attack can be classified as rebels, while Cao Cao can claim court-authorized suppression. The synthesis remains narrow because the available source demonstrates an interpretation of one campaign opening, not a general law or independently verified account of Cao Cao's intention.

## Key Claims

- A declared objective can create military pressure on an unstated target when the route or deployment crosses that target's security zone.
- Nominal submission makes direct attack politically costly even when the stronger actor sees the subordinate force as a strategic threat.
- Restraint over who attacks first can be instrumentally useful because sequence affects public classification, not only tactical position.
- A target may resist from genuine fear and still lose legitimacy when its first move is treated as breach of an existing arrangement.
- Family collateral can raise the cost of resistance and strengthen the stronger side's breach narrative without guaranteeing deterrence.
- The pattern requires caution because inferred intention can be mistaken for demonstrated planning.

## Evidence

- Indirect pressure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1120-tongguan-zhizhan-beihou-de-boyi-ma-chao-kaiju-jiu-zhongtao-lkchglvk7g5zxtohkuqi_4y4wdw3|Hanji 1120]] says Cao Cao's announced Hanzhong expedition must pass through Guanzhong and that Gao Rou predicts the western commanders will fear attack.
- First-move preservation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1120-tongguan-zhizhan-beihou-de-boyi-ma-chao-kaiju-jiu-zhongtao-lkchglvk7g5zxtohkuqi_4y4wdw3|Hanji 1120]] reports that Cao Cao orders Cao Ren not to attack the coalition first.
- Role reversal: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1120-tongguan-zhizhan-beihou-de-boyi-ma-chao-kaiju-jiu-zhongtao-lkchglvk7g5zxtohkuqi_4y4wdw3|Hanji 1120]] interprets the coalition's rising as the point at which Cao Cao becomes a suppressor of rebellion and Ma Chao becomes a breaker of the prior political arrangement.
- Collateral leverage: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1120-tongguan-zhizhan-beihou-de-boyi-ma-chao-kaiju-jiu-zhongtao-lkchglvk7g5zxtohkuqi_4y4wdw3|Hanji 1120]] links that role reversal to Ma Teng and the wider household remaining within Cao-side reach.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source itself reports Gao Rou's warning, which also supports a less intentional explanation: an ambiguous western movement could generate a security dilemma and preemptive resistance without requiring a fully designed provocation. The claim that Cao Cao already intended to force rebellion, knew Ma Chao would choose resistance, and used Zhang Lu only as a pretext is the host's reconstruction. Broader historical comparison is required before treating motive, exact orders, or public reception as settled.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Hanji 1120's explanation of the Tong Pass campaign opening.
- Separated observable sequencing from the stronger, qualified claim of deliberate provocation.
- Linked first-move legitimacy to both security fear and household collateral.
