## Definition
High merit threat management / 功高不震主 is the practice of preventing real contribution from becoming a perceived challenge to the superior or ruler who controls recognition, command, and punishment.

## Current Synthesis
Hanji 1065 defines the pattern by extending [[XuYouLateHan|Xu You]]'s fatal public credit claim into a general rule for power fields and workplaces. Merit is useful only while it remains aligned with the superior's current needs and status order. Once the organization has changed phase, old contribution can decay as protection while the perceived threat created by reputation, arrogance, troops, secrets, or public familiarity remains.

The episode distinguishes several mitigation routes. One can return credit upward, as the source says [[XunYu|荀彧]] does with [[CaoCao|曹操]]; reduce visibility and private entanglement after success, as it says [[JiaXu|贾诩]] does; exit or ritualize retreat, as with [[FanLi|范蠡]] and [[ZhangLiang|张良]]; or surrender command and keep humility after military glory, as with [[WeiQingHanGeneral|卫青]] and [[GuoZiyi|郭子仪]]. The concept is therefore not anti-achievement. It asks whether present indispensability is still greater than perceived threat, and whether public behavior lowers or raises that ratio.

## Key Claims
- High merit becomes dangerous when the superior's need for the contributor falls faster than the contributor's visible influence, entitlement, or command resources.
- Public scenes matter because credit claims, familiar address, and status joking in front of third parties can turn private history into visible hierarchy damage.
- Returning credit upward and sharing credit outward can preserve recognition without making the contributor look like the real center of authority.
- Lowering visibility after success can be rational when private networks, factional access, or public brilliance would create unnecessary suspicion.
- Surrendering command, accepting honor without active power, or exiting after success can convert dangerous capacity into safer reputation.
- Crisis and consolidation use different personnel standards: ability is more tolerated while the ruler needs help, but loyalty and etiquette become more salient once order stabilizes.
- The episode's "indispensability divided by threat" formula is a diagnostic analogy, not a measurable universal law.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is an interpretive podcast episode, not a full comparative study of every named person. Its formula is useful as a power-reading heuristic, but the wiki should not treat it as quantitative proof or as advice to erase all credit-seeking. High-merit actors can also be endangered by rulers' insecurity, factional needs, or succession politics even when they behave cautiously. The concept should therefore stay linked to [[PowerExitTrap|power exit trap]], [[SuperiorCreditConflictRisk|superior credit conflict risk]], and [[PostRestorationMeritThreat|post-restoration merit threat]] rather than replacing them.
