# High-Altitude Climbing Ethics

High-altitude climbing ethics is the debate in [[afghanistan-five-years-on-our-correspondent-visits-6a7ee547049c692a6b3ee0a9]] over what counts as a legitimate extreme mountaineering achievement. [[NirmalPurja]]'s [[ProjectPossible]] compressed all fourteen 8,000-metre peaks into six months and six days, but critics objected to his use of bottled oxygen, fixed ropes and helicopters between base camps.

The concept is not a simple purity test. The source says Purja argued that bottled oxygen helped him rescue climbers who had been abandoned after running out of oxygen, and that his military background made leaving people behind unacceptable. That makes method, speed, rescue obligation and recognition of Sherpa labor part of one ethical field.

## Key Claims
- Records in extreme environments depend on style as well as result: oxygen, ropes, route support and transport change how an achievement is judged.
- Safety aids can be criticized as unfair assistance while also enabling rescue and reducing death risk.
- Rescue ethos can conflict with summit ambition, but it can also be used to justify more supported climbing methods.
- Recognition matters because high-altitude success often depends on local workers and Sherpas whose labor is less visible than the named climber's record.
