# Claimant Lineage Political Capital / 宗室谱系政治资本

## Definition

Claimant lineage political capital / 宗室谱系政治资本 is the capacity of a recognized dynastic descent claim to make a political project legible, recruitable, and legitimate even when the claimant initially lacks territory or wealth.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1132-zhugeliang-ping-shenme-rending-liubei-neng-cheng-dashi-lgua1ohxt_ji_dytbgwvfbexjiho|Hanji 1132]] develops the concept through [[LiuBei|刘备]]. The episode argues that his claimed descent from [[LiuShengZhongshanKingWesternHan|中山王刘胜]] gives an otherwise mobile and materially weak actor a recognizable connection to Han continuity. In this reading, the claim helps distinguish Liu Bei's project from [[CaoCao|曹操]]'s control of the existing emperor and [[SunQuan|孙权]]'s regional family base.

Lineage does not substitute for capability. Its value appears when advisers such as [[ZhugeLiang|诸葛亮]] can join it to the [[LongzhongPlan|隆中对]], military followership, territorial acquisition, administration, and a restoration narrative. The source therefore supports a conditional synthesis: genealogy can lower the coalition-building cost of explaining who a claimant is and what cause supporters are joining, but years of organization and execution determine whether that symbolic asset becomes durable power.

## Key Claims

- A dynastic descent claim can provide recognition before a claimant holds stable territory.
- Lineage becomes political capital when it supplies a shared legitimacy story for advisers, soldiers, local supporters, and allies.
- Symbolic continuity can differentiate competing power projects without proving the claimant's competence or moral superiority.
- The value of lineage depends on public recognition, organizational conversion, and performance rather than genealogy alone.
- Greater genealogical distance and uncertain documentation weaken any claim that descent by itself explains political success.

## Evidence

- Claimed Han descent: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1132-zhugeliang-ping-shenme-rending-liubei-neng-cheng-dashi-lgua1ohxt_ji_dytbgwvfbexjiho|Hanji 1132]] presents Liu Bei's claimed descent from Liu Sheng as his most useful early political identifier.
- Adviser and coalition legibility: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1132-zhugeliang-ping-shenme-rending-liubei-neng-cheng-dashi-lgua1ohxt_ji_dytbgwvfbexjiho|Hanji 1132]] interprets the identity as one reason Zhuge Liang could see Liu Bei as the bearer of a Han-restoration project.
- Conversion into capacity: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1132-zhugeliang-ping-shenme-rending-liubei-neng-cheng-dashi-lgua1ohxt_ji_dytbgwvfbexjiho|Hanji 1132]] ties the symbolic claim to the Longzhong program, territorial growth, talent support, and later imperial continuity language.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept currently rests on one retrospective popular-history source. That source acknowledges the genealogical distance but does not verify Liu Bei's full descent chain, measure how contemporaries evaluated it, or isolate lineage from reputation, personal conduct, military followers, offices, alliances, and later success. The host's “personal brand” analogy is useful for accessibility but can flatten dynastic law, ritual status, elite recognition, and public sentiment into modern marketing language. Lineage should therefore be treated as one enabling resource, not a sufficient cause or proof of rightful rule.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to distinguish symbolic lineage recognition from the military, territorial, and administrative work needed to convert it into power.
