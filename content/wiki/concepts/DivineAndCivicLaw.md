# Divine and Civic Law

## Definition

Divine and civic law names the tragic conflict between sacred obligations understood as older or higher than enacted commands and the human authority required to govern a city.

## Current Synthesis

[[603-greek-myths-the-riddle-of-the-sphinx-part-2-glt9108845593]] presents the conflict through two Sophoclean cases. In [[OedipusTyrannos|Oedipus Tyrannos]], Apollo's oracle defines an unresolved murder as pollution and requires a civic remedy, but human inquiry reaches the divine truth only by destroying the ruler who leads it. In *Antigone*, Creon's prohibition on burying Polynices protects the city against a traitor, while Antigone treats burial as an unwritten sacred duty no decree can cancel.

Neither case yields a simple rule that private conscience always defeats public law. Divine obligation claims priority, but its content and consequences remain difficult for mortals to know; civic authority is necessary, but it becomes catastrophic when it treats its own command as complete. Tragedy preserves the collision rather than designing an institution that reconciles it.

## Key Claims

- Sacred obligation can make a legally authorized civic order morally or ritually incomplete.
- Civic law addresses real collective dangers and cannot be reduced to arbitrary villainy.
- Human beings may recognize that divine law is higher without being able to interpret it reliably.
- Oedipal pollution makes private family history a matter of public health and rule.
- Burial in *Antigone* makes the limit of state command visible through an obligation no author or ruler created.
- Tragic form preserves incompatible claims and catastrophic consequences rather than resolving them into doctrine.

## Evidence

- Pollution and remedy: [[603-greek-myths-the-riddle-of-the-sphinx-part-2-glt9108845593]] says Apollo requires Laius's murderer to be found and expelled before plague-struck Thebes can be purified.
- Burial conflict: [[603-greek-myths-the-riddle-of-the-sphinx-part-2-glt9108845593]] contrasts Creon's ban on burying an attacker with Antigone's appeal to unwritten divine obligation.
- Non-resolution: [[603-greek-myths-the-riddle-of-the-sphinx-part-2-glt9108845593]] treats the resulting deaths as evidence that neither civic command nor claimed sacred certainty produces a clean settlement.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source offers a literary-historical interpretation, not a complete account of ancient Greek law, religion, burial practice, or Sophoclean scholarship. “Divine law” is not a single transparent code, and the two plays do not prove that every conflict between religion and government is structurally equivalent.
