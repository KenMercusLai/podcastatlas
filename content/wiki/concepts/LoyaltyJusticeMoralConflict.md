## Definition

Loyalty–justice moral conflict is the clash that occurs when protecting a group, relationship, or institution seems to require silence while fairness, honesty, harm prevention, or public duty seems to require disclosure or intervention.

## Current Synthesis

[[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639|When Doing Right Feels Wrong]] develops the conflict through Edward Snowden as an opening disagreement and [[DarwinRocher]] as a sustained case. Rocher's colleagues were trusted friends and operational partners, but their proposed robbery could include murder. Reporting them therefore served justice and public duty while violating a real bond of mutual protection.

The episode's [[AdamWaytz]] interpretation treats loyalty and justice as moral foundations whose relative weight can shift with stakes, social norms, organizational culture, and recently activated values. A reported low-stakes experiment found more reporting after fairness prompts and less after loyalty prompts. This supports context sensitivity, not the claim that a brief cue explains dangerous real-world action.

The joined judgment is that whistleblowing may feel wrong even when it is justified because moral action can preserve one binding value by injuring another. The responsible response is not to erase loyalty but to clarify its proper object through [[WhistleblowingAsMissionLoyalty]] and to reduce the retaliation that converts a moral decision into prolonged personal harm.

## Key Claims

- Loyalty and justice can both be morally serious, so their collision cannot always be reduced to courage versus cowardice.
- The same disclosure can be interpreted as harm prevention or betrayal depending on which duty an observer prioritizes.
- Stakes matter: minor poor performance and a planned violent crime do not create equivalent obligations.
- Social context, crisis, group norms, and institutional identity can change the perceived cost of breaking ranks.
- A justified choice can still cause [[MoralStress]] when it violates valued relationships or group commitments.
- Institutional design should make loyalty to lawful mission more salient than loyalty to misconduct or personal authority.

- Opposed moral judgments - [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639]] uses Snowden and divided reactions to Rocher to show that disclosure can be coded as either courage or betrayal.
- Contextual salience - [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639]] reports that fairness prompts increased reporting of poor work while loyalty prompts reduced it.
- Organizational response - [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639]] recommends redirecting loyalty toward society, public purpose, and an institution's true mission.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source is a structured episode summary, not a transcript, systematic review, or full account of moral foundations research. The reporting experiment is low stakes and cannot reproduce violent danger, legal duties, retaliation, family risk, or police culture. Loyalty can protect trust and coordination, while reporting can be mistaken, malicious, premature, or unsafe; justice therefore still requires evidence, proportionality, and credible channels.

## What Changed

- Created a framework joining competing moral values, contextual salience, stakes, and organizational response.
