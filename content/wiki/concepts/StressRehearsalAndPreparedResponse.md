# Stress Rehearsal and Prepared Response

## Definition
Stress rehearsal and prepared response is the practice of training simple actions under relevant pressure so that a useful response is easier to retrieve when emotion, threat, fatigue, or arousal reduces deliberative capacity.

## Current Synthesis
Cold-state instruction often assumes that recognition will automatically produce action. The episode instead treats performance as a retrieval and sequencing problem: under pressure, a person must notice the situation, decide that action is warranted, accept responsibility, choose a response, and execute it while managing fear, shame, social cost, or physical stress. A short practiced phrase, a small next target, a drill, or an accessible protective resource can reduce those demands.

Realism and repetition matter, but the training should be proportionate and safe. Military stress inoculation is an extreme example; fire drills, self-defense practice, preselected harassment responses, and advance access to condoms are civilian variants. Preparation improves the chance of action rather than guaranteeing it, and institutions remain responsible for preventing hazards and retaliation.

## Key Claims
- Knowing a preferred response while calm does not ensure access to it under pressure.
- Breaking a large objective into small immediate targets reduces planning load during acute stress.
- Repeated practice can build response fluency and sometimes reduce the intensity of a familiar hot state.
- Prepared language can reduce the number of decisions required during sexist or coercive interaction.
- Environmental availability and decision delay can function as externalized preparation.
- Rehearsal must preserve safety, consent, proportionality, and institutional responsibility.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Transfer from simulation to real events is not guaranteed, and extreme or deceptive exposure can itself cause harm. Military training does not automatically generalize to workplaces, relationships, public policy, or clinical contexts. Prepared responses can be unsafe where retaliation or violence is credible, and inability to execute a rehearsed action is not moral failure. The episode does not provide comparative effect sizes, durability evidence, or an evaluation of the named program.
