# Conquest Financing Feedback Loop

## Definition

A conquest financing feedback loop occurs when a state cannot sustainably maintain its military establishment from existing revenue, uses borrowing or asset pledges to begin war, and then depends on captured land, people, treasuries, or tribute to pay for continued campaigning.

## Current Synthesis

The episode presents Macedon in 335-334 BCE as possessing an unusually capable professional army whose pay, supply, animals, equipment, and transport exceeded the kingdom's comfortable peacetime means. [[PhilipIIMacedon|Philip II's]] debts, [[AlexanderTheGreat|Alexander's]] tax reduction and spending, loans from companions, pledged royal property, and proceeds from the sale of Thebans create the starting constraint.

The invasion of the [[AchaemenidEmpire]] then resembles a high-risk financed venture. Companions contribute money and service in expectation of later reward; the army must win before its resources fail; and victory at the [[BattleOfGranicus]] opens cities and treasuries that relieve the immediate crisis. Success therefore relaxes the constraint that might have ended the campaign and supplies resources for the next stage.

This mechanism explains pressure to begin and continue war without reducing Alexander's expansion to finance alone. Structural need makes invasion likely; heroic ambition, political choice, coercion, and later strategic aims help explain why conquest continues after solvency is restored.

## Key Claims

- A professional military advantage can become a fiscal liability when demobilization is politically or strategically difficult.
- Borrowing, pledged property, and elite co-investment can move war costs forward in time.
- Plunder, enslavement, treasury capture, land, and tribute can convert victory into operating revenue.
- Early defeat is unusually dangerous because it leaves both military and financial obligations unresolved.
- Successful conquest weakens the fiscal stopping rule by funding the next campaign.
- Financial pressure can explain war initiation without fully explaining its ultimate extent.

## Evidence

- Starting constraint: [[dchha73-mania-for-subjugation-iii-dchha73-mania-for-subjugation-iii]] links inherited debt, taxation choices, spending, and high army costs to pressure for invasion.
- Forward financing: [[dchha73-mania-for-subjugation-iii-dchha73-mania-for-subjugation-iii]] reports loans, royal-land transfers or pledges, and companion participation.
- Coercive revenue: [[dchha73-mania-for-subjugation-iii-dchha73-mania-for-subjugation-iii]] connects Theban enslavement and Persian treasury capture to debt relief and campaign survival.
- Feedback after victory: [[dchha73-mania-for-subjugation-iii-dchha73-mania-for-subjugation-iii]] treats Granicus as the point where the expedition becomes self-financing.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source's debt totals, annual army-cost estimates, proceeds, property transfers, and companion expectations are reconstructed rather than independently audited here. Conquest also destroys productive capacity, imposes occupation costs, depends on future victory, and can fail before revenue arrives. The feedback loop is a mechanism, not proof that fiscal necessity compelled every decision or that success made continued war economically rational.

## What Changed

- Created a mechanism separating the fiscal pressure to begin conquest from the political and personal causes of its later expansion.
