## Definition
The aggregate indicators lived experience gap is the mismatch between broad statistics or national narratives and what particular households, workers, sectors, regions, or identity groups experience.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources show that accurate aggregates can still be incomplete. GDP, unemployment, disinflation, imports, equity indexes, consumer spending, and geopolitical narratives compress distributions, price levels, financing costs, sector composition, access, and timing. A strong headline can therefore coexist with weak entry-level hiring, unaffordable housing, high food or fuel costs, regional strain, or a narrow set of firms and wealthy consumers carrying growth.

The newest All-In episode makes the gap explicitly political. Upward GDP revisions, payroll gains, low unemployment, participation, income, poverty, manufacturing, and softer core inflation can support a recovery story, while voters still respond to cumulative prices, rates, diesel, housing, war, and personal labor-market exposure. Sentiment is not automatically more truthful than hard data, but it is evidence about the channels through which aggregate performance is—or is not—reaching people.

## Key Claims
- Aggregate statistics can be accurate while still missing distribution, affordability, and timing.
- Slowing inflation does not automatically mean households feel relief if the price level remains high.
- Housing costs, entry-level jobs, internships, and care disruptions can change lived conditions before headline indicators capture them.
- Listening to people is not anti-data; it can identify which data cuts are missing.
- Consumer sentiment is a useful companion measure, not a substitute for verified economic statistics.
- Political interpretation depends on salience: cumulative prices, credit costs, fuel, housing, and war may outweigh abstract growth rates for voters.
- Aggregate gaps can hide strength as well as pain, including industrial substitution, digital activity, overseas profits, or resilient sectors that old indicators measure poorly.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The gap does not prove that official data are false, that sentiment is unbiased, or that every negative experience represents the whole economy. Political identity, media selection, recent shocks, expectations, asset ownership, and partisan incentives can shape perception. Several numeric claims in the newest episode are supplied without the underlying releases or methodology, and the bank-loss and election arguments are explicitly model-based or speculative. The right response is disaggregation and transparent comparison, not automatic preference for either anecdotes or national aggregates.
