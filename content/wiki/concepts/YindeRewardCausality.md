## Definition

阴德福报因果 / Hidden virtue reward causality is the Xuan-era moral frame in Hanji 430 and Hanji 429 for the claim that good done without public display can later return as recognition, protection, reward, or descendant benefit.

## Current Synthesis

The concept is grounded first in [[BingJiWesternHan|丙吉]]'s long silence about protecting the future [[EmperorXuanOfHan|汉宣帝]]. Hanji 429 shows the hidden-service mechanism: only a later claim by the bondwoman Ze forces Bing Ji to tell the story, and even then he redirects credit toward Hu Zu and Guo Zhengqing before Xuan recognizes his role. Hanji 430 then adds [[XiahouShengWesternHan|夏侯胜]]'s explanation of Bing Ji's illness and recovery: such hidden virtue will be answered before death and extend to descendants. The episode branch also links this to [[LiaofanSixun|《了凡四训》]], Sima Guang's family teaching, and 《文昌帝君阴骘文》.

The wiki stores this as a source-scoped moral worldview. Its practical claim is stable inside the episodes: hidden, cumulative, non-performative virtue is treated as better moral capital than visible reward-seeking. Hanji 429 sharpens the non-display side through Laozi's "上德不德," the Liang Wudi and Bodhidharma anecdote, and "三轮体空." The stronger metaphysical claim that virtue directly causes recovery or fortune remains the host's interpretive frame.

## Key Claims

- Hidden good action matters precisely because it is not performed for immediate status.
- Bing Ji's quiet protection of Liu Xun is the episode's main historical example.
- Hidden virtue includes accurate credit distribution: Bing Ji does not use Ze's claim to inflate his own role and instead names other caregivers.
- Xiahou Sheng interprets Bing Ji's recovery as deferred repayment of hidden virtue.
- The frame extends virtue across descendants, not only the actor's own life.
- The episode branch ties the idea to Laozi, Liaofan-style fate change, later family-instruction texts, and Buddhist non-attachment language.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept records the host's moral-causal worldview, not an empirical rule. Bing Ji's illness and recovery are not medically explained by the source, and the source does not prove that all hidden virtue leads to worldly protection or descendant benefit. The Buddhist and Daoist examples in Hanji 429 are interpretive teaching analogies, not evidence that the historical mechanism is metaphysically settled.
