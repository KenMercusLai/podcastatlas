# Reproductive Technology Moral Classification

## Definition

Reproductive technology moral classification is the case-based assignment of an intervention to an ethical category by examining its purpose, mechanism, duration, physiological resemblance, and consequences rather than relying on one effect alone.

## Current Synthesis

[[dr-rock-s-taxonomy-dr-rock-s-taxonomy|Dr. Rock’s Taxonomy]] shows why classification can decide whether a new technology appears compatible with an inherited rule. [[JohnRock]] did not deny that the pill prevents pregnancy. He argued that endogenous hormones and a pregnancy-like physiological state made it more analogous to the Catholic-permitted rhythm method than to a condom or diaphragm that interrupts intercourse.

The episode's mTOR thought experiment makes the ambiguity sharper. A future drug that pauses follicle development could preserve eggs, delay reproductive aging, protect fertility during chemotherapy, and temporarily prevent ovulation. Calling it contraception highlights one present effect; calling it fertility preservation highlights its intended future capacity. Casuistry keeps both descriptions visible and asks which similarities are morally relevant.

## Key Claims

- A technology can fit several factually accurate categories because mechanism, immediate effect, long-term purpose, and user context may point in different directions.
- Classification has practical moral force when rules permit one category and prohibit another.
- Analogy is substantive only when it explains why selected similarities matter; renaming alone does not settle the ethics.
- Patient circumstances and intended use can expose distinctions that a broad label such as “contraception” conceals.
- Ethical classification should remain separate from claims about safety, efficacy, availability, or clinical appropriateness.

## Evidence

### Pill, rhythm, and barrier methods

- [[dr-rock-s-taxonomy-dr-rock-s-taxonomy]] presents Rock's argument that the pill's endogenous hormones and pregnancy-like state make it closer to periodic abstinence than to a physical barrier, while acknowledging that pregnancy prevention remains an effect.

### Fertility preservation thought experiment

- [[dr-rock-s-taxonomy-dr-rock-s-taxonomy]] describes a speculative mTOR-pathway intervention that could both pause ovulation and preserve future fertility, making purpose and temporal horizon central to classification.

### Institutional consequence

- [[dr-rock-s-taxonomy-dr-rock-s-taxonomy]] reports that some theologians and Church leaders accepted Rock's distinction and that a Vatican commission recommended permitting the pill before Pope Paul VI reaffirmed the prohibition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Casuistry can clarify morally relevant differences, but it can also become motivated hair-splitting that selects a convenient analogy after the desired conclusion is known. Purpose does not erase foreseeable effects, and “natural” or endogenous mechanisms are not automatically safe or morally permissible. The mTOR example is speculative, and the episode does not supply a validated ethical decision rule, clinical evidence review, or balanced account of Catholic natural-law theology.

## What Changed

- Established a framework separating an intervention's mechanism, present effect, future purpose, and lived context.
- Added the pill–rhythm analogy and the mTOR fertility-preservation thought experiment as contrasting classification tests.
- Kept moral categorization distinct from clinical safety and contraceptive effectiveness.
