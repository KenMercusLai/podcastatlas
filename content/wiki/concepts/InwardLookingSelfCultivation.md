# Inward-Looking Self-Cultivation / 向内求式自我修行

## Definition
Inward-looking self-cultivation / 向内求式自我修行 is the practice of shifting attention from how the external world treats the self toward one's own motives, reactions, speech, needs, and choices.

## Current Synthesis
The 天真不天真 episode frames 向内求 as a repeated correction after automatic reaction. The speakers do not deny jealousy, anger, hurt, defensiveness, or the wish to be understood. Instead, they treat the first reaction as human and the second response as the point where practice begins: notice the motive behind speech, stop making another person's emotion automatically about oneself, express a need without trying to control the other person, and apologize when old inertia has taken over.

This makes the concept practical rather than purely spiritual. It appears in ordinary situations: online criticism, workplace arrangements, meetings, intimate partners, old friends, birthdays, travel, study abroad, and language learning. The source's claim is not that inward attention solves all problems, but that it returns agency to the part of experience one can actually revise.

## Key Claims
- The first emotional reaction is not the whole self; the second thought can choose a different posture.
- Attention to "起心动念" makes speech a diagnostic surface for hidden jealousy, pride, insecurity, or kindness.
- Looking inward does not mean accepting mistreatment; it means separating another person's behavior from one's own response.
- Relationship needs can be expressed clearly without assuming the other person is obliged to understand or change.
- Apology matters because it interrupts emotional inertia after anger or control has already appeared.
- The practice is experiential: pausing, traveling, studying abroad, and being alone can reveal old dependency on speed, control, or external validation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source uses personal and spiritual language, so the concept should not be treated as clinical guidance or as a demand that people blame themselves for harmful treatment. Inward attention needs boundaries, outside support, and practical judgment when conflict, manipulation, or risk are present.
