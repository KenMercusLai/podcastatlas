# Older-Adult Home-Care Safety / 老年居家照护安全

## Definition
Older-adult home-care safety is a function-matched approach to daily routines, medication, feeding, skin, mobility, environment, cognition, and escalation that aims to preserve dignity while reducing preventable deterioration at home.

## Current Synthesis
The episode rejects one generic category of “elder care.” A relatively independent older person mainly needs stable routines and proportionate chronic-disease support; a bedbound person may need linked positioning, continence and skin care, airway clearance, safe feeding, nutrition, and passive movement; a cognitively impaired person needs patient communication, recognizable surroundings, participation, and wandering safeguards.

These practices work as a system. Poor swallowing can lead to aspiration, immobility can worsen secretion clearance and thrombosis risk, inadequate nutrition can delay wound healing, and shame or over-control can reduce cooperation. Family observation is useful when it detects change and connects it to an agreed care pathway, not when relatives improvise procedures or medication changes beyond their training.

## Key Claims
- Care intensity and technique should follow function, cognition, swallowing, mobility, continence, and chronic-disease context.
- Positioning, pressure relief, clean and dry skin, nutrition, airway care, and movement are interdependent rather than isolated chores.
- Feeding safety requires appropriate posture and texture, with tube feeding or airway procedures kept inside professional instruction.
- Stable routines and meaningful participation can protect both safety and dignity.
- Family caregivers need observable warning signs and escalation plans, not an expectation that they replace clinicians.
- Holiday routine changes can expose hidden problems in medication, sleep, eating, mobility, mood, or home design.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is broad public education rather than a home-care protocol or comparative study. Turning schedules, feeding position, food texture, catheter care, suction, nebulization, tube feeding, wound care, exercise, and emergency thresholds depend on the person's condition and professional plan. Sudden confusion, weakness, breathing difficulty, chest or abdominal pain, bleeding, repeated vomiting, or reduced responsiveness requires clinical assessment rather than routine caregiving alone.
