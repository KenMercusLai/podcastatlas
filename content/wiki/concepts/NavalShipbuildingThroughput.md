# Naval Shipbuilding Throughput

Naval shipbuilding throughput is the strategic-capacity problem of how many militarily useful vessels, payloads, and firepower units a country can build per year, at what cost, and with what industrial base. [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-saronic-founders-autonomous-warships-chinas-230x-advantage-swarms-of-robot-ships-42334230]] adds the concept through [[Saronic]]'s claim that [[China]] has a roughly 230-to-1 shipbuilding gross-tonnage advantage over the [[UnitedStates]].

The concept shifts naval comparison away from platform prestige alone. [[DinoMavroukis]] compares aircraft carriers, destroyers, and [[MarauderSaronic|Marauder]] by cost, build time, and VLS-equivalent firepower per year, making the argument that smaller autonomous vessels can add mass faster even if they do not replace every crewed ship.

## Key Claims
- Ship count can hide throughput: a fleet can be powerful but still structurally hard to expand or replace.
- Cost per ship and build time matter because slow replacement reduces usable deterrent mass.
- The source treats [[AutonomousNavalVessels]] as one possible answer to a shipyard bottleneck, not a complete substitute for crewed naval power.
- [[PortAlpha|Port Alpha]] is framed as an attempt to co-design product, process, yard, and supply chain around a faster shipbuilding model.
