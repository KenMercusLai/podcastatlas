# Battle of Tours

## Definition

The Battle of Tours was the 732 or 733 confrontation in which a Frankish-led force under [[CharlesMartel]], joined by [[OdoOfAquitaine|Odo of Aquitaine]], defeated an Umayyad army under [[AbdAlRahmanAlGhafiqi|Abd al-Rahman al-Ghafiqi]] before it could reach the shrine of Saint Martin at Tours.

## Current Synthesis

The episode reconstructs the battle as the convergence of imperial expansion, regional faction, plunder, sacred prestige, and dynastic consolidation. Umayyad forces had taken most of Visigothic Iberia, crossed the Pyrenees, and established themselves at Narbonne. Odo's 721 victory at Toulouse delayed further pressure, but his later alliance with the Berber leader Uthman drew Abd al-Rahman into the conflict; Charles meanwhile attacked Aquitaine from the north.

After Abd al-Rahman sacked Bordeaux and defeated Odo, Odo submitted to Charles in return for aid. The Umayyad force moved north with plunder toward Saint Martin's exceptionally wealthy shrine. Charles blocked the route and relied on trained infantry holding close formation. Abd al-Rahman died in the battle, and the Umayyad army withdrew overnight without the Franks breaking formation to pursue.

The battle's significance is neither the certainty that it “saved Christendom” nor the claim that it was merely trivial. It protected a major sacred and political symbol, increased Charles's authority, and helped subordinate Aquitaine, but Umayyad incursions continued and a later campaign reached Burgundy. Counterfactual claims about Muslim rule in southern Gaul, Italy, the papacy, or all Europe become increasingly uncertain as the causal chain lengthens.

Its afterlife is part of the subject. Gibbon, nineteenth- and twentieth-century writers, Hitler, French far-right movements, and the Christchurch terrorist all reused Charles Martel and Tours for incompatible political claims. That history makes a simplified Christianity-versus-Islam interpretation both influential and evidentially inadequate.

## Key Claims

- The campaign combined raiding, factional intervention, imperial reach, shrine attack, and possible territorial consequence rather than fitting one exclusive category.
- Cross-confessional alliances and conflicts within both camps undermine a simple two-civilization battlefield map.
- The shrine of Saint Martin mattered as concentrated wealth, sacred prestige, and a symbol of emerging Frankish identity.
- Charles's blocked route, disciplined infantry, defensive cohesion, and refusal to pursue shaped the outcome in the episode's reconstruction.
- Abd al-Rahman's death and concern for accumulated plunder contributed to the Umayyad withdrawal.
- Victory strengthened Charles and reduced Aquitanian independence without permanently ending Umayyad operations north of the Pyrenees.
- Later ideological use enlarged the battle into a civilizational symbol whose claims require separate historical testing.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The date may be 732 or 733, and the evidence does not securely establish force sizes, casualties, ethnic or religious composition, technology, formations, battlefield sequence, or the attackers' final objective. Bede's account may refer to the campaign despite its different date. The lack of strong emphasis in Umayyad chronicles and continued campaigning weigh against a single final civilizational check; the shrine's survival, Charles's strengthened position, and plausible danger to Aquitaine weigh against treating the event as meaningless. Every long-range counterfactual remains unobservable.

## What Changed

- Created the concept with a middle judgment between a salvation-of-Europe narrative and an inconsequential-raid dismissal.
- Separated immediate military and dynastic consequences from longer, less certain counterfactuals.
- Added sacred prestige and modern political appropriation to the battle's historical meaning.
