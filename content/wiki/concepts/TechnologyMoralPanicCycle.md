## Definition
The technology moral panic cycle is [[MarkAndreessen|Marc Andreessen]]'s source-scoped pattern in which a consequential new technology is first dismissed, then debated on substantive grounds, and finally attacked through status, identity, or moral condemnation as it becomes harder to ignore.

## Current Synthesis
The pattern is useful for detecting when social judgment substitutes for evidence, especially around unfamiliar media and machines. It is dangerous when used as an all-purpose shield: some technologies create genuine externalities, and criticism does not become a panic merely because earlier inventions were also criticized. Historical analogy should generate questions about mechanisms, affected groups, adaptation, and power rather than predetermine approval.

## Key Claims
- New technologies can move rapidly from implausibility to threat once their usefulness becomes visible.
- Status and institutional interests may intensify opposition when a technology disrupts established authority or distribution.
- Ridicule and moral labeling can replace mechanism-level argument on both pro- and anti-technology sides.
- Past overreaction does not prove present safety; technologies differ in scale, reversibility, concentration, and failure mode.
- A valid diagnosis requires evidence of disproportionate threat construction, not merely controversy or regulation.

## Evidence
- Proposed stages: [[how-risk-taking-innovation-artificial-intelligence-transform-human-experience-marc-andreessen-scim7464698870]] records Andreessen's sequence of denial, rational counterargument, and name-calling.
- Historical analogies: [[how-risk-taking-innovation-artificial-intelligence-transform-human-experience-marc-andreessen-scim7464698870]] invokes bicycles, cars, electric lighting, comic books, jazz, heavy metal, social media, and AI as recurring targets of alarm.
- Institutional mechanism: [[how-risk-taking-innovation-artificial-intelligence-transform-human-experience-marc-andreessen-scim7464698870]] connects panic to incumbent status, media amplification, and elite gatekeeping rather than treating public fear as wholly spontaneous.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode does not compare the historical cases systematically, and the examples differ substantially in physical risk, information power, labor effects, military use, and reversibility. Calling criticism a moral panic can itself become a rhetorical shortcut that avoids evidence, affected communities, or legitimate precaution. The concept should diagnose discourse only after the underlying risk claims are separately evaluated.

- Added a gate separating disproportionate threat construction from legitimate safety criticism.
