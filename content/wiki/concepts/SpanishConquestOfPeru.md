# Spanish Conquest of Peru

## Definition

The Spanish conquest of Peru was a prolonged expeditionary, military, hostage, coalition, political, and colonial transformation whose opening joined reconnaissance and royal authorization to epidemic and Inca civil-war fracture, and whose contested aftermath continued through Vilcabamba's fall in 1572.

## Current Synthesis

[[644-the-fall-of-the-incas-empire-of-gold-part-1-glt4676536558]] extends the conquest's opening before Cajamarca. Pizarro's company emerged from Caribbean and Panamanian conquest, repeated failed voyages, maritime reconnaissance, captured trade goods, uncertain interpretation, reports from Tumbes, and a 1529 royal grant that privileged [[FranciscoPizarro]] over [[DiegoDeAlmagro]]. The invaders therefore brought accumulated institutional, legal, and violent experience as well as horses and steel.

The target was not a simple or already collapsing society. [[Tawantinsuyu]] joined roads, terraces, irrigation, storehouses, quipus, labor mobilization, religion, and military power across a large and diverse empire. Yet smallpox killed Huayna Capac and a likely heir, and the [[IncaCivilWar|civil war]] between Huascar and [[Atahualpa]] damaged infrastructure and divided regional, dynastic, and subject loyalties. Pizarro entered after Atahualpa's battlefield victory but before stable consolidation.

[[645-the-fall-of-the-incas-massacre-in-the-andes-part-2-glt2938007438]] makes the opening contingent rather than technologically inevitable. Epidemic disruption and civil war create opportunity, but [[FranciscoPizarro]]'s small company remains vulnerable while crossing the Andes. [[Atahualpa]] gathers intelligence and allows the approach because he appears to judge the strangers useful or controllable, not because the evidence clearly shows that he considers them gods.

The [[CajamarcaMassacre|Cajamarca ambush]] concentrates Spanish capabilities inside an enclosed square. Concealed infantry and cavalry, artillery, trumpets, horses, steel, blocked exits, and surprise produce panic and mass killing, while the [[Requerimiento|Christian-royal demand]] supplies a coercive pretext. Pizarro preserves Atahualpa's life, turning battlefield shock into [[CapturedRulerLegitimacyContainment|captured-ruler leverage]].

[[646-the-fall-of-the-incas-death-to-the-emperor-part-3-glt8131543734]] follows that mechanism beyond the square. Atahualpa mobilizes his own [[AtahualpaRansomAndExecution|ransom]], orders armies not to resist, and helps draw [[Chalcuchima]] into captivity while continuing the civil war against Huascar. The Spaniards convert that compliance into bullion, reconnaissance, reinforcement, and access to Cusco, then execute the ruler whose authority made the expansion possible.

[[647-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-king-in-the-north-part-4-glt4740382633]] follows the resulting succession vacuum. [[FranciscoPizarro]] installs Huascar-aligned claimants, receives support along the road to Cusco, and enters the capital with [[MancoInca]]. The seizure and melting of sacred wealth run alongside continued northern resistance by [[Quisquis]] and [[Ruminawi|Rumiñawi]], while Cañari and other Indigenous allies make the campaigns coalition wars rather than a contest between two unified peoples.

[[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] and [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] follow the failure of that Spanish-backed order. Plunder, encomienda, sexual coercion, sacrilege, and factional abuse drive Manco toward [[SiegeOfCusco1536|the siege of Cusco]], which nearly destroys the conquerors' position. Spanish survival depends on cavalry, steel, armor, Indigenous allies, African labor, access to seaborne reinforcements, and an agrarian siege army's difficulty sustaining pressure.

By 1537, Manco remains active while [[FranciscoPizarro]] and [[DiegoDeAlmagro]] command rival centers. Control emerges through Lima's supply connection, factional war, royal legitimacy, negotiated Indigenous collaboration, punitive expeditions, and the gradual isolation of resistance rather than through Spanish weapons alone.

The conquest also becomes a colonial system before resistance fully ends. Encomienda estates, land seizure, forced labor, mining, Christian missions, new urban architecture, and imported settlers reshape society while the [[NeoIncaState|Neo-Inca state]] persists. Túpac Amaru I's 1572 execution closes independent dynastic rule, not the longer histories of Indigenous survival, colonial coercion, or Inca memory.

## Key Claims

- Failed voyages, reconnaissance, interpretation, and royal authorization converted rumor of southern wealth into an organized conquest venture.
- Epidemic disruption and Inca civil-war divisions created an opening, but Tawantinsuyu retained major capacity and the outcome was not inevitable.
- Concealment, enclosure, guns, horses, steel, sensory shock, and selective ruler capture worked together at Cajamarca.
- Captive imperial authority let a small Spanish force convert massacre into political access, treasure collection, army restraint, and commander capture.
- Atahualpa's death, Cusco's occupation, and Quito's fall did not end conquest; colonial abuse converted Manco's initially collaborative rule into a near-successful mass uprising.
- Spanish access to coastal supply, royal recognition, and indispensable Indigenous allies was as consequential as battlefield technology.
- Vilcabamba's survival to 1572 makes conquest a multi-decade process during which colonial land, labor, mining, religious, and urban systems developed alongside unfinished military control.

## Evidence

- Expeditionary and imperial preconditions: [[644-the-fall-of-the-incas-empire-of-gold-part-1-glt4676536558]] connects failed exploration, interpreters, Tumbes, royal authorization, Tawantinsuyu's capacity, smallpox, and civil war to Pizarro's opportunity.
- Opening contingency and massacre: [[645-the-fall-of-the-incas-massacre-in-the-andes-part-2-glt2938007438]] connects civil war, the vulnerable advance, mutual intelligence, planned ambush, mass killing, and Atahualpa's capture.
- Hostage conquest: [[645-the-fall-of-the-incas-massacre-in-the-andes-part-2-glt2938007438]] and [[646-the-fall-of-the-incas-death-to-the-emperor-part-3-glt8131543734]] connect selective survival and Atahualpa's continuing authority to ransom collection, army paralysis, Chalcuchima's capture, and the opening toward Cusco.
- Civil war and puppet succession: [[646-the-fall-of-the-incas-death-to-the-emperor-part-3-glt8131543734]] and [[647-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-king-in-the-north-part-4-glt4740382633]] connect Huascar's death, Atahualpa's execution, Spanish-backed rulers, Cusco's entry, and northern resistance.
- Sacred plunder and northern conquest: [[647-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-king-in-the-north-part-4-glt4740382633]] links the destruction of Cusco's wealth to campaigns for Quito and the collapse of Quisquis's and Rumiñawi's forces.
- Revolt and siege: [[647-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-king-in-the-north-part-4-glt4740382633]] and [[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] connect colonial settlement and abuse to Manco's escape, mass mobilization, assault on Cusco, and withdrawal.
- Fragmented power: [[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] and [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] identify Manco, Almagro, and Pizarro as rival centers in 1537.
- Coalition and logistics: [[648-the-fall-of-the-incas-battle-for-the-sacred-city-part-5-glt6392035011]] and [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] connect coastal access and royal communication to armies containing Indigenous allies and arriving reinforcements.
- Resistance duration: [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] follows Manco's escapes and Vilcabamba's rulers through Túpac Amaru's 1572 capture.
- Colonial formation: [[649-the-fall-of-the-incas-the-last-emperor-part-6-glt4505534064]] connects land grants, encomienda, forced labor, mining, settlement, missions, and the New Laws to the post-conquest order.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The current evidence begins with Pizarro's formation and gives only a compressed reconstruction of Tawantinsuyu and the earlier civil war; it does not represent every Indigenous polity's strategy. Spanish chronicles dominate much of the sequence, and population figures, property and market claims, numbers, speeches, motives, the gods claim, atrocity details, and causal ranking remain source-scoped. Extending the conquest to 1572 is analytically useful but should not imply uniform warfare across the whole period or one uncontested end date.

## What Changed

- Extended the opening back to Caribbean and Panamanian conquest experience, failed Pacific expeditions, reconnaissance, interpreters, and royal authorization.
- Added Tawantinsuyu's administrative capacity and coercive diversity as the necessary counterpart to its epidemic and civil-war vulnerability.
- Distinguished Atahualpa's military victory from stable political consolidation in 1532.
