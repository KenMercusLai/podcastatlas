# Marathon Destination Consumption / 马拉松目的地消费

## Definition
Marathon destination consumption is the spending generated when runners combine race participation with travel, lodging, food, local transport, equipment, recovery, and short leisure stays in a host city.

## Current Synthesis
The sources treat the marathon as both a scarce participation product and a city-weekend demand engine. Oversubscribed races can pull spending beyond registration and shoes into transport, lodging, food, recovery, and sightseeing, particularly when runners turn an away race into a short trip.

The Shanghai-Tokyo-Hong Kong comparison adds a crucial boundary: participant count is not the same as destination value. International or nonlocal share, distance mix, hotel price, route geography, same-day travel options, length of stay, merchant participation, and the location of start and finish all affect how much spending the host city captures. Aggregate consumption and economic-effect estimates signal possible scale, but neither source establishes net local benefit after event cost, substitution, leakage, or resident displacement.

## Key Claims
- Oversubscribed races can create travel demand even when participant capacity is fixed.
- Runner expenditure spans recurring equipment and event-specific destination costs.
- Away races bundle sport participation with short-trip consumption.
- Host-city benefit depends on visitor share, race-distance mix, length of stay, local capture, and event cost.
- Route design, start-finish logistics, merchant activation, hotel prices, and same-day transport can change where and whether spending occurs.

## Evidence
- Demand intensity: [[qianyu-jia-yingyuan-zhibo-tiyu-saishi-shangbannian-guonei-jinshi-xiaofeiliang-xiadie-sancheng-gkwridooqlkafaplwtreagx]] reports 32,000 Beijing Marathon entrants from more than 265,000 applicants.
- Spending scale: [[qianyu-jia-yingyuan-zhibo-tiyu-saishi-shangbannian-guonei-jinshi-xiaofeiliang-xiadie-sancheng-gkwridooqlkafaplwtreagx]] reports more than RMB 20 billion in consumption associated with certified full and half marathons; [[lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl-lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl|EP96]] separately reports Tokyo and Hong Kong economic-effect estimates while warning through comparison that scale and local capture differ.
- Consumption bundle: [[qianyu-jia-yingyuan-zhibo-tiyu-saishi-shangbannian-guonei-jinshi-xiaofeiliang-xiadie-sancheng-gkwridooqlkafaplwtreagx]] names equipment, supplies, registration, travel, lodging, and food around away races; [[lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl-lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl|EP96]] adds local transport, tourism, recovery, and the runner's difficulty returning from a distant finish.
- Visitor and distance mix: [[lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl-lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl|EP96]] contrasts Tokyo's reported foreign-runner share with Hong Kong's larger mix of local 10-kilometer and half-marathon participants.
- Urban capture mechanisms: [[lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl-lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl|EP96]] links Tokyo spending to lodging, food, sightseeing, residents, and merchant activation, while using Shenzhen proximity and high hotel prices to explain why some Hong Kong runners may not stay overnight.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Application volume is not visitor count, and gross associated spending is not net local economic impact. The sources do not provide consistent definitions for organizer revenue, associated consumption, multiplier effects, visitor origin, event subsidy, policing, road closure, substitution from other weekends, leakage, or resident displacement. Their Beijing, Shanghai, Tokyo, and Hong Kong figures span different years, currencies, race portfolios, and statistical boundaries and should not be treated as a controlled city ranking.

## What Changed
- Visitor share, distance mix, stay length, route logistics, hotel economics, and merchant activation now mediate the move from entrant count to host-city benefit.
- Cross-city totals are explicitly non-comparable without consistent accounting and counterfactual definitions.
