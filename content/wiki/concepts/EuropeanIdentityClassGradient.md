# European Identity Class Gradient

European identity class gradient is the source's observation that "being European" is not felt evenly across the continent. In [[139-cong-zuo-ri-de-shi-jie-dao-ru-jin-de-ou-zhou-mei-hao-nian-dai-belle-epoque-ta-hai-neng-hui-lai-ma-ltvsgov8ht9-l7yp9qamdgjmyrf]], [[FeiQizhulou|费]] says many educated and mobile European friends identify first as European, while the hosts note that local identity may remain stronger in places with less cross-border mobility or stronger local culture.

The concept links identity to use. A person who studies abroad through [[ErasmusProgramme|Erasmus]], works across borders, speaks multiple languages, and travels easily through [[SchengenArea|Schengen]] has more daily reasons to feel European than someone whose housing, job, language, and politics remain local.

## Key Claims
- European identity can be strongest among high-education, high-mobility, and economically secure groups.
- Local, regional, national, and European identities coexist rather than simply replacing each other.
- Identity formation depends on lived systems: education, work, language, passport, transport, and cultural events.

## Connections
- [[EuropeanUnion]], [[SchengenArea]], and [[ErasmusProgramme]] - institutional supports.
- [[EuropeanLanguageFriction]], [[PassportMobilityInequality]], and [[EliteCosmopolitanMobility]] - mechanisms that distribute identity unevenly.
- [[DigitalNomadHousingPressure]] and [[EurozoneInternalImbalance]] - tensions that can weaken common identity.
- [[Brexit]] - counterexample where European identity became politically contested.
