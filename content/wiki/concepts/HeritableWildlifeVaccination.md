# Heritable Wildlife Vaccination

## Definition

Heritable wildlife vaccination is the proposed use of genetic intervention to give a threatened animal disease resistance that can pass to offspring when repeated individual vaccination is impractical.

## Current Synthesis

The episode uses black-footed ferrets vulnerable to sylvatic plague as its motivating case. When a small wild population is geographically dispersed and difficult to vaccinate repeatedly, inherited protection could in principle change the conservation logistics by allowing resistance to persist across generations.

The same feature creates the governance problem. A heritable intervention is harder to recall than an injection, can alter future populations, and may affect ecology, fitness, and genetic diversity in ways not captured by initial protection. The source therefore treats possible human transfer as ethically different when ordinary vaccination can solve the medical problem without germline change.

## Key Claims

- Repeated individual vaccination can be impractical for small, dispersed wildlife populations.
- Heritable disease resistance could extend protection through reproduction rather than repeated capture and dosing.
- Conservation value depends on durable population outcomes, not only successful genetic insertion.
- Germline persistence raises containment, reversibility, consent, ecological, and intergenerational questions.
- Human application requires a stronger justification when non-heritable protection is available.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source summary does not provide the intervention's experimental status, mechanism, field evidence, inheritance stability, fitness effects, ecological modeling, regulatory review, or community governance. A proposed inherited protection should not be read as an established conservation outcome or as equivalent to human germline editing.

## What Changed

- Created a focused concept separating wildlife vaccination logistics from the governance of heritable intervention.
