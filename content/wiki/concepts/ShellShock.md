# Shell Shock

## Definition

Shell shock is the First World War psychological injury category that [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]] presents as a real breakdown under shelling and harrowing front-line experience rather than simple cowardice.

## Current Synthesis

The episode introduces shell shock through the Western Front's central terror: heavy artillery subjected soldiers to unpredictable force they could neither control nor escape. Captain Charles S. Myers coined the term in The Lancet in February 1915, while Dr Aldren Turner argued in May 1915 that such cases could be temporary nervous breakdowns after terrifying experience. The episode contrasts this emerging medical frame with military punishment systems that still executed soldiers for cowardice.

The page's current synthesis is that shell shock marks a boundary problem between discipline, medicine, courage, and trauma. Graves's claim that everyone had a breaking point makes bravery compatible with psychological collapse, while specialist hospitals such as Craiglockhart show institutional adaptation without implying full understanding or humane treatment.

## Key Claims

- Shell shock arose from exposure to terrifying, unpredictable industrial violence, especially artillery.
- The 1915 term gave a name to breakdown that armies had often treated through moral or disciplinary categories.
- Executions for cowardice show that medical recognition and punitive military discipline coexisted.
- Brave or decorated soldiers could break down, so shell shock cannot be reduced to weakness.
- Specialist hospitals marked a response to psychological injury but did not settle its causes, meanings, or treatment.

## Evidence

- Naming and diagnosis: [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]] identifies Charles S. Myers's February 1915 Lancet coinage and Aldren Turner's May 1915 nervous-breakdown interpretation.
- Military discipline: [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]] gives execution figures for British and Imperial, French, and German soldiers accused of cowardice.
- Breaking-point frame: [[671-the-first-world-war-blood-in-the-trenches-part-1-glt2309179565]] uses Graves's later fear of gas and shelling to argue that courage did not make soldiers psychologically invulnerable.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This page follows one podcast episode's presentation and does not yet provide a full medical history of trauma, neurasthenia, PTSD, malingering debates, class bias in treatment, or postwar disability policy. The term itself is historical and should not be treated as identical to later diagnostic categories.
