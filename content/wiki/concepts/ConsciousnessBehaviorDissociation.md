# Consciousness-Behavior Dissociation

## Definition
Consciousness-behavior dissociation is the principle that subjective experience, outward behavior, intelligence, communication, and self-consciousness are related but non-identical capacities.

## Current Synthesis
The source defines consciousness as lived experience and uses dreams, anesthesia, flow, meditation, psychedelics, derealization, and severe brain injury to test its boundaries. Intelligence concerns successful planning or action; self-consciousness concerns an experience of oneself; behavior is what an observer can detect. None is a sufficient synonym for consciousness.

This distinction matters most when channels diverge. A dreaming or meditating person may be conscious with reduced action or self-reference, while a behaviorally unresponsive patient may retain command-following brain activity. The principle does not make experience directly observable; it requires more careful inference from converging neural, physiological, behavioral, and contextual evidence.

## Key Claims
- Consciousness is not defined by speech, movement, planning, or task performance alone.
- Self-consciousness is one possible content or organization of experience, not the whole of consciousness.
- Dreaming and altered states show that experience can persist while ordinary action, sensory input, or self-modeling changes.
- Behavioral unresponsiveness cannot by itself establish unconsciousness.
- Separating these capacities improves both clinical assessment and debates about possible machine consciousness.

## Evidence
- State dissociation - [[scim8616450810-scim8616450810]] contrasts dreamless sleep and anesthesia with dreaming, meditation, flow, derealization, and psychedelic ego dissolution.
- Clinical dissociation - [[scim8616450810-scim8616450810]] describes patients who cannot communicate outwardly yet may voluntarily modulate brain activity in response to commands.
- Conceptual dissociation - [[scim8616450810-scim8616450810]] explicitly distinguishes subjective being from intelligence as planning and doing.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not solve the other-minds problem or provide a single necessary-and-sufficient test for consciousness. First-person report remains unavailable in many clinical, animal, infant, and AI cases; neural or behavioral correlates therefore support inference rather than direct access. Altered-state testimony can demonstrate a reported dissociation without proving a complete theory of consciousness.
