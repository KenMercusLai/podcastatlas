# Loss Aversion / 损失厌恶

## Definition
Loss aversion is the tendency for a possible or realized loss to carry more decision weight than a comparable gain, relative to a reference point.

## Current Synthesis
The sources join an everyday economic account with an attention mechanism. Loss aversion can make ownership losses, real-money trading, and tight-budget spending feel more threatening than nominally equal gains feel rewarding. The Platt interview adds that people often look longer at losses, that negative affect is associated with both loss-focused gaze and stronger aversion, and that visual framing can shift attention toward gains. This makes loss aversion partly responsive to presentation without reducing it to presentation alone.

## Key Claims
- Equal nominal gains and losses can have unequal subjective impact.
- Ownership and real-money exposure can sharpen loss sensitivity relative to hypothetical choices.
- Defensive bargain-seeking may hide opportunity costs or lower-quality tradeoffs.
- Visual attention often dwells longer on potential losses than comparable gains.
- Salience changes can shift choice, creating both decision-support and manipulation risks.

## Evidence
- Everyday economic asymmetry - [[155-meimao-neng-dang-fan-chi-ma-xiang-zhuanqian-gai-zuo-dian-sha-jieju-shi-ying-bimian-shenme-xingwei-jingjixue-siwei-you-shenme-yong-920167210]] applies loss aversion to real-money investing, defensive consumption, and hidden costs.
- Gaze and affect - [[how-to-make-better-decisions-dr-michael-platt-scim9606820714]] reports longer attention to possible losses and stronger loss aversion among participants with negative affect, anxiety, or depression.
- Visual framing - [[how-to-make-better-decisions-dr-michael-platt-scim9606820714]] reports that making gains visually larger or brighter shifted attention and reduced loss aversion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not show that all caution is bias: avoiding loss can be rational when downside threatens survival, liquidity, or obligations. Visual reframing can support balanced review, but it can also manipulate choice; anxiety and depression associations do not establish a simple one-way cause.
