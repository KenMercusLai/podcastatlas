# Pandemic As Historical Force

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-898-renzei-zuofu-kelian-zhiren-neng-you-duo-huangtang-lgfup6tyv85zhvnmzf6xlesmblcj|Hanji 898]] adds the earliest late-Han epidemic marker currently attached to this concept. The source records a 171 CE "大疫," says major epidemics recur in 171, 173, 179, 182, and 185 during [[EmperorLingOfHan|汉灵帝]]'s reign, and connects those outbreaks to a source-scoped [[LateHanPopulationCollapse|late-Han population-collapse]] frame.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-904-juezhao-neiguan-huode-xinling-da-ziyou-de-mijue-lopn-okbzr4ljvqmgzcsb7qav0n2|Hanji 904]] adds an earlier late-Han epidemic marker. The source records an epidemic in 173 CE, the death of Zong Ju, amnesty, and senior-office replacement; the episode uses the disease notice mainly as part of court chronology and [[NaturalDisasterPoliticalOmen|自然灾害政治征兆]], not as a developed public-health analysis.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-923-chengnianrende-dingji-henjin-guzi-lide-juejue-lifzh-gohpqnmdznnfv72tndqf8h|Hanji 923]] adds the immediate pre-Yellow-Turban disease setting. The episode places [[ZhangJiaoLateHan|张角]]'s rise in a 183 CE late-Han environment of epidemic, poverty, and weak medical access, arguing that the search for healing and survival made [[TaipingDao|太平道]]'s religious organization more compelling.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-931-rensheng-jiannan-chu-bianshi-xiuxin-shi-lpzmvxop8lmlwvxhoojzj5ok9qd6|Hanji 931]] adds an ancient late-Han case. The source says epidemic spreads in 185 CE and treats repeated outbreaks from 171 to 185 as part of the environment that weakens population, production, and social order before the broader Three Kingdoms transition.

The episode's use is contextual rather than medical. Epidemic is paired with palace fires in [[LuoyangLateHan|洛阳]], famine, refugees, war, peasant uprising, and local rebellion, making disease one force in [[WarDisasterCompounding]] rather than an isolated public-health event.

Pandemic as historical force is the source's frame for epidemics that reshape labor, politics, war, trust, migration, and social imagination. In [[yi-fen-laizi-bingchuang-shang-de-xiangguan-shudan-598284093]], the hosts move from Black Death to [[PaleRiderBook|《苍白的骑士》 / Pale Rider]], arguing that large outbreaks cannot be reduced to case counts or individual symptoms.

The concept preserves a key caution from the episode: indirect historical effects do not make a catastrophe good. Population loss, labor scarcity, mechanization pressure, xenophobia, expert mistrust, war disruption, independence movements, and political fracture can all be historically important while still being human disasters.

## Key Claims
- Hanji 898 adds the earliest attached late-Han epidemic marker and population-collapse frame, but its arithmetic should remain source-scoped rather than treated as complete mortality accounting.
- Hanji 904 adds an early late-Han epidemic-and-office marker, but the source does not yet develop disease as mobilization or state-capacity failure.
- Hanji 923 adds that epidemic pressure can become a trust and mobilization environment when state care is weak and religious healing offers practical hope.
- Hanji 931 adds a late-Han historical case in which epidemic is narrated together with famine, war, refugee movement, and revolt as a dynastic-decline pressure.
- Epidemics can become political events because they alter labor value, state legitimacy, border control, and collective fear.
- Modernity does not remove pandemic vulnerability; transport, war, media, and expert uncertainty can accelerate social consequences.
- Public blame and xenophobia are recurring social responses when disease origin and responsibility feel unclear.
- Historical impact claims should remain source-scoped unless separately verified; the episode itself uses broad causal examples to motivate reading, not to settle all debates.
