# Northern Ireland Direct Rule

## Definition
Northern Ireland direct rule is the crisis-governance shift in which Westminster suspends [[Stormont]] and takes direct responsibility for governing [[NorthernIreland]] after local institutions can no longer manage security and legitimacy.

## Current Synthesis
The episode frames direct rule as an admission of institutional failure rather than a clean solution. By March 1972, [[BloodySunday]], bombings, IRA escalation, loyalist mobilisation, and Faulkner's refusal of reduced authority made the old Stormont settlement untenable. Heath's government therefore suspends Stormont and sends [[WillieWhitelaw]] to Belfast, but direct rule inherits the same armed conflict, communal fear, and legitimacy problems that had overwhelmed local government.

## Key Claims
- Direct rule becomes politically plausible only after Stormont can no longer credibly hold law-and-order authority.
- Westminster control does not resolve the conflict by itself; it transfers responsibility for a collapsing security field.
- The shift follows both state violence and paramilitary escalation, not one isolated event.
- Direct rule can humiliate local leaders whose authority is already failing, making institutional repair politically costly.
- Crisis centralization may open negotiation channels while also concentrating blame on the central state.

## Evidence
- Stormont collapse: [[700-the-troubles-bloody-sunday-part-4-glt3909829334]] says by February 1972 Stormont was probably doomed, with British ministers discussing options including border revision, a partition referendum, and constitutional association with Ireland.
- Institutional choice: [[700-the-troubles-bloody-sunday-part-4-glt3909829334]] says Heath offered Faulkner reduced authority under British control of law and order or direct rule; Faulkner refused.
- Formal suspension: [[700-the-troubles-bloody-sunday-part-4-glt3909829334]] says Heath announced Stormont's suspension on 24 March 1972 and sent Willie Whitelaw to Belfast.
- Inherited crisis: [[700-the-troubles-bloody-sunday-part-4-glt3909829334]] places direct rule after Bloody Sunday, Aldershot, Abercorn, Lower Donegall Street, and rising loyalist mobilization.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode suggests Britain might have accepted something like the later Good Friday Agreement much earlier, but that is a counterfactual judgment. Direct rule should therefore be treated as a crisis response and bargaining reset, not proof that Westminster had a settled or generous long-term strategy.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to capture the March 1972 governance shift after Bloody Sunday and Stormont's collapse.
