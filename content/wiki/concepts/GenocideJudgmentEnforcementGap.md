# Genocide Judgment Enforcement Gap

## Definition
The genocide judgment enforcement gap is the distance between an authoritative international finding of state responsibility and the delivery of safe return, reparation, protection, or institutional change to the affected community.

## Current Synthesis
The Myanmar judgment shows that legal recognition and practical remedy move through different mechanisms. A court can validate survivors' testimony, name a persecuted community, clarify doctrine, and order remedies. It cannot by itself restore destroyed homes, end fighting, rebuild intercommunal trust, compel territorial actors, or deploy an enforcement force. Recognition is therefore a real outcome, not a consolation prize, but it should not be mistaken for implemented repair.

## Key Claims
- Formal recognition can restore dignity and establish an authoritative historical and legal record.
- A remedy order does not guarantee safe, voluntary, or materially possible return.
- Enforcement capacity, territorial control, ongoing conflict, and local trust shape whether a judgment changes lived conditions.
- Legal precedent can affect later cases even when compliance in the originating dispute remains weak.
- Reparation, return, accountability, and recognition should be evaluated separately.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Weak immediate enforcement does not make a judgment meaningless: recognition, doctrine, diplomacy, evidence preservation, and future claims can have delayed effects. Conversely, the source does not establish that those indirect effects will occur.
