# Military Success, Political Failure

## Definition

Military success, political failure is the strategic mismatch in which forces destroy targets, defeat an opponent, or remove leaders without producing a legitimate, governable, or sustainable postwar order.

## Current Synthesis

[[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-graham-allison-on-the-global-realignment-iran-china-israel-greenland-40373425]] uses the Iran war to separate extraordinary U.S.-Israeli military and intelligence performance from the unresolved political aftermath. [[GrahamAllison]] treats Iraq and Afghanistan as warnings that breaking a regime is easier than constructing its successor and identifies civil war, separatism, state fragmentation, and extended intervention as plausible consequences of destructive success.

The concept does not deny the strategic value of military capability. It requires objectives and evaluation to cross levels: target destruction must be connected to a political end state, exit terms, alliance effects, and second-order costs. Otherwise, operational metrics can improve while the strategic position deteriorates.

## Key Claims

- Battlefield dominance does not by itself create political authority or administrative capacity.
- Removing leaders can multiply rather than resolve conflicts over succession, territory, and legitimacy.
- Second-order effects may matter more than the initial campaign's speed or precision.
- A victory declaration can end participation politically without proving that the underlying order is stable.
- Strategic evaluation must connect military outputs to an attainable political settlement.

### Regime-change warning cases

- [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-graham-allison-on-the-global-realignment-iran-china-israel-greenland-40373425]] invokes Iraq and Afghanistan to argue that destruction is easier than postwar construction.

### Second-order strategic costs

- [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-graham-allison-on-the-global-realignment-iran-china-israel-greenland-40373425]] identifies energy prices, allied defense needs, Taiwan's energy exposure, civil war, separatism, and U.S.-Israeli exit divergence as possible consequences.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source evaluates an unfolding conflict and does not establish which consequences occurred or whether later diplomacy produced a stable settlement. Limited military operations can achieve bounded aims without rebuilding a state, and regime survival can also carry severe costs. Iraq and Afghanistan are cautionary analogies rather than proof that Iran must follow the same path.
