# Medicine–Poison Boundary / 药物与毒物边界

## Definition

The medicine–poison boundary is the context-dependent distinction between therapeutic and harmful exposure, shaped by substance identity, purity, preparation, dose, route, duration, user condition, and intended use.

## Current Synthesis

The episode uses Chinese semantic history and material cases to show why “medicine” and “poison” cannot always be separated by ingredient name. Early wording could use “毒” for powerful medicinal action; later lexicographic distinction sharpened medicine as healing and poison as harming, while expressions such as “以毒攻毒” retained the older overlap.

Aconite and arsenic make the boundary concrete. Both entered medical practice, while aconite-containing homemade alcohol and arsenic’s pest-control or agricultural uses also created routes to accidental or intentional poisoning. Standardization, purity control, preparation, testing, labeling, and clinical judgment therefore matter alongside the raw material. The boundary does not imply that every poison is a useful medicine or that dose alone makes any exposure safe.

## Key Claims

- Ingredient names do not by themselves determine therapeutic benefit or toxic harm.
- Preparation, purity, dose, route, duration, and user vulnerability can change an exposure’s effect.
- Medical, domestic, and agricultural usefulness can expand access to materials that are also lethally misused.
- Standardization and testing reduce uncertainty but do not eliminate contraindications, errors, contamination, or deliberate abuse.
- Historical language preserves changing classifications rather than a timeless scientific taxonomy.
- A plausible therapeutic tradition does not validate homemade preparations, unknown alcohol infusions, or unidentified remedies.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode is a broad historical explainer, not a pharmacopoeia, toxicology review, or clinical guide. It does not establish safe doses, processing standards, antidotes, or diagnostic rules for aconite, arsenic, medicinal alcohol, or any other agent. “The dose makes the poison” is incomplete when identity, route, duration, interactions, purity, and host condition differ. Historical medical use is not proof of efficacy, and standard manufacture is not a guarantee of safety; the durable judgment is that controlled evidence and exposure context matter.

## What Changed

- Established a historical-toxicological framework connecting semantic change, therapeutic use, household availability, preparation, and poisoning risk.
- Distinguished controlled standardization from the unsupported inference that traditional or homemade use is safe.
