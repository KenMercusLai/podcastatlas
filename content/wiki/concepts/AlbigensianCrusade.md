# Albigensian Crusade

## Definition

The Albigensian Crusade was the early-thirteenth-century sanctified war against alleged heresy in Languedoc that joined papal reform, armed pilgrimage, exemplary massacre, regional rivalry, territorial conquest, and eventual French royal expansion.

## Current Synthesis

The episode begins with [[InnocentIII]] mobilizing armed pilgrims after a papal envoy's murder in 1208. [[RaymondVIOfToulouse]]'s submission and participation did not contain the campaign: Béziers and Carcassonne opened a war that [[SimonDeMontfort]] extended through occupation, seasonal reinforcement, siege, devastation, and mass burning. Violence on both sides and resistance in Toulouse prevented a simple one-directional conquest narrative.

The war changed institutional and territorial structures even when its first conquerors could not hold them. Royal intervention, the 1229 Treaty of Paris, dynastic marriage, and childlessness transferred Toulouse to the French crown in 1271. Meanwhile the anti-heresy project continued through mendicant preaching and inquisition, making battlefield victory only one phase of a longer attempt to classify and govern religious difference.

## Key Claims

- Papal anti-heresy policy was converted into armed pilgrimage against an internal Christian enemy.
- Raymond VI's submission shows that crusade politics included survival and local rivalry as well as belief.
- Béziers operated as exemplary terror even though casualty numbers and the famous command are uncertain.
- Simon de Montfort transformed an expedition into prolonged territorial occupation.
- Repeated burnings could intensify rather than dissolve the solidarity of the accused.
- Private crusading conquest failed to stabilize without direct French royal power.
- The war helped expand both Capetian territory and institutions for investigating religious conformity.

## Evidence

- Mobilization and submission: [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] follows Innocent's summons and Raymond's public penance.
- Massacre and occupation: [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] narrates Béziers, Carcassonne, Minerve, Lavaur, Muret, and Toulouse.
- Royal consolidation: [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] traces Amaury's failure, Louis VIII's intervention, the Treaty of Paris, and Toulouse's 1271 transfer.
- Institutional aftermath: [[303-the-bloodiest-crusade-glt7887097153]] follows surviving accused heretics into mendicant and inquisitorial suppression.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The campaign cannot be reduced either to disinterested theology or to a French land grab. Religious conviction, institutional authority, aristocratic rivalry, crusading vows, local identity, and territorial opportunity interacted. Atrocity stories, numbers, attributed speeches, and later doctrinal reconstructions require source criticism.

## What Changed

- Established the crusade as a linked religious, military, institutional, and state-formation process rather than a single massacre or conquest.
