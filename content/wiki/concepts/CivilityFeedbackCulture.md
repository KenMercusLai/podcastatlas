# Civility Feedback Culture

## Definition
A civility feedback culture combines everyday respect with enough trust and directness for people to identify harmful behavior, challenge one another clearly, and repair mistakes without humiliation or silence.

## Current Synthesis
The episode treats unintentional incivility as partly a self-awareness problem: people can overestimate how clearly they see themselves while remaining blind to interruption, dismissiveness, accusatory email, or other habits visible to colleagues. Behavioral feedback makes the blind spot observable when it is specific, timely, and delivered within a relationship that communicates care and belief in improvement.

Civility does not require avoiding disagreement. The stronger norm joins personal regard to direct challenge, so people can contribute ideas, admit uncertainty, identify errors, and ask for help. A history of respect also creates repair capacity when someone misjudges tone or impact.

## Key Claims
- Self-assessed self-awareness can greatly exceed externally demonstrated self-awareness.
- Trusted people can reveal recurring behaviors that remain invisible to the actor.
- Clear feedback is more usable when it identifies behavior and impact without degrading identity.
- Respect and direct challenge are complements rather than opposites.
- Trust and psychological safety improve idea sharing, error reporting, help-seeking, and repair.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Care language can become manipulative when conduct, power, or consequences contradict it. Feedback does not require a target to remain in an unsafe exchange, and civility must not become a demand for silence about injustice. Cultural and regional norms change how tone and directness are perceived, while email removes many cues; shared expectations therefore need revision and repair rather than a single universal style.

## What Changed
- Established a culture model joining self-awareness, behavioral feedback, caring directness, trust, and repair.
