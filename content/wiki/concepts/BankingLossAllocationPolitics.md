# Banking Loss Allocation Politics

## Definition
Banking loss allocation politics is the contest over who absorbs the costs of financial failure and resilience: shareholders, bank creditors, depositors, taxpayers, the institution through retained capital, or external lenders and guarantors.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources show two time horizons of the same distributional problem. In [[Lebanon]], already-realized banking losses block reform, reconstruction, and an [[InternationalMonetaryFund|IMF]] agreement because no coalition accepts the allocation. In [[Switzerland]], the 2023 rescue of [[CreditSuisse|Credit Suisse]] prevented wider collapse but left a larger [[UBS|UBS]]; the proposed response asks the bank and its shareholders to pre-fund more resilience through parent-level common equity. Crisis resolution can therefore move costs through time without making them disappear.

## Key Claims
- A banking rescue is also a distributional fight among shareholders, depositors, creditors, banks, states, and outside lenders.
- Unallocated realized losses can block reform and reconstruction even when foreign capital is available in principle.
- Preventive capital requirements allocate prospective crisis cost by retaining loss-absorbing funds before another failure.
- Preventing contagion may create a larger or more concentrated institution whose future safety still requires funding.
- Financial credibility becomes state legitimacy when reconstruction, security, taxation, or national reputation depends on resolving the banking system.

## Evidence
- Realized-loss allocation: [[hard-cedar-lebanon-seeks-trumps-help-6a5f41e97bddc0bea5542de5]] reports roughly $80bn in unresolved Lebanese banking losses blocking an IMF path and wider state rebuilding.
- Prospective safety-cost allocation: [[shangye-xiaoyang-51-shenme-ruiyin-yaoba-zongbu-banli-ruishi-gkwrijiotaj1agndggtvuvds]] presents Switzerland's proposed UBS capital rule as a choice between stronger loss absorption and lower shareholder distributions or a smaller balance sheet.
- Rescue-aftereffect: [[shangye-xiaoyang-51-shenme-ruiyin-yaoba-zongbu-banli-ruishi-gkwrijiotaj1agndggtvuvds]] shows how absorbing Credit Suisse avoided an immediate crisis while increasing the surviving bank's scale relative to the state.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The cases are not interchangeable. Lebanon concerns a system-wide collapse with realized losses and stalled reconstruction; Switzerland concerns an unfinished prudential proposal after a state-backed acquisition. Neither source supplies a complete legal waterfall, audited loss model, or welfare comparison. Stronger capital can reduce expected public exposure without eliminating run, governance, liquidity, or resolution risk, while delayed allocation may sometimes reflect legitimate disputes about valuation and fairness rather than simple obstruction.

## What Changed
- Extended the concept from realized post-collapse losses to prospective resilience costs.
- Added shareholder distributions and retained common equity to the allocation map.
- Added rescue-created concentration: stopping one collapse can enlarge the next systemic-risk problem.
