# Hong Kong Chief Executive Legitimacy / 香港特首認受性

## Definition
Hong Kong Chief Executive Legitimacy is the source's concept for the gap between executive authority and public recognition in the post-1997 chief-executive system.

## Current Synthesis
The source argues that [[TungCheeHwa|董建華]]'s administration revealed a legitimacy gap built into the early [[HongKongChiefExecutive]] office. The chief executive was expected to lead through administrative authority and elite consultation, but lacked universal-suffrage authorization. During economic collapse, housing controversy, legal interpretation, SARS, and Article 23, that gap became politically explosive.

## Key Claims
- The chief executive's formal authority did not automatically produce public recognition.
- Lack of universal suffrage made crisis management harder when policy mistakes accumulated.
- Consultation with business and establishment elites could not substitute for broad democratic accountability.
- Beijing confidence and local public credibility became separate but both necessary survival conditions.

## Evidence
- Structural claim: [[dong-jianhua-shishi-aiguozhe-yuanlao-de-xianggang-rensheng-7d743a8515cf65334add1a55cd20148e]] says Beijing's design emphasized executive-led governance but lacked universal-suffrage legitimacy.
- Crisis evidence: [[dong-jianhua-shishi-aiguozhe-yuanlao-de-xianggang-rensheng-7d743a8515cf65334add1a55cd20148e]] places the Article 23 protests and Beijing's loss of confidence as the end point of Tung's governing crisis.
- Public-space contrast: [[dong-jianhua-shishi-aiguozhe-yuanlao-de-xianggang-rensheng-7d743a8515cf65334add1a55cd20148e]] contrasts Tung-era mass public pressure with today's changed political atmosphere.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is based on the source's interpretation and does not yet include legal defenses of the executive-led model, later electoral-system changes, or polling/election data across administrations.
