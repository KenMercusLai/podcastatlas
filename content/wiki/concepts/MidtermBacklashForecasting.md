# Midterm Backlash Forecasting

## Definition
Midterm backlash forecasting estimates congressional and state control by combining presidential approval, economic mood, special-election evidence, map structure, shocks, and candidate quality, then comparing those expectations with later outcomes.

## Current Synthesis
The June source separated a strongly Democratic House environment from a harder Senate path requiring red-state wins. Two November sources report a broader result than that base case: Democrats won both chambers and contests across traditionally Republican states. The forecast correctly identified presidential backlash, economic anxiety, candidate selection, and the possibility that a Senate win would imply a wider sweep, while underweighting or at least not resolving the eventual scale of Senate and state-level movement.

The historical comparison adds a second boundary: magnitude is not the same as durable realignment. Midterm losses are common for a president's party, while 1994 and 2010 mattered longer because they changed congressional control or party organization. County-level Democratic vote retention and Republican losses can produce a wave through [[PartisanTurnoutMotivationAsymmetry]] before dissatisfied voters adopt a new durable party identity.

The newest source makes causal attribution an explicit part of forecast evaluation. Its panel agrees on the magnitude of defeat but disputes whether the Iran war and energy-price shock were decisive or whether they interacted with tariffs, immigration enforcement, deficits, housing, healthcare, trust, and a longer [[USEconomicExperienceSplit|asset-owner/wage-earner split]]. The 2026 result therefore supports probabilistic rather than binary evaluation while leaving both the causal weights and structural interpretation open.

## Key Claims
- Presidential approval, economic anxiety, and special-election signals can align into a strong opposition-party environment.
- Senate geography can make one chamber harder to win without making it immune to a sufficiently broad wave.
- Candidate quality and visible prices shape how national conditions translate into individual contests.
- A Senate win in a difficult map can signal an even wider House and gubernatorial sweep.
- Forecast quality should be evaluated against both direction and magnitude, not only whether one chamber changed hands.
- Wave size alone cannot establish realignment; later party organization, coalition persistence, and presidential-election performance remain necessary tests.
- Forecast explanations should distinguish persuasion and party switching from unequal mobilization and abstention, then preserve competing causal accounts when the available evidence cannot assign weights.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The earlier probabilities are a June snapshot and all later files are immediate interpretive summaries rather than official results or a formal forecast scorecard. A lower-probability Senate win is not a failed probabilistic forecast by itself; evaluating calibration requires many comparable forecasts, not one outcome. County totals cannot identify individual switching or abstention. Panel arguments, cited polls, price series, and event timing do not isolate the causal effect of war, tariffs, immigration, deficits, candidate quality, or household costs. Historical analogy cannot establish whether 2026 will resemble an ordinary backlash, 1994-style congressional change, or 2010-style party reorganization.

## What Changed
- Added the distinction between agreeing on an outcome and disagreeing over its causes.
- Preserved the Iran-war/energy thesis and the broader affordability/trust thesis as competing explanations rather than collapsing them.
- Made causal identification, not only direction and magnitude, part of forecast evaluation.
