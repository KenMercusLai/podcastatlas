# Midterm Backlash Forecasting

## Definition
Midterm backlash forecasting estimates congressional and state control by combining presidential approval, economic mood, special-election evidence, map structure, shocks, and candidate quality, then comparing those expectations with later outcomes.

## Current Synthesis
The June source separated a strongly Democratic House environment from a harder Senate path requiring red-state wins. Two November sources report a broader result than that base case: Democrats won both chambers and contests across traditionally Republican states. The forecast correctly identified presidential backlash, economic anxiety, candidate selection, and the possibility that a Senate win would imply a wider sweep, while underweighting or at least not resolving the eventual scale of Senate and state-level movement.

The new historical comparison adds a second boundary: magnitude is not the same as durable realignment. Midterm losses are common for a president's party, while 1994 and 2010 mattered longer because they changed congressional control or party organization. The 2026 result therefore supports probabilistic rather than binary forecast evaluation but leaves the structural interpretation open.

## Key Claims
- Presidential approval, economic anxiety, and special-election signals can align into a strong opposition-party environment.
- Senate geography can make one chamber harder to win without making it immune to a sufficiently broad wave.
- Candidate quality and visible prices shape how national conditions translate into individual contests.
- A Senate win in a difficult map can signal an even wider House and gubernatorial sweep.
- Forecast quality should be evaluated against both direction and magnitude, not only whether one chamber changed hands.
- Wave size alone cannot establish realignment; later party organization, coalition persistence, and presidential-election performance remain necessary tests.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The earlier probabilities are a June snapshot and both later files are immediate interpretive summaries rather than official results or a formal forecast scorecard. A lower-probability Senate win is not a failed probabilistic forecast by itself; evaluating calibration requires many comparable forecasts, not one outcome. Historical analogy also cannot establish whether 2026 will resemble an ordinary backlash, 1994-style congressional change, or 2010-style party reorganization.

## What Changed
