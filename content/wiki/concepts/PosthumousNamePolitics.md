## Definition

Posthumous name politics / 谥号政治 is the use, contestation, or abolition of names assigned after death to compress official judgment, ritual status, dynastic memory, and political authority into the label by which a ruler or noble is remembered.

## Current Synthesis

Across the current sources, a posthumous name is never merely an ordinary personal name. Zhou-annals examples show the basic interpretive function: characters such as 威烈, 安, 声, 悼, and 幽 present later readers with praise, blame, or mourning already embedded in chronicle language. Liu De's “献” extends that function to a learned vassal king, while also raising a credibility problem: officials who answer to powerful descendants may turn retrospective judgment into formulaic praise.

The politics can operate through inclusion as well as word choice. In the late-Han burial dispute around [[EmpressDowagerDouMiao|窦皇太后]], the title 桓思皇后 seals her recognition within the legitimate emperor-empress order after officials defeat attempts to exclude her through family guilt. Qin supplies the inverse strategy: [[QinShiHuang|嬴政 / 秦始皇]] abolishes 谥法 and substitutes a numbered imperial succession, removing the residual premise that later ministers may morally label the ruler.

## Key Claims

- Posthumous names are retrospective political and ritual judgments, not ordinary names used during life.
- Compact characters can encode praise, criticism, mourning, learning, or virtue and thereby guide later historical memory.
- The judgment is institutionally vulnerable because fear of descendants or current rulers can encourage conventional praise rather than candid evaluation.
- A posthumous title can settle contested ritual inclusion, as Dou Miao's recognition shows.
- Abolishing posthumous naming can itself be political by denying successors and ministers a formal vocabulary of retrospective judgment.
- Chronicle readers should treat these labels as interpretations whose production conditions matter.

## Evidence

- Basic retrospective judgment: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-06-weiwenhou-xuan-shui-dang-zaixiang-ne-2-lmfzo4sl9nd5kz3mrxdl1dl9efei|Zhouji 06 part 2]] explains that deceased rulers receive characters chosen according to life record and that labels such as 幽 can be negative.
- Praise and institutional distortion: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-268-hejianxianwang-liude-ruhe-yu-shijing-jieyuan-2-lse66eacijdfcyjk76aso6vzp6b_|Hanji 268 part 2]] links Liu De's “献” to intelligence and learning, then argues that political pressure can make titles increasingly flattering and less candid.
- Ritual inclusion: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-901-jixingzi-de-ren-hui-shi-shenme-xiachang-lh1yfyjzqptxvtnv9taiqms9yjud|Hanji 901]] records Dou Miao's burial with Emperor Huan and recognition as 桓思皇后 after a contested court decision.
- Abolition of later judgment: [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-120-2-qinchao-di-yi-tao-vi-sheji-linian-lno-aeljks2vt1k3wjrizsanirtq|Qinji 120 part 2]] presents Qin's numbered emperor sequence as a replacement for posthumous evaluation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources are podcast summaries, not a comprehensive institutional history of 谥法. Hanji 268's account of Zhou invention, evaluation procedure, exact character rules, and growing title length needs primary-text comparison, and its criticism of flattering titles does not prove that every positive title is insincere. Qin's abolition does not eliminate all later historical judgment; it removes one formal naming mechanism. Dou Miao's case shows that title, burial, and legitimacy can converge, but not that all posthumous titles resolve an active dispute.
