## Definition

治理能力型合法性 / governance-capacity legitimacy is the source's contested claim that a regime's right to rule should be judged substantially by its ability to end violence, restrain powerful actors, maintain subsistence, and make ordinary life secure, rather than only by inherited title or formal dynastic continuity.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1131-wo-wei-shenme-zhichi-caowei-cuanhan-lvcjau_k5voaz6efl9rafcrn5px6|Hanji 1131]] applies this frame to the late Han. It contrasts [[EmperorXianOfHan|汉献帝]]'s recognized title but missing independent staff, army, and territory with [[CaoCao|曹操]]'s northern consolidation and administrative capacity. In the host's judgment, a sovereign form that cannot prevent war or elite predation should not automatically outweigh a power capable of rebuilding order.

The concept is comparative, not self-validating. Capacity must be demonstrated through durable public outcomes rather than inferred from military strength, and order cannot be assessed without counting coercion, civilian harm, succession risk, and who receives protection. The episode supplies a forceful welfare-first argument but not a complete comparison of Han and Cao-Wei governance.

## Key Claims

- Formal title and actual governing capacity can diverge sharply during state collapse.
- Security, food, predictable administration, and restraint of private coercion are public goods relevant to political legitimacy.
- Military unification can create the conditions for order without proving that the victorious ruler will govern for public benefit.
- A powerless sovereign may remain symbolically valuable while lacking the organization needed for independent rule.
- Invoking necessity does not remove responsibility for coercion, atrocity, predation, or failed succession.

## Evidence

### Title-capacity divergence

- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1131-wo-wei-shenme-zhichi-caowei-cuanhan-lvcjau_k5voaz6efl9rafcrn5px6|Hanji 1131]] uses Emperor Xian's lack of an independent staff, army, and territory to question whether formal restoration could produce effective rule.

### Order and public welfare

- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1131-wo-wei-shenme-zhichi-caowei-cuanhan-lvcjau_k5voaz6efl9rafcrn5px6|Hanji 1131]] treats the end of war, restraint of strong families, subsistence, and personal security as the relevant outcomes behind its preference for Cao Cao's order-building.

### Cross-camp moral qualification

- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1131-wo-wei-shenme-zhichi-caowei-cuanhan-lvcjau_k5voaz6efl9rafcrn5px6|Hanji 1131]] uses Liu Bei's selection of Pang Tong's deceptive middle plan to argue that restoration language does not place one camp outside ordinary power competition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source argues a position rather than establishing a comparative governance record. Its Western Jin analogy crosses periods and institutions, while its account of later devastation does not prove that retaining Emperor Xian would have caused the same outcome or that Cao-Wei replacement uniquely prevented it. Military success, centralization, and the capacity to coerce can protect ordinary people or expose them to organized harm. Any application therefore needs evidence about distribution, durability, law, extraction, civilian cost, feedback, and succession rather than a strong-ruler label alone.

## What Changed

- Created a qualified welfare-and-order frame for the episode's defense of dynastic replacement.
- Made the distinction between demonstrated public capacity and military strength explicit.
- Preserved coercion, civilian harm, and succession as legitimacy tests rather than external objections.
