# Revolutionary Ideological War

## Definition

Revolutionary ideological war is armed conflict justified not only by defence or territorial interest but by a claim to transform the political and social order of other peoples.

## Current Synthesis

The ideological program preceded French military success. [[JacquesPierreBrissot|Brissot]] described war as sacred, regenerative, equalizing, financially restorative, and capable of liberating humanity, while [[MaximilienRobespierre|Robespierre]] warned that foreign peoples would reject armed missionaries and that expansion could create a military dictator. [[CharlesFrancoisDumouriez|Dumouriez]] then treated a Belgian rising as the premise of an easy campaign. After [[BattleOfValmy|Valmy]] and French victories, the Convention promised aid and anti-feudal, anti-clerical, and republican reform to peoples seeking liberty. Across both phases, universal aspiration left consent, strategic interest, and conquest in unresolved tension.

## Key Claims

- Military survival can shift revolutionary war from defence to expansion.
- Ideological war can begin before combat when leaders treat national regeneration and foreign liberation as one project.
- Universal political principles make borders appear morally provisional.
- Liberation policy can join aid to institutional redesign imposed by an occupying army.
- Abolition of feudal and church privilege turned war aims into social transformation.
- Patriotic victory and ideological confidence can reinforce one another.
- A declared wish to free peoples does not settle whether those peoples consent to the liberator's program.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The episode poses but does not resolve the difference between liberation, strategic expansion, and conquest.
- The episode's retrospective link from Robespierre's warning to Napoleon is suggestive, not proof of inevitability.
- “The people” of another territory cannot be assumed to speak with one voice or to welcome the same reforms.
- The source covers the declaration and early advances, not the full later record of French occupation.

## What Changed

- The concept now begins with the prewar rhetoric and factional incentives that made armed liberation thinkable.
- Robespierre's warning and Dumouriez's untested Belgian premise make consent, aggression, and dictatorship risks explicit.
