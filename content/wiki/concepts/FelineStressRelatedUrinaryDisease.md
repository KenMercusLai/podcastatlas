# Feline Stress-Related Urinary Disease / 猫应激相关泌尿疾病

## Definition
Feline stress-related urinary disease is the source's clinical frame linking a cat's stress response and environment to lower-urinary signs such as cystitis, blood in urine, painful urination, or urinary obstruction.

## Current Synthesis
The episode treats feline illness as inseparable from species behavior and the household environment. Cats may conceal pain, remain highly vigilant, produce concentrated urine, and react to environmental or emotional disruption in ways humans overlook. In male cats, a narrow urethra can make inability to urinate an urgent escalation problem rather than a minor behavior change.

The source's distinctive contribution is relational: temperature shifts, heating changes, transport, anxious handling, noise, or family conflict may become stressors, while caregiver anxiety can intensify the animal's arousal during care. This does not mean every urinary problem is psychological or seasonal. It means environment and behavior belong in the history alongside physical examination and veterinary diagnosis.

## Key Claims
- Cats can conceal pain and illness, so withdrawal or subtle behavior change may carry clinical information.
- Stress can coincide with bladder inflammation, bleeding, gastrointestinal symptoms, or cardiovascular strain in susceptible cats.
- Male-cat urinary obstruction can become an emergency because of urethral anatomy.
- Household conflict, noise, transport, caregiver anxiety, and environmental change may contribute to stress exposure.
- Seasonal clusters reported by a clinician do not establish a known causal mechanism.
- Environmental history should complement, not replace, physical diagnosis and urgent veterinary assessment.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
One interview and several clinical anecdotes cannot establish causation, prevalence, or a seasonal mechanism. Blood in urine, straining, pain, repeated litter-box visits, or inability to urinate can have multiple causes and require veterinary evaluation; this page is not a diagnostic checklist or treatment guide.
