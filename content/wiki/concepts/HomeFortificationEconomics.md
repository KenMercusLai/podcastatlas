# Home Fortification Economics

## Definition
Home fortification economics studies when modest changes to a building's materials, connections, spacing, or water and ember defenses can avert much larger disaster losses, and how grants, insurance incentives, codes, and certifications affect adoption.

## Current Synthesis
The [[InstituteForBusinessAndHomeSafety|Institute for Business and Home Safety]] cases in [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790]] show unusually high apparent leverage from some measures: a fortified test house survived where a standard house failed, and roof-deck sealing sharply reduced water damage. Adoption nonetheless depends on up-front cost, policy support, credible verification, neighboring structures, and whether insurers recognize mitigation in prices or coverage.

## Key Claims
- Small construction-cost increases can prevent nonlinear losses when they preserve the building envelope during wind or fire exposure.
- Roof integrity matters because modest exterior damage can become extensive interior water damage.
- Performance differences among shingles and assemblies give builders, homeowners, and insurers a shared reason to compare tested products.
- Grants and required discounts can align household affordability with insurer claim reduction, as the episode's Alabama case illustrates.
- Wildfire protection is a package rather than a single upgrade because embers, vegetation, vents, fences, and nearby structures interact.
- Certification can make mitigation legible to insurers, but it cannot guarantee lower premiums or continuing coverage.

## Evidence
### Wind and water leverage
- [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790]] reports that a fortified house costing less than 5% more survived a wind test that destroyed the standard house.
- [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790]] says an inexpensive sealed roof-deck layer prevented as much as 95% of wind-driven rain damage in testing.

### Adoption policy
- [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790]] describes [[Alabama]] grants, required insurer discounts, more than 50,000 fortified roofs, and reported performance during Hurricane Sally.

### Wildfire package and limits
- [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790]] identifies defensible space, ember-resistant vents, and non-wooden fence transitions while noting that the complete package can be unaffordable.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- The figures are reported by the episode without the underlying engineering studies, comparison design, lifecycle costs, maintenance assumptions, or independent replication.
- A laboratory survival result does not establish identical performance across building types, installation quality, hazards, or real disasters.
- Discounts may not offset rising baseline premiums, and certification may not overcome insurer concentration limits or neighborhood exposure.

## What Changed
- Established a focused framework for the cost-to-loss leverage of structural mitigation.
- Added policy adoption, package completeness, and insurer recognition as conditions on technical performance.
