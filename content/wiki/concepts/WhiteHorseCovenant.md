## Definition

The White Horse Covenant / 白马之盟 is the reported early [[WesternHanDynasty|Western Han]] compact that restricted royal rank to the Liu clan and marquis rank to people with merit, backed by a collective duty to attack violators. The supplied episodes present it as an oath-based agreement among the founder, imperial relatives, kings, marquises, generals, and ministers rather than as a fully reconstructed statute; it later becomes inherited legitimacy language for judging whether a regent may elevate her natal family.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-192-liubang-zhongshang-jing-ganyuan-dengsi-lozkwavngz30qzynceptext1qurx|Hanji 192]] supplies the covenant's reported founding setting near [[LiuBang|刘邦]]'s death. It describes a white-horse sacrifice and blood oath at Chang'an, probably in the third month of Gaozu's twelfth year, and presents the compact as a mutual assurance between the Liu imperial house and the founding merit coalition. Its two boundaries join clan and achievement: non-Liu persons may not become kings, and persons without merit may not become marquises.

The same episode pairs customary oath with formal edict. The covenant reportedly asks participants and Heaven to secure collective compliance, while a publicly issued legal document confirms the rulers, marquises, fiefs, and service exemptions produced by joint conquest and pacification. This does not prove the precise legal category of either instrument, but it supports a dual-stabilization reading: personal-customary commitment and public administrative guarantee address the same succession-era anxiety through different forms.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-199-zhongguo-lishishang-di-yi-wei-nvhuangdi-bu-shi-wuhou-1-lmap8nkd8om21u-uptmzhhinsla-|Hanji 199 part 1]] then makes the covenant politically active after [[CaoShen|曹参]]'s death. When [[LuZhi|吕雉]] proposes posthumous royal honors for her father and [[LuZe|吕泽]], [[WangLingHanMinister|王陵]] treats the reported compact as a binding dynastic limit. [[ChenPing|陈平]] and [[ZhouBo|周勃]] instead accommodate the ruler who controls the present court, while claiming that longer-run preservation of the state and Liu line may require something other than immediate frontal opposition.

The episode therefore exposes two functions of a founding covenant. It supplies a clear public standard against natal-family kingship, but it does not enforce itself: ministers must decide whether to invoke it openly, bend under the current power balance, or preserve capacity for later action. The source favors a strategic reading of Chen Ping and Zhou Bo, but their private intention and any direct causal line from accommodation to later restoration remain unproven.

## Key Claims

- The covenant makes clan identity and demonstrated merit explicit boundaries around the highest early Han ranks.
- Its reported oath ceremony addresses reciprocal trust between the imperial clan and the founding merit coalition rather than operating only as a unilateral imperial command.
- Pairing the covenant with a public edict suggests complementary customary and legal-administrative strategies for preserving rewards and political order.
- Wang Ling uses the covenant as a public constraint on the regent rather than as a ceremonial memory with no present force.
- A founding rule can remain normatively strong while being institutionally weak when the current ruler controls appointments and coercive power.
- Chen Ping and Zhou Bo's accommodation separates public compliance from possible longer-run loyalty, but the strategic motive remains source-attributed.
- The dispute turns Lü-family kingship into a legitimacy conflict between dynastic continuity, regent authority, and natal-kin advancement.

## Evidence

- Founding setting and participants: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-192-liubang-zhongshang-jing-ganyuan-dengsi-lozkwavngz30qzynceptext1qurx|Hanji 192]] places a reconstructed oath ceremony near Liu Bang's death in Chang'an and names imperial relatives, Liu kings, marquises, generals, and senior ministers as participants.
- Rank boundaries and enforcement formula: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-192-liubang-zhongshang-jing-ganyuan-dengsi-lozkwavngz30qzynceptext1qurx|Hanji 192]] reports the non-Liu kingship rule, merit requirement for marquises, and collective action against violators.
- Dual-instrument stabilization: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-192-liubang-zhongshang-jing-ganyuan-dengsi-lozkwavngz30qzynceptext1qurx|Hanji 192]] pairs the customary covenant with a formal edict confirming founding rewards and service exemptions.
- Covenant formula and dispute: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-199-zhongguo-lishishang-di-yi-wei-nvhuangdi-bu-shi-wuhou-1-lmap8nkd8om21u-uptmzhhinsla-|Hanji 199 part 1]] reports the “non-Liu shall not be king / without merit shall not be marquis” rule and Wang Ling's use of it against the proposed honors.
- Competing ministerial responses: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-199-zhongguo-lishishang-di-yi-wei-nvhuangdi-bu-shi-wuhou-1-lmap8nkd8om21u-uptmzhhinsla-|Hanji 199 part 1]] contrasts Wang Ling's direct remonstrance with Chen Ping and Zhou Bo's acceptance under Lu Zhi's effective rule.
- Later institutional consequence: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-199-zhongguo-lishishang-di-yi-wei-nvhuangdi-bu-shi-wuhou-2-luhqch956l2lwf_n-kfyaseyodpr|Hanji 199 part 2]] turns the initial dispute into Wang Ling's appointment to a prestigious but practically weaker office; it extends the covenant's political effect without independently verifying its origin.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

These three same-series source notes do not independently establish the compact's exact date, ceremony, wording, participant list, transmission history, or legal status. Hanji 192 acknowledges that the details are reconstructed from incomplete records, and its sharp distinction between customary oath and legally effective edict remains the host's framing pending direct textual comparison. The formulation may compress different early Han rules and later narrative memory. Lu Zhi's initial proposal concerns posthumous honors for her father and brother in the supplied account; the wider program of Lü-family kingship develops beyond part 1. Later anti-Lü action makes the source's strategic interpretation of Chen Ping and Zhou Bo plausible, but retrospective success cannot by itself prove their earlier private design.

## What Changed

- Added the reported founding ceremony, participant groups, and reciprocal founder–merit-coalition function.
- Added the pairing of oath custom with a public edict while preserving uncertainty about exact legal status.
- Reframed the later regency dispute as a test of a founding assurance whose public force still depended on political enforcement.
