# Riemann Hypothesis

## Definition

The Riemann hypothesis is a foundational conjecture in number theory concerning the zeros of the Riemann zeta function and their relationship to the distribution of prime numbers.

## Current Synthesis

The wiki records a consequential but unverified episode claim that [[LevanAlpoge]] used [[Claude]] and [[LeanTheoremProver|Lean]] to prove the hypothesis. The appropriate current judgment is not that the theorem is settled, but that the report offers an extreme test of [[AIForMath]] governance: a huge artifact may compile while the intended statement, dependencies, originality, conceptual route, and human-readable explanation still require expert scrutiny.

## Key Claims

- The conjecture links analytic structure in the zeta function to the distribution of prime numbers.
- Many conditional number-theory results depend on assuming it, so a valid proof would have broad mathematical consequences.
- The episode reports a Claude-assisted Lean artifact of about 17 million lines and says others recompiled it.
- Machine recompilation and mathematical acceptance are different thresholds.
- A proof too large for practical human reading creates a severe interpretation, explanation, and governance problem even if its formal checks succeed.

## Evidence

- Mathematical importance - [[in-the-life-of-prime-ais-latest-maths-triumph-0543079e679f77fa9c07ce929a73b21f]] describes the hypothesis as a long-standing central problem tied to prime distribution and downstream conditional results.
- Claimed formal proof - [[in-the-life-of-prime-ais-latest-maths-triumph-0543079e679f77fa9c07ce929a73b21f]] reports the Claude-assisted Lean artifact and independent recompilation.
- Interpretability boundary - [[in-the-life-of-prime-ais-latest-maths-triumph-0543079e679f77fa9c07ce929a73b21f]] says no accessible account was released and estimates an enormous human-readable expansion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The supplied summary does not include the formal statement, code, build environment, axioms, dependencies, review record, or a conventional proof. Recompilation can verify a particular formal artifact relative to its environment without establishing that it encodes the intended theorem, contains no problematic assumptions, is original, or has been accepted by the mathematical community.
