## Definition

Status-driven command failure / 面子驱动的指挥失误 is a command failure pattern in which a leader's need to preserve reputation, prove worth, or protect office overrides troop condition, timing, and sound counsel, turning a solvable operational problem into unnecessary defeat.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 688 grounds the concept through [[DengYuEasternHan|邓禹]] during the Chimei campaign. Deng Yu carries high expectations but lacks matching victory, so the source reads his repeated attacks less as neutral military judgment than as a status repair attempt. Hungry soldiers, uncertain timing, and [[FengYiEasternHan|冯异]]'s caution all become secondary to the need for a visible win.

The failure is not only pride in the abstract. It changes what information counts. Deng Yu treats Chimei movement as proof of his own success, pushes exhausted troops into repeated attacks, rejects gradual inducement, and insists on fighting when Feng Yi says the soldiers need rest. The result is defeat, heavy casualties, and Deng Yu returning to Yiyang with only a small mounted remnant.

The concept therefore sits between battlefield judgment and leadership psychology. It marks the point where a commander stops asking whether the army can win under current conditions and starts needing battle to repair personal standing. In Hanji 688, Feng Yi supplies the contrast: he recommends timing and rest before battle, then later wins by waiting for morale and confusion to favor his side.

## Key Claims

- A commander's status anxiety can distort how enemy movement, troop condition, and timing are interpreted.
- Visible achievement pressure is especially dangerous when the army is hungry, tired, or recovering from earlier mistakes.
- Ignoring competent counsel is a diagnostic sign when the rejected advice names concrete conditions rather than abstract caution.
- The failure mode converts battle from a strategic instrument into a reputation-repair attempt.
- Recovery requires shifting attention back to timing, troop condition, and enemy vulnerability rather than to the commander's need for face.
- The pattern should be distinguished from calculated risk: the problem is not boldness, but boldness detached from available conditions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is currently grounded in one source's interpretation of Deng Yu's motives. The material facts support bad timing, exhausted troops, rejected counsel, and defeat; the "face" diagnosis is the host's interpretive layer and should not be generalized into a full psychological biography of Deng Yu. The page should also not treat all urgent attacks as status-driven. Urgency becomes a failure here because the source repeatedly shows concrete reasons to wait and a commander overriding them to repair visible standing.
