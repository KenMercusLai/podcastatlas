# Occult Politics

## Definition

Occult politics is the use or management of prophecy, magic, astrology, ritual, witchcraft, spiritualism, and occult reputation in struggles over political advice, legitimacy, enemy definition, decision-making, and state security.

## Current Synthesis

The source identifies several mechanisms that should not be collapsed into one claim about credulity. Sacred or cosmic symbolism can magnify royal authority; specialists can supply advice or hidden knowledge; prophecy can organize rebellion or national destiny; sorcery accusation can stigmatize opponents; and alleged revelation can displace practical judgment. By the twentieth century, authorities could treat an occult performance as a rumor or leakage risk without accepting its supernatural explanation.

The historical change is therefore institutional as well as intellectual. [[Merlin]] belongs to a world where prophetic counsel and kingship can reinforce each other, [[JohnDee]] occupies a contested continuum of mathematics, astrology, empire, and angelic inquiry, and [[HelenDuncan]] faces a state concerned with information control. In each case the political effect depends on how claims are used, circulated, and acted upon, not simply whether they are true.

## Key Claims

- Occult symbolism can legitimate authority without dictating day-to-day policy.
- Prophecy and astrology can function as counsel, but become dangerous when they replace reality testing.
- Occult reputation can be conferred by enemies as readily as cultivated by practitioners.
- Religious and political conflict can turn ritual difference into accusations of sorcery or satanic alliance.
- States can respond to occult claims as fraud, rumor, morale, or security problems without endorsing supernatural causation.
- The meaning of “occult” changes with the boundary among learned knowledge, religion, entertainment, law, and state administration.

## Evidence

### Legitimation and counsel

- [[174-merlin-magic-and-the-british-glt5141910845]] connects Solomonic kingship, Merlinic prophecy, coronation astrology, the Westminster pavement, Roger Bacon, and John Dee to royal authority and advice.

### Decision capture

- [[174-merlin-magic-and-the-british-glt5141910845]] presents Richard II's “magical quietism” and Dee's acceptance of purported angelic commands as cases where occult interpretation could override practical judgment.

### Accusation and propaganda

- [[174-merlin-magic-and-the-british-glt5141910845]] follows sorcery language through Joan of Arc, Reformation anti-Catholic polemic, Civil War pamphlets, and anti-James II ridicule.

### Security management

- [[174-merlin-magic-and-the-british-glt5141910845]] treats Helen Duncan's wartime seances as potential information leakage or careless talk rather than proof of official supernatural belief.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The framework does not imply that rulers, accusers, audiences, and officials shared one belief or motive. Symbolic use is not proof of literal belief, accusation is not proof of occult practice, and government response is not proof that officials accepted claimed powers. The source ranges widely and cannot by itself establish each episode's chronology, causation, or representativeness.

## What Changed

- Created a comparative framework separating legitimation, counsel, decision capture, accusation, and security management.
