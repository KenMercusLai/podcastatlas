# Crime-Fiction Authority Taxonomy

## Definition

The crime-fiction authority taxonomy classifies stories by the role assigned to official power: the Eastern presents excellent authority, the Western removes effective authority, the Northern makes authority incompetent, and the Southern makes authority corrupt or malignant.

## Current Synthesis

[[taxonomy-of-the-modern-mystery-story]] offers the scheme as a deliberately reductive critical tool. The categories identify who restores order and what blocks justice: capable institutions, institutional absence, official failure, or institutional corruption. Hybrids remain possible; the episode's “Northeastern” initially codes characters as failures before revealing their competence.

The taxonomy is most useful when it exposes what a story asks audiences to assume about institutions. It is not evidence that every work has one stable meaning or that repeated exposure directly causes a political belief. Its stronger claim is that a narrow repertoire of roles can make heroic, helpless, inept, or rotten authority feel culturally familiar while obscuring public cooperation, mixed performance, legal limits, and variation within real departments.

## Key Claims

- The taxonomy tracks the narrative competence and moral status of authority rather than geography.
- Easterns make institutional professionalism the source of order.
- Westerns make private or exceptional action necessary because effective authority is absent.
- Northerns justify gifted outsiders by making official investigators unable to solve the case.
- Southerns make the institution itself an obstacle through corruption or malign power.
- Hybrids combine roles or reverse the audience's initial judgment of competence.
- Cultural usefulness does not establish a measurable media effect or exhaustive genre law.

## Evidence

### Institutional competence and absence

- [[taxonomy-of-the-modern-mystery-story]] uses Ed McBain's 87th Precinct and *Dragnet* as Easterns, while Jack Reacher supplies a Western in which order depends on action outside effective policing.

### Incompetence, corruption, and hybrids

- [[taxonomy-of-the-modern-mystery-story]] uses Sherlock Holmes and Hercule Poirot for the Northern, John Grisham's subverted institutions for the Southern, and *Slow Horses* for a hybrid “Northeastern.”

### Cultural stakes

- [[taxonomy-of-the-modern-mystery-story]] argues that repeatedly assigning police a small set of roles can flatten real differences among misconduct, institutional failure, professional competence, and public cooperation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

One lecture and a selective set of examples cannot establish that all crime fiction fits the model, that audiences accept the intended role, or that the categories are culturally universal. Genre, point of view, historical setting, race, class, law, and adaptation can change a work's meaning. The episode's links from fiction to political belief, juror conduct, police identity, or public trust are plausible hypotheses and illustrations rather than demonstrated causal estimates.

## What Changed

- Created a qualified four-role model for comparing how crime stories represent official authority.
- Separated its interpretive value from unproven claims about audience and political effects.
