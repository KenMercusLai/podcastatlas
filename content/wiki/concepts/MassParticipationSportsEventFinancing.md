# Mass-Participation Sports Event Financing / 群众体育赛事融资

## Definition
Mass-participation sports event financing is the mixed funding and resource system through which participant fees, commercial sponsorship, in-kind supply, memberships or premium access, and public services support a large race whose organizer costs exceed ordinary entry-fee revenue.

## Current Synthesis
[[lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl-lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl|EP96]] separates the price paid by one runner from the cost of producing a city-scale event. Registration can fund part of the race, but route control, timing, media, structures, supplies, prizes, elite-athlete support, medicine, security, and restoration of normal traffic create a wider cost stack.

The financing system is therefore partly monetary and partly operational. Sponsors may pay cash or supply goods, while government support may appear as a budget contribution or as police, medical, transport, and road-management capacity outside the organizer's accounts. The race's value also extends beyond the organizer when visitors and local audiences create [[MarathonDestinationConsumption]], so organizer profitability and city-level benefit should be evaluated separately.

## Key Claims
- Ordinary entry fees need not cover the full cost of a large road race for the event to have a viable financing model.
- Sponsor value can combine cash, in-kind inputs, category exclusivity, media exposure, hospitality, and on-course activation.
- Public support includes both visible subsidy and operational services whose economic cost may not appear in the organizer's revenue statement.
- Scarce access, charity places, memberships, and international-runner pricing can segment willingness to pay without replacing sponsors or public capacity.
- Organizer revenue, public cost, and host-city economic benefit are different accounting boundaries and should not be treated as one number.

## Evidence
- **Fee-cost gap:** [[lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl-lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl|EP96]] estimates Shanghai entry and charity-place revenue against broadcast, structures, timing, supplies, prizes, elite-athlete support, medical, security, and traffic-management costs and concludes that fees alone are insufficient.
- **Commercial resource stack:** [[lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl-lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl|EP96]] describes sponsors contributing either money or apparel, water, bibs, and signage, commonly with category exclusivity.
- **Public-service layer:** [[lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl-lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl|EP96]] distinguishes stated government subsidy from medical, policing, road-closure, and traffic-restoration support that may sit outside the organizer's reported budget.
- **Price segmentation and recurring access:** [[lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl-lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl|EP96]] contrasts domestic and overseas entry fees, Shanghai charity places, and Tokyo's paid membership and extra-lottery mechanism.
- **Different accounting boundaries:** [[lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl-lv8ss3l8pykybfppj7hf90kgb7fl|EP96]] uses Tokyo to contrast event revenue with a much larger claimed city economic effect.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode does not supply audited organizer accounts, procurement contracts, sponsor contracts, municipal cost allocation, counterfactual tax receipts, or a consistent comparison across the three cities. In-kind support is difficult to value, and a claimed city multiplier can include displaced or non-incremental spending. A fee-cost gap does not by itself prove that a race is efficient, equitable, profitable, or worth its public opportunity cost.

## What Changed
- Separated runner pricing from the full organizer and public-service cost stack.
- Made in-kind sponsorship and off-budget public operations visible alongside cash revenue.
- Distinguished organizer viability from host-city spillovers and public return.
