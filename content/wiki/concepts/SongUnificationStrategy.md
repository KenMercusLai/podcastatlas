# 先南后北 / Song Unification Strategy

Song unification strategy / 先南后北 is the strategic frame [[170-songtaizu-zhisi-zhuying-fusheng-yinmoulun-zhong-de-renxing-mituan-xia-958158115]] gives to [[ZhaoKuangyin|赵匡胤 / 宋太祖]] and [[ZhaoPu|赵普]]'s early statecraft. Rather than attacking the northern frontier first, the source says the early [[NorthernSong|北宋]] chose to absorb southern regimes before confronting enemies tied to northern alliances.

The episode treats the strategy as constraint-driven. A new regime with limited troops, fragile legitimacy, finite revenue, and difficult supply lines needed southern manpower, grain, and tax base before a major northern war. This links strategic patience to [[KaifengCapitalTradeoff|开封定都取舍]] and [[SongMilitaryCentralization|宋初军事集权]].

The concept also carries a moral warning. Strategic rationality does not guarantee humane execution: the Shu campaign and [[SichuanPostConquestResistance|川蜀征服后反抗]] show how unification can end one set of local abuses while creating another through conquest violence and extraction.

[[ep252-taipingnian-zhiwai-de-wudai-shiguo-shenme-yang-lnxm7tfs3zmve3erm9qlbehlefkh]] adds the southern-regime background that makes the strategy more legible. If [[WudaiSouthernRegimeStability|southern regimes]] preserved wealth, population, and culture during northern turnover, then "先南后北" was also a way for the early Song to absorb the accumulated base of [[EconomicCulturalCenterSouthwardShift|economic and cultural southward shift]].

## Key Claims
- Sequencing can be a decisive part of state-building strategy.
- A small northern enemy may be strategically expensive when it is tied to a larger ally.
- Logistics and tax base can matter as much as battlefield ambition.
- Unification narratives should be tested against local experience after conquest.
- Political prudence and moral cost can coexist in the same successful strategy.
