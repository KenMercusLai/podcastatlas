# Succession Non-Designation Risk / 未定继承人风险

## Definition
Succession non-designation risk / 未定继承人风险 is the risk created when a ruler, founder, or family head leaves no legible and enforceable successor. The danger is not silence alone; it is the combination of plausible rival claimants, factional access, armed bases, and outsiders who can turn ambiguity into violence, intervention, or dependency.

## Current Synthesis
The concept began with Warring States and imperial cases in which succession silence exported conflict into the next transition. [[WeiWuhou|魏武侯]] dies without clearly naming a successor, leaving [[LiangHuiWang|魏罃]] and [[GongzhongHuan|公中缓]] to fight. The fight then creates an opening for [[HanState|韩国]] and [[ZhaoState|赵国]], and [[WeiState|魏国]] survives only because the victorious coalition cannot agree on the settlement after [[ZhuozeBattle|浊泽之战]].

Other sources add variants. [[ZhaoXiangzi|赵襄子]] is a contrast case: he designates [[ZhaoXianzi|赵献子]], but the line is still vulnerable when [[ZhaoHuanzi|赵桓子]] expels him temporarily, showing that designation must be enforceable. [[QinWuwang|秦武王]] dies without a son, producing brother struggle until [[WeiRan|魏冉]] secures [[QinZhaoxiangwang|秦昭襄王]]. [[MiWan|楚考烈王]]'s childless situation creates space for [[PregnancySuccessionSubstitution|孕身继承替换]], and the later transition lets [[LiYuanWarringStates|李源 / 李元]] eliminate [[Chunshenjun|黄歇 / 春申君]] and his household. The [[ZhaoZhengShu|《赵正书》]] variant for [[QinShiHuang|秦始皇]] adds a deathbed-proximity version, where [[QinErshi|胡亥 / 秦二世]] is nearby while [[FuSu|扶苏]] is far away.

Hanji 943 adds a late-Han imperial version. [[EmperorLingOfHan|汉灵帝]] dies without naming a crown prince even though [[LiuBianHongnongWang|刘辩 / 弘农王]] and Liu Xie, later [[EmperorXianOfHan|汉献帝]], have different support bases. Private preference for Liu Xie and deathbed trust in [[JianShuoLateHan|蹇硕]] do not substitute for public designation; the result is immediate factional violence involving [[HeJin|何进]], Jian Shuo, [[EmpressDowagerDongLateHan|董太后]], and [[DongZhongLateHan|董重]].

Hanji 1056 and Hanji 1058 add the warlord-family version through [[YuanShao|袁绍]]. Hanji 1056 shows the mechanical bridge from private preference to civil split: Yuan Shao favors [[YuanShang|袁尚]] and has support from Liu's preference, but he does not publicly designate him; [[YuanTan|袁谭]] has been adopted into another line and sent to Qingzhou, Yuan Xi is in Youzhou, and [[GaoGanLateHan|高干]] holds Bingzhou, giving possible claimants and relatives provincial bases. [[JuShou|沮授]] warns that succession must be explicit, but after Yuan Shao dies, [[ShenPeiLateHan|审配]] and [[FengJiLateHan|逢纪]] fear Yuan Tan's retaliation and use a claimed final order to install Yuan Shang. Yuan Tan's self-styled command and killing of Feng Ji show ambiguity turning into armed grievance before open civil war.

Hanji 1058 abstracts the same fracture into enemy strategy. [[GuoJia|郭嘉]] tells [[CaoCao|曹操]] that Yuan Shao loved both Yuan Tan and Yuan Shang but left no clear successor, while both sons retain comparable support. Under external pressure they will help each other; once that pressure relaxes, the succession fracture will reopen. The concept therefore now includes not only palace and state transitions but also military-family blocs whose internal ambiguity can be exploited by an enemy through [[PressureReleaseInfightingStrategy|撤压诱发内斗]].

## Key Claims
- Not naming a successor can preserve the incumbent's power in the short term while exporting conflict to the next transition.
- Private preference, deathbed entrustment, or proximity to the dying ruler does not replace a public and enforceable settlement.
- A plausible alternative claimant is enough to turn silence into factional competition; distributed provincial or military bases make the competition harder to contain.
- Even a named successor can be displaced if the designation lacks immediate elite acceptance and enforcement.
- Internal succession uncertainty can invite external intervention when claimants seek help or enemies wait for fragmentation.
- Childless or secret-backed succession variants shift the problem from "who is heir" to "who can control the transition story."
- Factional self-protection can create or harden a succession story when the unclear transition threatens advisers themselves.

## Evidence
- Public designation failure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-943-quanshi-kaoyan-renxing-beihou-cangzhe-juda-xiongxian-lg7ia5xok-swdmhml35yv9qqkvt|Hanji 943]] shows Emperor Ling's private preference and deathbed trust failing to prevent factional violence around Liu Bian and Liu Xie.
- Deathbed proximity variant: [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-127-3-haiwai-huigui-yipi-zhujian-jiekai-huhai-lidi-zhi-mi-lja0pankcx4ugtylbxxqkwuavdnw|Qinji 127-3]] uses the Zhao Zheng Shu account to show how distance from the capital can make a nearby heir politically attractive when no settled designation is legible.
- Childless and secret-backed transition: [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-109-1-lishishang-zui-zhuming-de-huangdi-bei-lv-le-liawtywj4c9n0zpii-rxl7cguzyy|Qinji 109-1]] and [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-109-2-chunshenjun-bei-huangdi-dajiuge-quanzu-miemen-locma2plu7v-vsssm5dr5frc3szw|Qinji 109-2]] show how Mi Wan's heir problem creates room for substitution and later preemptive murder.
- Sonless royal death: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-59-yichao-junwang-yichaochen-ljq-qkueix0rnzfekzuadicce1bh|Zhouji 59]] shows Qin Wuwang's death without a son leading to brother struggle and Wei Ran's force-backed accession settlement.
- Designation contrast: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-04-yurang-wei-zhiyao-fuchou-ln34tydha6iqwlrgmw7iugqfgfik|Zhouji 04]] shows Zhao Xiangzi naming Zhao Xianzi, but the succession remains vulnerable when elite enforcement fails.
- Wei succession conflict and intervention: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-10-yi-jiazu-danwei-you-mayi-chi-daxiang-de-jingshen-2-lmnqfjmydygqnfec6xhzpixralvi|Zhouji 10 part 2]] and [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-10-yi-jiazu-danwei-you-mayi-chi-daxiang-de-jingshen-3-lmhj4le2mwu1yhhl-qmuoqkfmhjf|Zhouji 10 part 3]] show Wei Wuhou's non-designation producing claimant war and external intervention.
- Yuan-family transition mechanics: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1056-yuanshao-sihou-tade-erzimen-zhijie-fanmu-chengchou-lqxhhayal-srq4fm4yy-hnfu15ye|Hanji 1056]] shows Yuan Shao's private preference for Yuan Shang, Ju Shou's warning, Yuan Tan's adoption/provincial base, Shen Pei and Feng Ji's claimed final-order installation, and Yuan Tan's killing of Feng Ji.
- Yuan-family battlefield strategy: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1058-shaonian-sunquan-chuzhang-jiangdong-ruhe-yingdui-caocao-fanan-lgqs4-a9qgf21woeh-ppinw4z9jg|Hanji 1058]] has Guo Jia treat Yuan Shao's non-designation and the comparable support of Yuan Tan and Yuan Shang as a fracture Cao Cao can exploit by easing pressure.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Not every succession conflict is caused by non-designation. The Zhao Xiangzi case shows that even a named successor can be contested if enforcement fails. The Qin Shi Huang material is explicitly tied to the Zhao Zheng Shu account and should not be used as a settled resolution of the forged-edict tradition. The Yuan-family case depends on Hanji 1056's and Hanji 1058's causal emphasis; Yuan Tan and Yuan Shang's conflict also rests on prior adoption choices, provincial deployments, adviser fears, and external military pressure, not silence alone.

## What Changed
- Added Hanji 1056 as the direct Yuan-family transition-mechanics layer before Hanji 1058's strategic abstraction.
- Added the distributed-base variant: assigning sons and relatives to provinces without an enforceable succession settlement gives ambiguity armed form.
- Added factional self-protection as a mechanism that can manufacture or harden a succession story after the ruler dies.
