# Third Punic War / 第三次布匿战争

## Definition

The Third Punic War was Rome's 149-146 BC war against a disarmed and strategically constrained [[Carthage]], ending in urban destruction, enslavement, and a prohibition on resettlement.

## Current Synthesis

The episode explains the war as an escalation built from unequal postwar rules, protected proxy pressure, remembered trauma, material interest, and a legal-religious pretext. The 201 BC treaty deprived Carthage of independent war-making, yet [[Massinissa]] repeatedly encroached with Roman favor. When Carthage finally fought back without permission in 151 BC, it supplied the treaty breach Rome could present as justified cause.

The decisive escalation came after Carthage tried to submit. Roman commanders first demanded hostages and weapons, then revealed that the population must abandon the coast and allow the city to be levelled. Refusal transformed a coercive settlement into total resistance: workshops, temples, enslaved laborers, metal, food stores, walls, harbors, and cisterns were mobilized for survival.

Early Roman failure and [[HasdrubalThirdPunicWar]]'s attacks prolonged the siege. [[ScipioAemilianus]] then restored discipline, blocked the harbor, forced starvation, and entered through the harbor district. Six days of house-to-house destruction ended in surrender, enslavement, demolition, and a curse against renewed settlement. The war was therefore not a balanced continuation of the earlier Punic conflicts; it was the annihilation of a much weaker rival after a contested path from breach to punishment.

## Key Claims

- Rome's treaty system constrained Carthaginian self-defense while tolerating allied Numidian encroachment.
- Carthage's unauthorized war supplied a real treaty breach without making destruction the only possible response.
- Roman sequencing of disarmament before the evacuation demand removed the practical basis for submission.
- Carthaginian urban mobilization and Roman operational failure turned expected capitulation into a three-year siege.
- Scipio's harbor blockade and assault converted material superiority into starvation and street-by-street destruction.
- The war ended in annihilation, enslavement, and deterrent spectacle rather than ordinary territorial settlement.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Carthage did breach its treaty, and the episode does not portray every Roman as favoring annihilation or every Carthaginian decision as prudent. Modern genocide language can clarify the intended destruction of a city and people while remaining anachronistic. Exact force and survivor totals, atrocity stories, speeches, motives, workshop details, and the legal meaning of Roman demands remain source-scoped. The salt-sowing story is explicitly rejected.

## What Changed

- Established the war as escalation from constrained self-defense and treaty breach to coerced disarmament and annihilation.
