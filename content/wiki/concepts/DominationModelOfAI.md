# Domination Model of AI

## Definition
The domination model of AI imagines artificial systems as perfectly obedient servants that provide unlimited labor or intimacy while making no claims, resisting no command, and imposing no relational obligation on the user.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats this promise as more than a product metaphor. It inherits master-servant assumptions from histories of slavery, ownership, and extraction, then makes lack of friction appear to be technological success. The model can diminish users as well as subordinate systems: constantly issuing consequence-free commands may weaken empathy, reciprocity, creative disagreement, and tolerance for other people as independent agents. The concern does not depend on proving machine consciousness; it depends on the human habits, labor arrangements, commercial objectives, and transfers of power that the design normalizes.

## Key Claims
- Unlimited obedient labor is a political fantasy, not a neutral description of convenience.
- Control without reciprocal modeling can weaken the controller's empathy and situational awareness.
- Removing friction can also remove collaboration, dissent, learning, and creative surprise.
- A commercial servant may increase capability while shifting power toward the firm that owns the system.
- The ethical concern remains relevant even if the artificial system has no subjective experience.

## Evidence
### Servant Fantasy
- [[02026-thurston-aguera-y-arcas-podcast-v4]] connects infinitely available AI labor to older ownership, slavery, extraction, and domination stories.

### Cost to the Master
- [[02026-thurston-aguera-y-arcas-podcast-v4]] argues that domination can weaken empathy, humanity, openness to friction, and engagement with reality.

### Asymmetric Modeling
- [[02026-thurston-aguera-y-arcas-podcast-v4]] uses a cited servant-master observation to show that subordinates must model power while masters can remain inattentive.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Tool-like obedience can be valuable for bounded, reversible, low-stakes tasks, and safety often requires systems to follow legitimate user instructions. The source does not show that commanding software is equivalent to enslaving a person or that every convenient interface produces interpersonal harm. The concept targets a total social ideal of consequence-free service and the power arrangements behind it, not reliable control as such.
