# Historical Fantasy Source Layering

Historical fantasy source layering is the method [[31-bing-yu-huo-zhi-ge-suiran-cong-bu-zicheng-lishi-594509848]] identifies in [[ASongOfIceAndFire|《冰与火之歌》 / A Song of Ice and Fire]]. A fantasy world gains historical texture by recombining many source layers: wars, royal marriages, conquest memories, successor states, religions, legends, diseases, map imagination, city stereotypes, and institutional habits.

The concept is close to [[MythicSourceLayering]], but it works at worldbuilding scale rather than at the level of one mythic figure. In the source, the [[WarsOfTheRoses|Wars of the Roses / 玫瑰战争]], Norman conquest, Roman and Greek history, crusades, Valyria, Andal migrations, old gods, the Seven, R'hllor, the Drowned God, [[CelticMythology]], and [[NorseMythology]] all sit in the same constructed world without forming one clean genealogy.

The guardrail is [[InterpretationAndOverinterpretation]]. A historical analogy can make a fictional institution legible, but the episode treats direct mapping as weak unless the source itself gives repeated support. Martin's method is strongest when one historical figure can be split across several characters, or when one fictional family gathers several historical pressures.

## Key Claims
- Historical fantasy can feel old because it gives institutions, beliefs, and records their own backstory, not because it reproduces a single real period.
- Source layers can come from different centuries, regions, genres, and evidentiary strengths.
- A useful analogy explains texture, conflict, or plausibility; it does not automatically identify a prototype.
- Fantasy can preserve historical thinking by staging conquest, succession, religion, disease stigma, archival uncertainty, and public legend inside an invented world.
- The method needs link discipline: source resemblance should be marked as analogy or speculation unless the text makes it stronger.
