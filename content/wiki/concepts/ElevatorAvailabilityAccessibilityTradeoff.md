# Elevator Availability–Accessibility Tradeoff

## Definition
The elevator availability–accessibility tradeoff is the possibility that maximizing the capability of each required elevator can raise its cost and footprint enough that fewer buildings install elevators, reducing total access.

## Current Synthesis
Compact European cabins in the episode can carry a wheelchair but may require the user to enter or exit backward. Larger American cabins provide more maneuvering room and can accommodate a fully extended stretcher, potentially allowing emergency workers to continue chest compressions during transport.

The countervailing effect is omission. When a large elevator is unaffordable or physically impossible in a small building, residents who cannot use stairs lose access to every upper floor, older people may be unable to age in place, and emergency workers must carry patients down stairs. The relevant policy measure is therefore not only performance per elevator but useful access across the building stock.

## Key Claims
- A compact cabin can provide meaningful wheelchair access without matching the maneuvering space of a larger cabin.
- A large cabin can improve rare emergency-stretcher performance.
- Higher price and larger shafts can cause small or affordable projects to omit elevators.
- No elevator produces a more severe everyday barrier than an imperfect but usable compact elevator.
- Accessibility analysis should compare total availability and user outcomes, not only the ideal capability of one installed unit.

### Everyday mobility
- [[the-great-american-elevator-tragedy-the-mistakes-series-the-great-american-elevator-tragedy-the-mistakes-series]] uses Smith's POTS experience and older walker users to contrast widespread daily needs with uncommon emergency scenarios.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not quantify how many elevators were omitted because of cabin rules, how often stretcher transport requires a fully flat patient, or how disabled users evaluate compact alternatives. Accessibility needs vary, and a smaller cabin may exclude some wheelchairs, companions, attendants, or emergency procedures. The episode presents a policy tradeoff, not proof that every smaller standard yields better net access.

## What Changed
- Created a system-level accessibility concept that preserves both compact-elevator benefits and large-cabin safety advantages.
