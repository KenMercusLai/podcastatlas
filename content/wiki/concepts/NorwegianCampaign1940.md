# Norwegian Campaign (1940)

## Definition

The Norwegian campaign was the 1940 German invasion of Denmark and Norway and the failed Allied effort to contest Norway, driven by iron-ore supply, Narvik's ice-free port, and control of the Scandinavian coast.

## Current Synthesis

[[621-the-nazis-at-war-blitzkrieg-part-2-glt8711886512]] presents the campaign as a mutual preemption problem. Germany depended on Swedish iron ore shipped through Narvik, while Britain and France considered mining Norwegian waters, occupying ports, reaching the Swedish mines, and potentially assisting Finland. Both camps moved while misreading the other's timing and intentions.

The military outcomes diverged. Denmark's geography and overwhelming exposure produced capitulation within hours. Norway was badly prepared but not passive: Oscarsborg's guns sank the Blücher, the king and government escaped, about 40,000 volunteers fought, and the campaign lasted roughly two months. Allied forces nevertheless arrived late and poorly equipped, then withdrew when the western crisis took priority.

Germany secured the coast, ore route, and bases, but lost or damaged substantial naval capacity and lost many aircraft. In Britain, Allied failure triggered the Norway Debate and Chamberlain's resignation, allowing [[WinstonChurchill]] to benefit politically despite his own responsibility for the operation.

## Key Claims

- Swedish iron ore and Narvik's winter access made Norwegian neutrality strategically unstable.
- German and Allied plans interacted as rival preemptive moves rather than a one-sided response.
- Norwegian resistance created meaningful delay and imposed costs despite weak preparation.
- Denmark's rapid capitulation reflected geography and military impossibility, not a shared Scandinavian absence of resistance.
- Allied intervention failed through delay, intelligence weakness, inadequate equipment, and command problems.
- German victory gained bases and resource security while degrading naval and air strength.
- Campaign failure changed British leadership through the Norway Debate.

## Evidence

- Strategic motive: [[621-the-nazis-at-war-blitzkrieg-part-2-glt8711886512]] links Sweden's ore, Narvik, Raeder's warning, and Allied mining and occupation plans.
- Norwegian resistance: [[621-the-nazis-at-war-blitzkrieg-part-2-glt8711886512]] connects the Blücher's sinking, royal escape, local resistance, volunteers, and the campaign's duration.
- Allied failure: [[621-the-nazis-at-war-blitzkrieg-part-2-glt8711886512]] describes troops without adequate maps, radios, heavy weapons, or suitable footwear.
- Cost and political consequence: [[621-the-nazis-at-war-blitzkrieg-part-2-glt8711886512]] links German ship and aircraft losses to the Norway Debate, Chamberlain's fall, and Churchill's accession.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source does not provide a complete naval, air, land, diplomatic, or occupation history. Iron-ore dependence, force and loss totals, Allied intentions, German intelligence use, exact campaign duration, Churchill's share of responsibility, and the later effect of German losses remain source-scoped. Calling the campaign a German victory should not erase Norwegian resistance or turn Allied failure into proof that intervention could never have succeeded.

## What Changed

- Created a campaign-level synthesis joining resource routes, mutual preemption, resistance, Allied failure, German cost, and British leadership change.
