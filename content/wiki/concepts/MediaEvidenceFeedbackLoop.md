# Media–Evidence Feedback Loop

## Definition

A media–evidence feedback loop occurs when publicity teaches audiences what a phenomenon should look like, and later reports or fabricated evidence matching that template are then treated as independent confirmation.

## Current Synthesis

The Nessie sequence shows evidence and representation co-producing one another. Long-necked witness accounts, plesiosaur comparison, dinosaur imagery, sensational headlines, and photographs established a recognizable form. Subsequent ambiguous observations gained credibility when they resembled that form, even though resemblance could result from expectation, selection, cropping, or deliberate construction.

The [[DailyMail]] track investigation and the “surgeon's photograph” show the loop's institutional side. A newspaper's demand for spectacle elevated claims before verification; respectable attribution and cropping then made a small model appear evidentially weighty. Exposure weakened particular artifacts but did not erase the standardized image they had already distributed.

## Key Claims

- Public representation can shape the content and interpretation of later sightings.
- Matching an established image is not independent corroboration when the image was already widely circulated.
- Headlines, cropping, credentials, and institutional prestige can increase apparent evidentiary strength without improving underlying data.
- Competitive demand for a decisive story can move publicity ahead of verification.
- Hoax exposure can destroy an artifact while leaving its visual template culturally intact.
- Ambiguous evidence should be tested against the possibility that expectation selected or constructed the match.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The feedback-loop model does not prove that every witness copied media imagery or knowingly fabricated a report. Similar descriptions may arise from waves, animals, boats, memory, or genuine uncertainty. Nor does sensationalism make every newspaper investigation false. The concept identifies a dependence problem: apparent repetitions are weaker evidence when a shared public template can generate them.
