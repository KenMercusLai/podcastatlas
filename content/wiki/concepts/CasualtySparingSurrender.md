# Casualty-Sparing Surrender / 保全生灵式投降

Casualty-sparing surrender / 保全生灵式投降 is the ethical and political tradeoff [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-120-1-shui-shi-huangdi-zhong-de-mabaonan-lhw8vsj6ta6rjdkugcvfohaqqxc0]] draws from [[QiWangJian|齐王建 / 田建]]'s surrender to [[QinState|秦国]] in 221 BCE. A ruler may lose the state through weakness, misjudgment, or deception, while the same decision still prevents mass killing among soldiers and civilians.

The concept does not turn surrender into virtue by default. In this source, Qi Wang Jian trusts Qin's promise of five hundred li of land and is later starved at Gong; [[SimaGuang|司马光]]'s criticism of late [[QiState|齐国]] remains serious because non-intervention helped Qin remove every surrounding state. The concept names the remaining fact that political failure and humanitarian outcome can diverge.

It sits beside [[DistantStateAppeasementTrap|远国安抚误读]] and [[AppeasementLogic|绥靖逻辑]]. Qi's long caution narrowed its strategic options, but it also gave ordinary people more than forty years of rare peace and made the final conquest far less bloody than a last stand around [[Linzi|临淄]] could have been.

## Key Claims
- Surrender can be politically disastrous and still reduce immediate human suffering.
- A weak ruler's personal fate does not necessarily match the population's short-term outcome.
- The humanitarian result should not erase the strategic failure that made surrender the only plausible endpoint.
- Promised terms matter: Qin's unfulfilled five-hundred-li promise makes Qi Wang Jian's surrender a deception case as well as a low-casualty case.
- The concept asks the wiki to separate three judgments: ruler competence, state survival, and civilian harm.
