# 君主依附型权臣权力 / Court-Dependent Ministerial Power

## Definition

Court-dependent ministerial power is extraordinary subordinate authority that exceeds the ordinary boundary of office but remains grounded in a monarch's personal confidence rather than an autonomous and transferable institutional base.

## Current Synthesis

The comparison of [[MarquisDePombal|Pombal]] and [[YanSongMing|Yan Song]] separates the scale of delegated power from its public value. Both became unusually powerful second figures, yet Pombal used part of that authority for reconstruction, education, administration, and defense while Yan devoted more of his capacity to imperial service, survival, household enrichment, and rival removal. “Powerful minister” therefore does not itself specify governing performance or moral character.

The shared mechanism is fragility. A ruler can expand a minister's access, resources, protection, and capacity to bypass ordinary constraints, but the same personal foundation lets changed favor or succession remove them quickly. José I's death exposes Pombal to Maria I, while Yan's aging, declining usefulness, and loss of Jiajing's confidence let Xu Jie's network convert long-known corruption into effective prosecution.

## Key Claims

- Extraordinary practical power can exceed formal office boundaries without becoming institutionally independent.
- Personal confidence can support both effective administration and coercive or predatory conduct.
- Serving a ruler's preferences may be a source of power, not merely a constraint on it.
- Household gatekeepers and informal intermediaries can grow around ruler-backed ministerial access.
- Known misconduct may remain politically unusable until the patron's confidence weakens.
- Succession, age, changed usefulness, or a better-positioned rival can rapidly dissolve protection.

## Evidence

- Delegated capacity: [[44-jianchen-ziji-tiao-chulaile-yansong-vs-pengbaer-houjue-lmx4poxetwxy625kriodizxlubou]] presents José I's confidence as the basis for Pombal's reform, reconstruction, wartime, and repressive authority.
- Personalized service: [[44-jianchen-ziji-tiao-chulaile-yansong-vs-pengbaer-houjue-lmx4poxetwxy625kriodizxlubou]] links Yan Song's rise to Great Rites compliance, literary service, deference, and anticipation of Jiajing's reactions.
- Informal household extension: [[44-jianchen-ziji-tiao-chulaile-yansong-vs-pengbaer-houjue-lmx4poxetwxy625kriodizxlubou]] describes Yan Shifan as both drafting support and access broker as Yan aged.
- Protection collapse: [[44-jianchen-ziji-tiao-chulaile-yansong-vs-pengbaer-houjue-lmx4poxetwxy625kriodizxlubou]] contrasts Pombal's removal after royal succession with Yan's fall after declining usefulness and Xu Jie's successful court campaign.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept rests on one comparative narrative and should not make Pombal's and Yan Song's offices institutionally equivalent. Portugal and Ming China had different administrative, legal, religious, colonial, and succession systems. Personal patronage also did not eliminate bureaucracy, formal office, elite coalitions, or either ruler's agency. Pombal's public achievements do not validate coercion, and Yan's hostile posthumous image requires source criticism.

## What Changed

- Established the concept through a paired Portuguese and Ming comparison that separates governing performance from the shared fragility of personal royal delegation.
