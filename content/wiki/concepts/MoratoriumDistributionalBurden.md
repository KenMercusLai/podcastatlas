## Definition
Moratorium distributional burden is the unequal loss of time, priority, income, career opportunity, or voice that a collective pause can impose even when the pause produces a wider safety benefit.

## Current Synthesis
Collective restraint is usually evaluated through aggregate risk reduction and defection control, but the Asilomar account adds a person-level ledger. [[JanetMertz]] says the recombinant-DNA moratorium eliminated a substantial lead and harmed her career, while senior scientists negotiated rules at a conference she did not attend. Her judgment that the pause was still worthwhile does not erase the cost; it makes compensation, representation, credit, and burden-sharing part of legitimate governance.

The concept does not imply that a costly moratorium is wrong. It requires decision-makers to identify who loses first-mover advantage, who has a voice in setting scope and exit conditions, and whether institutions can reduce concentrated harm without weakening the safety objective.

## Key Claims
- Aggregate safety gains can coexist with severe individual opportunity costs.
- Early-career researchers and smaller organizations may lose more from delay than established leaders with diversified resources.
- Exclusion from rule-making compounds material loss by separating burden from voice.
- Consent to a policy's public value does not waive a fair accounting of its private cost.
- Compensation, credit protection, representation, and explicit exit criteria can improve legitimacy without rejecting restraint.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This concept is grounded in one retrospective case and does not quantify Mertz's counterfactual career path. Broader participation can improve legitimacy but can also slow urgent negotiation, and compensation may be hard to calculate when scientific priority is uncertain. Distributional review should therefore qualify, not automatically veto, emergency safety action.

## What Changed
- Established unequal career cost and excluded voice as explicit criteria for evaluating technology moratoriums.
