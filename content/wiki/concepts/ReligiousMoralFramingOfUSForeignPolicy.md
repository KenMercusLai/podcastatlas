# Religious Moral Framing of U.S. Foreign Policy

## Definition

Religious moral framing of U.S. foreign policy is the recurrent interpretation of international conflict through providence, good and evil, religious freedom, pluralism, mission, crusade, and opposition to irreligious tyranny.

## Current Synthesis

The sources reject the idea that religion is only ceremonial decoration. [[152-american-crusades-glt4400059090]] traces a repertoire from Puritan exemplary mission and colonial war through Manifest Destiny, abolition, Civil War emancipation, and Protestant empire in the Philippines. [[153-god-and-the-american-empire-glt8752091834]] carries it through the Second World War, Cold War, 9/11, and Trump-era coalition politics.

The “sword of the spirit” names militant or crusading action, while the “shield of faith” names reformist, pacifist, pluralist, or rights-oriented action. Because both draw on religious commitments, faith cannot be mapped mechanically onto intervention or restraint. McKinley's Philippine uplift claim, Truman's religious summit proposal and Israel recognition, Reagan's “evil empire” language and religious-freedom diplomacy, Bush's crusade slip and defense of Islam, and Trump's evangelical alignment show the frame changing across periods, coalitions, and levels of personal belief.

The post-9/11 branch adds the strongest consequence test through [[95-9-11-glt8208243510]]. Bush's good-and-evil language, belief in a pivotal historical role, and universal claims about freedom helped turn national injury into global mission. The episode argues that Iraq, torture, Guantanamo, and Abu Ghraib then damaged the credibility of the rights and law invoked to justify action. Moral framing can therefore motivate solidarity and pluralism while also widening war and raising standards that state conduct may violate.

## Key Claims

- Religious belief can shape perceived national purpose rather than merely decorate a prior strategic choice.
- Militant and restraint-oriented policies can both draw legitimacy from religious traditions.
- Civilizational hierarchy can translate conquest into an asserted duty to educate, uplift, convert, or liberate.
- Emancipation and humanitarian language can transform the stated purpose and remembered model of a war without erasing its prior causes or mixed motives.
- Religious freedom can serve as a diplomatic issue and confidence-building mechanism as well as a moral slogan.
- Pluralist claims about “true” religion can coexist with warlike language after a security shock, while universal moral mission can turn a bounded response into regime-changing war.
- Conduct that violates professed rights and legal norms can convert moral framing into reputational self-undermining.

## Evidence

### Colonial, expansionist, and imperial framing

- [[152-american-crusades-glt4400059090]] connects Puritan exemplary mission, colonial religious legitimation, Manifest Destiny, Civil War emancipation, and McKinley's Protestant rationale for Philippine annexation.

### Cold War mission and diplomacy

- [[153-god-and-the-american-empire-glt8752091834]] connects Truman's religious mobilization, Reagan's “evil empire” speech, the Siberian Seven negotiations, and pressure on Gorbachev to an explicitly religious American Cold War.

### Post-Cold War transformation

- [[153-god-and-the-american-empire-glt8752091834]] links Bush's crusade vocabulary and Islamic pluralism, and Trump's evangelical and pro-Israel coalition, to continuing but changing religious frames.
- [[95-9-11-glt8208243510]] connects Bush's good-and-evil and universal-freedom language to Afghanistan, Iraq, the war on terror, and the later credibility damage caused by torture and detention abuse.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Religious rhetoric does not by itself establish causation, private belief, policy consistency, or benevolent outcomes. The sources emphasize presidents and elite interpretation more than Indigenous, Filipino, Muslim, non-Christian, and other people affected by American power. Land, slavery, markets, settlement, bureaucracy, strategy, nationalism, and public fear remain interacting causes, while “humanitarian war” can retrospectively simplify domestic conflict and coercive outcomes. The claim that Iraq rather than Afghanistan marks the decisive wrong turn is a source judgment, not a settled counterfactual.
