# Early Christian Exaltation

## Definition

Early Christian exaltation is the rapid post-crucifixion conviction that [[Jesus]] had risen, was the Messiah, and occupied an elevated relationship to God expressed through titles such as Lord and Son of God and through early Christian devotion.

## Current Synthesis

The episode treats the emergence of this belief as historically secure even while leaving its supernatural cause unresolved. Followers experienced or reported something after Jesus's execution, resurrection language appeared very early, and Paul's letters already transmit Last Supper tradition and exalted titles. The historical fact to explain is therefore not merely a late legend but an early change in the followers' interpretation of Jesus.

Resurrection belief alone is not presented as a sufficient explanation for Christianity's growth. Its force depended on the remembered teacher, a willing and humiliating death, Jewish prophetic and sacrificial interpretation, and language capable of giving those events durable meaning. The crucifixion episode further argues that resurrection stories were not themselves unusual in antiquity; the sharper cultural innovation was making the servile shame of the cross central to divine victory. The result joined apparent defeat to divine power rather than simply reversing death as an isolated wonder claim.

## Key Claims

- Jesus's followers very early believed that he had risen and was the Messiah.
- Pauline evidence predates the written Gospels and already contains Last Supper tradition and exalted titles.
- Historical method can establish the belief and its effects without determining whether resurrection occurred supernaturally.
- Empty-tomb and post-death narratives resist reduction to one uncontested natural explanation.
- Resurrection belief gained explanatory power through Jesus's teaching, execution, and prophetic framing.
- Early devotion placed Jesus in an unusually elevated relationship to God.
- Exaltation retained and reversed the cross's humiliating status meaning instead of merely bypassing it.

## Evidence

- Early attestation: [[288-jesus-christ-the-history-part-2-glt4776258433]] invokes Paul's Last Supper formula and First Thessalonians' use of Lord and Son of God.
- Post-death experience: [[288-jesus-christ-the-history-part-2-glt4776258433]] says followers experienced or reported something that changed their understanding after the crucifixion.
- Multi-factor emergence: [[288-jesus-christ-the-history-part-2-glt4776258433]] combines resurrection belief, willingly accepted humiliating death, remarkable teaching, and prophetic interpretation.
- Explanatory limits: [[288-jesus-christ-the-history-part-2-glt4776258433]] reviews theft, survival, hallucination, substitution, mythic, drug, and extraterrestrial theories without adopting one as settled.
- Scandal and inversion: [[175-crucifixion-part-1-glt5186420966]] uses Paul's early acknowledgement of the cross's embarrassment and later Christian symbolism to show humiliation becoming central to victory.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept describes historically observable belief and devotion, not proof of a miracle or a complete account of early Christology. The sources do not adjudicate every resurrection theory, date every formula precisely, establish how uniform belief was across the earliest communities, or prove that crucifixion was the sole cause of Christianity's later growth. The claim that followers' experiences cannot be neatly reduced and the contrast with other ancient resurrection stories remain interpretive judgments.

## What Changed

- Clarified that early exaltation reversed rather than erased the social shame of crucifixion.
- Distinguished the cross-centered scandal from resurrection belief considered in isolation.
