# Metabolic Capacity Model

## Definition
The metabolic capacity model is Casey Means's source-scoped account of health as the ability of cells and mitochondria to receive, transform, and use incoming energy without persistent overload, damaging byproducts, or defensive signaling.

## Current Synthesis
The model separates energy supply from energy-processing capacity. More substrate is not automatically useful when mitochondrial number, quality, oxidative capacity, fusion, or damaged-organelle clearance cannot meet demand. In that frame, insulin resistance may sometimes function as a protective brake on further substrate entry rather than only as an isolated blood-glucose defect.

This is an integrating hypothesis, not a complete causal theory of chronic disease. Mitochondrial dysfunction, inflammation, oxidative stress, hormones, organ-specific pathology, genetics, infection, medication, and social or environmental conditions can interact differently across people and diagnoses. The practical implication is to support capacity and reduce overload while retaining disease-specific evaluation.

The Lustig interview adds a nutrient-specific overload proposal. It claims repeated fructose exposure can inhibit mitochondrial energy-burning pathways, promote triglyceride and liver-fat production, and contribute to insulin resistance, inflammation, and reduced energy expenditure. This strengthens the need to distinguish substrate type and food matrix, but the named enzyme mechanisms and broad disease claims remain source-scoped because the summary supplies no primary methods.

## Key Claims
- Metabolism is fundamentally about converting food-derived substrate into usable cellular energy.
- Mitochondrial number, quality, oxidative capacity, fusion, and mitophagy contribute different aspects of processing capacity.
- Insulin resistance can be interpreted in the source as a cellular response to energy-processing overload.
- Dysfunctional energy processing may contribute to inflammation and oxidative byproducts, but it is not the sole cause of every chronic disease.
- Movement, sleep, food quality, stress, light, temperature, toxins, and other inputs may influence capacity through different pathways.
- Nutrient type, dose, food matrix, and delivery pattern may change the burden placed on metabolic capacity.

## Evidence
- Capacity definition - [[transform-your-health-by-improving-metabolism-hormone-blood-sugar-regulation-dr-casey-means-scim3664199211]] defines metabolism as food-energy conversion and describes underpowered cells as a shared mechanism with organ-specific symptoms.
- Capacity components - [[transform-your-health-by-improving-metabolism-hormone-blood-sugar-regulation-dr-casey-means-scim3664199211]] names mitochondrial biogenesis, oxidative capacity, fusion, and mitophagy as distinct targets.
- Insulin-resistance interpretation - [[transform-your-health-by-improving-metabolism-hormone-blood-sugar-regulation-dr-casey-means-scim3664199211]] proposes that a cell can restrict additional substrate when mitochondria cannot process the incoming load.
- Fructose-specific proposal - [[how-sugar-processed-foods-impact-your-health-dr-robert-lustig-scim6504027968]] links repeated fructose exposure to inhibition of AMP kinase, ACAD-L, and CPT1, triglyceride production, liver fat, inflammation, and insulin resistance.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources supply broad syntheses rather than complete primary-study methods. The model should not collapse heterogeneous diseases into one cause, turn association into mechanism, or replace disease-specific diagnosis and treatment. Its accounts of insulin resistance and fructose-driven enzyme inhibition are useful interpretations but are not established here as exclusive or universal mechanisms.

- Preserved named enzyme and cross-disease claims as source-scoped mechanisms.
