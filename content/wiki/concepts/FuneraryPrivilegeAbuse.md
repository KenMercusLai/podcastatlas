## Definition

丧葬名分僭越 / Funerary Privilege Abuse is the pattern where a politically connected household or posthumous status claimant uses burial goods, garments, seals, titles, or rites above its rank to convert private or factional power into public status, making enforcement of ritual hierarchy a politically dangerous case.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 839 gives the late-Han local-enforcement source case through [[ZhaoZhongLateHan|赵忠]]'s father. Zhao Zhong, a powerful eunuch, allegedly has his father buried in garments reserved for emperors or kings. [[ZhuMuLateHan|朱穆]], serving as [[JizhouLateHan|冀州]] inspector, orders the commandery administrator to verify the violation; officials open the tomb and expose the corpse.

Hanji 626 adds the late-Western-Han court-retrospective version. [[WangMang|王莽]] says [[FuTaihouWesternHan|傅太后]] and [[DingJiWesternHan|丁姬]] should not have imperial-level seals, ribbons, coffin materials, jeweled garments, tomb scale, or temple honors because their proper rank is below that status. Enforcement here does not protect local law from a palace favorite; it lets a dominant court actor recode earlier consort honors as illegitimate and turn correction into [[TombDesecrationPoliticalViolence|tomb-desecration political violence]].

The enforcement problem is not only ritual. Once a tomb is opened, the act makes rank correction visible and politically explosive. In Hanji 839, Huan becomes angry and Zhu is sentenced, while [[LiuTaoLateHan|刘陶 / 刘桃]] and Taixue petitioners argue that eunuch relatives are using imperial proximity to oppress local society. In Hanji 626, Wang Zhengjun's initial refusal shows that correcting alleged privilege can itself violate moral and ritual limits when it reaches into the dead.

## Key Claims

- Burial goods, seals, ribbons, tomb scale, and temple honors can function as public rank claims, not only as private mourning.
- Misusing imperial garments extends palace privilege into the status of an unelected kin household.
- Enforcing ritual hierarchy against a palace-connected family can become more dangerous than enforcing ordinary local law.
- Retrospective correction of consort-side burial rank can become posthumous punishment rather than neutral ritual repair.
- Tomb-opening verification creates political shock because it visibly reverses the offender's attempted status display.
- The violation becomes a feedback case when other actors contest whether enforcement protects order or abuses the dead.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept is not every expensive funeral or every burial-status dispute. It requires rank-signaling burial privilege plus political stakes around protection, prosecution, or retrospective legitimacy. Hanji 839 is an enforcement-against-palace-kin case; Hanji 626 is a dominant-actor enforcement case against dead consort-side figures. The exact legal categories, title forms, tomb-opening procedures, and motives remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 626 as a court-retrospective version involving Fu Taihou and Ding Ji.
- Expanded the status-sign list beyond garments to include seals, ribbons, tomb scale, coffins, and temple honors.
- Distinguished enforcement against protected privilege from enforcement that becomes posthumous punishment.
