# Magnetic Resonance Safety and Appropriateness / 磁共振安全与适应证

## Definition
Magnetic resonance safety and appropriateness is the episode's framework for deciding both whether a person, implant, device, or attached object can enter an MRI environment and whether the proposed scan answers a justified clinical question.

## Current Synthesis
VOL.131 separates two questions that patients often collapse: “can it enter the MRI room?” and “should this examination be done?” Phones and battery- or metal-containing equipment may be unsafe around the magnet, while some implants are conditional rather than simply permitted or forbidden. Compatibility can depend on the exact model, removal of an external component, field strength, programming, and local protocol.

The same boundary applies to scan scope. Pregnancy or an additional spinal region does not produce an automatic yes or no. Clinicians need the indication, symptoms, examination findings, expected diagnostic value, alternatives, and local capability. MRI safety is therefore a device-identification and workflow problem, while MRI appropriateness is a clinical-reasoning problem.

VOL.100 makes the external-object branch concrete. Coins, hairpins, badges, identity cards, removable dentures, and prosthetic limbs can be overlooked, attracted, damaged, or left around the scanner. Screening must therefore cover pockets, clothing, accessories, removable dental work, and prostheses rather than only implanted devices.

VOL.01 adds the mechanism distinction: MRI does not use ionizing radiation, but that does not make the scanner environment risk-free. The persistent magnetic field, radiofrequency heating, ferromagnetic projectile risk, implant artifacts, and the exceptional cost and complexity of shutting down or recovering a superconducting system require strict room-entry control even when no X-ray dose is involved.

VOL.224 adds the patient-experience layer. Hearing protection and an alarm device support communication during a long, noisy scan; stopping an acquisition sequence is different from switching off the main magnet; and dental materials or implants may be safe enough to scan while still creating artifacts. It also reinforces that MRI is not universally the “clearest” test: air-filled anatomy and region-specific protocols mean that moving the body or buying a more expensive modality does not automatically answer a different clinical question.

VOL.225 makes two parts more operational without changing the safety boundary. Conductive jewelry can heat in the radiofrequency environment even when it is not strongly attracted to the magnet, so screening cannot stop at a simple “magnetic metal” test. Implant clearance should use the exact product record or operative documentation and its conditions, including field strength or scan-duration limits, rather than memory or material category. Noise protection also depends on fit: foam earplugs must be rolled evenly, inserted promptly, and allowed to expand, while MRI-compatible air-tube headphones can preserve both attenuation and operator instructions.

## Key Claims
- MRI-room safety requires exact identification of devices, implants, pumps, metal-containing parts, and detachable components; absence of ionizing radiation does not remove magnetic, heating, device-interaction, or emergency-response hazards.
- Some implants are conditionally compatible only under specified field strength, programming, positioning, or component-removal rules.
- Pregnancy does not make MRI categorically impossible, but timing and indication still require qualified judgment.
- A tube or drain may be compatible while its spring, pump, or attached hardware is not, so the complete assembly matters.
- Extending a scan to another anatomical region should follow symptoms, examination, and diagnostic value rather than convenience alone.
- Pediatric inability to remain still can make sedation part of the imaging pathway, but the choice of drug and setting remains institution- and patient-specific.
- External and removable objects require active screening because ferromagnetic items can become projectiles and conductive objects may heat even without strong attraction; correctly fitted MRI-compatible hearing protection and a reliable patient-to-operator alert path support safe completion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources provide public examples, not a current compatibility database, hearing-protection standard, or imaging guideline. Device labeling, magnet strength, scan duration, software or programming, implant age, dental material, pregnancy context, sedation capability, local policy, and the clinical indication can all change the decision. Statements about stents, heating, liquid helium, quench recovery, sequence interruption, artifacts, physically removing a trapped object, earplug replacement, or earplug insertion are source-scoped and must not be used as operational instructions. Patients and staff should not use a category-level statement about “ear implants,” “pacemakers,” “pumps,” stents, pregnancy, dental work, precious-metal jewelry, or metal to self-clear an MRI.

## What Changed
- Added conductive-object radiofrequency heating to the screening model rather than limiting concern to magnetic attraction.
- Made exact implant records, field strength, scan duration, and labeled conditions the basis of compatibility decisions.
- Refined hearing protection from general availability to correct foam-earplug fit and MRI-compatible instruction delivery.
