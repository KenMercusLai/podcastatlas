## Definition

Moral substitution for institutions is the source's diagnosis of public administration relying on Confucian ethical doctrine, exemplary character, and discretionary judgment to fill gaps left by coarse rules, weak standardization, and limited implementation capacity.

## Current Synthesis

The episode does not say that the [[MingDynasty|明朝]] lacked law. It says law and administration did not cover or reliably execute enough public affairs, so officials repeatedly translated practical disputes into judgments about virtue, propriety, loyalty, and ideological correctness.

The six lives in [[WanliFifteenYear|《万历十五年》]] expose different consequences. [[WanliEmperor|万历帝]] loses trust in moral performance; [[ZhangJuzheng|张居正]] enforces reform through personal power; [[ShenShixing|申时行]] mediates declared ideals and private interests; [[HaiRui|海瑞]] becomes symbolically useful but operationally unmanageable; [[QiJiguang|戚继光]] seeks results through patronage; and [[LiZhiMing|李贽]] attacks orthodoxy without an organizational replacement.

## Key Claims

- Moral language can guide conduct but cannot by itself standardize complex administration.
- Heavy reliance on exemplary individuals makes continuity vulnerable to personnel change.
- Public virtue claims can conceal private interests or make disagreement appear as moral failure.
- Institutions may honor moral exemplars while denying them practical authority.
- The alternative is not value-free administration but values joined to legible rules, evidence, accountability, and implementation capacity.

## Evidence

- Core diagnosis: [[vol-362-wanli-shiwunian-da-shibai-shidai-de-ren-hai-neng-nadao-da-jieguo-ma-gkwriw4o2at-a-vjhw-t-4dvr|Vol.362]] distinguishes the existence of law from gaps filled by Confucian ethics and official judgment.
- Trust and inconsistency: [[vol-362-wanli-shiwunian-da-shibai-shidai-de-ren-hai-neng-nadao-da-jieguo-ma-gkwriw4o2at-a-vjhw-t-4dvr|Vol.362]] uses Zhang's alleged private conduct and Wanli's reaction to show the fragility of public moral authority.
- Exemplary containment: [[vol-362-wanli-shiwunian-da-shibai-shidai-de-ren-hai-neng-nadao-da-jieguo-ma-gkwriw4o2at-a-vjhw-t-4dvr|Vol.362]] uses Hai Rui to show symbolic praise without sustained operational empowerment.
- Patronage workaround: [[vol-362-wanli-shiwunian-da-shibai-shidai-de-ren-hai-neng-nadao-da-jieguo-ma-gkwriw4o2at-a-vjhw-t-4dvr|Vol.362]] uses Qi Jiguang to show private relationships substituting for stable organizational support.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This is Huang's represented historical interpretation, not a settled binary between “morality” and “law.” Moral norms can support institutions, formal rules can be unjust or unenforceable, and discretion is unavoidable. The source does not compare regions or periods systematically, quantify administrative coverage, or establish that one technical reform could have prevented Ming decline.

## What Changed

- Established a qualified concept for moral judgment filling administrative and legal capacity gaps.
