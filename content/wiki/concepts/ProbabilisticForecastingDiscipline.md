# Probabilistic Forecasting Discipline

## Definition
Probabilistic forecasting discipline is a repeatable practice of expressing uncertainty numerically, starting from comparable-case base rates, adjusting for case evidence, updating estimates, and scoring results over time.

## Current Synthesis
[[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-ce411815c1cca7882f5b93d662620544-51879597-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-ce411815c1cca7882f5b93d662620544-51879597]] presents [[PhilipTetlock]]'s method as an alternative to pundit confidence and crystal-ball imagery. Numerical probabilities expose fine distinctions and permit calibration checks; base rates anchor judgment outside a compelling case story; and frequent small updates let evidence change confidence without demanding total reversal.

Accountability requires a series of recorded forecasts. One correct call may be luck, and hindsight can disguise earlier error. Even a strong scored record remains bounded by the questions selected: narrow, resolvable questions are easier to evaluate than the broad policy problems people most want answered.

## Key Claims
- Numerical probabilities are more accountable than elastic phrases such as “a distinct possibility.”
- Calibration evaluates whether events occur at roughly the frequencies attached to them.
- Base rates provide an outside view before case-specific details are added.
- Frequent small updates are usually more defensible than dramatic swings based on one vivid fact.
- Repeated recorded forecasts help distinguish skill from confidence, selective memory, and luck.
- Curiosity and belief in trainable skill matter more than prestige alone in the episode's tournament account.
- Narrative vividness can reverse probability intuition by making a narrow subset feel likelier than its broader containing class.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- Calibration does not show that the forecaster selected consequential questions or modeled causal structure well.
- A strong record on short-horizon resolvable events may not transfer to rare, long-horizon, reflexive, or morally contested problems.
- Fine numerical distinctions can create false precision when evidence is weak.
- The supplied source does not provide tournament design, scoring rules, samples, effect sizes, or replication evidence.
