# Historical Narrative as Territorial Legitimation

## Definition

Historical narrative as territorial legitimation is the use of selected origins, treaties, imperial names, demographic changes, administrative transfers, or remembered victories to present current control over a territory as natural, restorative, or historically required.

## Current Synthesis

[[155-ukraine-and-russia-glt2859413707]] shows the mechanism through competing readings of Ukrainian history. A shared claim on [[KievanRus]], the 1654 agreement between Cossacks and Moscow, Catherine the Great's Novorossiya, Soviet recognition of Ukraine, and the transfer of [[Crimea]] can each be arranged into a story of unity, submission, autonomy, conquest, or later correction. The facts do not select their own modern legal conclusion; political actors choose a sequence and assign it normative force.

Territorial legitimation becomes misleading when historical connection is treated as exclusive ownership or when incorporation is emphasized while coercion, cultural suppression, rival institutions, later referendums, and the political agency of present inhabitants disappear. The corrective is not to deny shared history, but to separate description of past rule from a present entitlement to sovereignty.

## Key Claims

- Shared origin and long interaction do not by themselves establish exclusive modern territorial ownership.
- Ambiguous agreements can acquire incompatible afterlives when one side remembers submission and another remembers conditional alliance or retained autonomy.
- Imperial labels such as Novorossiya can make a past administrative project appear to be a timeless regional identity.
- Administrative transfers gain new political meaning after the larger state or empire that made them has dissolved.
- Selective chronology can turn conquest and incorporation into continuity while omitting resistance, suppression, competing institutions, and later democratic consent.
- Historical explanation and current legal-political legitimacy are different questions and require different evidence.

- [[155-ukraine-and-russia-glt2859413707]] presents [[KievanRus]] as a shared point of reference for Ukrainian and Russian narratives rather than an uncomplicated possession of either modern state.

### Treaty and empire afterlives

- [[155-ukraine-and-russia-glt2859413707]] contrasts Russian and Ukrainian readings of the 1654 Cossack-Moscow agreement and traces the later reuse of Novorossiya.

### Administrative history versus democratic sovereignty

- [[155-ukraine-and-russia-glt2859413707]] places the Soviet-era transfer of [[Crimea]] and federal recognition of Ukraine beside Russia's later acceptance of borders and Ukraine's 1991 independence referendum.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Historical continuity can be relevant to identity, diplomacy, minority protection, heritage, and some legal disputes; the concept does not make all historical arguments invalid. Nor does identifying selective narration resolve the status of every contested territory. The episode compresses disputed treaty meanings, changing borders, demographic movements, NATO assurances, the Budapest settlement, and international-law questions, so the framework should diagnose argumentative structure rather than substitute for specialized historical or legal analysis.

## What Changed

- Created a framework separating genuine historical connection from the additional claim that connection authorizes present territorial control.
