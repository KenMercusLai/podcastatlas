## Definition

Surrendered force dispersal is the post-surrender practice of breaking a formerly unified armed population into separately supervised settlements, preserving enough internal continuity for administration while limiting remobilization and redirecting capacity toward frontier defense.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-315-1-hanwudi-gao-qiangchai-yiju-shoufu-xiongnu-wang-llyk4i76izmxe_el_ewpbbayqq5i|Hanji 315-1]] presents the five subordinate-state communities associated with [[HunyeKingWesternHan|浑邪王]] as more than rewards or ordinary migration. The former followers are said to be distributed among Longxi, Beidi, Shang, Shuofang, and Wuyuan, allowed to retain Xiongnu customs, supervised by Han personnel, and positioned along the frontier facing [[Xiongnu|匈奴]].

The arrangement therefore combines four functions: fragmentation reduces the danger of a concentrated force; supervised cultural continuity lowers the immediate cost of forced assimilation; frontier placement turns incorporation into defense capacity; and exposure to the former opponent tests commitment. The source calls this “以夷制夷” and credits it with lower frontier pressure, but it does not establish the legal structure of each 属国, the degree of coercion or autonomy, or a clean causal estimate of the security effect.

## Key Claims

- Post-surrender security depends on the spatial and command structure of the incorporated force, not only on its leader's declared loyalty.
- Dividing one armed population among several jurisdictions can reduce coordinated remobilization while increasing monitoring demands.
- Retaining customs under external supervision can serve as an administrative compromise between immediate assimilation and uncontrolled autonomy.
- Frontier placement converts surrendered manpower into a buffer or auxiliary capacity while simultaneously exposing it to a loyalty test.
- Lower alarms and garrison needs may be consistent with successful dispersal, but they do not isolate its effect from prior campaigns, territorial change, or wider Xiongnu weakness.

## Evidence

Fragmentation and supervision:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-315-1-hanwudi-gao-qiangchai-yiju-shoufu-xiongnu-wang-llyk4i76izmxe_el_ewpbbayqq5i|Hanji 315-1]] says Hunye King's former followers were split among five northwestern commanderies and that Han officers were stationed in the resulting communities.

Cultural continuity and frontier use:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-315-1-hanwudi-gao-qiangchai-yiju-shoufu-xiongnu-wang-llyk4i76izmxe_el_ewpbbayqq5i|Hanji 315-1]] says the communities retained Xiongnu customs while occupying the front line against remaining Xiongnu groups.

Claimed security outcome:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-315-1-hanwudi-gao-qiangchai-yiju-shoufu-xiongnu-wang-llyk4i76izmxe_el_ewpbbayqq5i|Hanji 315-1]] links the new position to fewer Xiongnu traces and beacon alarms and to halving garrisons in three commanderies.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept currently rests on one compact podcast summary rather than administrative records or a comparative case set. The source does not establish whether the five 属国 were grants personally controlled by Hunye King, how many people or troops occupied each, which Han officials held authority, how customs were legally preserved, or how voluntary the relocation was. Its “以夷制夷” label may describe the host's strategic interpretation more than a documented contemporary policy formula. Reduced frontier pressure may also reflect Huo Qubing's campaigns, the loss of Hexi, Xiongnu redeployment, or several causes acting together.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to distinguish long-term settlement and containment from battlefield surrender stabilization and reception ceremony.
- Identified fragmentation, supervised continuity, frontier use, and commitment testing as the arrangement's linked mechanisms.
