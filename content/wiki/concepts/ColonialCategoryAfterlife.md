## Definition
Colonial category afterlife is the persistence of territorial partitions, ethnic classifications, institutional rules, and economic positions after formal colonial rule ends.

## Current Synthesis
[[405-cong-dili-bianjie-dao-siwei-bianjie-women-ruhe-bei-huajie-suzao-16a80abc]] argues that decolonization does not automatically remove the categories through which colonial power governed. Successor states may inherit borders and administrative identities because they need a workable territory and legal order, while communities continue to live with forced separation and unequal positions in the global economy.

[[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] extends the mechanism from categorical sorting into state formation. It uses Nigeria's many ethnic and religious communities and the Biafran War to argue that colonial borders could enclose populations without a settled shared national identity, while the Congo case shows that inherited territory can also arrive with deliberately restricted administrative capacity. The deeper claim is not that borders mechanically cause conflict, but that apparent ethnic or state failure should not end analysis of how the governing unit and its institutions were made.

## Key Claims
- Formal independence can leave colonial borders, classifications, and institutions intact.
- Administrative categories can harden previously overlapping religious, ethnic, or local identities.
- Successor states may rely on colonial territorial forms to secure their own legitimacy.
- Cultural reconciliation is limited when underlying political-economic hierarchy remains unchanged.
- Explaining inequality as group character obscures colonial and economic structure.
- Inherited borders can impose a nation-building problem even when successor governments must retain them.
- Colonial administrative exclusion can persist as a capacity deficit after formal sovereignty.

## Evidence
- Partition case: [[405-cong-dili-bianjie-dao-siwei-bianjie-women-ruhe-bei-huajie-suzao-16a80abc]] presents India-Pakistan partition as a colonial proposal implemented through mass sorting and migration by successor governments.
- Rwanda case: [[405-cong-dili-bianjie-dao-siwei-bianjie-women-ruhe-bei-huajie-suzao-16a80abc]] treats Hutu-Tutsi hardening and later reconciliation as evidence that classifications are politically made and difficult to undo.
- Palestine case: [[405-cong-dili-bianjie-dao-siwei-bianjie-women-ruhe-bei-huajie-suzao-16a80abc]] argues that a partition plan cannot be judged only from later outcomes without asking who designed it and why those affected would accept it.
- Economic afterlife: [[405-cong-dili-bianjie-dao-siwei-bianjie-women-ruhe-bei-huajie-suzao-16a80abc]] says many postcolonial states entered world production in low-value positions with limited room to build autonomous industry.
- African border inheritance: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] uses Nigeria's internal diversity and the Biafran War as a compressed example of difficult integration within colonial boundaries.
- Institutional inheritance: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] connects Belgian exclusion from senior professions and command to the independent Congo's capacity crisis.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Both episodes offer broad comparative interpretations, not detailed histories of each case. Colonial inheritance is not a total explanation: local political choices, class interests, institutions, violence, and later international interventions also matter. Nigeria's diversity does not itself explain the Biafran War, and the Congo's later crises cannot be derived from one capacity claim. The first source's accounts of Rwanda and South Africa and the second source's border and casualty claims remain source-scoped.

## What Changed
- Extended the concept from partitions and classifications into inherited nation-state boundaries and administrative capacity.
- Added Nigeria and Congo while explicitly rejecting border determinism.
