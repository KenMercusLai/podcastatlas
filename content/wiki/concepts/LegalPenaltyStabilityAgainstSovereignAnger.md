## Definition

法定刑阶约束君怒 / legal penalty stability against sovereign anger is the principle that an adjudicated offense should retain its prescribed and proportionate penalty even when a ruler's fear, anger, honor, or family reverence creates pressure for escalation.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-214-tiemian-zhangshizhi-dui-huangdi-lan-taizi-2-lixkic0bkzjc0ehyupeolnqmuxs|Hanji 214 part 2]] grounds the concept in two [[ZhangShizhiWesternHan|张释之]] cases. A pedestrian who startles the imperial horses receives the fine attached to obstructing the imperial procession; a thief who takes a jade ring from [[LiuBang|汉高祖刘邦]]'s temple receives the capital penalty attached to that offense. In each case [[LiuHeng|汉文帝刘恒]] asks for more because the act feels personally or dynastically grave.

Zhang's answer combines predictability and proportionality. If廷尉 changes a lawful judgment after seeing imperial emotion, ordinary people cannot know what conduct will produce what punishment. If the maximum collective penalty is consumed by the temple theft, the legal order also loses a way to distinguish an even more serious later attack on the imperial tomb. Stable law therefore limits both arbitrary escalation and collapse of the penalty ladder.

## Key Claims

- Public trust requires the penalty attached to an offense to remain stable after formal adjudication.
- Delegation to a judicial office loses meaning if the ruler can demand a new sentence whenever personal emotion rises.
- Proportionality preserves distinctions among offenses and reserves more severe penalties for more severe wrongdoing.
- Legal restraint can protect the ruler from converting fear, honor, or filial display into arbitrary punishment.
- The mechanism depends on an official willing to resist and a ruler ultimately willing to accept the boundary.

## Evidence

Predictable judgment after personal fear:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-214-tiemian-zhangshizhi-dui-huangdi-lan-taizi-2-lixkic0bkzjc0ehyupeolnqmuxs|Hanji 214 part 2]] says Zhang retains the prescribed fine after Liu Heng argues that the startled horses could have endangered him, and links later escalation to public distrust of law.

Proportional punishment under dynastic offense:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-214-tiemian-zhangshizhi-dui-huangdi-lan-taizi-2-lixkic0bkzjc0ehyupeolnqmuxs|Hanji 214 part 2]] says Zhang imposes the reported temple-theft capital sentence but rejects extermination of the offender's family.

Preserved penalty ladder:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-214-tiemian-zhangshizhi-dui-huangdi-lan-taizi-2-lixkic0bkzjc0ehyupeolnqmuxs|Hanji 214 part 2]] gives Zhang's hypothetical about a later attack on the imperial tomb to show why the harshest penalty cannot be spent on a lesser offense.

Ruler acceptance:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-214-tiemian-zhangshizhi-dui-huangdi-lan-taizi-2-lixkic0bkzjc0ehyupeolnqmuxs|Hanji 214 part 2]] says Liu Heng ultimately accepts both廷尉 judgments, with dowager mediation reported in the second case.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept is grounded in one popular-history retelling of two ancient cases, not a complete reconstruction of Western Han criminal law or normal廷尉 independence. The temple sentence remains a death penalty, so resistance to clan extermination should not be mistaken for general leniency. Liu Heng's ability to demand reconsideration and the reported dowager mediation also show that this is not modern judicial independence; the narrower evidence is that Zhang articulates and successfully defends stable, ranked punishment in these instances.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Zhang Shizhi's two reported廷尉 judgments against imperial pressure for harsher punishment.
- Joined legal predictability to proportional ranking rather than treating the cases only as personal courage.
