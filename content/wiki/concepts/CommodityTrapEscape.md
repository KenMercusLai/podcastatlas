# Commodity Trap Escape

## Definition
Commodity trap escape is the strategy of reducing exposure to interchangeable price competition by creating meaningful differentiation in product traits, brand, quality, protected supply, or customer preference.

## Current Synthesis
The episode gives the concept a produce-market form. Generic fruits and vegetables can trap growers in competition where buyers treat one unit as substitutable for another and price dominates. Branded varieties such as [[Honeycrisp]], [[CosmicCrisp|Cosmic Crisp]], and [[ScarletSunrise|Scarlet Sunrise]] try to escape that trap by making consumers ask for a particular product rather than any apple or tomato.

## Key Claims
- Commodity competition pressures producers when goods are interchangeable and price becomes the main decision variable.
- Differentiation can come from genetics, flavor, visual identity, packaging, brand, or legal supply control.
- Escape is economically stronger when consumers recognize the difference and retailers can market it.
- Legal protection and university commercialization can turn differentiation into licensable value.
- Escape attempts can fail or saturate when production costs are high or consumers face too many similar choices.

## Evidence
- Price-competition frame: [[branded-fruit-how-produce-got-honeycrisp-ified|Branded fruit]] explicitly labels generic produce competition as a commodity trap.
- Branded examples: [[branded-fruit-how-produce-got-honeycrisp-ified|Branded fruit]] uses Honeycrisp, Cosmic Crisp, and Scarlet Sunrise to show named products that resist interchangeability.
- Economist framing: [[branded-fruit-how-produce-got-honeycrisp-ified|Branded fruit]] has [[MiguelGomez]] describe specialty-crop markets as a race for differentiation.
- Limits: [[branded-fruit-how-produce-got-honeycrisp-ified|Branded fruit]] keeps search costs, apple saturation, and expensive tomato production as constraints on escape.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Escaping a commodity trap is not the same as eliminating commodity exposure. Growers still face input costs, labor costs, weather, distribution, and retailer power; the episode shows differentiation as one strategic lever, not a complete business model.
