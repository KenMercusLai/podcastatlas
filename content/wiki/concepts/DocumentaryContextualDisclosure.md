# Documentary Contextual Disclosure

## Definition

Documentary contextual disclosure is the ethical and editorial decision about how much potentially prejudicial background to provide when that information may inform understanding but also divert judgment from the conduct under investigation.

## Current Synthesis

The Alabama source applies the problem to incarcerated subjects' convictions. Test audiences wanted every participant labeled by offense, while [[AndrewJarecki]] argued that a guard's permission to abuse or kill cannot depend on whether the imprisoned person was convicted of burglary or another crime.

The film nevertheless discloses some history because total omission can invite viewers to imagine only the most extreme offenders. The resulting rule is neither full biography nor strategic concealment: include context needed to understand the claim, identify what remains unknown, and do not let irrelevant character evidence ration constitutional protection or human concern.

## Key Claims

- More information is not automatically more relevant information.
- Conviction history can become a moral permission slip unrelated to the alleged abuse being assessed.
- Total omission can also distort by encouraging audiences to fill gaps with extreme assumptions.
- Limited disclosure should be tied to the question the documentary asks and the running-time constraints it faces.
- Childhood images and other humanizing details can restore individuality without proving innocence or virtue.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Selective context can manipulate sympathy, conceal material facts, or impose the filmmaker's preferred moral frame. The source does not provide audience research, a full edit history, subject objections, or a general rule for when offense history becomes directly relevant.

## What Changed

- Established a relevance-based disclosure framework that distinguishes humanization from exoneration.
