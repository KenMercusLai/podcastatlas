# Sortition

## Definition

Sortition is the assignment of offices or participation opportunities by lot rather than by competitive election or fine-grained ranking.

## Current Synthesis

The Bolivian student-council experiments suggest that voluntary sortition can lower the personal and performative costs of entering public service, widen the pool beyond popular campaigners, and reveal capabilities that are hard to observe in advance. Randomness does not itself guarantee representative participation, competence, accountability, or good outcomes. The most defensible use in this source is therefore bounded: define eligibility, invite participation, preserve support and removal mechanisms, and avoid claiming more predictive precision than the evidence warrants.

## Key Claims

- Removing campaign costs can draw capable volunteers who would not stand for election.
- Random selection can weaken the advantage attached to popularity, visibility, and polished performance.
- Broader social composition may improve the range of experience inside a governing team.
- Responsibility can reveal or develop leadership that preselection failed to detect.
- Voluntary entry and small samples prevent a lottery from automatically representing the full population.
- Sortition requires separate design for eligibility, preparation, accountability, term completion, and institutional authority.

## Evidence

### Entry and representation
- [[the-powerball-revolution-the-powerball-revolution]] reports that many Bolivian participants would not have campaigned and that lottery councils crossed social circles more broadly.

### Capability after selection
- [[the-powerball-revolution-the-powerball-revolution]] describes initially underestimated students becoming dependable contributors and councils completing practical school projects.

### Legitimacy objections
- [[the-powerball-revolution-the-powerball-revolution]] records Lawrenceville students' concern that chance weakens control, earned authority, readiness, and immediate public-facing competence.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode supplies suggestive cases, not randomized comparison of selection systems. Volunteers remain self-selected; project completion does not establish superior governance; and the source gives little information about noncompletion, accountability, removal, conflict, or durability. Election performance may sometimes reveal job-relevant communication skills, while public consent may depend on more than equal selection odds.
