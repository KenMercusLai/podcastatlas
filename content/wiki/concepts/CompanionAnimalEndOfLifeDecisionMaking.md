# Companion Animal End-of-Life Decision-Making / 伴侣动物终末期决策

## Definition
Companion-animal end-of-life decision-making is the repeated assessment of reversibility, treatment benefit, suffering, daily function, comorbidity, cost, and caregiver goals when cure or meaningful recovery becomes uncertain.

## Current Synthesis
The source frames the decision as dynamic triage rather than one final yes-or-no choice. An older animal can have heart failure, cancer, and kidney disease at the same time; the immediate plan depends on which condition is most dangerous, which causes the greatest suffering, which is treatable now, and whether intervention can restore a life the animal can use. Priorities can change as one disease stabilizes or another becomes dominant.

Technical possibility is not sufficient benefit. Intubation or resuscitation can be physically possible while unable to change a fatal trajectory, and expensive cancer treatment can be inappropriate when another disease controls prognosis. Conversely, limited budgets do not reduce the choice to maximal treatment or abandonment: lower-cost comfort medication or less effective alternatives may still have value when their goals and limits are explicit. Euthanasia remains a morally weighty welfare option, not an automatic answer to diagnosis, age, or expense.

## Key Claims
- End-of-life planning should be revisited as disease burden, prognosis, response, and daily function change.
- Multimorbidity requires prioritizing the condition that is most immediately dangerous or most damaging to quality of life.
- A technically available rescue is non-beneficial when it cannot plausibly change the outcome or restore meaningful function.
- Treatment intensity should be judged by expected benefit and burden rather than by technology or price alone.
- Budget-aware alternatives can still pursue comfort when cure-directed care is unaffordable or disproportionate.
- Euthanasia decisions require welfare, uncertainty, caregiver values, and the animal's nonverbal condition to be considered together.
- Candid discussion of goals, time, money, and likely outcomes is part of care rather than evidence of reduced commitment.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source offers clinician cases rather than a validated quality-of-life instrument, ethics guideline, or legal protocol. Prognosis, pain, distress, capacity for recovery, euthanasia law, provider duties, financial constraints, family disagreement, and available palliative care differ across cases and places. A podcast account cannot determine when a specific animal should receive treatment, resuscitation, comfort care, or euthanasia.

## What Changed
- Defined end-of-life care as repeated prioritization across multiple changing diseases.
- Distinguished technical possibility from intervention likely to provide meaningful benefit.
- Added budget-aware comfort care as a positive option between maximal treatment and abandonment.
