# Commercial Manufacturing Surge Capacity

## Definition
Commercial manufacturing surge capacity is the ability of a productive civilian industrial base to redirect design and factory capability toward urgent national needs without depending on continuous peacetime defense work to preserve every supplier.

## Current Synthesis
[[BlakeScholl]] presents [[BoomSupersonic]] as an aspiring example: build turbines and aircraft for commercial markets, use digital engineering and reconfigurable production to avoid excessive hard tooling, then pivot capacity during an emergency. The argument contrasts this with defense incumbents sustained by government programs and with World War II manufacturers that converted civilian factories. It identifies adaptability as a resilience asset, but the episode does not demonstrate that Boom has executed a defense conversion or that commercial incentives alone preserve all strategically necessary capabilities.

## Key Claims
- A strong civilian order book can maintain people, equipment, suppliers, and learning that become useful during emergencies.
- Digital definitions and reconfigurable manufacturing can reduce the time and tooling needed to switch components or missions.
- Permanent government support may preserve domestic capacity while weakening urgency, cost discipline, or rapid scaling if procurement rewards continuity over output.
- Commercial capability does not automatically cover low-volume, classified, uniquely military, or mobilization-only requirements.

## Evidence
- Historical and organizational thesis: [[tsr-s6-blakescholl-v3-tsr-s6-blakescholl-v3]] contrasts source-described World War II factory conversion with slow defense incumbents maintained by continuous government work.
- Boom's proposed role: [[tsr-s6-blakescholl-v3-tsr-s6-blakescholl-v3]] says the company wants reconfigurable systems that can move from a design idea to a high-performance turbine component in 24 hours and pivot production during wartime.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source offers a founder's industrial-policy argument, not comparative procurement evidence or a demonstrated mobilization test. Civilian factories may lack security, materials, tolerances, certification, workforce depth, spare capacity, or incentives needed for defense production. World War II conversion occurred under extraordinary state coordination and demand, so it does not show that commercial strength substitutes for deliberate defense planning.
