# Positive Airway Pressure Therapy

## Definition
Positive airway pressure therapy is noninvasive respiratory support that sends pressurized air through a mask during sleep to keep a collapsible upper airway open while the user continues breathing spontaneously.

## Current Synthesis
The current source places positive airway pressure inside an indication-based OSA treatment plan rather than treating a home machine as either an ICU ventilator or a universal anti-snoring product. It presents the therapy as a common option for moderate-to-severe OSA and for selected symptomatic or complicated milder disease, with pressure titration and clinical follow-up used to match support to the patient.

Adherence is part of efficacy. A device that is physiologically suitable but poorly fitted, dry, loud, leaky, claustrophobic, abruptly pressurized, inconvenient to travel with, or unsupported by follow-up may not be used consistently. Mask style, humidity, leak compensation, ramping, noise, portability, recorded data, warranty, and service therefore belong to treatment implementation, while weight management, positional measures, oral appliances, surgery, nasal care, and comorbidity treatment remain adjacent or alternative branches.

## Key Claims
- Home positive-airway-pressure therapy is generally noninvasive and differs materially from invasive ICU ventilation.
- Pressurized air splints the upper airway open; it does not mean the machine performs every breath for the patient.
- Single-level and bilevel devices provide different pressure patterns and fit different respiratory contexts.
- Pressure should be individualized through assessment and titration rather than copied from another user.
- Mask fit, leak, humidity, ramp settings, noise, portability, and data access can determine long-term adherence.
- Device data can support patient-clinician review, but recorded metrics do not replace symptom, comorbidity, and treatment-context interpretation.
- Positive airway pressure is one treatment branch, not a substitute for every lifestyle, anatomical, nasal, oral-appliance, surgical, or broader medical need.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is sponsored public medical education and does not independently compare brands, devices, masks, pressure modes, surgery, oral appliances, or long-term outcomes. Reported decibel levels, price ranges, severity cutoffs, oxygen values, short-term case changes, and “first-line” language remain source-scoped. Device choice and pressure can be affected by OSA phenotype, lung disease, respiratory failure, nasal obstruction, anatomy, comorbidity, tolerability, and clinician judgment.
