# Antonine Plague

## Definition

The Antonine Plague is the pandemic beginning in the 160s that spread across the Roman Empire and produced a demographic and psychological shock beyond ordinary disease experience.

## Current Synthesis

The source treats the outbreak as the largest and deadliest disease event known to the Roman world up to that point, with severe urban effects and reach across the empire's three continents. It nevertheless distinguishes a major shock from immediate structural collapse: the post-plague empire remained recognizably Augustan.

The pathogen, precise origin, and mortality are not settled. Roman accounts connected the disease to troops returning from Parthia, while the episode also allows that it may already have circulated in Asia Minor; estimates range from low totals to claims of 25-30 percent empire-wide loss that [[KyleHarper]] doubts.

## Key Claims

- The outbreak was a trans-imperial pandemic rather than a local epidemic.
- Military and commercial connectivity helped disease move across regions.
- Urban mortality may have been much worse than an empire-wide average.
- Social shock and perceived novelty are historically important even where totals remain uncertain.
- The pandemic weakened the empire without by itself creating the later third-century political order.

## Evidence

- Scale and chronology - [[217-plague-and-the-decline-of-the-roman-empire-glt2583859060]] places onset in the 160s and describes spread across the empire's three continents.
- Transmission traditions - [[217-plague-and-the-decline-of-the-roman-empire-glt2583859060]] records the returning-army account while preserving an Asia Minor alternative.
- Mortality boundary - [[217-plague-and-the-decline-of-the-roman-empire-glt2583859060]] reports wide estimates and rejects confidence in the highest empire-wide figure.
- Institutional continuity - [[217-plague-and-the-decline-of-the-roman-empire-glt2583859060]] distinguishes this shock from the structurally transformative third-century crisis.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source does not identify the pathogen or establish a reliable total death count. Literary origin traditions may encode blame or retrospective explanation, and severe city evidence cannot automatically be generalized across the empire. The event belongs in Roman decline history without being treated as a sufficient cause of collapse.

## What Changed

- Created the concept as a severe but bounded second-century pandemic shock.
