# Marriage Alliance War Exposure / 联姻同盟战场暴露

## Definition
Marriage alliance war exposure is the pattern in which a marriage tie changes military and political risk rather than merely expressing family friendship. It can move battlefield exposure, legitimacy cost, or temporary control burdens onto one party, especially when the surrounding alliance is unstable.

## Current Synthesis
The concept now has two crisis uses. In the rejection branch, [[YuanShu|袁术]] sends [[HanYinLateHan|韩胤]] to arrange a marriage between his son and [[LyuBu|吕布]]'s daughter. [[ChenGui|陈珪]] argues that the marriage would bind Lü Bu to Yuan Shu's illegitimate imperial claim and make [[Xuzhou|徐州]] part of Yuan Shu's northern strategic path. The risk is therefore reputational and geographic: the marriage would pull Lü Bu into another power's war.

In the acceptance-as-control branch, [[YuanTan|袁谭]] secretly gives general seals to [[LyuKuangLateHan|吕旷]] and [[GaoXiangLateHan|高翔]], and [[CaoCao|曹操]] reads the maneuver as deceptive. Cao then marries [[CaoZhengLateHan|曹整]] to Yuan Tan's daughter to hold Yuan Tan temporarily. The marriage does not prove trust; it is a mechanism for stabilizing a useful but suspect collaborator.

The concept therefore sits between marriage diplomacy and wartime risk management. Compared with [[WarringStatesMarriageDiplomacy|战国联姻外交]], the current emphasis is less on ruling-house relation-building and more on what the tie does to military exposure, leverage, and trust under campaign pressure.

## Key Claims
- A marriage alliance can transfer not only prestige and kinship ties but also military exposure and legitimacy cost.
- Local actors may reject a marriage path because it turns their territory into another power's forward line.
- A stronger party can also use marriage to stabilize a distrusted collaborator when open coercion would be premature.
- Marriage is not reliable evidence of trust; in crisis settings it may mark precisely the absence of trust.
- The same instrument can either create exposure for the weaker party or buy time for the stronger party, depending on surrounding leverage.

## Evidence
- Exposure through Yuan Shu: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1016-sanguo-luanshi-li-chengui-jiazu-de-mingyun-jueze-ltuckdcxpyxk-xmzp8rt-2gjfzcs|Hanji 1016]] has Chen Gui argue that Lü Bu's acceptance of Yuan Shu's marriage would attach Xuzhou to Yuan Shu's war and imperial-legitimacy problem.
- Reversal through Chen Gui: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1016-sanguo-luanshi-li-chengui-jiazu-de-mingyun-jueze-ltuckdcxpyxk-xmzp8rt-2gjfzcs|Hanji 1016]] shows the marriage path failing once Lü Bu's immediate security, resentment, and legitimacy concerns are reframed against Yuan Shu.
- Stabilization without trust: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1061-yuanshao-zhizi-he-shafu-chouren-jiecheng-qinjia-lnsnj6ipiyscw-vopnjjgoxpnt5r|Hanji 1061]] says Cao Cao uses Cao Zheng's marriage to Yuan Tan's daughter after discovering Yuan Tan's secret seal-lure for Lyu Kuang and Gao Xiang.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The current evidence comes from crisis cases, so the concept should not be generalized to every political marriage. Some marriages may genuinely build durable kinship diplomacy; this page tracks the narrower wartime pattern where marriage is used or rejected because of exposure, legitimacy, leverage, or distrust. Hanji 1061 also does not develop Cao Zheng's agency or the long-term marital outcome.
