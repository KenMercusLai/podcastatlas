## Definition

Shared-cause factional erosion / 共同目标下的派系侵蚀 is the process by which a coalition's declared common objective fails to control rivalry over claimants, succession, kinship, office, prestige, or private advantage, eventually directing more conflict inward than the shared cause can absorb.

## Current Synthesis

The episode uses two nested cases in [[LuDingJi|《鹿鼎记》]]. [[Tiandihui|天地会]] and the Mu Prince's Residence both oppose Qing rule but fight over which Ming descendant deserves restoration. Inside the Zheng regime, competing heirs gather different supporters, and Chen Jinnan's alignment against his rival is complicated by family interest. A common enemy supplies a slogan and some coordination, but not a settled rule for succession, legitimacy, or distribution of power.

The process becomes tragic when loyalty to the coalition prevents exit even as internal struggle turns lethal. [[ChenJinnan|陈近南]] refuses to abandon or retaliate against the Zheng order and is killed by the faction he continues to protect. The case suggests that shared purpose requires conflict-resolution institutions and an agreed decision rule; moral commitment alone cannot substitute for them.

## Key Claims

- A shared enemy does not settle who should rule after victory or speak for the cause now.
- Rival legitimacy claims can turn nominal allies into active competitors.
- Succession uncertainty invites networks organized around heirs, kinship, and private advantage.
- Personal virtue at the top cannot replace explicit mechanisms for resolving internal disputes.
- Refusal to exit or revise loyalty can let a coalition consume its most committed members.
- Internal erosion can defeat a cause before its external opponent does.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept is grounded in a fictional and interpretive case. Factional disagreement can expose real differences, prevent domination, or improve strategy rather than merely corrode a cause. The source does not establish the historical structure of Tiandihui or the Zheng succession struggle, nor does it prove that formal rules alone would have resolved conflicts rooted in legitimacy, violence, and personal power.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from the episode's paired restoration-coalition and Zheng-succession conflicts.
- Added conflict-resolution rules and loyalty revision as organizational boundaries.
