# Columbus Second-Voyage Colonization

## Definition

Columbus second-voyage colonization is the 1493 shift from a small westward reconnaissance to an enlarged, state-backed project of settlement, conversion, gold extraction, strategic claim, and colonial government.

## Current Synthesis

[[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] defines the shift through scale and purpose: 17 ships, roughly 1,500 mostly male settlers, a priest, experienced warriors, plants and animals, and explicit plans for towns, mines, converts, and control. Papal backing and anxiety about Portugal made the expedition geopolitical as well as commercial and religious.

The project failed on its own advertised terms. La Navidad had been destroyed; [[LaIsabela]] was badly sited and afflicted by shortages, illness, and conflict; gold remained scarce; and [[ChristopherColumbus]] proved a much weaker governor than navigator. Rather than relaxing extraction, failure helped intensify captivity, slave export, tribute, and compelled labor, prompting resistance and royal intervention.

## Key Claims

- Fleet scale, settlers, clergy, animals, and settlement instructions mark a transition from exploration to colonization.
- Conversion, territorial claim, gold, royal revenue, and Portuguese rivalry operated together.
- Reconquista and Canary precedents shaped the use of towns, Christianization, and coercive frontier assumptions.
- Settlement and administrative failure intensified rather than displaced pressure for commercial return.
- Royal investigation and the end of Columbus's monopoly shifted control toward a broader imperial system.

## Evidence

- Colonial scale and intent: [[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] contrasts the first voyage's three ships with the second voyage's 17 ships, settlers, priest, warriors, livestock, mining hopes, and town-building plans.
- Institutional inheritance: [[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] connects the Spanish model to Canary settlement and Reconquista conversion language.
- Failure and coercion: [[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] links La Isabela's breakdown and absent gold to captive export, mass seizure, tribute, and labor demands.
- Imperial transition: [[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] describes private-voyage permission and Juan Aguado's investigation as limits on Columbus's personal control.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source does not reduce colonization to one motive, a fully formed state plan, or a smoothly implemented policy. Royal protective language and objections to enslavement had real force without ending territorial appropriation or conversion pressure. Numbers, motives, institutional continuity from Granada and the Canaries, and the balance between deliberate policy and improvised failure remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Established the second voyage as the threshold from reconnaissance to state-backed settlement.
- Connected administrative failure to escalating coercion and later imperial diversification.
