# American Religious Persistence

## Definition

American religious persistence is the episode's institutional explanation for why modernization did not displace public religion in the United States to the same degree expected by a simple secularization model.

## Current Synthesis

[[153-god-and-the-american-empire-glt8752091834]] combines church-state separation with competition. Because churches were not state establishments, political discredit did not automatically discredit religion; because institutions had to attract adherents without state support, a “marketplace of faith” rewarded adaptation, innovation, and new movements.

The concept concerns persistence and political availability, not uniform belief. It does not imply that all Americans are religious, that Europe followed one secular path, or that religious competition necessarily produces social benefit.

## Key Claims

- Modernization and religiosity can coexist rather than move in a single inverse relationship.
- Disestablishment can protect religious institutions from the legitimacy failures of government.
- Competition for adherents can encourage religious innovation and organizational adaptation.
- Persistent public religion supplies language and coalitions for domestic politics and foreign policy.
- Institutional explanation should remain separate from claims about the sincerity or theology of individual leaders.

## Evidence

### Separation and institutional insulation

- [[153-god-and-the-american-empire-glt8752091834]] argues that church-state separation prevented churches from being discredited whenever political authority was discredited.

### Competition and innovation

- [[153-god-and-the-american-empire-glt8752091834]] describes the absence of state support as producing a competitive “marketplace of faith” and new religious movements.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source offers a high-level U.S.-Western Europe comparison rather than a systematic cross-national test. It does not measure religiosity across regions, denominations, race, class, or time, and the marketplace metaphor may understate coercion, inherited identity, institutional inequality, and secular countertrends.

## What Changed

- Created the concept by separating the episode's institutional persistence mechanism from its later foreign-policy applications.
