## Definition

Earned reward refusal / 有功辞赏 is the tension created when a contributor rejects a deserved title or material reward on principle while the awarding authority needs visible recognition to keep merit, trust, and future participation aligned.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 1110 distinguishes earned-reward refusal from declining an improper favor. [[TianChou|田畴]] does not deny that his [[LulongRoadLateHan|卢龙道]] advice helped [[CaoCao|曹操]] defeat [[Wuhuan|乌桓]]; he denies that service to the state should be converted into his pursuit of a marquisate. Cao Cao sees a different level of the problem: if major contribution receives no visible return, other capable people may read the system as unable or unwilling to recognize merit.

The resulting conflict is not resolved by proving one side wrong. Four orders and [[XiahouDun|夏侯惇]]'s personal persuasion fail because Tian Chou treats acceptance as a violation of motive, not a bargaining question. Cao Cao's final yilang appointment is therefore a negotiated change in reward form: it preserves public recognition and access to advice while abandoning the contested hereditary title and fief.

## Key Claims

- A reward can be deserved yet still be refused because the contributor rejects what acceptance would imply about motive or identity.
- Rulers and organizations reward contribution partly for third-party credibility, not only to benefit the recipient.
- Repeated pressure can turn recognition into coercion when the recipient treats refusal as a principle.
- Personal intermediaries do not solve a conflict when the disagreement concerns the legitimacy of the reward form itself.
- Changing the form of recognition can reconcile institutional signaling with individual boundaries better than escalating the original offer.

## Evidence

- Deserved contribution and motive boundary: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1110-caocao-gei-ta-si-ci-fengshang-ta-jing-si-bu-daying-ln8zkblsgq3zi7krrhgjpnb4mokb|Hanji 1110]] ties Tian Chou's reward to his Lulong Road advice but says he served the state rather than seeking a marquisate.
- Third-party reward signal: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1110-caocao-gei-ta-si-ci-fengshang-ta-jing-si-bu-daying-ln8zkblsgq3zi7krrhgjpnb4mokb|Hanji 1110]] says Cao Cao fears that leaving real merit unrewarded will discourage other talent.
- Coercion threshold: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1110-caocao-gei-ta-si-ci-fengshang-ta-jing-si-bu-daying-ln8zkblsgq3zi7krrhgjpnb4mokb|Hanji 1110]] says four orders and Xiahou Dun's persuasion lead Tian Chou to threaten death rather than acceptance.
- Reward-form compromise: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1110-caocao-gei-ta-si-ci-fengshang-ta-jing-si-bu-daying-ln8zkblsgq3zi7krrhgjpnb4mokb|Hanji 1110]] presents the yilang appointment as a compromise that recognizes Tian Chou without forcing the original marquisate.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source is a short popular-history episode and does not establish that Tian Chou's inner motive was purely apolitical, that every detail of the four-order sequence is uncontested, or that the yilang appointment had no substantive duties. It also does not imply that refusing deserved reward is always beneficial: Cao Cao's incentive concern remains valid at the institutional level, and refusal can impose reputational and coordination costs on others.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Tian Chou's refusal of a reward explicitly grounded in real campaign merit.
- Separated earned-reward refusal from refusal of unearned favor or illegitimate title.
- Identified reward-form redesign as the episode's practical compromise.
