# Religious Shame–Grace Gap

## Definition

A religious shame–grace gap arises when a community strongly defines and publicly disciplines transgression but offers no equally credible, practical route to confession, forgiveness, continued belonging, and repair.

## Current Synthesis

Part 1 of [[TheAlabamaMurders]] presents this gap through [[LeeCamp]] and [[GrantAsbell]]'s retrospective insider accounts of the [[ChurchOfChrist]]. Love, learning, simplicity, discipline, and belonging coexisted with restored-church certainty, strict rules, disfellowshipping, and fear that unrepented sin could bring damnation. In that environment, grace might exist as doctrine without functioning as a trusted social path after serious failure.

The episode applies the idea cautiously to [[CharlesSennett]]. Divorce, another affair, loss of ministry, public disgrace, and fear about salvation may have made concealment appear preferable to admission and repair. These pressures can help explain a closing trap, but they do not cause or excuse abuse, deception, or violence and do not establish Sennett's actual reasoning.

## Key Claims

- Moral clarity can support belonging and conduct while also increasing the stakes of visible failure.
- Public discipline and exclusion can turn shame into a threat to identity, livelihood, relationships, and salvation.
- Grace is practically weak when members do not trust that confession can lead to forgiveness, repair, and continued belonging.
- A perceived lack of acceptable exit can encourage concealment, compartmentalization, or crisis escalation.
- Religious pressure is one contextual factor, not a sufficient cause of abuse, mental illness, or violence.
- Insider accounts can preserve both gratitude for a tradition and criticism of harms within it.

## Evidence

### Formation and discipline

- [[the-alabama-murders-part-1-the-true-church]] reports Camp's account of love and formation alongside certainty, exclusion, and disfellowshipping.

### Fear and weak practical grace

- [[the-alabama-murders-part-1-the-true-church]] reports Asbell's fear of damnation and his account of resistance to grace- and forgiveness-centered preaching.

### Concealment under pressure

- [[the-alabama-murders-part-1-the-true-church]] connects Sennett's divorce fear, lost status, affairs, debt, abuse, psychiatric history, and concealment without establishing a single causal path.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The framework derives from one episode and two retrospective insider accounts, not a denominational survey, doctrinal history, clinical study, or comparison with other religious communities. Strict moral teaching does not necessarily produce concealment, and grace-centered language does not ensure safe disclosure or repair. Sennett's mental illness, debt, abuse, affairs, and later suspected conduct cannot be reduced to religious culture, and the concept must not transfer responsibility from an actor to a community.

## What Changed

- Created a framework distinguishing strong norms from the availability of a trusted route back after failure.
- Preserved the episode's causal caution and the coexistence of religious benefit and harm.
