# Cross-Border Disaster Early Warning

## Definition
Cross-border disaster early warning is the monitoring, information-sharing, and public-alert chain needed when a hazard forms in one jurisdiction but threatens downstream communities in another.

## Current Synthesis
The Nepal flood accounts show that a functioning domestic alert cannot recover time lost upstream. Nepalese authorities reportedly received information only after the surge had entered the country, while no standing bilateral mechanism fully shared upstream rainfall, river-level, glacier, and hazard data. Yet the newest account prevents a simple coordination-failure story: Chinese weather messages reportedly existed, no public record shows real-time movement data for the failing ice mass, and sudden high-mountain collapse may remain extremely difficult to predict even with better cooperation.

## Key Claims
- Warning time depends on the entire upstream-to-household chain, not only the speed of the final text message.
- Hazards that cross borders require shared monitoring, thresholds, contact points, and practiced notification procedures before an emergency.
- An unimplemented recommendation is not preparedness; institutional follow-through determines whether technical knowledge becomes evacuation time.
- Warning systems cannot eliminate exposure, but they can change the time available for evacuation, shutdown, and rescue staging.
- Data-sharing gaps and hazard predictability are separate questions; improving the former does not guarantee prediction of every sudden failure.

## Evidence
- Compressed alert window: [[niboer-teda-hongzai-xianchang-yichang-meiyou-yiti-de-zangli-cd38365e015f056efaa8b0506c123504]] says Rasuwa authorities learned of the flood about sixteen minutes after it entered Nepal and then sent mobile warnings.
- Prior institutional knowledge: [[niboer-teda-hongzai-xianchang-yichang-meiyou-yiti-de-zangli-cd38365e015f056efaa8b0506c123504]] says Nepal had proposed a Nepal-China warning and river-risk system after an earlier cross-border flood, but the proposals remained on paper.
- Regional exposure: [[niboer-teda-hongzai-xianchang-yichang-meiyou-yiti-de-zangli-cd38365e015f056efaa8b0506c123504]] cites research identifying dozens of potential Himalayan danger sites affecting millions of people.
- Institutional scope: [[niboer-teda-hongzai-weihe-jiang-kouan-jian-zai-zaihai-pinfa-de-shangu-fe064a422e32872349638d39a97d8c19]] describes existing cooperation, a Chinese-supported earthquake network, May 2026 talks, and the absence of a standing upstream hydrometeorological data-sharing mechanism.
- Predictability boundary: [[niboer-teda-hongzai-weihe-jiang-kouan-jian-zai-zaihai-pinfa-de-shangu-fe064a422e32872349638d39a97d8c19]] reports that weather warnings were sent but that sparse monitoring and sudden ice-rock failure made this event intrinsically difficult to forecast.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not provide a complete Chinese-side monitoring record, a technical incident timeline, the contents of every warning, or evidence that a particular interval would have prevented specified losses. They support a governance-gap finding and a case for formal data sharing, not a complete allocation of responsibility or proof that cooperation would have predicted this collapse.

## What Changed
- Added the existing cooperation, May 2026 negotiations, and missing standing hydrometeorological exchange mechanism.
- Separated the institutional data-sharing gap from the physical predictability of a sudden ice-rock collapse.
