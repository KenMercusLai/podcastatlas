## Definition

Mastery purity, human attachment, and moral integrity is a framework for separating three questions often collapsed in stories of elite performance: how much attention a craft demands, whether attachment necessarily lowers performance, and whether fidelity to technique can excuse dishonesty or harm toward people.

## Current Synthesis

The bounded [[lo1lhzgf7cwbtwjy6no8a9i2laa6|纸醉金迷FM episode]] uses [[DuelBeforeAndAfterGuLong|《决战前后》]] to resist a simple choice between feeling and mastery. Marriage and impending fatherhood make [[XimenChuixue|西门吹雪]] less deathlike: responsibility, smiling, rescue, and reluctance to kill enlarge the person. The same bonds can create fear, divided attention, and hesitation in a lethal duel. Attachment is therefore neither automatically a defect nor automatically performance-enhancing; it changes both what can be lost and what mastery is for.

[[YeGucheng|叶孤城]] and Ximen then separate technical commitment from moral integrity. “诚于剑” treats the craft as a self-contained standard, while “诚于人” argues that excellence involving life and death cannot be abstracted from honesty and judgment. The episode's comparisons with music, traditional martial ethics, and [[LuXiaofeng|陆小凤]]'s emotionally engaged competence further qualify the claim that absolute exclusion of feeling is the only route to a peak.

## Key Claims

- Elite practice can require exceptional concentration and sacrifice without requiring the abolition of every relationship.
- Attachment creates responsibilities, meaning, fear of loss, and possible hesitation; these effects must be distinguished rather than labeled collectively as weakness.
- Humanization and peak performance may conflict in a specific moment without establishing that dehumanization is a superior life or moral ideal.
- Technical sincerity does not by itself establish honesty, legitimate purpose, or moral permission to harm.
- When a practice acts directly on other people, its objects and consequences cannot be treated as ethically irrelevant material.
- Emotion supports some arts and actions while disrupting others, so “purity” is a value choice rather than a universal performance law.
- A rival can function as witness and instrument of self-testing without reducing the contest to public fame or rank.

## Evidence

### Attachment as humanization and vulnerability

- [[lo1lhzgf7cwbtwjy6no8a9i2laa6]] links Ximen's family to care, rescue, smiling, future provision, fear of defeat, and possible hesitation.

### Emotion does not have one technical effect

- [[lo1lhzgf7cwbtwjy6no8a9i2laa6]] contrasts emotionally expressive music and love-dependent martial systems with swordsmanship framed as emotional exclusion, while using Lu's appetites and friendships as counterevidence to a universal rule.

### Integrity beyond technique

- [[lo1lhzgf7cwbtwjy6no8a9i2laa6]] places “诚于人” against “诚于剑” and connects the emperor's moral judgment to the claim that victory cannot alone redefine right and wrong.

### Self-testing beyond public reputation

- [[lo1lhzgf7cwbtwjy6no8a9i2laa6]] preserves disagreement between Lu's critique of invisible fame and the reading that each swordsman needs the only adequate opponent to test his own path.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The framework currently rests on one podcast's interpretation of one wuxia novel, not empirical expertise research or a complete philosophy of art. Lethal swordsmanship, music, sport, family responsibility, and ordinary work impose different demands. The episode does not prove that attachment caused hesitation, that emotional detachment caused mastery, or that either swordsman consciously represents one stable doctrine. Dialogue, motive, combat sequence, and comparisons across fictional systems remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Created the framework to distinguish attention costs, human attachment, technical fidelity, and moral integrity.
- Preserved both the performance burden and the human value of attachment without treating either as a universal verdict.
