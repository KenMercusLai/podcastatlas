# Contextual Moral Judgment

## Definition

Contextual moral judgment evaluates conduct and outcomes in light of the constraints, alternatives, information, and execution difficulty faced by an actor, without treating those conditions as automatic moral exoneration.

## Current Synthesis

[[MalcolmGladwell]] proposes a “degree of difficulty” correction to outcome-only judgment: the same result may reflect very different competence or responsibility when resources, options, and operating conditions differ. [[AdamGrant]] accepts the need for context but presses on the danger that sympathy for difficult circumstances can soften attention to inflicted harm.

[[goodbye-farewell-and-amen-goodbye-farewell-and-amen]] sharpens the framework through incendiary war. The defense of bombing Japan compares mass civilian death with invasion, partition, and starvation that might have followed a later surrender. The episode does not dismiss those alternatives, but its return to Tokyo survivor testimony prevents the counterfactual from turning actual victims into an abstraction.

The resulting synthesis is two-sided. Judgment should compare feasible alternatives and implementation obstacles, yet still examine victims, legal and moral limits, uncertainty, and whether the actor's framing excluded political possibilities. Context changes attribution; it does not settle permissibility, and forecasts of lives saved require more scrutiny when they authorize irreversible violence.

## Key Claims

- Outcomes alone are insufficient evidence of competence, intent, or responsibility.
- Feasible alternatives matter more than ideal options unavailable to the actor.
- Attempt difficulty and execution quality should be assessed separately.
- Context can mitigate or explain responsibility without erasing harm.
- Any numerical “degree of difficulty” embeds contestable judgments about which obstacles count.
- Counterfactual lives-saved arguments require explicit uncertainty because their predicted beneficiaries cannot be observed.
- Civilian testimony constrains strategic abstraction by keeping actual harms inside the judgment.

## Evidence

- Wartime constraint - [[malcolm-gladwell-and-adam-grant-malcolm-gladwell-and-adam-grant]] compares precision bombing, firebombing, atomic bombing, and prolonged war as morally grave alternatives.
- Cross-domain application - [[malcolm-gladwell-and-adam-grant-malcolm-gladwell-and-adam-grant]] applies difficulty-sensitive judgment to an underfunded historically Black university, policing, diving, and war.
- Accountability challenge - [[malcolm-gladwell-and-adam-grant-malcolm-gladwell-and-adam-grant]] records Grant's concern that contextual sympathy can become excessive forgiveness for large-scale suffering.
- Japan counterfactual - [[goodbye-farewell-and-amen-goodbye-farewell-and-amen]] presents avoided invasion, partition, and starvation as the strongest defense of bombing while refusing to treat them as a complete moral resolution.
- Civilian standpoint - [[goodbye-farewell-and-amen-goodbye-farewell-and-amen]] closes with Tokyo museum evidence and survivor testimony rather than letting aggregate casualty reasoning stand alone.
- Cross-war test - [[goodbye-farewell-and-amen-goodbye-farewell-and-amen]] uses Korea and Vietnam to show that an actor's preferred alternatives may be narrowed by doctrinal commitment.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Constraints may be misdescribed by participants, and counterfactual alternatives are often uncertain. Difficulty metrics can reward dramatic attempts, obscure preventable harms, or encode the evaluator's preferences. The wartime examples in both sources are incomplete and cannot establish the relative consequences or permissibility of each strategy. Remembering victims does not by itself resolve the counterfactual, just as a plausible counterfactual does not erase victims.

## What Changed

- Added explicit scrutiny of lives-saved counterfactuals that authorize irreversible violence.
- Added civilian testimony as a necessary constraint on aggregate strategic reasoning.
