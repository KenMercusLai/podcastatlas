## Definition

受制君主的残余威胁 / captured sovereign residual threat is the danger a materially controlled ruler still poses through recognized title, palace access, armed attendants, succession symbolism, advisers, and the possibility that opponents may rally around the sovereign or household.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 1150 shows why effective power and felt security can diverge. [[CaoCao|曹操]] can kill [[ZhaoYanYilangLateHan|赵彦]], [[DongGuirenLateHan|董贵人]], and [[FuShouEmpress|伏皇后]], yet the episode also reports him fearing that [[EmperorXianOfHan|汉献帝]] could use palace guards or a sudden audience to reproduce earlier overmighty-minister collapses. This is not balanced dual sovereignty: coercive resources are radically unequal, but the controlled title is not politically empty.

The same mechanism explains why advice, pregnancy, marriage, and audience practice all become security questions. An adviser can restore independent judgment; an unborn imperial child can be imagined as a rallying point; an empress can preserve an anti-controller household channel; and close palace access can temporarily narrow the controller's military advantage.

## Key Claims

- Materially weak sovereigns can retain symbolic, dynastic, and close-access forms of danger.
- A powerholder may purge advisers because independent information can reconnect title to action.
- Imperial spouses and children can be treated as political infrastructure rather than private household members.
- Palace guards matter because proximity can compress a large external power imbalance into a brief local vulnerability.
- Reciprocal fear does not imply reciprocal capacity; Emperor Xian's exposure and Cao Cao's risk are categorically unequal.
- Control strategies can combine killing, household replacement, reduced audiences, and eventual formal dynastic transfer.

## Evidence

Independent counsel and household channels:

Close-access danger:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1150-caocao-weishenme-yao-sha-huaiyun-de-dong-guiren-ltsk2hxmdh9is39ejkypzqruoeiu|Hanji 1150]] says Cao Cao reacts fearfully to Emperor Xian's plea and the sight of armed attendants, then avoids later personal audiences.

Household replacement and formal exit:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1150-caocao-weishenme-yao-sha-huaiyun-de-dong-guiren-ltsk2hxmdh9is39ejkypzqruoeiu|Hanji 1150]] connects the installation of Cao daughters, Cao Jie's empress status, Cao Pi's accession, and Liu Xie's Shanyang settlement.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode supplies one late-Han case and dramatizes motives and dialogue. It does not establish that Cao Cao faced an imminent palace coup, that Dong Guiren's unborn child already had an organized faction, or that every purge followed one coherent security design. The Liang Ji and Chunshen Jun comparisons illustrate perceived vulnerability but do not prove direct causal influence on Cao Cao.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to distinguish a controlled sovereign's residual danger from actual power-sharing.
- Separated palace proximity, dynastic symbolism, and adviser access as different threat channels.
