# Trademark Similarity and Consumer Confusion

## Definition
Trademark similarity and consumer confusion is the risk that a newer visual mark makes buyers infer affiliation, origin, or sponsorship because it resembles an established brand mark in the same market.

## Current Synthesis
The New Balance-Decathlon dispute shows that trademark conflict is not limited to identical words or symbols. In performance footwear, a letter-like mark, its geometry, product placement, market context, and accumulated brand recognition can matter together. The durable question is consumer interpretation, not whether designers can articulate different inspirations after the fact.

## Key Claims
- Visual marks can conflict even when they represent different letters.
- Product category and placement affect whether similarity implies affiliation.
- Long use and promotion strengthen the commercial significance of a simple mark.
- A lawsuit is evidence of a disputed boundary, not proof that confusion occurred.

## Evidence
- Similarity allegation: [[jinghu-gaotie-zhongqiujie-qian-chuxian-jiangjia-xinbailun-qisu-dikanong-qinquan-1017386449]] reports New Balance's claim that Keep Run's K mark resembles its N mark closely enough to suggest a relationship.
- Competing histories: [[jinghu-gaotie-zhongqiujie-qian-chuxian-jiangjia-xinbailun-qisu-dikanong-qinquan-1017386449]] contrasts Keep Run's starting-block explanation with New Balance's decades of use and promotion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source gives the plaintiff's framing but not Decathlon's full response, consumer-survey evidence, or a ruling. Different design inspiration does not by itself defeat confusion, but visual resemblance alone also does not establish infringement.
