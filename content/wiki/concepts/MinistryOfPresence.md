# Ministry of Presence

## Definition

Ministry of presence is a care practice in which reliable accompaniment, including staying through distress and aftermath, matters more than finding sufficient words or securing a particular response.

## Current Synthesis

In the death-row setting described by [[TomPerryJr]], presence requires repeated visits over years and a willingness to remain “all the way” through execution if the condemned person asks. It does not settle innocence, negate the victim's harm, or guarantee insight into the person's physical experience. Its moral force comes from refusing abandonment: the minister recognizes the condemned person, supports both the person and family, accepts emotional cost, and preserves memory when institutional procedure tends to reduce a life to sentence and protocol.

## Key Claims

- Reliable return can communicate care more effectively than religious instruction alone.
- Presence becomes meaningful through duration, consistency, and willingness to remain during the hardest stage.
- Care for a condemned person need not deny culpability or displace the victim.
- Accompaniment supports families and witnesses as well as the person facing death.
- Witnessing creates relational knowledge but does not defeat the physiological opacity of a sedated and paralyzed execution.

## Evidence

### Repeated accompaniment

- [[the-alabama-murders-part-4-the-protocol]] describes Perry's monthly visits, extended visits, decades-long relationship with Parker, and commitment to attend executions when asked.

### Final-day care and aftermath

- [[the-alabama-murders-part-4-the-protocol]] follows Perry through Parker's final family gathering, execution, conversation with the family afterward, emotional collapse, and continued preservation of Parker's photograph and memory.

### Limits of presence

- [[the-alabama-murders-part-4-the-protocol]] preserves Parker's admission of participation in Sennett's assault and Perry's uncertainty about internal suffering despite witnessing the execution.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The concept is derived from one minister's account in one structured episode summary rather than a comparative study of prison chaplaincy or pastoral-care outcomes.
- Sustained presence can humanize a condemned person without resolving guilt, proportional punishment, victim-family needs, or the legitimacy of capital punishment.
- Perry's Christian framework is central to this case, but the supplied source does not establish that the practice requires a particular faith or institutional role.
- Emotional commitment can deepen care while also shaping memory and interpretation; Perry's closeness to Parker does not independently verify Parker's account.
- Presence at an execution may reduce abandonment but cannot make a protocol transparent when drugs and restraint suppress observable distress.

## What Changed

- Established reliable accompaniment, family support, and memory preservation as a unified care practice.
- Made explicit that relational presence and evidentiary access are different: a witness can care deeply without knowing the condemned person's internal experience.
