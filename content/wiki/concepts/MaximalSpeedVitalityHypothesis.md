# Maximal Speed Vitality Hypothesis

## Definition
The maximal speed vitality hypothesis is McMillan's proposal that the capacity to safely express one's own top running speed may integrate enough power, coordination, eccentric control, confidence, and movement reserve to serve as a marker of vitality.

## Current Synthesis
The hypothesis is appealing because maximal speed requires multiple systems to work together: neural drive, force production, force acceptance, balance, joint and tissue tolerance, rhythm, coordination, and willingness to move intensely. It also aligns with aging concerns around falling, immobility, fast-twitch decline, and loss of eccentric control.

The current evidence does not make it a clinical health proxy. The episode supplies a coach's argument and family example, not prospective validation, normative ranges, test-retest reliability, disease prediction, or a safe universal testing protocol. Its strongest use in the wiki is as a research question and training-design prompt: preserving speed-related qualities may matter even when all-out sprint testing is inappropriate.

## Key Claims
- Safely expressed personal top speed integrates several movement capacities that can decline with age.
- Power, eccentric control, coordination, posture, and confidence may reveal reserve not captured by slower exercise alone.
- Fall and immobility risks make braking and reactive movement relevant to longevity.
- Skipping, walking, hills, and striding can train component qualities without using maximal sprinting as the starting point.
- The proposition is a vitality hypothesis, not a validated biomarker or universal field test.

## Evidence
- Health-proxy proposal: [[how-to-increase-your-speed-mobility-longevity-with-plyometrics-sprinting-stuart-mcmillan-scim1307532744]] records McMillan asking whether safe personal maximal speed could outperform common health metrics as a vitality proxy.
- Aging rationale: [[how-to-increase-your-speed-mobility-longevity-with-plyometrics-sprinting-stuart-mcmillan-scim1307532744]] links aging with lost eccentric control, falls, immobility, and serious downstream consequences.
- Family example: [[how-to-increase-your-speed-mobility-longevity-with-plyometrics-sprinting-stuart-mcmillan-scim1307532744]] describes McMillan's 78-year-old father combining walking, skipping, running, and striding.
- Graduated alternative: [[how-to-increase-your-speed-mobility-longevity-with-plyometrics-sprinting-stuart-mcmillan-scim1307532744]] repeatedly states that many adults are not ready for maximal sprinting and should build capacity first.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
No validation evidence, threshold, protocol, or longitudinal outcome is supplied. Inability or unwillingness to sprint can reflect disability, pain, environment, skill, fear, medication, cardiovascular risk, neurological disease, or injury history rather than global vitality. Attempting maximal speed can itself create harm, so the hypothesis must not be used for self-clearance or to rank health across bodies.
