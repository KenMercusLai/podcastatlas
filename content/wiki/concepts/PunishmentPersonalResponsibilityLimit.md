## Definition

刑罚个人责任边界 / Punishment Personal Responsibility Limit is the principle that legal punishment for wrongdoing should stop with the culpable actor, even when rewards for virtue or merit may extend to descendants.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-796-donghan-mingchen-vs-diwang-shenbianren-shui-neng-ying-lkyzp2v-cjysd65syoyuldlbdb5p|Hanji 796]] grounds the concept through [[LiuKaiEasternHan|刘恺]]'s objection to using the Shusun Guang precedent against Fan Bin's descendants. The court wants to bar descendants of another corrupt official from office for two generations; Liu Kai argues that anti-corruption punishment should not disable innocent descendants.

The episode's important contrast is that Liu Kai is not defending corruption. He accepts punishment for the offender, but rejects a hereditary disability that would frighten and harm people who have not committed the offense. [[ChenZhongLateHan|陈忠]] agrees, and [[EmperorAnOfHan|汉安帝]] accepts the argument.

This makes the concept a narrow legal boundary rather than a general leniency slogan. It resists [[CollateralPunishmentAndGuiltByAssociation|株连与连坐]] where inherited burden exceeds personal culpability, while preserving room for punishment of the actual official who took corrupt gains.

## Key Claims

- Punishment legitimacy depends on attaching penalty to the responsible actor rather than to innocent kin.
- Reward and punishment are asymmetric: rewards may extend as public gratitude, but punishment extension requires a stronger culpability basis.
- Anti-corruption enforcement can become overbroad when deterrence turns into hereditary office exclusion.
- A ruler can accept a narrow legal-limit argument even while ignoring broader institutional remonstrance.
- The concept complements modern culpability-limit ideas without claiming that the Han case matches modern criminal-law doctrine.

## Evidence

- Corruption precedent: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-796-donghan-mingchen-vs-diwang-shenbianren-shui-neng-ying-lkyzp2v-cjysd65syoyuldlbdb5p|Hanji 796]] says the court considers applying a prior Shusun Guang rule barring corrupt officials' descendants from office for two generations.
- Liu Kai's distinction: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-796-donghan-mingchen-vs-diwang-shenbianren-shui-neng-ying-lkyzp2v-cjysd65syoyuldlbdb5p|Hanji 796]] says Liu Kai distinguishes reward extension to descendants from punishment that should stop with the offender.
- Innocent-descendant concern: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-796-donghan-mingchen-vs-diwang-shenbianren-shui-neng-ying-lkyzp2v-cjysd65syoyuldlbdb5p|Hanji 796]] says Liu Kai warns that barring descendants from office frightens good and guiltless later generations.
- Accepted legal correction: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-796-donghan-mingchen-vs-diwang-shenbianren-shui-neng-ying-lkyzp2v-cjysd65syoyuldlbdb5p|Hanji 796]] says Chen Zhong agrees and Emperor An approves Liu Kai's position.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is currently grounded in one source note, so it should not be inflated into a complete Han legal doctrine. Hanji 796 does not deny punishment for corruption, nor does it resolve whether office-eligibility disabilities are civil, administrative, ritual, or penal in modern terms. The stable claim is narrower: Liu Kai and Chen Zhong limit hereditary consequences where the descendants themselves are not culpable.
