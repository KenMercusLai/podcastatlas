# Ecological Timekeeping

## Definition
Ecological timekeeping is the use of living or environmental processes as timekeepers, making growth, climate, and ecological conditions part of how time is marked.

## Current Synthesis
[[salt-020260914-archive-keats-podcast]] grounds the concept through [[CenturiesOfTheBristlecone]], where a bristlecone pine would keep a 5,000-year calendar. The point is not only a novel clock. It challenges the authority of standardized calendar time by asking whether time should be judged by abstract regularity or by the changing conditions through which life persists.

## Key Claims
- Ecological timekeeping treats time as embodied in living growth rather than only measured by external units.
- A living calendar can intentionally drift from standardized calendars because ecological conditions are part of the record.
- The method makes climate and environmental change visible as temporal evidence.
- It reframes calendar authority as a philosophical and political question, not only a technical convention.

## Evidence
### Bristlecone Calendar
- [[salt-020260914-archive-keats-podcast]] says [[JonathanKeats]] proposed using a bristlecone pine's rings and growth as the basis for a 5,000-year calendar.

### Drift From Standard Time
- [[salt-020260914-archive-keats-podcast]] says the calendar would fall out of sync with the Gregorian calendar, raising the question of which system should be considered right.

### Climate As Record
- [[salt-020260914-archive-keats-podcast]] frames bristlecone rings as reflecting climate and growth conditions rather than abstract interval alone.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source gives the conceptual design, not a finished conservation or measurement protocol. Ecological timekeeping may be vulnerable to tree health, site management, climate stress, human intervention, and interpretation disputes.

## What Changed
- Added ecological timekeeping as the concept opened by Keats's bristlecone calendar.
- Linked Long Now time practice to living systems rather than only clocks, archives, or institutions.
