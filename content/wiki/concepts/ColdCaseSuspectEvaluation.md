# Cold-Case Suspect Evaluation

## Definition

Cold-case suspect evaluation is the disciplined comparison of named and profiled suspects against alibi, access, geography, witness description, physical capacity, known conduct, method, documentary provenance, and alternative explanations without treating narrative fit or cumulative suspicion as identification.

## Current Synthesis

[[628-jack-the-ripper-the-killer-unmasked-part-5-glt3796910737]] develops the method by moving from spectacular [[JackTheRipper|Jack the Ripper]] theories toward progressively more ordinary possibilities. Royal rank, artistic darkness, medical status, foreignness, queerness, mental illness, police interest, or appearance in a purported diary can make a suspect memorable, but each remains evidentially weak unless it survives chronology, location, behavior, provenance, and method tests.

The episode's positive profile is deliberately narrower than a name: a local man comfortable in Whitechapel at night, able to approach victims, use a knife, and perhaps explain blood or anatomical familiarity through slaughterhouse or butchery work. That profile can rank possibilities while remaining too broad to establish identity. The method therefore treats “more plausible” and “proved” as different conclusions.

## Key Claims

- A crime's emotional or cultural scale does not require an equally famous, elite, or conspiratorial offender.
- Suspect assessment should test chronology, geography, access, physical feasibility, witness fit, known behavior, method, and provenance separately before combining them.
- Official interest, retrospective memoranda, later criminality, mental illness, or psychological resemblance can justify scrutiny but cannot substitute for offense-specific evidence.
- Hoax-prone letters, contested objects, old biological traces, and weakly sourced diaries require provenance analysis before they can identify anyone.
- A profile may explain opportunity and capability better than named suspects while still being too nonspecific to prove identity.
- Method changes, such as a move from mutilation to slow poisoning, require explanation rather than being ignored because the candidate is otherwise attractive.

## Evidence

### Celebrity and conspiracy suspects

- [[628-jack-the-ripper-the-killer-unmasked-part-5-glt3796910737]] rejects royal, political, medical, artistic, and famous Victorian candidates through alibi, recognizability, logistics, physical condition, and lack of direct evidence.

### Police-backed and later named suspects

- [[628-jack-the-ripper-the-killer-unmasked-part-5-glt3796910737]] compares George Chapman, Aaron Kosminski, Montague John Druitt, Michael Ostrog, James Maybrick, Francis Tumblety, Joseph Barnett, George Hutchinson, and Charles Lechmere without treating police attention or later notoriety as proof.

### Profile without identification

- [[628-jack-the-ripper-the-killer-unmasked-part-5-glt3796910737]] finds local butchery or slaughterhouse work more economical than celebrity theories because it can jointly explain night movement, knives, blood, bodily familiarity, and local escape, while explicitly preserving the absence of a named culprit.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

A profile can reproduce the investigator's assumptions, and surviving evidence may be incomplete, contaminated, misremembered, or unevenly preserved. Method consistency is useful but serial offenders need not behave identically in every offense. Alibis, witness descriptions, asylum records, and occupational inferences also vary in quality. The method ranks hypotheses; it cannot recover evidence that no longer exists or turn absence of disproof into positive proof.

## What Changed

- Added a reusable distinction between suspect plausibility, comparative ranking, and proof.
- Added method consistency and provenance as explicit checks on attractive named suspects.
- Added anonymous occupational profiling as useful but insufficient evidence.
