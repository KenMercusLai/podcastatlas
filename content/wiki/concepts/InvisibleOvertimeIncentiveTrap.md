# Invisible Overtime Incentive Trap / 隐形加班激励陷阱

## Definition
The invisible-overtime incentive trap is a workplace arrangement in which employees continue working beyond nominal hours but have reasons not to record, request, or fully disclose that labor.

## Current Synthesis
[[ep196-renlei-gaoxiao-gongzuo-yanjiu-baogao-ckwrijifxc-uabaaaad057ge|EP196]] describes a three-sided trap. Overtime reporting can increase the manager’s labor budget, suggest that the employee is inefficient, and make a low-overtime team appear capable of accepting more work. The formal approval mechanism therefore need not eliminate overtime; it can move the work off the record.

The mechanism also explains why a new schedule label is insufficient. If workload, deadlines, staffing, project ownership, and performance evaluation do not change together, the worker still owns the unfinished task while the organization gains a nominal boundary that may not be safe to use.

EP157 adds the proof and communication layer. Overtime may be produced through targets, oral conversations, peer comparison, manager presence, and instructions to keep finding problems rather than through a written hours rule. The same informality that makes refusal costly can leave the worker unable to prove employer direction later, linking the trap to [[LaborEvidencePreservation]].

## Key Claims
- Overtime disclosure depends on its consequences, not merely on whether a reporting form exists.
- Budget pressure can make managers reluctant to approve recorded overtime.
- Efficiency stigma can make employees reluctant to request it.
- Teams that report little overtime may be assigned more work, rewarding concealment with a higher future load.
- A credible hours reform must align workload, deadlines, staffing, compensation, evaluation, and stopping authority.
- Unwritten targets and social signals can compel overtime while weakening the evidence available in a later dispute.

## Evidence
- Cost and performance coupling - [[ep196-renlei-gaoxiao-gongzuo-yanjiu-baogao-ckwrijifxc-uabaaaad057ge]] describes overtime applications as potentially affecting both payroll budgets and employee performance judgments.
- Target ratchet - [[ep196-renlei-gaoxiao-gongzuo-yanjiu-baogao-ckwrijifxc-uabaaaad057ge]] argues that teams reporting less overtime may be treated as having spare capacity and receive additional work.
- Formal-versus-real hours - [[ep196-renlei-gaoxiao-gongzuo-yanjiu-baogao-ckwrijifxc-uabaaaad057ge]] reports skepticism that a nominal 10-to-7 schedule can hold while personal task responsibility remains unchanged.
- Oral and social enforcement - [[ep157-kun-zai-pinduoduo-de-xitong-li-weida-de-gongsi-bu-yinggai-rang-ren-gandao-haipa-ckwriued61wwabaaaacbzp2q]] describes targets, peer comparison, manager presence, and non-written requests as mechanisms that can extend work without creating a clear formal record.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not provide company records showing how often overtime was requested, approved, paid, or concealed. Their ByteDance and Pinduoduo accounts are experiential or based on a small number of employee conversations. Approval systems can protect workers when reporting is safe, compensation is real, managers are accountable, and workload falls when limits are reached. The concept should therefore be used as an incentive-risk test, not as proof that every overtime process is cosmetic.

## What Changed
- Established a distinct mechanism for why formal overtime controls can produce hidden rather than reduced labor.
- Added unwritten targets, social enforcement, and later proof difficulty as a second route into hidden overtime.
