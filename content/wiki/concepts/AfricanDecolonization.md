## Definition

African decolonization is the uneven transfer from European colonial rule to independent African states, shaped by colonial institutions, settler power, mass nationalism, international ideology, inherited borders, and Cold War intervention.

## Current Synthesis

[[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] rejects a single model of independence. [[Ghana]] represents a comparatively organized transfer driven by elite networks and mass politics; [[Algeria]] shows how a large settler population and coercive rule intensified reciprocal violence; and the [[BelgianCongo]] shows how exclusion from professional and military authority could make abrupt sovereignty institutionally fragile.

The same source also resists treating decolonization as culturally pure separation from Europe. Leaders and movements drew on African constituencies and goals while using [[PanAfricanism]], human rights, Marxism, Christianity, Islam, and education acquired in imperial centers. Independence therefore changed sovereignty without automatically dissolving inherited state forms, borders, economic dependence, or geopolitical vulnerability.

## Key Claims

- African independence followed multiple pathways rather than one continental sequence.
- Settler demography could intensify resistance to majority rule and make transfer more violent.
- Colonial exclusion from administration, professions, and command could weaken successor-state capacity.
- Western education and political vocabularies could become resources for anti-colonial mobilization.
- Mass support depended on workers, teachers, unions, local networks, and political performance, not only elite ideology.
- Formal independence did not automatically remove inherited borders, institutions, or foreign intervention.

## Evidence

- Negotiated mass politics: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] uses Nkrumah and Ghana to connect elite organization with urban workers, unions, teachers, imprisonment, elections, and immediate-independence demands.
- Settler conflict: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] contrasts Algeria and Kenya with colonies having smaller European populations and links settler fear to violent resistance.
- Institutional exclusion: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] says Belgian colonial policy sharply restricted African access to professions and senior military positions before abrupt independence.
- Cold War afterlife: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] follows Lumumba's Soviet appeal, murder, and Mobutu's Western-backed consolidation.
- Border inheritance: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] uses Nigeria's internal diversity and the Biafran War as a compressed example of difficult postcolonial nation-building.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode's comparisons are interpretive and highly compressed. Settler population, education, borders, or administrative preparation cannot serve as single-cause explanations, and negotiated independence did not guarantee just or democratic postcolonial government. The source gives limited space to ordinary African voices, women, rural politics, liberation organizations, and country-specific chronology; its treatment of Nigeria, Kenya, Rhodesia, South Africa, and China is explicitly incomplete.

## What Changed

- Created a comparative concept that separates negotiated, settler-war, abrupt-withdrawal, and Cold War pathways.
- Made ideological hybridity part of decolonization without reducing African agency to imported ideas.
