# Precautionary Action Under Uncertainty

## Definition
Precautionary action under uncertainty is the principle that credible evidence of severe or irreversible harm can justify proportionate intervention before every causal question or population effect is settled.

## Current Synthesis
[[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof|Burden of Proof]] develops the principle through a coal-dust analogy and college football. Frederick Hoffman's 1918 miner evidence was incomplete, yet delay left workers exposed for decades; [[OwenThomas|Owen Thomas's]] CTE diagnosis similarly confronts Penn with a potentially grave harm whose exact prevalence and individual causal chain remain unresolved.

The principle does not erase evidentiary standards. [[ChronicTraumaticEncephalopathyEvidenceBoundary]] prevents a selected brain bank from becoming a population estimate and prevents a postmortem diagnosis from becoming a single-cause account of suicide. Precaution instead changes the decision question: institutions should compare the expected cost, reversibility, and effectiveness of possible interventions with the severity and credibility of the risk, and should explain why continued exposure is justified rather than treating uncertainty as automatically favoring the status quo.

## Key Claims
- Waiting for definitive proof is itself a policy choice when exposure and possible harm continue.
- The severity, irreversibility, and credibility of a danger affect how much certainty action should require.
- Institutions with control over exposure bear a stronger duty to investigate, disclose uncertainty, and justify continuation.
- Memorial gestures and general concern do not substitute for evaluating the activity that produces the risk.
- Precaution can support monitoring, exposure reduction, redesign, pause, or cessation; evidence does not make every remedy equally effective.
- Uncertainty must constrain causal claims even when it no longer justifies institutional inaction.

## Evidence
- Historical analogy - [[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof]] uses Hoffman's coal-miner findings and delayed recognition of dust harm to show the cost of demanding certainty.
- Severe contemporary risk - [[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof]] joins Thomas's diagnosis to repeated-impact concern and asks whether continuing Penn football can be justified.
- Institutional contrast - [[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof]] compares Penn's continuation with New York University's escalating physical barriers after suicides.
- Partial mitigation - [[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof]] reports closer concussion monitoring, less tackling in practice, and opposition to youth tackle football without establishing their comparative effectiveness.
- Explicit action demand - [[burden-of-proof-burden-of-proof]] urges boycott, protest, and administrative justification rather than passive concern.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Analogies do not establish identical mechanisms, incidence, alternatives, or remedy costs. Coal dust, suicide barriers, and football differ in exposure, benefits, agency, institutional role, and available interventions. The source argues for ending Penn football but does not compare closure with rule changes, contact reduction, monitoring, age limits, informed consent, or other safeguards. Precaution should therefore be proportionate and revisable, not a license to treat any speculative harm as decisive or to overstate individual causation.

## What Changed
- Created a general decision principle from the episode's coal-dust and football comparison.
- Distinguished sufficient evidence for precaution from sufficient evidence for prevalence or individual causation.
- Added proportionality and remedy comparison as qualifications to the episode's forceful cessation argument.
