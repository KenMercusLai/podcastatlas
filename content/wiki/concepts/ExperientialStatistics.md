# Experiential Statistics

## Definition
Experiential statistics is Tye's term for expectations learned from repeated experience about scarcity, punishment, safety, trust, and what is likely to happen next.

## Current Synthesis
Behavior that looks irrational in one environment can be adaptive under a different history. A child who takes more food than an immediate share may be responding to prior evidence that future supply is unreliable or that delay is punished. Repeated abundance can change that prediction only when it becomes credible. The same principle extends to attention and institutions: different life histories change which questions, risks, threats, and opportunities people notice, so experiential diversity can improve collective judgment rather than merely diversify appearance.

## Key Claims
- Learned expectations are built from repeated environmental outcomes rather than abstract instruction alone.
- Scarcity history can make immediate acquisition rational even when current supply appears sufficient.
- Trust changes when reliable new evidence accumulates, not simply when reassurance is offered.
- Prior experience shapes what feels interesting, safe, threatening, or plausible.
- Institutions can gain decision-relevant information from people with different experiential histories.

## Evidence
- Scarcity example - [[the-biology-of-social-interactions-and-emotions-dr-kay-tye-scim4105255786]] interprets food-hoarding behavior through prior scarcity, punishment, and low trust.
- Learning update - [[the-biology-of-social-interactions-and-emotions-dr-kay-tye-scim4105255786]] describes behavior changing after children learn that more food will reliably remain available.
- Institutional implication - [[the-biology-of-social-interactions-and-emotions-dr-kay-tye-scim4105255786]] connects varied backgrounds to a wider range of scientific questions and decisions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source offers a conceptual interpretation, not a complete developmental model or a claim that every scarcity-linked behavior has the same cause. Individual histories should not be inferred from one action, and diversity does not automatically improve decisions without real participation and influence.
