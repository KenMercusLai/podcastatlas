# Medium-Specific Adaptation Salience / 媒介特定的改编显著性

## Definition

Medium-specific adaptation salience is the principle that prose and screen media make information noticeable in different ways, so preserving a story function may require changing, relocating, or removing the original device rather than reproducing it literally.

## Current Synthesis

The bounded Harry Potter comparison separates adaptation from simple deletion. A novel can embed Nicolas Flamel's name among many words on a collectible card, keep the Sorting Hat exchange inside Harry's awareness, and suspend action for a verbal logic puzzle. Film must decide what receives a close-up, audible dialogue, screen time, and visible movement. A faithful close-up can reveal a clue too early; silent interior thought can become unintelligible; a static reasoning task can interrupt an otherwise physical sequence.

The film therefore externalizes the Sorting Hat's words, removes the potion puzzle, shifts more visible problem-solving into the Devil's Snare scene, and intensifies Harry's direct physical role against Quirrell. These choices preserve accessibility, pace, spectacle, and protagonist focus while changing clue difficulty, competence distribution, and the apparent limit of a first-year student's power.

## Key Claims

- Information density lets prose hide a meaningful name or clue without announcing its importance.
- Camera framing, duration, dialogue, music, and reaction shots can make the same clue disproportionately conspicuous on screen.
- Interior speech must be externalized, visualized, or omitted if viewers need access to it.
- Removing a scene also redistributes character competence and thematic emphasis.
- Visual action can improve pace and spectacle while weakening logic, uncertainty, or limits on the protagonist.
- Functional fidelity may require formal difference rather than literal reproduction.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept comes from one podcast's comparison of one adaptation and does not establish the filmmakers' documented intentions. Visual media can conceal clues through staging and editing, and prose can also overemphasize information. The framework describes a recurring pressure, not a rule that films must favor spectacle or that omission is automatically justified.
