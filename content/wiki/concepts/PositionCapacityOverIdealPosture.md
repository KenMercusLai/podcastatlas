# Position Capacity Over Ideal Posture

## Definition
Position capacity over ideal posture is the view that healthy posture is the ability to enter, control, vary, and leave positions that fit the task, rather than holding one supposedly correct static alignment at all times.

## Current Synthesis
[[how-to-improve-your-mobility-posture-flexibility-dr-kelly-starrett-scim3685343544]] reframes posture as position. Some positions can improve breathing, force transfer, or movement output for a given task, but no single arrangement serves desk work, lifting, running, resting, and play equally. Capacity therefore includes variation and access: a person should be able to organize the trunk and pelvis, reach hip extension, use the glutes, and move the spine through flexion, extension, rotation, and side bending when the task calls for it.

This does not make technique irrelevant. Start and finish positions, speed, load, volume, range, and fatigue all change the demand. The durable rule is to assess position by function, symptoms, control, and task fit while avoiding both rigid posture policing and the claim that every position is equally useful under every load.

## Key Claims
- Posture is task-dependent; a useful position supports the physiology, force transfer, comfort, and movement demanded at that moment.
- Healthy capacity includes changing positions rather than preserving one static shape indefinitely.
- Hip extension, pelvic control, glute contraction, trunk motion, and breathing interact, so an isolated visual cue may miss the functional constraint.
- Loaded training should evaluate start and finish positions, speed, range, volume, and control instead of naming muscles alone.
- Persistent pain, neurological change, trauma, or major functional loss requires more than posture correction.

## Evidence
- Posture reframing - [[how-to-improve-your-mobility-posture-flexibility-dr-kelly-starrett-scim3685343544]] replaces a moralized ideal posture with positions judged by breathing, force transfer, and output.
- Hip and pelvic example - [[how-to-improve-your-mobility-posture-flexibility-dr-kelly-starrett-scim3685343544]] links limited hip extension with pelvic position, glute control, and running or athletic mechanics.
- Trunk-capacity example - [[how-to-improve-your-mobility-posture-flexibility-dr-kelly-starrett-scim3685343544]] recommends training flexion, extension, rotation, side bending, and complex trunk positions rather than crunches alone.
- Loading context - [[how-to-improve-your-mobility-posture-flexibility-dr-kelly-starrett-scim3685343544]] organizes movement around archetypal positions plus speed, load, volume, and range.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source offers a clinical and coaching framework, not proof that posture is unrelated to pain or that one cue suits every body. Anatomy, disability, injury history, task duration, fatigue, equipment, sport rules, and symptoms can constrain available positions. Visual asymmetry alone does not establish pathology, while normal-looking posture does not rule it out. Acute trauma, progressive weakness or numbness, bowel or bladder change, fever, unexplained weight change, or severe functional loss needs appropriate assessment.
