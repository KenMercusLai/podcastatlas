# Dark Patterns

## Definition

Dark patterns are designs that exploit attention, inertia, confusion, or asymmetric effort to steer people toward outcomes that advantage the designer while making material consequences, refusal, correction, or exit harder to use.

## Current Synthesis

[[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-434eaf8f105a4e37e33cf9afe4af65dd-52053896-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-434eaf8f105a4e37e33cf9afe4af65dd-52053896|Choose Carefully]] places dark patterns inside the wider category of [[ChoiceArchitecture]]. The same general tools that can reduce unnecessary work can be inverted: subscriptions can be easy to start but hard to cancel, automatic renewals can convert inaction into payment, contact details can be hidden, and exit can require long waits or additional steps.

The episode calls an easy-entry, hard-exit pattern a “roach motel.” The core problem is not that a design influences behavior; influence is unavoidable. It is that the design creates a one-sided action-cost structure or hides information needed for meaningful consent. An ethical alternative makes price, duration, renewal, consequences, and cancellation legible, and gives refusal or exit a practical path proportionate to enrollment.

## Key Claims

- Dark patterns are a harmful use of choice architecture rather than a separate absence of choice.
- Asymmetric friction can make enrollment or purchase easy while making cancellation, refusal, or correction difficult.
- Automatic renewal can convert omission or inattention into an ongoing transaction.
- Hidden contact paths and long waits can function as behavioral barriers even when formal cancellation remains available.
- Ethical assessment should examine material disclosure, symmetry of action costs, reversibility, and whose interests the design serves.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This page is grounded in one structured episode summary rather than an interface audit, legal standard, regulatory record, or cross-industry taxonomy. Not every default, retention step, security check, or cancellation delay is deceptive; fraud prevention, identity verification, service continuity, and operational constraints can create legitimate friction. The relevant distinction depends on disclosure, proportionality, symmetry, user intent, and practical exit rather than friction alone. The source does not measure prevalence, consumer harm, or the effectiveness of any specific remedy.
