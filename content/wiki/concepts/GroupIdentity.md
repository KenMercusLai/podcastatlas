# Group Identity

## Definition
Group identity is the context-sensitive incorporation of a category, affiliation, or collective into the self, allowing membership to shape belonging, valuation, attention, perception, and action.

## Current Synthesis
The episode presents identity as a lens rather than a static demographic label. Which membership becomes salient depends on context: nationality may matter little at home and strongly abroad, while a randomly assigned art preference can produce immediate favoritism. Once active, identity can influence product choice, sensory judgment, reward, threat estimation, political interpretation, and relationship decisions without requiring conscious dishonesty.

The same speed creates both danger and possibility. Thin categories can become rivalry, moral condemnation, and selective risk judgment, but identities are nested and revisable. Shared goals, visible inclusion, valued exemplars, cooperative dependence, and leadership can activate a wider “us.” The strongest current judgment is therefore conditional: group identity is powerful but not morally fixed, and its consequences depend on norms, institutions, incentives, history, and which boundary becomes salient.

## Key Claims
- Context determines which of a person's many memberships becomes psychologically salient.
- Even arbitrary categories can produce in-group liking, allocation bias, and emotional investment.
- Identity can shape sincere perception and attention, not only public rhetoric or deliberate deception.
- Group success and out-group loss can acquire self-relevant emotional value.
- Moralized identity can turn disagreement into threat, relationship sorting, and support for otherwise distrusted allies.
- Group boundaries can widen through shared purpose, cooperation, representation, and admired cross-group members.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode does not show that every identity prime changes behavior, that minimal-group laboratory effects predict severe real-world discrimination, or that observed group differences arise only from identity rather than institutions, incentives, ideology, material interests, or history. Brain and behavioral effects do not establish moral essence or inevitability. Broader identity can also conceal unequal power or demand symbolic unity without justice, so inclusion must be evaluated by conduct and institutions as well as language.

## What Changed
