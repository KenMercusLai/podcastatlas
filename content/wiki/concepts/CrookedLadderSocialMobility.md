# Crooked-Ladder Social Mobility

## Definition

Crooked-ladder social mobility is the episode's model of illicit activity as a temporary route through which excluded or marginalized families accumulate resources and status before later generations enter conventional institutions and reject criminal inheritance.

## Current Synthesis

The concept, named in the episode through sociologist James O'Kane and grounded in [[FrancisIanni|Francis Ianni]]'s family study, reverses the assumption that organized crime necessarily expresses permanent separation from mainstream society. Some participants may believe intensely in the American Dream but perceive the legitimate lower rungs as inaccessible. Crime then becomes a dangerous and coercive substitute route toward wealth, education, professional entry, and respectability.

The intergenerational unit matters. Ianni's fourth-generation occupational pattern, Meyer Lansky's pride in his son's West Point graduation, and the Gravano family's uneven attempts to leave crime behind illustrate different points on the ladder. Later generations can reject illicit proceeds precisely because earlier accumulation has widened their legitimate options, but the episode's founders analogy does not erase victims, excuse coercion, or make every criminal family upwardly mobile.

## Key Claims

- Organized crime can coexist with a strong desire for conventional success and recognition.
- Exclusion from legitimate opportunity can make illicit enterprise appear to supply missing lower rungs.
- Mobility should be evaluated across generations rather than inferred from one participant's criminal identity.
- Education, professions, affluent residence, and rejection of illicit money mark movement toward conventional legitimacy.
- A criminal past can become a cautionary inheritance rather than an endlessly repeated family destiny.
- Political change can relabel formerly illicit actors when they gain authority to define legitimate institutions.

## Evidence

- Family study: [[tempest-in-a-teacup-tempest-in-a-teacup]] reports that most fourth-generation members of Ianni's studied family had entered legitimate professions and institutions by 1970.
- Rejection by descendants: [[tempest-in-a-teacup-tempest-in-a-teacup]] uses the Lansky and Gravano cases to show children or grandchildren resisting criminal money and identity.
- Founding comparison: [[tempest-in-a-teacup-tempest-in-a-teacup]] analogizes colonial smugglers becoming officeholders after independence to movement from the crooked to the straight side of the ladder.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The framework describes a possible trajectory, not a defense of crime or a general law of mobility. It can understate victims, violence, unequal enforcement, inherited criminal advantage, failed exits, and families that remain criminal or never gain legitimacy. The Ianni, Lansky, Gravano, and Sons of Liberty examples differ sharply in evidence, commodity, harm, organization, period, and political setting. The episode's generation counts and family identifications remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Created a framework for the episode's intergenerational link among exclusion, illicit accumulation, professional entry, and legitimacy.
