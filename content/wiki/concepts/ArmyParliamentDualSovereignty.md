# Army-Parliament Dual Sovereignty

## Definition

Army-Parliament dual sovereignty is an unstable order in which a representative legislature claims civil authority while the military force that created or protects the regime retains an independent claim to define, purge, or replace that legislature.

## Current Synthesis

The English Republic's dual sovereignty was visible before it formally existed. Rank-and-file fear of prosecution, distrust of a soft parliamentary peace, the Putney debates, and the Remonstrance pushed the New Model Army from military victory toward an independent political claim. When Parliament resisted trying Charles, Pride's Purge removed or detained MPs; the narrowed Commons then declared itself the supreme representative of a people from whom all just power originated.

The resulting order depended on both the purged Commons and an army that was more religiously and politically radical. Because the army had reconstituted the legislature and enabled regicide, Parliament could not fully subordinate it; because officers lacked an accepted civilian mandate, the army could not turn coercive power or a claim to embody the people into durable constitutional legitimacy.

[[OliverCromwell]] temporarily mediated the conflict through personal authority, but repeated dissolutions reproduced rather than solved it. Barebone's Parliament failed partly because it did not meet the army's financial needs, while Cromwell's suppression of the [[Levellers]] at Burford showed army unity itself being enforced against a democratic rank-and-file challenge. The [[InstrumentOfGovernment1653|Instrument of Government]] reorganized offices without placing coercive power securely under Parliament. After Cromwell's death, [[RichardCromwell]]'s lack of army support, rapid fall, and renewed army-junta rule made the unresolved dual sovereignty visible again.

## Key Claims

- Military creation of a legislature's governing space can compromise civilian supremacy from the beginning.
- An army may convert soldiers' security fears and ideological radicalization into a claim to represent the people independently of elected representatives.
- Ideological differences between army and Parliament turn institutional rivalry into a dispute over the revolution's meaning.
- Personal leadership can suppress dual sovereignty without constitutionalizing it.
- Legislative purges narrow representative legitimacy even when justified as revolutionary necessity.
- Succession exposes whether authority belongs to an office, an assembly, or armed force.
- Fiscal dependence and internal military discipline can preserve army power even under a written civilian constitution.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Neither “the army” nor “Parliament” was internally uniform. Rank-and-file soldiers, Levellers, senior officers, moderate MPs, the purged Commons, the nominated assembly, and the Lords differed among themselves and over time. The army's claim to represent the people was politically generative but not proof of a popular mandate, while the Levellers should not be treated as representative of all soldiers or a fully modern democracy. Dual sovereignty is an analytical compression, not a claim that exactly two coherent institutions controlled every phase.
