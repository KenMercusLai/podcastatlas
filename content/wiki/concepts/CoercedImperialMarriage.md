## Definition

Coerced Imperial Marriage / 强制赐婚 is the pattern in which a ruler's marriage arrangement becomes command pressure, overriding consent or existing obligations and sometimes extending natal-family surveillance, accusation, and punishment into the resulting household.

## Current Synthesis

The evidence now shows coercion at three stages. At entry, [[EmperorHuanOfHan|汉桓帝]]'s admiration becomes fatal pressure when [[YangQiaoLateHan|杨桥]] refuses a princess marriage and dies after a hunger strike. The [[LiuXiu|刘秀 / 光武帝]] and [[SongHongEasternHan|宋弘]] comparison supplies the restraint boundary: imperial interest does not become coercion when the ruler accepts an existing marital obligation and stops.

After marriage, the two early Western Han Zhao cases show household coercion taking different forms. [[LiuHuiZhaoKingWesternHan|刘恢]] is reportedly watched by Lü-aligned attendants, loses his favored consort to poisoning, and dies by suicide. [[LiuYouZhaoKingWesternHan|刘友]] is accused by his Lü-family queen of opposing Lü kingship, summoned to Chang'an, isolated, denied food, and starved. In both accounts, marriage connects the spouse's natal political network to royal household conduct; it does not remain a private union or one-time ceremony.

The Liu You branch adds a necessary distinction between accusation and proven guilt. The source attributes anti-Lü speech to the queen's report but does not independently establish whether he said it, whether the wording is exact, or whether the marital grievance alone caused Lu Zhi's decision. The concept therefore identifies the coercive channel and consequence without assuming every reported motive is verified.

## Key Claims

- Coerced imperial marriage turns palace favor into pressure because the ruler's desire carries command force.
- The victim may face a bad choice between dishonoring an existing obligation and resisting the ruler.
- The harm can be fatal without a formal execution, through hunger strike, poisoning-linked suicide, guarded isolation, or denial of food.
- A contrasting case of ruler restraint shows that the boundary failure is not inevitable in imperial marriage politics.
- The Song Hong case shows the boundary positively: the ruler can make the other party's existing obligation legible, then stop.
- The Liu Hui and Liu You cases extend the concept from compelled entry into marriage to surveillance, accusation, and punishment inside the married household.

## Evidence

- Forced pressure and death: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-878-bu-shou-yueshu-de-quanli-hairen-buqian-lvevnahcxshgbz5zyqbaf36pa7nk|Hanji 878]] says Huan pressures Yang Qiao toward a princess marriage and Yang dies after a seven-day hunger strike.
- Favor as danger: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-878-bu-shou-yueshu-de-quanli-hairen-buqian-lvevnahcxshgbz5zyqbaf36pa7nk|Hanji 878]] frames Huan's appreciation of Yang's talent and appearance as the path by which attention becomes coercive danger.
- Restraint contrast: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-878-bu-shou-yueshu-de-quanli-hairen-buqian-lvevnahcxshgbz5zyqbaf36pa7nk|Hanji 878]] contrasts Liu Xiu's withdrawal after Song Hong's answer with Huan's unwillingness to release Yang.
- Primary Song Hong case: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-685-donghan-dachen-songhong-weihe-buken-zuo-guangwudi-jiefu-ljafg8giyhg5wmumv-g-09lywcu2|Hanji 685]] gives the underlying scene: Princess Huyang praises Song, Liu Xiu tests him from behind a screen setup, Song cites "糟糠之妻不下堂," and Liu Xiu says the matter cannot be arranged.
- Household-control extension: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-202-lishishang-yin-lengbaoli-bei-esi-de-ren-shi-ta-2-lrs7h5hhj0m0oixd6gw8flqpi0nd|Hanji 202 part 2]] says Liu Hui is married to Lü Chan's daughter, monitored by her entourage, loses his favored concubine to poisoning, and dies by suicide.
- Accusation-to-punishment extension: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-202-lishishang-yin-lengbaoli-bei-esi-de-ren-shi-ta-1-lopntgvnbsfgw_d9juvitmrk2kju|Hanji 202 part 1]] says Liu You's Lü-family queen reports alleged anti-Lü speech, after which he is summoned, isolated, denied food, and buried as a commoner.
- Identity boundary: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-202-lishishang-yin-lengbaoli-bei-esi-de-ren-shi-ta-2-lrs7h5hhj0m0oixd6gw8flqpi0nd|Hanji 202 part 2]] identifies the earlier starved Zhao king as Liu You and separates him from Liu Hui's death.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources do not argue that every imperial marriage arrangement is coercive. Song Hong's case shows that imperial suggestion can remain bounded when the ruler accepts refusal or moral hesitation. Hanji 685 does not show harm to Song or punishment for refusal; it is evidence for the non-coercive boundary. Both Hanji 202 documents are structured popular-history summaries and do not independently verify consent, surveillance procedure, jealousy, poisoning, suicide motive, the truth of the accusation against Liu You, the exact command chain, or either king's complete biography. Part 1 does not name Liu You internally; that identification depends on part 2's recap. The concept should remain tied to cases where command or natal-family household pressure, not merely status marriage or royal interest, is visible.

## What Changed

- Added Liu You as an accusation-to-confinement case in which a Lü-family marriage channels household conflict into fatal regency punishment.
- Distinguished Liu You's starvation from Liu Hui's poisoning-and-suicide branch while retaining both as post-wedding coercion.
