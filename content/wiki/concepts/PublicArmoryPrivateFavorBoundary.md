## Definition

武库公器私赏边界 / public armory private favor boundary is the principle that state weapons, armor, command symbols, and publicly funded military stores belong to public defense and office authority, not to a ruler's private gift economy.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-587-shui-hui-ba-wuqi-dang-liwu-songgei-airen-llt5femzyvgmi2yldvvbefj-0e9t|Hanji 587]] creates the concept through [[WuJianglongWesternHan|武将龙]]'s objection to [[HanAidi|汉哀帝]] repeatedly sending officials into the armory to choose weapons for [[DongXian|董贤]] and Dong-linked households. Wu Jianglong's argument is institutional: weapons produced and maintained from public funds are not equivalent to jewels, clothing, or household gifts.

The episode sharpens the boundary by distinguishing fiscal channels. The Da Sinong funds state activity, while the Shaofu supports imperial-household needs. A ruler may have private expenses, but moving armory weapons through favorite reward collapses military supply, public finance, and private affection into one channel.

The concept also has a status-order side. Wu Jianglong invokes axes, halberds, frontier weapons, and the classical rule that ministers' households should not hold arms, then compares the problem to ritual overreach by powerful houses. Weapons are therefore not neutral objects: they signal command, coercive capacity, and rank. Giving them as love gifts makes public force look personally owned.

## Key Claims

- Publicly funded military stores are institutionally different from private treasures or household gifts.
- The public-private boundary depends partly on fiscal source: Da Sinong-funded resources should not be spent as Shaofu-style private favor.
- Weapons and armor carry command and coercive symbolism, so private possession by favorite households can signal political overreach.
- Classical and ritual precedent can be used to explain why private accumulation of public force damages rank order.
- The boundary applies beyond weapons when official property or dependents are moved into court-connected private hands below public value.
- Remonstrance can identify a clear public-resource violation yet still fail when the ruler protects private affection.

## Evidence

- Repeated armory gifts: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-587-shui-hui-ba-wuqi-dang-liwu-songgei-airen-llt5femzyvgmi2yldvvbefj-0e9t|Hanji 587]] says Aidi sends officials to the armory more than ten times to select weapons for Dong Xian and related households.
- Public-resource claim: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-587-shui-hui-ba-wuqi-dang-liwu-songgei-airen-llt5femzyvgmi2yldvvbefj-0e9t|Hanji 587]] says Wu Jianglong argues armory weapons belong to the realm and are funded by public finance.
- Fiscal-channel distinction: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-587-shui-hui-ba-wuqi-dang-liwu-songgei-airen-llt5femzyvgmi2yldvvbefj-0e9t|Hanji 587]] says Wu Jianglong distinguishes Da Sinong court finance from Shaofu imperial-household finance.
- Command-symbol precedent: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-587-shui-hui-ba-wuqi-dang-liwu-songgei-airen-llt5femzyvgmi2yldvvbefj-0e9t|Hanji 587]] says axes, halberds, frontier weapons, and the rule against ministers' households holding arms are used to mark legitimate military use.
- Official-slave price dispute: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-587-shui-hui-ba-wuqi-dang-liwu-songgei-airen-llt5femzyvgmi2yldvvbefj-0e9t|Hanji 587]] says Fu Taihou's underpriced purchase of Zhijinwu official slaves prompts a second fair-price remonstrance.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is currently grounded in one source note. The source does not provide the exact weapon types, quantities, legal title of the armory goods, full slave-sale procedure, or a later policy outcome. It should therefore be used as a focused boundary concept rather than as a general theory of all military procurement or all imperial gifts.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Hanji 587's armory-weapons and official-slave purchase cases.
