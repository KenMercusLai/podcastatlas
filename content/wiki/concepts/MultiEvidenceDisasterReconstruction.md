# Multi-Evidence Disaster Reconstruction

## Definition

Multi-evidence disaster reconstruction combines literary testimony, archaeology, human and animal remains, objects, environmental traces, and physical science to infer how a catastrophe unfolded while retaining the limits of each evidence type.

## Current Synthesis

The Vesuvius episodes derive their vividness from convergence without treating every detail as equally secure. Pliny's letters supply cloud shape, sensory experience, family action, darkness, and broad timing; deposits and ruins supply hazard sequence and structural effects; bodies and possessions make movement and social difference visible; seasonal food, clothing, wine activity, manuscripts, graffiti, and a disputed coin test the transmitted date; volcanology explains column collapse, surge, and flow. The method is strongest when sources constrain one another and when negative findings or later scans are allowed to remove an attractive story.

## Key Claims

- Literary testimony can preserve sensory sequence and human response but may be retrospective, interested, transmitted imperfectly, and spatially limited.
- Archaeological context can recover behavior and timing only when objects, bodies, buildings, and deposits are interpreted together.
- Scientific hazard models can explain physical mechanisms without independently proving every narrated scene or motive.
- Conflicts among evidence types, such as the August date versus autumnal finds, are productive signals for qualification and revision.
- Representative individual traces can humanize catastrophe without licensing certainty about all identities, intentions, or movements.
- A suggestive date or identity claim must retain missing fields, preservation limits, and competing explanations rather than borrowing certainty from the wider reconstruction.

## Evidence

- Literary contribution: [[355-roman-apocalypse-pompeii-79-ad-glt6664993493]] uses Pliny the Younger's letters for the cloud, Misenum evacuation, darkness, and his uncle's rescue.
- Material contribution: [[355-roman-apocalypse-pompeii-79-ad-glt6664993493]] cites buildings, victims, clothing, food, tools, sacred objects, shackles, and animal remains.
- Scientific contribution: [[355-roman-apocalypse-pompeii-79-ad-glt6664993493]] organizes the event through pumice fall, surge, and column collapse.
- Evidentiary conflict: [[355-roman-apocalypse-pompeii-79-ad-glt6664993493]] sets the transmitted 24 August date against pomegranates, wine, heavy clothing, and a 17 October inscription.
- Revision and missing data: [[16-pompeii-glt3349011345]] adds manuscript variants, the graffito's absent year, an unreadable coin legend, cast reidentification, and disputed Christian and Jewish traces.
- Cumulative biography: [[16-pompeii-glt3349011345]] shows rental, games, and tomb inscriptions gradually reconstructing [[GaiusAlleiusNigidiusMaius]] without producing a complete life.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Convergence can still produce an overconfident story if dating, provenance, preservation, missing metadata, terminology, representativeness, or literary shaping is ignored. These episodes are secondary narrative syntheses, so victim totals, exact chronology, body identities, particular escape stories, religious presence, and causal interpretations require direct-source checking before being treated as settled scholarship.
