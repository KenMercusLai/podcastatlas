# California Corporate Emissions Disclosure

## Definition
California corporate emissions disclosure is the source-described state requirement for large public and private companies doing business in California to publish greenhouse-gas information, subject to different legal treatment for company and purchased-energy emissions than for supply-chain emissions.

## Current Synthesis
[[tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128]] presents the requirement as a response to incomplete voluntary reporting. Companies doing at least $1 billion in business in [[California]] must report greenhouse-gas emissions and emissions associated with purchased energy, reportedly bringing about 2,500 firms into scope, including AI and data-center companies that had not previously issued carbon reports.

The disclosure regime is partial and legally contested. In the episode's account, a federal appeals court upheld the initial reports shortly before their deadline but ruled that more detailed supply-chain reports due the following year were illegal. The surviving layer may improve company-level comparison and community visibility, yet it does not itself produce [[AIEmissionsAccounting|AI-specific workload attribution]] or prove that public pressure will reduce emissions.

## Key Claims

- Mandatory coverage can expose emissions from large private as well as public companies that voluntary reporting misses.
- A common reporting obligation can improve comparability only to the extent that methods and organizational boundaries are standardized.
- Direct and purchased-energy disclosure is less complete than supply-chain disclosure and does not isolate individual products or AI workloads.
- Public inventories can support researchers, communities, consultants, and sustainability platforms while also imposing compliance costs.
- Disclosure can create reputational pressure, but the source does not establish a causal reduction in emissions.
- Judicial review can preserve a core reporting layer while invalidating a broader one, making legal scope central to environmental visibility.

## Evidence

- Coverage and threshold - [[tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128]] reports a $1 billion business threshold and coverage of roughly 2,500 companies.
- AI-industry reach - [[tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128]] says [[OpenAI]], [[Anthropic]], and some major data-center firms had not previously published carbon reports.
- Community rationale - [[tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128]] records [[MaryNichols]]'s argument that communities need data on resource use and grid capacity.
- Inventory practice - [[tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128]] describes consultants combining travel, energy bills, trucking, and other operational records into more granular inventories.
- Litigation boundary - [[tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261127-1127-mp-tech-pod-128]] says initial reports survived appellate review while the following year's supply-chain reports were ruled illegal.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a brief structured episode summary, not the legislation, implementing regulations, judicial opinion, company filings, or an evaluation of compliance costs and emissions outcomes. The phrases used here follow the source rather than assigning statutory scope categories not stated in it. The number of covered companies, deadline posture, appeal options, disclosure histories, and legal holdings remain episode-scoped and may change through further litigation or implementation.

## What Changed

- Established California's large-company disclosure requirement as a distinct transparency mechanism for AI and data-center emissions.
- Separated the surviving direct and purchased-energy layer from the rejected supply-chain layer.
- Preserved the distinction between better disclosure, AI-specific attribution, and demonstrated emissions reduction.
