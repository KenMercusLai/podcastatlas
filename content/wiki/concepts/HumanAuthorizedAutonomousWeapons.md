# Human-Authorized Autonomous Weapons

## Definition

Human-authorized autonomous weapons is the governance pattern where AI and autonomy support sensing, classification, navigation, targeting context, or mission execution, while humans set mission intent, authorization thresholds, and responsibility boundaries.

## Current Synthesis

The naval version comes from the Saronic interview: autonomous vessels may use AI for perception and classification, but the guest says humans still set mission intent and approval thresholds. The Palantir-Anduril episode contributes the accountability version: [[TreyStephens|Trey Stephens]] argues that autonomous weapons are not wholly new and can remain accountable if a responsible human is answerable for how the system is used.

The new [[EmilMichael|Emil Michael]] source makes the boundary mission-dependent. He distinguishes defensive interception from operation near civilians and argues that hypersonic missile-defense timelines may be too short for ordinary human deliberation. The concept is therefore not a claim that autonomous weapons are automatically legitimate or that one approval pattern fits every mission. It records an executive governance argument: humans must set authority, mission, and risk thresholds in advance, while the meaningfulness of real-time review falls as speed and scale increase.

## Key Claims

- Human authorization is presented as a design and policy boundary, not just a public-relations phrase.
- AI can support perception, classification, routing, and mission execution while humans define allowed use.
- Accountability depends on traceable human responsibility for system operation, not only moment-by-moment manual control.
- The boundary becomes harder under high-intensity conflict, where speed, jamming, and mass may pressure human review.
- The concept extends [[HumanJudgmentUnderAI]] into defense autonomy and weapons governance.
- Defensive urgency and civilian exposure can justify different automation thresholds without eliminating accountability.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The page preserves source-scoped executive claims rather than a legal conclusion. It does not resolve international humanitarian law, civilian-harm risk, accidental escalation, command-responsibility disputes, or whether pre-authorized thresholds count as meaningful human control when decisions happen at machine speed. Michael's examples do not establish the reliability or actual deployment of the systems he describes.
