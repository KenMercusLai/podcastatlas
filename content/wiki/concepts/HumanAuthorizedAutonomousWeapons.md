# Human-Authorized Autonomous Weapons

## Definition

Human-authorized autonomous weapons is the governance pattern where AI and autonomy support sensing, classification, navigation, targeting context, or mission execution, while humans set mission intent, authorization thresholds, and responsibility boundaries.

## Current Synthesis

The naval version comes from the Saronic interview: autonomous vessels may use AI for perception and classification, but the guest says humans still set mission intent and approval thresholds. The Palantir-Anduril episode contributes the accountability version: [[TreyStephens|Trey Stephens]] argues that autonomous weapons are not wholly new and can remain accountable if a responsible human is answerable for how the system is used.

The concept is therefore not a claim that autonomous weapons are automatically legitimate. It records an executive governance argument: autonomy can increase speed and scale, but the system still needs a traceable human authority who defines use, accepts risk, and can be held responsible for illegal or wrongful operation.

## Key Claims

- Human authorization is presented as a design and policy boundary, not just a public-relations phrase.
- AI can support perception, classification, routing, and mission execution while humans define allowed use.
- Accountability depends on traceable human responsibility for system operation, not only moment-by-moment manual control.
- The boundary becomes harder under high-intensity conflict, where speed, jamming, and mass may pressure human review.
- The concept extends [[HumanJudgmentUnderAI]] into defense autonomy and weapons governance.

## Evidence

- Naval autonomy boundary: [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-saronic-founders-autonomous-warships-chinas-230x-advantage-swarms-of-robot-ships-42334230]] says AI can support classification and mission execution while people set intent and thresholds.
- Accountability claim: [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-the-companies-changing-warfare-forever-palantir-and-anduril-execs-on-drones-ai-and-the-future-of-war-40743095]] has Stephens cite existing autonomous defensive systems and argue that a responsible human can remain accountable for operation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The page preserves source-scoped executive claims rather than a legal conclusion. It does not resolve international humanitarian law, civilian-harm risk, accidental escalation, command-responsibility disputes, or whether human authorization remains meaningful when decisions must happen at machine speed.
