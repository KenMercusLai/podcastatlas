# Brain–Computer Interface

## Definition
A brain–computer interface records neural or related physiological activity and translates selected patterns into commands for a computer, prosthesis, or other device, sometimes with stimulation back into the nervous system.

## Current Synthesis
The Neuralink interview and its condensation present the most grounded implanted use as assistive computer control. Preserved motor-cortex activity can be mapped to cursor and keyboard actions for a person with severe spinal-cord injury, with the decoder and user adapting through feedback. This is narrower than restoring natural movement, unrestricted thought reading, or general cognitive enhancement.

The newest episode broadens the implementation landscape. [[BrainCo]] uses residual signals in an amputee’s arm to control a bionic hand without brain surgery, while Chinese implant developers pursue clinical trials and consumer devices target concentration or sleep. The combined evidence therefore separates cortical implants, peripheral-signal prosthetics, and consumer neurotechnology by signal source, invasiveness, use case, and evidentiary burden rather than treating them as one mature product class.

## Key Claims
- Preserved neural intention can support assistive control even when ordinary motor pathways are interrupted.
- Decoder performance is co-adaptive, with software and user learning through repeated feedback.
- Initial digital control is narrower and more tractable than natural-limb restoration or unrestricted thought decoding.
- Neural-interface products span implanted, non-invasive, and residual-peripheral-signal architectures.
- Medical and consumer interfaces require different evidence, safety, and adoption judgments.
- Industrial ecosystems can accelerate trials and lower costs without proving frontier leadership or clinical value.
- Silent speech, memory enhancement, drone-swarm control, and generalized thought sharing remain speculative here.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The two Neuralink notes derive from one April 2023 company-insider interview and are not independent confirmation. The newest episode is a journalistic overview, not a comparative clinical review. None of these sources establishes current device performance, long-term durability, comparative safety, broad eligibility, reimbursement, consumer efficacy, or a change in global technological leadership.

## What Changed
- Added non-surgical prosthetic, consumer-device, and Chinese implant-trial pathways.
- Distinguished interface architectures and adoption contexts rather than treating all neurotechnology as cortical implantation.
- Preserved the boundary between demonstrated assistive aims and speculative augmentation.
