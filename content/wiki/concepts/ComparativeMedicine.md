# Comparative Medicine / 比较医学

## Definition
Comparative medicine studies similarities and differences across human and animal disease so that mechanisms, diagnostics, and treatments can inform one another without assuming species equivalence.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents comparative medicine as a way around professional silos. Naturally occurring cancers in dogs and cats can resemble human tumors in selected biological features, while human oncology can expose veterinarians to mature imaging, radiotherapy, ablation, and interventional techniques. Exchange can therefore generate hypotheses, training routes, and practical tools in both directions.

The value depends on disciplined translation. Similarity in one tumor feature does not erase differences in anatomy, body scale, metabolism, tumor spectrum, breed structure, equipment access, case volume, endpoints, or acceptable burden. Comparative medicine is strongest when it identifies what can be tested next and what must be revalidated, not when it treats a human guideline as a veterinary instruction sheet.

## Key Claims
- Human and veterinary medicine can share mechanisms, imaging concepts, procedural knowledge, and naturally occurring disease models.
- Cross-disciplinary contact can reduce duplicated learning and expose a young specialty to more mature techniques.
- Tumor analogy is feature-specific; resemblance does not make diseases or treatment responses identical.
- Species anatomy, size, physiology, breed structure, case volume, equipment, and outcome evidence constrain transfer.
- A human indication threshold cannot be converted into a veterinary threshold by simple proportional scaling.
- Comparative translation should produce hypotheses and validation plans rather than borrowed certainty.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source provides illustrative comparisons rather than a comprehensive account of comparative oncology or One Health research. Each proposed analogy needs disease-specific evidence, and this page does not establish clinical equivalence or authorize cross-species use of a drug, device, or procedure.
