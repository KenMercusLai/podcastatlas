## Definition

推恩令 / Favor Extension Order is a Western Han centralization mechanism that permits vassal kings to distribute territory and titles among additional sons, converting repeated inheritance grants into gradual fragmentation of concentrated royal power.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-279-tuienling-weishenme-bei-chengwei-qiangu-diyi-yangmou-lk0wssrgq0ac9uapfxc2pv9fn7hh|Hanji 279]] presents the measure as a public, incentive-compatible strategy rather than a hidden trick. The court describes division as extending a king's favor to sons excluded by eldest-son succession. Those sons gain territory and rank, while each division reduces the parent kingdom's capacity to act as a unified regional power.

The episode's stronger point is about preconditions. [[LiuHeng|Emperor Wen]]'s gradual partitioning and [[HanJingdi|Emperor Jing]]'s defeat of the Seven States rebellion have already reduced royal autonomy, appointment power, revenue control, and military leverage. [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] can therefore make refusal politically costly. The policy works through aligned family incentives and benevolent language, but only after the central court has acquired credible enforcement power.

A Jingdi-era precursor appears in the posthumous settlement of [[LiuWuLiangKingWesternHan|梁孝王刘武]]'s kingdom. Jingdi grants kingships to all five sons, including [[LiuPengliJidongKingWesternHan|刘彭离]], while dividing the former Liang concentration. The episode says this both consoles [[EmpressDowagerDouWesternHan|窦太后]] and weakens a unified kingdom. This is not the 推恩令 itself: it is a one-house succession settlement, but it demonstrates before Wudi how multiple favorable grants can disperse royal power without presenting the act as naked confiscation. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-242-cong-chengxiang-dao-mianguan-zhouyafu-fanle-zhichang-daji-2-lvwwjv0w-widjjzafmweqdoepydg|Hanji 242]]

This also locates the order inside the lifecycle of [[SameSurnameKingEnfeoffment|同姓王分封]]. Large Liu-family kingdoms initially solve a founding-era trust and territorial-control problem; they later become autonomy risks; partition and rollback solve that risk at the cost of leaving royal kin less able to defend the dynasty. The order is thus neither a self-sufficient masterstroke nor a neutral family reform, but one stage in a longer centralization tradeoff.

## Key Claims

- The measure fragments concentrated royal territory through repeated grants to additional sons rather than a single openly punitive confiscation.
- Jingdi's post-Liu-Wu division of Liang is a bounded precursor in logic: multiple honors can simultaneously disperse a kingdom.
- Its public justification and distributive effect point in different directions: family “favor” legitimizes a process that strengthens the center.
- Non-heir sons become internal beneficiaries, so the royal house does not face the policy as a unitary opponent.
- Refusal becomes costly only because earlier centralization has made imperial command credible.
- The Rebellion of the Seven States is a precondition in this source's account: military defeat and administrative rollback precede the policy's safer implementation.
- The measure resolves part of the autonomy problem while contributing to the later weakness of royal kin as a dynastic defense layer.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The current bounded evidence consists of two compact popular-history episodes, not a complete legal or administrative history. Hanji 242's Liang division resembles the later policy in effect but differs in legal form, scope, timing, and immediate trigger; its grant details and anti-concentration intent require primary-text comparison. Hanji 279's “千古第一阳谋,” “无解,” and universal princely welcome are rhetorical claims rather than demonstrated cross-kingdom findings. Its summary does not name the proposing minister, so [[ZhuFuYan|主父偃]]'s authorship comes from adjacent sources and cannot be treated as explicit wording here. The exact edict, date, title-creation process, implementation differences, resistance, and long-term results require comparison. Most importantly, design alone does not explain success: coercive superiority and prior rollback remain necessary parts of the causal account.

## What Changed

- Added the five-way division of Liang as a Jingdi-era precursor in logic, not as the 推恩令 itself.
- Clarified how visible family generosity can coexist with anti-concentration effects.
- Preserved prior coercive centralization as a necessary condition of the later systematic policy.
- Preserved the long-run tradeoff between limiting rebellion and weakening royal-house defense.
