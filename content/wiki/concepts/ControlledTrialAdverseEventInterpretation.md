# Controlled-Trial Adverse-Event Interpretation

## Definition

Controlled-trial adverse-event interpretation is the practice of comparing outcome rates between assigned treatment and control groups before inferring that events observed after an intervention were caused by it.

## Current Synthesis

[[the-rfk-jr-problem-the-rfk-jr-problem|The RFK Jr. Problem]] uses the [[RotaTeq]] trial to distinguish temporal sequence from causal evidence. Illnesses and deaths will occur in any large infant cohort; the relevant safety question is whether they occur more often, more severely, or in a pattern meaningfully different from placebo.

The same boundary applies to passive reporting. A VAERS report is a signal that an event followed vaccination and may warrant investigation, not a standalone causal verdict. Randomization, comparator rates, timing, outcome definition, statistical uncertainty, biological plausibility, and post-market follow-up play different roles and should not be collapsed into one count.

## Key Claims

- Events after treatment are not automatically events caused by treatment.
- A control group estimates the background rate that would occur without the tested intervention.
- Serious-adverse-event interpretation requires comparative rates and uncertainty, not a list from one study arm.
- Passive reports can generate safety signals without establishing incidence or causation by themselves.
- Efficacy and safety are separate comparisons that must both preserve denominators and outcome definitions.

## Evidence

### Randomized comparison

- [[the-rfk-jr-problem-the-rfk-jr-problem]] reports an approximately 68,000-infant RotaTeq trial and no meaningful serious-adverse-event difference between vaccine and placebo groups.

### Efficacy contrast

- [[the-rfk-jr-problem-the-rfk-jr-problem]] highlights one diarrhea or gastroenteritis case in the vaccine group versus 51 in the placebo group.

### Passive-reporting boundary

- [[the-rfk-jr-problem-the-rfk-jr-problem]] explains that a VAERS report records temporal sequence but does not alone demonstrate vaccine causation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Randomized trials may be too small or too short to detect rare, delayed, subgroup-specific, or post-licensure harms. A non-significant group difference is not proof of zero risk, and passive surveillance can reveal signals missed before approval. Proper interpretation therefore joins trial comparison with active surveillance, clinical review, replication, and transparent uncertainty.
