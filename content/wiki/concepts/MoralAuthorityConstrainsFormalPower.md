## Definition

道德威望约束权力 / moral authority constrains formal power is the pattern in which a person with less coercive or institutional power induces a ruler or superior to observe standards, accept status limits, or seek correction because that person's principled reputation makes disregard costly or shameful.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-294-1-wudi-bu-pa-quanchen-bu-pa-xiongnu-weihe-dudu-pa-ta-ljpqul0y4ep-pyufaow-vsqwp9ir|Hanji 294-1]] grounds the concept through [[JiAnWesternHan|汲黯]]. Ji does not command [[WeiQingHanGeneral|卫青]] or [[HanWudi|汉武帝]], yet he refuses courtwide flattery toward Wei, defends an equal-standing guest salute, and is said to make the emperor attend to cap, posture, and audience form.

The mechanism is not simple rudeness. Ji's conduct is presented as stable across targets and tied to a reputation for public principle; Wei therefore treats him as worthy and seeks advice rather than reading the refusal to bow as personal rebellion. Wudi's avoidance likewise shows a behavioral constraint rather than a transfer of office or force: the emperor can dismiss Ji, but cannot comfortably make Ji witness imperial impropriety.

This differs from popularity or generic prestige. Moral authority becomes constraining when observers believe the person will apply a credible standard consistently and when violating that standard would expose their own conduct. It can alter etiquette and access without guaranteeing policy victory, safety, or institutional reform.

## Key Claims

- Formal rank and coercive capacity do not exhaust political influence; credible moral judgment can alter how superiors behave.
- Consistency across powerful targets makes principled nondeference more legible as integrity than as selective disrespect.
- A secure superior can convert nonflattery into useful counsel, as the episode portrays Wei Qing doing with Ji An.
- Ruler self-regulation may appear first in dress, posture, greeting, or audience procedure rather than in explicit policy concession.
- Reputation-based constraint remains personal and fragile when it depends on one critic rather than protected institutions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept currently rests on one short popular-history source whose transcript contains name errors and colloquial paraphrase. The anecdotes do not prove Wudi's private emotion, that Ji alone caused the etiquette difference, or that ritual caution translated into consistent policy acceptance. “Fear” may compress respect, embarrassment, political tolerance, retrospective moral praise, and concern about criticism. The concept therefore names a bounded behavioral mechanism, not a claim that moral standing reliably defeats coercive power or protects every critic.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Ji An's differentiated effects on Wei Qing and Han Wudi.
- Separated reputation-based behavioral constraint from formal command, popularity, and guaranteed policy success.
- Treated audience etiquette as evidence of self-regulation while preserving uncertainty about Wudi's emotion and motive.
