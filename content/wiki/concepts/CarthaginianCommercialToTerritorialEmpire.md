# Carthaginian Commercial-to-Territorial Empire / 迦太基商业至领土帝国

## Definition

The Carthaginian commercial-to-territorial empire is the episode's model of expansion from Phoenician trading settlements, maritime routes, alliances, and market coercion toward direct control of land, cities, agricultural resources, and fortified frontiers.

## Current Synthesis

Early [[Carthage]] grows through location and connection. Its harbors sit near routes linking Phoenicia to Spain and Sicily to North Africa; settlements across Sardinia and the western Mediterranean function as trading posts, forts, and markets backed by force. Weakening Tyrian control allows this western network to become increasingly Carthaginian without requiring immediate uniform annexation.

Two developments deepen territorial power. In Africa, agricultural transformation makes the hinterland a productive base of grain, orchards, irrigation, estates, livestock, vines, and olives. In Sicily, competition with Syracuse and other Greek cities turns older Phoenician settlements into a military frontier. By the fourth century BC, the absorption of Panormus and Motya's sphere and the construction of heavily fortified Lilybaeum show a move from protecting exchange to holding territory directly.

Phoenician identity helps bind previously separate settlements into a Carthaginian system, while coinage and religious symbols connect imperial authority to shared origin. The sequence is not linear or invulnerable: defeat at Himera, Syracuse's destruction of Motya, and Agathocles' African invasion expose reversals, yet reconstruction and renewed prosperity show resilience.

## Key Claims

- Strategic geography and harbors made trade-route control the initial base of Carthaginian power.
- Trading settlements combined exchange with coercion rather than constituting peaceful commerce alone.
- African agriculture converted maritime reach into a more self-sustaining territorial resource base.
- Greek expansion in Sicily transformed a commercial sphere into a defended military frontier.
- Fortification and absorption of western Sicilian settlements mark movement toward direct rule.
- Shared Phoenician identity supplied political glue for incorporating older Phoenician communities.
- Repeated defeat and recovery show an uneven transition rather than inevitable expansion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Commercial and territorial power overlap rather than forming clean consecutive stages. Carthage used violence early, continued trade after annexation, and governed different places through different arrangements. Settlement chronology, demographic scale, causal claims about Tyre and silver, exact jurisdiction, and the political meaning of coin symbols remain source-scoped. The model should not imply that all Phoenician settlements passively accepted Carthaginian authority.

## What Changed

- Created a bounded model connecting commerce, agriculture, identity, fortification, and territorial rule.
