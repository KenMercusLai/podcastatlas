# Carceral Labor–Parole Conflict

## Definition

Carceral labor–parole conflict is the potential institutional incentive problem created when incarcerated people are judged safe and reliable enough for economically useful work while release would remove that labor from the prison system.

## Current Synthesis

The Alabama episode joins compelled work, disciplinary consequences, very low pay, outside job assignments, and an estimated large annual labor value to a reported parole anomaly: people classified as safe enough for community work allegedly had lower parole chances than people at higher custody levels.

That pattern is consistent with a retention incentive but does not prove that labor value caused an individual denial. A sound analysis must separate statutory eligibility, offense category, sentence structure, hearing opposition, disciplinary history, risk assessment, board discretion, and labor placement before inferring motive.

## Key Claims

- Community work can reveal operational trust without guaranteeing legal release eligibility.
- Compelled labor and punishment for refusal weaken claims that work participation is freely chosen.
- Economic benefit creates a plausible institutional conflict when release removes reliable workers.
- Aggregate classification and parole patterns cannot establish the cause of an individual decision.
- Labor valuation, wages, custody level, parole eligibility, and release outcomes require linked records.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source does not supply the underlying labor-accounting method, contracts, wage records, prisoner-level parole dataset, eligibility controls, board reasoning, or current court findings. The $450 million estimate, 8 percent parole rate, and causal interpretation remain episode-scoped.

## What Changed

- Established the concept while separating a plausible structural incentive from proof of decision-level causation.
