# Monarchical Deposition Legitimacy

## Definition

Monarchical deposition legitimacy is the problem of turning a ruler's loss of coercive power into an accepted transfer of sovereignty when anointing, hereditary right, forced signatures, captivity, and rival government make removal legally and theologically contestable.

## Current Synthesis

Mary's fall shows that practical deposition can precede legitimate deposition. Her authority eroded through scandal, noble defection, loss of access to her heir, military desertion, and capture before documents at Loch Leven formally transferred the crown to the infant James and the regency to [[JamesStewartEarlOfMoray|Moray]]. Because the documents followed threats of harsher confinement, drowning, and death, form did not erase coercion.

The episode also shows why neighboring monarchies could split principle from policy. [[ElizabethI|Elizabeth I]] opposed rebellion against an anointed ruler and disliked the prospect of subjects judging sovereigns, yet restoring [[MaryQueenOfScots|Mary, Queen of Scots]] risked Scottish civil war and English insecurity. Inquiry, disputed evidence, and detention offered a third path: withhold recognition of innocence and freedom without clearly endorsing either deposition or execution.

## Key Claims

- A monarch can lose operational authority before a rival regime secures uncontested legitimacy.
- Abdication documents do not settle legitimacy when imprisonment and threats make consent defective.
- Control of the heir, armed force, capital, castles, and elite allegiance can make deposition effective before it is normatively accepted.
- Anointed monarchy makes deposition a precedent problem for neighboring rulers as well as a domestic succession issue.
- Ambiguous inquiry and indefinite custody can contain a deposed ruler when restoration, acquittal, and execution all carry unacceptable costs.
- Disputed evidence can stabilize a replacement regime politically without producing a decisive legal verdict.

## Evidence

- Authority before form: [[589-mary-queen-of-scots-downfall-part-6-glt8999516788]] follows the refusal to release James, Edinburgh Castle's defection, desertion at Carberry Hill, and Mary's custody before abdication.
- Coerced transfer: [[589-mary-queen-of-scots-downfall-part-6-glt8999516788]] reports threats at Loch Leven before Mary signed the abdication and regency instruments.
- Failed restoration: [[589-mary-queen-of-scots-downfall-part-6-glt8999516788]] shows Mary's escape, rallying army, Langside defeat, and flight to England.
- Neighboring-monarch dilemma: [[589-mary-queen-of-scots-downfall-part-6-glt8999516788]] contrasts Elizabeth's horror at deposition with her refusal to enter Scottish civil war.
- Containment by ambiguity: [[589-mary-queen-of-scots-downfall-part-6-glt8999516788]] links the [[CasketLetters]], managed inquiry, adjournment, and continuing detention.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is currently grounded in one interpretive podcast account of Mary's fall. It does not establish that every deposition follows the same sequence or that coerced abdication is always ineffective; recognition, institutions, religion, military control, and later settlement vary. The reported threats, pregnancy, sexual coercion, letter authenticity, and actors' private motives remain source-scoped. Elizabeth's principled objection also coexisted with strategic preference for Moray's government.

## What Changed

- Established the distinction between practical removal, coerced legal form, and durable legitimacy.
