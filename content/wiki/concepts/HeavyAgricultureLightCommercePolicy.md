## Definition

Heavy agriculture and light commerce policy / 重农轻商政策 is an agrarian statecraft pattern that elevates farming as the favored productive base while lowering merchants' legal, political, or social status.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 179 introduces the concept through an early [[WesternHanDynasty|西汉]] governing move. The episode says Gaozu eighth-year policy restricted merchants' clothing, weapons, vehicles, and status display, making commerce a managed and degraded occupation relative to farming.

That source's interpretation is skeptical of simple suppression. It argues that commercial behavior is difficult to stop by decree; when official policy marks merchants as low-status but still needs goods, money, and exchange, trade may continue through official-merchant collusion or forms of "official commerce" instead of disappearing.

Hanji 400-2 adds a sharper Ming daily-life case. In [[ZhuYuanzhang|朱元璋]]'s [[MingDynasty|明朝]], the episode says farmers could wear fabrics denied to merchants, and that if one household member engaged in business, the whole family could lose access to higher-status fabrics. The policy is therefore not only economic preference; it becomes [[StatusCodedEverydayRegulation|身份编码式日常管制]] that turns merchant identity into a visible household stigma.

Hanji 396-5 adds the broadest economic critique. It argues that merchants supply [[MerchantExchangeInfrastructure|exchange infrastructure]] by lowering transport, storage, and transaction costs, so treating them as lowly misunderstands their function. The episode also adds a political-economy consequence: when wealth is secure only through office or official access, anti-merchant hierarchy feeds [[PowerDominatedWealthInsecurity|power-dominated wealth insecurity]], political speculation, weak reinvestment, and official corruption.

## Key Claims

- The policy raises agriculture as the favored productive base while lowering merchants' social and political standing.
- Restricting merchant display does not necessarily reduce exchange; it can drive commerce into official channels or collusive arrangements.
- Anti-merchant ideology can slow commercial development if it confuses moral hierarchy with economic function.
- Ming fabric restrictions show that anti-merchant hierarchy can be enforced through family-level status coding, not only occupational law.
- Merchants can be devalued even though they lower trade, storage, and circulation costs for producers and buyers.
- Anti-merchant hierarchy can push wealth-seeking toward political access when office is safer than commercial property.
- These sources are bounded examples rather than a complete account of all Chinese commercial regulation.

## Evidence

- Early Han merchant status restriction: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-179-lishishang-heqin-zenme-laide-lslks-ovbomneornk4j-q6-ns0x1|Hanji 179]] says merchants' clothing, weapons, vehicles, and status display were restricted under Liu Bang's early Han.
- Suppression limit: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-179-lishishang-heqin-zenme-laide-lslks-ovbomneornk4j-q6-ns0x1|Hanji 179]] argues that commerce remains difficult to stop and may shift into official-merchant collusion.
- Ming fabric hierarchy: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-400-2-yidai-qiong-shidai-qiong-nandao-qiongren-jiu-gai-daidai-qiong-lluvrlqzbzx4bb_aatfmtyqibdym|Hanji 400-2]] says farmers and merchants were assigned different permitted fabrics.
- Household stigma: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-400-2-yidai-qiong-shidai-qiong-nandao-qiongren-jiu-gai-daidai-qiong-lluvrlqzbzx4bb_aatfmtyqibdym|Hanji 400-2]] says a farming household with a business-engaged member could be denied fabrics otherwise available to farmers.
- Merchant function: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-396-5-shui-zai-biandi-shangren-youxiehua-butubukuai-liwmisetjnlvfzarfmzpqq4-wdsq|Hanji 396-5]] argues that merchants reduce transport, storage, and transaction costs and make circulation cheaper for both sellers and buyers.
- Wealth-security consequence: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-396-5-shui-zai-biandi-shangren-youxiehua-butubukuai-liwmisetjnlvfzarfmzpqq4-wdsq|Hanji 396-5]] says power-dominated wealth rules make political office and access more attractive than ordinary commercial reinvestment.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The page does not claim that all commerce was uniformly suppressed or that merchants lacked wealth. Hanji 179 explicitly says commerce is difficult to stop by decree, while Hanji 400-2 focuses on status and visible discrimination rather than market mechanics. Hanji 396-5 makes a broad comparative economic-history argument about China, Europe, GDP, and the Macartney embassy; those claims remain source-scoped until supported by more specialized sources. Exact law, enforcement, regional variation, and periodization remain open.
