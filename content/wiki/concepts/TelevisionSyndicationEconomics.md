# Television Syndication Economics

## Definition

Television syndication economics is the secondary market in which completed programs are licensed to local stations, cable channels, international outlets, or later digital services separately from their original network broadcast.

## Current Synthesis

The [[SeinfeldTVSeries|Seinfeld]] case shows why a program can become more valuable after enough episodes exist to support repeated scheduling. Original broadcasts finance production and establish audience demand; syndication then sells a proven library across buyers, territories, and time windows. Because the episodes already exist, later revenue can carry much lower incremental production cost.

The upside depends on rights, not merely popularity. Creators and independent producers participate only if their contracts preserve backend claims, and station or platform demand depends on a catalog's ability to attract or retain viewers. Syndication therefore joins creative durability, episode volume, distribution competition, and ownership structure.

## Key Claims

- Original broadcast and syndication are distinct markets with different buyers and revenue waterfalls.
- A sufficiently large episode library makes repeated scheduling easier for distributors.
- Proven audience demand can let reruns compete with newly produced programming.
- Backend participants capture upside only when rights survive beyond salary and first-run fees.
- Successive windows can renew value, but private contracts make precise attribution difficult.

## Evidence

- Market separation: [[seinfeld-ca0df1168651e0a51be7d6012432411f]] distinguishes NBC first-run broadcasting from local station, cable, international, and streaming licensing.
- Library scale: [[seinfeld-ca0df1168651e0a51be7d6012432411f]] says roughly 100 episodes traditionally made a show more attractive to syndicators needing repeatable inventory.
- Buyer demand: [[seinfeld-ca0df1168651e0a51be7d6012432411f]] reports a first five-year sale to 224 U.S. stations for more than $500 million, excluding cable.
- Rights waterfall: [[seinfeld-ca0df1168651e0a51be7d6012432411f]] explains that creator and producer backend economics differed from original-production salaries.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Seinfeld is an extreme success case rather than a base rate. Episode count alone does not create demand, and a valuable first window does not guarantee later relevance. The station count, deal value, ownership shares, and lifetime proceeds are episode-attributed because private contracts are not supplied.

## What Changed

- Added syndication as a distinct secondary market linking episode volume, demand, ownership, and repeated licensing.
