# Crowd Sovereignty and Representative Conflict

## Definition

Crowd sovereignty and representative conflict is the unresolved rivalry between elected institutions claiming to speak for the nation and mobilized citizens claiming authority to enforce the people's will directly.

## Current Synthesis

The October Days show that popular sovereignty did not specify one authorized channel. The National Assembly claimed national representation, yet marchers entered its chamber, demanded action, and compelled the king and legislature to move within reach of Paris. Direct pressure could break royal obstruction and obtain bread promises, but its threats and killings also made representatives and the monarchy dependent on armed street power.

## Key Claims

- Declaring popular sovereignty does not by itself settle who may act in the people's name.
- Direct mobilization can correct institutional blockage while simultaneously constraining representative independence.
- Control of political geography matters because proximity enables surveillance, petition, intimidation, and rapid mobilization.
- Revolutionary authorities may recreate coercive policing when old repression loses legitimacy but disorder persists.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- "The crowd" was not a unified actor: market women, armed men, National Guardsmen, activists, and onlookers had differing roles and aims.
- Institutional representation was itself restricted and unsettled, so independence from crowds did not automatically equal democratic legitimacy.
- Direct action's effectiveness does not resolve whether its coercive methods were legitimate or representative.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to separate the principle of popular sovereignty from the institutional question of who may enact it.
