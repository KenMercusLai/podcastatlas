# Australian Constitutional Crisis of 1975

## Definition

The Australian constitutional crisis of 1975 was the confrontation in which the opposition-controlled Senate blocked government supply, Governor-General John Kerr dismissed Prime Minister Gough Whitlam, and Malcolm Fraser took office before winning the ensuing election.

## Current Synthesis

[[188-australian-prime-ministers-harold-holt-malcolm-fraser-part-2-glt4635125602]] presents the crisis as a collision of powers and legitimacy claims. [[MalcolmFraser|Malcolm Fraser]] used the Senate's formal capacity to block supply; [[GoughWhitlam|Gough Whitlam]] argued that an elected lower-house government should not be forced out that way; and [[JohnKerr|John Kerr]] used reserve powers to dismiss Whitlam as funds ran down. Each step could be described in legal or constitutional terms, but their combination broke political expectations and produced an exceptional transfer of executive authority.

The December election matters without retrospectively settling every dispute. Fraser's landslide established electoral authority for the replacement government, while critics could still argue that the path to that election violated democratic convention. The episode credits Whitlam and [[BobHawke|Bob Hawke]] with limiting escalation, making restraint part of the crisis outcome rather than proof that the institutions were never under strain.

## Key Claims

- An upper house's supply power can threaten a government even when that government retains lower-house legitimacy.
- Formal constitutional capacity and accepted convention are not identical.
- Reserve powers converted parliamentary deadlock into immediate prime-ministerial removal.
- Personal distrust and uncertainty between Whitlam and Kerr intensified institutional conflict.
- Fraser's later landslide settled officeholding but not the retrospective legitimacy debate.
- Elite restraint helped prevent constitutional conflict from becoming a broader civil crisis.

- Dismissal mechanism: [[188-australian-prime-ministers-harold-holt-malcolm-fraser-part-2-glt4635125602]] traces Whitlam's expectations, Kerr's action, and Fraser's immediate commissioning.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This account is a short narrative treatment, not a complete constitutional-law analysis. The episode says blocking supply was legal but does not exhaust the convention governing its use or Kerr's alternatives. CIA involvement remains conspiracy theory in this source, while electoral endorsement after the event cannot alone validate every preceding constitutional act.

## What Changed

- Created a mechanism-level account joining supply, reserve powers, legitimacy, and electoral aftermath.
- Distinguished legality from convention and subsequent electoral approval.
- Added political restraint as a factor in containing the crisis.
