# Guadalcanal Campaign

## Definition

The Guadalcanal Campaign was the Allied offensive begun in August 1942 to seize and hold the Japanese airfield in the southern Solomons, opening a prolonged contest over Henderson Field, sea access, reinforcement, and the conversion of limited resources into sustained combat power.

## Current Synthesis

[[dchha65-supernova-in-the-east-iv-dchha65-supernova-in-the-east-iv|Supernova in the East IV]] presents Guadalcanal as an improvised first offensive rather than the effortless exploitation of Midway. Army-Navy conflict produced a phased command compromise; Japan's construction of an airfield created urgency; and inexperienced American forces mounted a major amphibious operation before doctrine, unloading practice, night fighting, communications, and joint command were mature.

The opening contained sharply different experiences. Guadalcanal's unfinished airfield was taken with limited ground resistance, while nearby Tulagi cost heavily against defenders who rarely surrendered. The Battle of Savo Island then destroyed four Allied cruisers and helped drive covering and transport forces away before unloading was complete. Marines left around Henderson Field nevertheless prepared defenses and destroyed an understrength Japanese attack whose commander had underestimated their numbers.

## Key Claims

- Guadalcanal began through urgency and inter-service compromise before American material superiority was fully deployable.
- Capturing an airfield was easier than sustaining it under contested sea and air access.
- Tulagi revealed the high cost of eliminating small forces when surrender was rare.
- Savo Island exposed severe Allied weaknesses in night fighting, warning, communications, readiness, and unified command.
- Premature Japanese counterattack showed how early-war confidence and poor force estimates could turn aggressiveness into disaster.
- The campaign's opening converted Midway's carrier check into a longer struggle over logistics, air cover, and replacements.

## Evidence

- Strategic formation: [[dchha65-supernova-in-the-east-iv-dchha65-supernova-in-the-east-iv]] describes MacArthur's Rabaul proposal, King's Solomons route, Marshall's phased compromise, and the urgency created by Japanese airfield construction.
- Amphibious opening: [[dchha65-supernova-in-the-east-iv-dchha65-supernova-in-the-east-iv]] contrasts the lightly opposed Guadalcanal landing with near-total Japanese resistance on Tulagi and neighboring islands.
- Naval crisis: [[dchha65-supernova-in-the-east-iv-dchha65-supernova-in-the-east-iv]] describes Mikawa's night attack, the loss of Canberra and three American cruisers, and the withdrawal of support before full unloading.
- Henderson defense: [[dchha65-supernova-in-the-east-iv-dchha65-supernova-in-the-east-iv]] connects airfield preparation, perimeter defenses, Japanese underestimation, and the destruction of the first counterattack.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This source covers only the campaign's opening. It does not by itself establish the later naval sequence, final casualty balance, disease burden, reinforcement totals, or ultimate operational assessment. [[dchha66-supernova-in-the-east-v-dchha66-supernova-in-the-east-v|Supernova in the East V]] continues the campaign as a mutual siege governed by daylight air power, nighttime supply, hunger, disease, and shipping loss.

## What Changed

- Established Guadalcanal's opening as the bridge from Midway's carrier losses to a sustained contest over airfields, sea movement, unloading, command, and replacements.
