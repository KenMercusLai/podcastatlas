# First Party System

## Definition
The First Party System is the founding-era U.S. party conflict between [[ThomasJefferson|Thomas Jefferson]]'s [[DemocraticRepublicanParty|Democratic-Republicans]] and the [[FederalistParty|Federalists]] associated with [[AlexanderHamilton|Alexander Hamilton]] and John Adams.

## Current Synthesis
The Jefferson and Hamilton episodes now make the First Party System a dual institutional and personal rivalry. Jefferson's episode shows party competition becoming an organized democratic test: bitter Federalist and Democratic-Republican attacks still ended with a constitutional transfer in 1800. The Hamilton episode adds the rival's view: disagreement over federal power, banking, manufacturing, Britain, agrarian republicanism, and Union survival also created personal enmities, especially between Hamilton and Burr. The current judgment is that early party politics helped make democratic competition possible while also giving reputation conflicts enough public stakes to spill into honor violence.

## Key Claims
- Early U.S. party conflict emerged around competing visions of federal power, finance, foreign alignment, and republican liberty.
- Jefferson's philosopher image depended on practical party organization, especially the work attributed to James Madison.
- The 1796 election exposed an unstable constitutional design because the runner-up became vice president.
- The 1800 Jefferson-Burr tie showed how party machinery could fail inside the Electoral College and force elite judgment in the House.
- Hamilton's intervention against Burr shows that party rivalry could be framed as a test of personal character and Union survival, not only policy.
- The peaceful resolution of the 1800 crisis made party competition compatible with democratic continuity, but Burr's later resentment shows that institutional resolution did not erase personal political injury.

## Evidence
- Ideological split: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] lists federal power, banks, debt, Britain, France, farmers, and tyranny as the main Jefferson-Hamilton disputes, while [[685-hamilton-duel-to-the-death-part-3-glt9231431046]] adds Hamilton's support for central government, national banking, manufacturing, and British alignment.
- Party roles: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] presents Jefferson as Democratic-Republican standard-bearer and James Madison as party organizer.
- Constitutional awkwardness: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] says Jefferson became Adams's vice president after losing the 1796 election.
- Electoral crisis: [[686-jefferson-the-betrayal-of-liberty-part-4-glt6535571769]] says Jefferson and Aaron Burr tied at 73 electoral votes and the House chose Jefferson on the 36th ballot; [[685-hamilton-duel-to-the-death-part-3-glt9231431046]] says Hamilton urged Federalists to prefer Jefferson because Burr seemed more dangerous and power-seeking.
- Honor spillover: [[685-hamilton-duel-to-the-death-part-3-glt9231431046]] connects Burr's resentment to Hamilton's repeated opposition and the failed 1804 New York governorship campaign.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources remain biographical and narrative rather than a complete party history. They do not fully reconstruct local party organization, congressional blocs, newspapers, constitutional amendment mechanics, or regional voting infrastructure.

## What Changed
- Added the Hamilton-Burr episode's rival-centered account of the 1800 deadlock and Burr's later honor grievance.
- Reframed the concept around both democratic institutionalization and the personal reputation pressure party politics created.
