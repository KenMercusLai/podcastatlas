# Imperial Constitutional Mismatch

## Definition

Imperial constitutional mismatch is the conflict between Parliament's claim to sovereign legislative authority and colonists' expectation that taxation and practical local government required consent through their own representative institutions.

## Current Synthesis

The concept explains why policies that looked ordinary in Westminster appeared unconstitutional in America. British officials treated parliamentary supremacy after the Glorious Revolution, stamp duties, customs enforcement, and vice-admiralty jurisdiction as legitimate tools of government. Colonists appealed to the king and to their rights as Englishmen, but their desired settlement asked the crown to neutralize Parliament in a way British constitutional practice did not permit. Because each side reasoned from a different location of final authority, administrative disputes became legitimacy disputes.

## Key Claims

- Parliamentary sovereignty and colonial consent located final taxing authority in different institutions.
- Earlier ambiguity remained tolerable while imperial enforcement was light.
- Post-1763 taxation and customs administration made an abstract disagreement materially visible.
- Appeals to the king could not produce the colonial settlement sought without disrupting Britain's constitutional order.
- Each side could act intelligibly from its own premises while treating the other as lawless or tyrannical.

## Evidence

- Sovereignty: [[347-the-american-revolution-part-1-glt3042850673]] says British politicians regarded Parliament's supremacy as non-negotiable.
- Colonial consent: [[347-the-american-revolution-part-1-glt3042850673]] presents colonists as seeking self-taxation and practical self-government inside the empire.
- Legal experience: [[347-the-american-revolution-part-1-glt3042850673]] connects stronger Sugar Act enforcement and juryless vice-admiralty proceedings to perceived violations of English rights.
- Failed dialogue: [[347-the-american-revolution-part-1-glt3042850673]] characterizes the two sides as talking past each other while suspicion intensified.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Constitutional mismatch does not make escalation automatic or remove material interests, elite ambition, slavery, smuggling, land speculation, economic stress, propaganda, and crowd coercion from the explanation. The episode also treats independence as avoidable in 1770, so incompatible doctrine alone did not determine the final rupture.
