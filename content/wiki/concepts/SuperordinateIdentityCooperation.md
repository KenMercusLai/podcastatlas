# Superordinate Identity Cooperation

## Definition
Superordinate identity cooperation is the creation or activation of a broader shared membership under which previously divided groups work toward a goal that makes mutual contribution visible and valuable.

## Current Synthesis
The episode's cases suggest that prejudice reduction requires more than placing different people near one another. Mandela's Springbok strategy redefined a charged symbol through leadership and public ritual; Mohamed Salah became an admired Muslim member of an existing Liverpool identity; and mixed Christian-Muslim football teams in northern Iraq made success depend on cross-group teammates. Across the cases, broader identity becomes credible through belonging, interdependence, norms, and shared achievement.

The mechanism remains conditional. Crisis can unite strangers or deepen blame, sport can stage recognition without redistributing power, and successful exemplars can be treated as exceptions rather than changing the category. A wider “us” is most promising when institutions support equal membership, cooperation has real stakes, and symbolic inclusion is joined to repeated fair treatment.

## Key Claims
- A broader identity can make former outsiders legible as teammates rather than only as rival categories.
- Cooperative interdependence is stronger than passive contact because each participant contributes to a shared outcome.
- Visible success can reinforce cross-group bonds and update group norms.
- Admired members can weaken stereotypes by becoming valued representatives of an existing in-group.
- Leaders can reframe inherited symbols, but credibility depends on trust, sacrifice, and institutional context.
- Symbolic unity does not by itself resolve inequality, trauma, or conflicts of material interest.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source summarizes rather than reproduces the underlying studies, so causal mechanisms, comparison groups, duration, transfer beyond teammates, and effect persistence remain uncertain. Contact can fail under competition, coercion, status inequality, or unsafe conditions. A superordinate identity can suppress legitimate subgroup difference, protect dominant norms, or ask injured groups to accept symbolic belonging without accountability and structural change.

## What Changed
- Established a conditional cooperation model joining broader membership, interdependence, visible success, leadership, and representation.
