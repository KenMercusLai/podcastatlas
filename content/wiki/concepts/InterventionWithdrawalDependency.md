# Intervention–Withdrawal Dependency

## Definition

Intervention–withdrawal dependency is the process by which a foreign intervention creates local reliance, promises, mixed institutions, and personal obligations that make later withdrawal materially and morally difficult.

## Current Synthesis

The fall of Saigon supplies the central case. Part 1 shows dependence before evacuation: American money, firepower, institutions, economic demand, and political assurances shaped South Vietnamese military and social capacity. Once that support contracted, retreat, refugee movement, and confidence failure accelerated together. Part 2 shows dependence at the rescue boundary, where aircraft capacity, citizenship, paperwork, embassy access, and command priorities converted accumulated relationships into a hierarchy of escape.

The concept does not establish that every intervention should continue indefinitely. It explains why entry and exit cannot be judged as independent moments: creating reliance changes the future cost of departure, while remaining can deepen the same dependency and prolong harm.

## Key Claims

- Intervention creates obligations through conduct and reliance, not only through formal treaties.
- Financial, military, economic, and psychological reliance can fail together when external support is removed.
- Dependence becomes visible during withdrawal when limited rescue capacity forces priority decisions.
- Operational success for withdrawing personnel can coexist with abandonment of local partners.
- Delaying withdrawal preparation may preserve confidence briefly while worsening later chaos.
- The dependency problem complicates both interventionist and isolationist policy rules.

## Evidence

### Reliance and promises

- [[324-fall-of-saigon-the-nightmare-begins-part-1-glt6047251362]] describes South Vietnam's dependence on U.S. aid, military presence, economic demand, and retaliation assurances before the final evacuation.
- [[325-fall-of-saigon-apocalypse-now-part-2-glt1698435670]] describes Vietnamese employees, allies, dependents, and mixed families expecting protection after years of American involvement.

### Withdrawal shock and collapse

- [[324-fall-of-saigon-the-nightmare-begins-part-1-glt6047251362]] links shrinking support to military retreat, refugee congestion, confidence loss, and the impossibility of evacuating everyone judged vulnerable.

### Rescue hierarchy

- [[325-fall-of-saigon-apocalypse-now-part-2-glt1698435670]] shows embassy access, U.S. priority, helicopter space, and final extraction orders determining who could leave.

### Policy ambiguity

- [[325-fall-of-saigon-apocalypse-now-part-2-glt1698435670]] closes without a universal answer about intervention because non-intervention, continued intervention, and withdrawal can each expose people to harm.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- Dependence does not prove that the original intervention was legitimate or that indefinite continuation would improve outcomes.
- Local actors retain agency; the concept should not reduce South Vietnamese people to passive clients of the United States.
- The sources provide a vivid historical case, not a comparative model proving that Saigon, Kabul, and other withdrawals share identical causes.
- Better contingency planning can reduce evacuation harm but cannot necessarily rescue everyone or preserve a collapsing allied state.

## What Changed

- Extended dependency from final rescue triage backward into finance, firepower, economic structure, confidence, and deterrent promises.
- Connected withdrawal shock to state-collapse dynamics without removing South Vietnamese agency.
