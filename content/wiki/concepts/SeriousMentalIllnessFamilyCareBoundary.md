# Serious Mental Illness Family Care Boundary / 重性精神障碍家属照护边界

## Definition
Serious mental illness family care boundary / 重性精神障碍家属照护边界 is the episode's rule that families can be responsible for treatment access, material support, and patient safety without being morally required to sacrifice their own stability through unlimited proximity or rescue.

## Current Synthesis
The episode grounds the concept in the host's family experience with a father diagnosed with schizophrenia. [[JiangTaoPsychiatrist|江涛]] says timely hospitalization, care, material help, and follow-up already count as serious family responsibility; physical separation can be legitimate when close contact would endanger the caregiver or pull the family into the patient's paranoid or disordered world.

The boundary does not dismiss patient dignity or need. Jiang Tao argues that untreated severe illness can expose the patient to hallucination- or delusion-driven self-harm, suicide, public incidents, bodily neglect, alcohol, smoking, or damaging diet. The care question is therefore not "freedom versus cruelty" in the abstract, but how to combine treatment, rehabilitation, family capacity, and intermediate institutions that are still too weak.

The boundary now extends from long-term psychosis care into crisis and adolescent family response. Jiang Tao says high-suicide-risk patients need fast hospitalization and family cooperation, while children who cannot attend school are harmed when parents swing between coercive academic pressure and guilt-driven indulgence. Family care therefore requires presence and support, but also stable limits and willingness to work with regular professionals.

The Taiwan case adds a life-course and succession problem. A mother spent roughly three decades coordinating treatment, income, debt, work, and daily stability for two adult sons; in her seventies, the unresolved question is not whether she cares enough but whether any trusted system can assume support after her death. [[CommunityMentalHealthSupport]] is therefore part of the family boundary itself: without credible housing, rehabilitation, work, case management, caregiver support, and trust-building, advice to set limits can leave families with nowhere safe to transfer responsibility.

## Key Claims
- Family responsibility is real, but it is not unlimited co-residence, guilt, or self-erasure.
- Caregiver self-protection can be part of responsible care when severe symptoms create risk or emotional contamination.
- Hospitalization and medication can be justified when untreated illness threatens patient safety, public safety, or basic living order.
- Institutional care still needs scrutiny because medication side effects, closed settings, and family invisibility can create guilt and distrust.
- Families need credible middle layers between hospital and home, such as community rehabilitation, supported living, work, case management, and medical-care-plus-living support.
- Care boundaries should protect patient dignity and caregiver stability at the same time.
- Crisis cooperation and stable adolescent boundaries matter because psychiatric help can fail when families deny severity, apply excessive pressure, or compensate through complete permissiveness.

- Self-protection rule - [[shidai-zhenghou-yu-anding-cixin-lphaw0qek8ibr0jt44yygou6akql]] has Jiang Tao say that keeping distance need not create guilt when treatment, care, and material support are being provided.
- Untreated-risk frame - [[shidai-zhenghou-yu-anding-cixin-lphaw0qek8ibr0jt44yygou6akql]] links untreated psychotic symptoms to self-harm, suicide, public-risk, and bodily self-neglect.
- Institutional ambivalence - [[shidai-zhenghou-yu-anding-cixin-lphaw0qek8ibr0jt44yygou6akql]] acknowledges medication reactions and the family's inability to see the treatment process while still distinguishing hospitals from punishment or camps.
- Missing bridge - [[shidai-zhenghou-yu-anding-cixin-lphaw0qek8ibr0jt44yygou6akql]] says society needs more transition layers between hospital and family.
- Suicide-risk cooperation - [[shi-di-fu-shuo-466-jiangtao-5wan-jingshenke-yisheng-dui-1yi-jingshen-jibing-huanzhe-lpdsk96akpwtccxyesjdgtw8skbn]] says doctors can reduce high suicide risk through hospitalization only when family and social systems cooperate.
- Adolescent family stance - [[shi-di-fu-shuo-466-jiangtao-5wan-jingshenke-yisheng-dui-1yi-jingshen-jibing-huanzhe-lpdsk96akpwtccxyesjdgtw8skbn]] criticizes the pattern of forced study before breakdown and full compensation afterward.
- Life-course burden - [[97a55906e72b40e89f4d9bf783c20ef4-97a55906e72b40e89f4d9bf783c20ef4]] follows a mother through roughly thirty years of illness, debt, bereavement, work loss, and care for two adult sons.
- Succession and trust - [[97a55906e72b40e89f4d9bf783c20ef4-97a55906e72b40e89f4d9bf783c20ef4]] connects an older caregiver's fear of death to weak confidence that external services can assume responsibility.
- Community bridge - [[97a55906e72b40e89f4d9bf783c20ef4-97a55906e72b40e89f4d9bf783c20ef4]] reports that Taiwan's new legal commitment to community support still faces uneven local preparation and limited non-medical services.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is source-scoped to public psychiatry conversations and a small number of family perspectives. It does not decide when involuntary hospitalization, discharge, guardianship, institutional placement, school return, family therapy, or medication is appropriate for a specific patient. Local law, clinical risk, patient rights, caregiver safety, and available services all matter. The Taiwan report shows policy implementation through one family and one workshop, not a representative evaluation of national coverage or outcomes.
