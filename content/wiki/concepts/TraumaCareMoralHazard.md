# Trauma-Care Moral Hazard

## Definition

Trauma-care moral hazard is the hypothesis that successful medical rescue reduces the visible consequences of shootings and thereby weakens social or political incentives to prevent the violence that produced the injuries.

## Current Synthesis

The episode builds the hypothesis in two steps. First, improved emergency and trauma care can allow homicide counts to fall or remain lower even when shooting exposure or injury severity does not. Second, lawmakers, advocates, media, or the public may read the lower death count as evidence that the underlying problem has eased.

The first step is a measurement mechanism supported by the episode's clinical comparisons and city examples. The second is a behavioral and political inference for which the supplied source gives no direct study. Medical rescue protects victims rather than risk-takers, so the analogy also differs from classic insurance moral hazard and should not imply that saving lives causes shootings or that care should be withheld.

## Key Claims

- Medical success can reduce observed deaths without reducing the number of people shot.
- Homicide-centered attention may understate nonfatal injury and the resources used to treat it.
- Lower visible mortality could weaken prevention pressure if observers mistake survival gains for lower violence.
- The political-response mechanism requires evidence about beliefs, media, advocacy, legislation, and resource allocation.
- Trauma care is a protective achievement and ethical obligation, not the cause of the original violence.
- Better measurement can reduce masking without sacrificing medical capability.

## Evidence

### Statistical masking mechanism

- [[guns-part-4-moral-hazard]] uses Kennedy's treatment counterfactual, trauma-improvement estimates, and Memphis trends to argue that medical survival can separate shooting injury from homicide.

### Political interpretation

- [[guns-part-4-moral-hazard]] explicitly applies moral hazard to public indifference, arguing that doctors absorb consequences that would otherwise appear in the death toll.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode does not show that particular observers changed beliefs or behavior because trauma care lowered mortality. Attention can respond to nonfatal injury, treatment cost, highly publicized shootings, or measures other than homicide, and prevention policy can change while medical outcomes improve. Because victims are protected by third-party rescue rather than choosing more risk under insurance, “moral hazard” is an analogy with important limits.

## What Changed

- Created a qualified hypothesis separating demonstrated statistical masking from untested political-response effects.
