# War-Aim Expansion After Initial Success

## Definition

War-aim expansion after initial success is the process by which achieving a limited defensive objective creates pressure for a more ambitious offensive because governments, allies, or publics judge the original success too invisible to justify mobilization, loss, and promised glory.

## Current Synthesis

[[722-the-charge-of-the-light-brigade-the-tsars-terrible-siege-part-2-glt7837711407]] grounds the concept in the opening [[CrimeanWar|Crimean War]] campaign. Britain and France entered the war to prevent Russia from dismembering the Ottoman Empire or taking Constantinople. Russian withdrawal from Silistria and the Danubian principalities removed that immediate threat, meaning the defensive purpose had substantially succeeded before the Allied armies won a major battle.

Success did not end the campaign because it created a political accounting problem. Governments had mobilized fleets and armies, the press exposed failures, disease had already imposed loss, and publics expected action and victory. Britain identified Sevastopol and the Russian Black Sea Fleet as a visible target that could turn prevention into a durable strategic result; Napoleon III sought glory and alliance continuity; and French commanders faced a Varna camp so unhealthy that movement could appear safer than waiting. Prestige, strategy, alliance management, and operational deterioration therefore reinforced one another in expanding the aim.

## Key Claims

- A limited defensive objective can be achieved without producing a publicly legible victory.
- Mobilization and sunk losses can create political demand for a more visible return rather than a reason to stop.
- Public expectation and press visibility can raise the reputational cost of ending a war without a major battle.
- Allies may accept a larger objective for different reasons, including prestige, alliance maintenance, strategic prevention, or escape from a deteriorating position.
- A replacement target can be genuinely strategic while still representing mission expansion.
- Operational misery can make a riskier offensive look preferable to remaining in place.

## Evidence

- Initial objective - [[722-the-charge-of-the-light-brigade-the-tsars-terrible-siege-part-2-glt7837711407]] identifies protection of Constantinople and the Ottoman balance as the reason for entering the war.
- Objective achieved - [[722-the-charge-of-the-light-brigade-the-tsars-terrible-siege-part-2-glt7837711407]] treats Russia’s withdrawal across the Danube as removal of the immediate threat.
- Political pressure - [[722-the-charge-of-the-light-brigade-the-tsars-terrible-siege-part-2-glt7837711407]] connects British and French expectations of action, glory, and prestige to reluctance to end the campaign.
- Replacement objective - [[722-the-charge-of-the-light-brigade-the-tsars-terrible-siege-part-2-glt7837711407]] presents Sevastopol and the Black Sea Fleet as the target capable of producing visible and longer-term strategic effect.
- Coalition and operational motives - [[722-the-charge-of-the-light-brigade-the-tsars-terrible-siege-part-2-glt7837711407]] adds Napoleon III’s alliance calculations and Saint-Arnaud’s judgment that Varna’s disease made invasion comparatively tolerable.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept does not imply that the Sevastopol objective was merely symbolic or irrational. Russian Black Sea power posed a continuing strategic problem, and withdrawal from the Danube did not settle every cause of the war. The relative causal weight of public pressure, alliance politics, military planning, disease, ministerial decisions, and long-term strategy remains source-scoped; the episode does not supply opinion data or a complete policy archive.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from the shift between successful defense of Constantinople and the decision to invade Crimea.
