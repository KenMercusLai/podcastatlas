## Definition

Conservation–livelihood conflict arises when protecting habitat or wildlife changes access to land, grazing, movement, income, or authority for people who live with that landscape.

## Current Synthesis

The Kenya discussion treats private reserves as institutionally mixed. Wildlife protection can require boundaries and reduced disturbance, while the same fences can restrict pastoral access to customary grazing and shift the value of land toward tourism or private control. People inside and outside the reserve boundary may both bear losses produced by changing property and conservation regimes.

This frame rejects a simple opposition between virtuous conservationists and destructive residents. Evaluation has to include ecological outcomes, historical use, ownership, livelihood alternatives, benefit distribution, local authority, and who absorbs the cost of protecting wildlife.

## Key Claims

- Ecological protection and local livelihood can conflict even when neither aim is illegitimate.
- Fences and reserve rules redistribute access, authority, and risk rather than merely preserving nature.
- Long coexistence with wildlife does not guarantee continued access under modern property regimes.
- Tourism revenue matters ethically only alongside its distribution and effects on affected communities.
- Conservation narratives should identify institutional victims on multiple sides of a boundary.
- Good intervention requires ecological feedback and social legitimacy rather than species outcomes alone.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode summary does not identify the specific reserve, land tenure, community agreements, wildlife outcomes, revenue flows, or pastoral testimony. It therefore establishes a decision frame rather than a verdict about one institution. Some access restrictions may be ecologically necessary, and some conservation programs may share governance or benefits more equitably than the general tension suggests.
