# Reader-Time Ethics / 读者时间伦理

## Definition
Reader-time ethics treats a reader's finite attention as something an author must justify through meaningful, intelligible work rather than something owed to the author by status, intention, or genre.

## Current Synthesis
[[LiuZhenyun|刘震云]] frames wasted reader time as a moral failure and rejects the complaint that audiences simply refuse serious writing. The claim joins craft and ethics: a work should offer insight, experience, pleasure, recognition, or another defensible gain, and the writer remains responsible for how form makes that value available.

This standard does not reduce literature to immediate ease or popularity. The same interview treats good books as having long “adolescence,” capable of yielding different things at different ages, and defends canonical works as both readable and deep. Reader benefit can therefore be delayed, revisited, difficult, or plural, but it cannot be replaced by authorial entitlement to attention.

## Key Claims
- Reader attention is finite and carries opportunity cost.
- Authorial seriousness or good intention does not by itself justify the reader's time.
- Accessibility can be an ethical and craft concern without requiring simplification.
- Popularity is neither sufficient nor necessary evidence that a work rewards attention.
- A durable book may justify rereading by changing with the reader's age and experience.

## Evidence
- Moral formulation: [[lu_1rrenfntcaoijjpbqjlbcao_-lu_1rrenfntcaoijjpbqjlbcao_]] reports Liu's claim that wasting another person's time is especially unethical.
- Responsibility rather than blame: [[lu_1rrenfntcaoijjpbqjlbcao_-lu_1rrenfntcaoijjpbqjlbcao_]] records his refusal to explain disengagement by simply blaming non-reading audiences.
- Durability and readability: [[lu_1rrenfntcaoijjpbqjlbcao_-lu_1rrenfntcaoijjpbqjlbcao_]] links changing rereadings, canonical accessibility, and long-term resonance rather than current trend-following.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not define a universal measure of reader gain, and readers reasonably differ in background, purpose, patience, and taste. Difficult or initially unrewarding work can still become valuable, while highly readable work can waste attention. The concept is an authorial responsibility principle, not a market-success formula.

## What Changed
- Established reader attention as an ethical constraint on writing.
- Separated accessibility from simplification and popularity from value.
- Added rereading across life stages as one form of durable reader return.
