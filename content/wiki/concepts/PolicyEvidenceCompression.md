# Policy Evidence Compression

## Definition

Policy evidence compression is the conversion of complex research, uncertainty, disagreement, and consequences into a short decision-ready recommendation.

## Current Synthesis

Compression is unavoidable in executive work: leaders cannot personally reproduce every study or administrative record. [[the-prime-minister-and-the-prof-the-prime-minister-and-the-prof]] shows why the method must preserve decision-relevant uncertainty. [[FrederickLindemann]]'s roughly ten-line recommendations were useful to [[WinstonChurchill]] precisely because they selected and simplified, but the episode argues that the selection sometimes removed findings and warnings that should have changed the judgment.

The strategic-bombing case separates underlying observation from recommended policy: reported research found no panic, while the memo advocated civilian bombing to break morale. The Bengal case adds consequence omission: a transport warning about regional upheaval reportedly did not survive into the advice against relief. Good compression therefore needs traceable evidence, alternatives, uncertainty, adverse consequences, and a route back to the fuller record.

## Key Claims

- Decision support must simplify, but simplification is itself an exercise of power.
- A concise recommendation can reverse the practical meaning of underlying evidence without falsifying every included fact.
- Omitted uncertainty and adverse consequences can matter as much as included estimates.
- Traceability to source material and dissent is necessary when recommendations affect large populations.
- Confidence and brevity should not be mistaken for evidential strength.
- Independent review is especially important when an adviser shares the leader's preferred answer.

## Evidence

- Memo format: [[the-prime-minister-and-the-prof-the-prime-minister-and-the-prof]] says Lindemann produced thousands of brief memoranda, with recommendations constrained to roughly ten lines.
- Finding-to-conclusion gap: [[the-prime-minister-and-the-prof-the-prime-minister-and-the-prof]] reports that the Hull and Birmingham study found no panic while Lindemann predicted morale collapse from housing destruction.
- Estimate challenge: [[the-prime-minister-and-the-prof-the-prime-minister-and-the-prof]] says government experts judged his housing-destruction estimates five or six times too high.
- Consequence omission: [[the-prime-minister-and-the-prof-the-prime-minister-and-the-prof]] reports that a transport warning about violent regional upheaval did not appear in his Bengal advice.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source does not provide the original studies and memoranda, so its account of omission and contradiction remains episode-attributed. Short advice can be accurate and longer analysis can still mislead. The appropriate standard depends on urgency, stakes, reversibility, and the leader's access to parallel review; the concept is not an argument that every executive brief reproduce the full evidence base.

## What Changed

- Established a distinction between necessary simplification and compression that changes the practical meaning of evidence.
- Added traceability, dissent, uncertainty, adverse consequences, and reversibility as review criteria.
