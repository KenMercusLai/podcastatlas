# Late Civil-Rights Economic Justice

## Definition

Late civil-rights economic justice is the strategic expansion from formal desegregation and equal legal access toward the income, employment, housing, labor power, and material capacity needed to make equality usable.

## Current Synthesis

The episode presents [[MartinLutherKingJr|Martin Luther King Jr.]]'s late work as an effort to expose the incompleteness of legal access without economic power. A person might be permitted to sit at an integrated lunch counter yet still lack enough income to buy coffee. Northern housing segregation, unemployment, unequal schools, the [[MemphisSanitationStrike]], and the multiracial [[PoorPeoplesCampaign]] therefore belong to one widening diagnosis rather than separate causes.

This expansion increased political difficulty. It challenged structures outside the legally segregated South, crossed into labor and redistribution, and coincided with King's opposition to the Vietnam War. The source treats the program as morally coherent but does not show that its coalition or implementation had become institutionally durable before King's death.

## Key Claims

- Formal access does not by itself create the material capacity to exercise equal rights.
- Housing, schools, employment, wages, and labor dignity made Northern inequality part of the civil-rights agenda.
- Local labor conflict can embody a national economic-justice diagnosis.
- A multiracial poverty coalition expands the movement's reach while increasing coordination difficulty.
- Opposition to war linked domestic deprivation to a broader critique of national priorities.

### Local and national organization

- [[509-america-in-68-the-assassination-of-martin-luther-king-jr-part-2-glt4373367265]] links the sanitation strike's union and dignity claims to the Poor People's Campaign's proposed multiracial Washington mobilization.

### Political cost

- [[509-america-in-68-the-assassination-of-martin-luther-king-jr-part-2-glt4373367265]] presents Northern campaigns and Vietnam opposition as sources of declining popularity and estrangement from liberal allies.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept is grounded in one episode's account of King's final years. It does not establish that earlier civil-rights work ignored economics, that all participants shared one program, or that opposition to King arose only from the expanded agenda. The failure of Resurrection City qualifies organizational execution but does not by itself refute the underlying diagnosis.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from the connection among Northern inequality, Memphis labor demands, and the Poor People's Campaign.
