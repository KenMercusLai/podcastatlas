# Legalist Ruler Technique / 法家君术

Legalist ruler technique / 法家君术 is the governance frame [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-08-yidai-mingjiang-wuqi-cansi-2-lvaqlgyccrugfo0l-wuwf1mt1kcm]] draws from [[HanFei|韩非]]'s treatment of [[ZhaoJinghou|赵靖侯]]. The episode presents Zhao Jinghou as personally indulgent and violent, yet uses Han Fei's point that a ruler's ability to employ people and detect truth or falsehood in ministers' speech can still preserve the state.

The concept is deliberately cold. It separates political effectiveness from conventional moral virtue: what matters in this frame is not whether the ruler is admirable, but whether he can control appointments, read incentives, and prevent ministers from deceiving him. The source uses the example to show why Legalist reasoning can treat governance as a technique before it treats it as a moral relationship.

[[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-13-gongsunyang-keke-bianfa-re-shashen-1-lgmjapc2eecefbdshnpenzfcv6ga]] adds [[ShangYang|公孙鞅 / 商鞅]]'s institutional version of the same coldness. Law is made by the capable and obeyed by the rest; household groups, informant rewards, harsh punishment, and [[MilitaryMeritRankSystem|军功爵制]] turn governance into enforceable incentive architecture. The source therefore moves the concept from ruler judgment alone into full social redesign.

## Key Claims
- Ruler competence is measured through control of people, information, and speech.
- Personal vice does not automatically imply political collapse in this frame.
- The episode uses the idea as a contrast with virtue-centered or benevolent-rule traditions.
- Legalist ruler technique can explain durability while still feeling morally severe.
- Shang Yang's reform case adds rule through standardization, surveillance, and rank incentives rather than only minister control.
