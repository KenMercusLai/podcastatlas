# Moralized War Pretext / 道德化战争借口

Moralized war pretext / 道德化战争借口 is the pattern [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-68-jingtan-songkangwang-huangyinwudu-de-gouxue-lishi-2-lkn073gbtpmg11hcrcm8h6uvawzt]] draws from the destruction of [[SongState|宋国]]. [[SongJunYan|宋康王 / 宋君偃]] creates real strategic enemies by taking land from [[QiState|齐国]], [[ChuState|楚国]], and [[WeiState|魏国]] and by destroying [[TengState|滕国]], but the episode stresses that the attack still needs a legitimating story: Song Kang Wang is presented as more tyrannical than Shang Zhou, and punishing him becomes the public rationale for war.

The concept does not mean the moral charges are automatically false. In the source, many cruelty stories are part of Song Kang Wang's remembered record, and the loss of [[PeopleBasedPoliticalSecurity|民心型政治安全]] is treated as politically real. The pretext layer names the second move: rival states publicize, coordinate, and amplify moral claims so that territorial revenge and strategic containment can look like collective punishment of a criminal ruler.

This sits between [[CommonEnemyAlliance|共同敌人联盟]] and [[MoralReputationPoliticalCapital|道德名望政治资本]]. A common enemy supplies strategic direction; moralized pretext supplies permission, recruitment, and public cover. In the episode, [[QiMinWang|齐湣王]]'s agents spread Song Kang Wang's cruelty stories before Qi, Chu, and Wei move together, turning reputation into war infrastructure.

[[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-120-1-shui-shi-huangdi-zhong-de-mabaonan-lhw8vsj6ta6rjdkugcvfohaqqxc0]] adds a post-victory imperial version through [[ConquestSelfDefenseNarrative|兼并自卫叙事]]. After [[QinState|秦国]] has already destroyed the six states, its explanation still moralizes each war: the fallen states are described as oath-breakers, rebels, attackers, assassination sponsors, or conspirators. Here the pretext is less about coordinating a coalition than about making conquest governable after the fact.

[[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-130-2-lishi-jilu-shouci-tucheng-lm4kuzdpnqn-hicyuvvb4p2wdudm|Qinji 130-2]] adds a rebel-internal version. [[XiangLiang|项梁]] wants to attack [[QinJiaRebel|秦嘉]] and [[JingJu|景居 / 景驹]], who are also anti-Qin actors. He therefore argues that [[ChenSheng|陈胜]] was the first rebel king and that Qin Jia betrayed his own ruler by installing Jing Ju. The moral claim converts a rival-consolidation war into punishment of disloyalty and reduces the soldiers' fear that they are merely attacking another anti-Qin camp.

## Key Claims
- A war can have both material incentives and moral language; the concept tracks the conversion between them.
- Publicizing a ruler's cruelty can help coordinate states that already have territorial or security reasons to attack.
- Moral pretext works best when the target has already damaged domestic support and elite reputation.
- The pattern is compatible with true wrongdoing, exaggerated wrongdoing, or a mixture of both.
- The Song case links pretext to [[VanquishedRulerReputationConstruction|亡国君污名建构]] because defeat can freeze the hostile version of the ruler's image.
- After victory, moralized pretext can become a public memory framework that presents conquest as necessary punishment.
- The pattern can operate inside one broad coalition when leaders need to justify attacking a rival claimant who nominally shares the same external enemy.
