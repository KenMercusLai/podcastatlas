# Medication Self-Combination Risk / 药物自行叠加风险

## Definition
Medication self-combination risk / 药物自行叠加风险 is the harm pattern that appears when people combine over-the-counter or prescription drugs by symptom or social-media suggestion without recognizing overlapping ingredients, metabolism burden, contraindications, or interaction risk.

## Current Synthesis
[[vol-222-aoye-xintiao-kuangbiao-shi-cusi-qianzhao-aed-hui-dian-cuo-huoren-dai-ni-bikai-zhiming-changshi-shijie-jijiu-ri-teji-lnemogq8gb9o3e2835jvtfx3emiy|VOL.222]] uses cold medicines, painkillers, acetaminophen overlap, NSAIDs, aspirin, liver metabolism, kidney burden, and gastric injury to turn self-medication into a first-aid literacy problem. [[vol-17-jizhen-weizhongbingke-yangkang-de-jizhen-yisheng-liao-xinguan-zhi-yongyao-wuqu-dui-azifuding-he-paxlovid-de-jinshen-taidu-lqdru5slonvj7tu2rmhjlf0vlnzx|VOL.17]] adds the consumer-literacy problem behind that risk: people often recognize a brand or nickname without knowing whether it already contains acetaminophen, aspirin, caffeine, a cough suppressant, or another active ingredient.

The concept belongs to the wiki's broader medical-risk pattern: symptom relief, product familiarity, or online recommendation does not remove the need to check ingredients, current medications, age, disease background, and worsening symptoms.

## Key Claims
- Multiple products for the same symptom can contain the same active ingredient.
- Acetaminophen overlap across cold medicines can raise liver-injury risk when users unknowingly stack doses.
- NSAID-containing products can add gastric and bleeding risk, especially when combined with long-term aspirin or similar medication contexts.
- More drug types increase the chance of liver, kidney, stomach, allergy, or interaction problems.
- Social-media medicine combinations should not replace package instructions, clinician advice, or pharmacist guidance.
- Acute or worsening symptoms should be routed to care rather than repeatedly adding more oral medicine at home.

## Evidence
- Overlapping cold medicine: [[vol-222-aoye-xintiao-kuangbiao-shi-cusi-qianzhao-aed-hui-dian-cuo-huoren-dai-ni-bikai-zhiming-changshi-shijie-jijiu-ri-teji-lnemogq8gb9o3e2835jvtfx3emiy]] says many cold medicines share acetaminophen and that excessive intake can cause drug-induced liver injury.
- Older-medication context: [[vol-222-aoye-xintiao-kuangbiao-shi-cusi-qianzhao-aed-hui-dian-cuo-huoren-dai-ni-bikai-zhiming-changshi-shijie-jijiu-ri-teji-lnemogq8gb9o3e2835jvtfx3emiy]] says older adults taking aspirin can face additional gastric mucosal risk when NSAID-containing cold or pain medicines are added.
- Interaction frame: [[vol-222-aoye-xintiao-kuangbiao-shi-cusi-qianzhao-aed-hui-dian-cuo-huoren-dai-ni-bikai-zhiming-changshi-shijie-jijiu-ri-teji-lnemogq8gb9o3e2835jvtfx3emiy]] explains that many drugs require liver and kidney metabolism and can interact when stacked casually.
- Brand-versus-ingredient literacy: [[vol-17-jizhen-weizhongbingke-yangkang-de-jizhen-yisheng-liao-xinguan-zhi-yongyao-wuqu-dui-azifuding-he-paxlovid-de-jinshen-taidu-lqdru5slonvj7tu2rmhjlf0vlnzx]] explains that familiar cold-medicine names can conceal several active components and that prior tolerance by one person does not establish suitability for another.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This concept does not provide dosing advice, medication selection, or contraindication rules. It records public medical-literacy warnings from two podcast notes that should be checked against clinician, pharmacist, package-label, and local regulatory guidance. Pregnancy, lactation, older age, allergy, organ dysfunction, and current medicines can all change the risk.

## What Changed
- Added brand-name and nickname opacity as a pathway to unrecognized active-ingredient duplication.
- Added patient history and prior drug reaction as necessary context before copying another person's regimen.
