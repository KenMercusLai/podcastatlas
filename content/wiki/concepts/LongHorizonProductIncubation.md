# Long-Horizon Product Incubation

## Definition
Long-horizon product incubation is the deliberate retention and improvement of a product whose early demand or economics are weak because the company expects category habits, consumer needs, or portfolio fit to mature over time.

## Current Synthesis
[[OrientalLeaf|东方树叶]] is the episode's core case. Launched in 2011 as an early zero-sugar, zero-calorie unsweetened tea, it reportedly suffered poor reception and years of losses before health-oriented consumption and wider unsweetened-tea acceptance strengthened demand. The case suggests that patient capital and control can preserve an option until the market catches up, but it does not show that every persistent loss-maker deserves continued funding.

## Key Claims
- Early consumer rejection can reflect timing or habit mismatch rather than permanent product failure.
- Incubation requires enough capital, operational continuity, and decision authority to survive weak early economics.
- Portfolio fit matters: a patient product can later diversify growth away from a pressured core category.
- Retention should rest on improving evidence about category direction, not persistence for its own sake.
- Later success does not erase the opportunity cost or prove that the original forecast was uniquely correct.

## Evidence
- Patient retention: [[fengwei-chihe-nongfu-shanquan-zhongshanshan-ruhe-sandu-wending-zhongguo-shoufu-gkwrijiotjlral0wegtwycfd]] says Oriental Leaf remained in the portfolio despite poor early reception and seven years of losses.
- Category catch-up: [[fengwei-chihe-nongfu-shanquan-zhongshanshan-ruhe-sandu-wending-zhongguo-shoufu-gkwrijiotjlral0wegtwycfd]] connects later growth to greater health awareness and acceptance of zero-sugar tea.
- Portfolio effect: [[fengwei-chihe-nongfu-shanquan-zhongshanshan-ruhe-sandu-wending-zhongguo-shoufu-gkwrijiotjlral0wegtwycfd]] frames tea as a higher-margin growth engine that reduces reliance on packaged water.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The loss duration, causal role of the public-health period, margins, and financial contribution are episode-attributed. Survivorship bias is substantial: a successful patient product does not identify ex ante which weak products should be retained.
