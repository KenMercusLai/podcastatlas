# Institutional Innovation Adoption Resistance

## Definition

Institutional innovation adoption resistance is the gap between evidence that a method may improve outcomes and an organization's willingness and capacity to replace established skills, authority, identity, incentives, and delivery systems with the new method.

## Current Synthesis

The episode argues that slow adoption is not always disbelief or ignorance. A proposed solution may be understood yet resisted because it makes the practitioner's work harder, redistributes control, requires an entire team to retrain, creates dependence on other institutions, demands durable funding, or threatens a profession's established self-image.

Minimally invasive bypass and focused deterrence make this visible in different domains. Both are presented as promising, both require tightly coordinated teams, and both can fail to spread when institutions treat them as isolated techniques instead of operating-model changes. Political leadership and generational turnover can open adoption, but neither substitutes for evidence, local capability, implementation fidelity, or accountability.

## Key Claims

- Evidence of benefit is necessary but often insufficient for institutional adoption.
- Adoption slows when a method requires a new skill stack rather than incremental improvement of familiar practice.
- Resistance can protect professional autonomy, status, identity, routines, and control even when the underlying problem is acknowledged.
- Whole-team retraining, retention, coordination, budgets, and external partners make diffusion harder than copying a technique.
- Partial adoption can preserve the appearance of change while omitting the components that produce the claimed result.
- Generational turnover may change receptivity, but it is not a universal or ethically sufficient change strategy.

## Evidence

### Clinical branch

- [[wolfgang-s-law-part-two]] says minimally invasive bypass requires new manual skills and sustained team coordination while remaining a small fraction of US bypass operations after decades of development.

### Public-policy branch

- [[wolfgang-s-law-part-two]] says Baltimore rejected or incompletely implemented [[FocusedDeterrence]] for more than two decades despite reported results elsewhere, until political leadership required a comprehensive approach.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode's cross-domain comparison is explanatory, not a diffusion study. Slow adoption can be rational when evidence is incomplete, benefits depend on selection, training introduces temporary risk, costs are high, local conditions differ, or competing methods exist. Funding, organizational turnover, legal constraints, and alternative research receive less attention than identity and generational change in the supplied summary.

## What Changed

- Established a synthesis in which adoption resistance can reflect identity, authority, skills, coordination, and implementation burden rather than simple rejection of evidence.
- Qualified generational replacement as one possible mechanism rather than a general law of change.
