# Oil Nationalization

## Definition
Oil nationalization is the shift of formal ownership and control over oil resources from foreign or private companies to the state, usually justified as a way to capture more public value from a strategic national resource.

## Current Synthesis
The evidence now spans Venezuela and Iran. [[chevron-venezuela-and-the-paradox-of-plenty]] presents [[Venezuela]]'s 1976 nationalization as the creation of [[PDVSA]] and a move from foreign-company dominance toward state control. [[trump-drinks-venezuelas-milkshake]] shows that ownership did not end dependence on foreign capital, technology, and operating discipline. [[259-iran-england-the-little-satan-glt8347276204]] adds [[MohammadMossadegh]]'s 1951 nationalization of the [[AngloIranianOilCompany]] as a sovereignty response to opaque profit-based royalties and asymmetric strategic benefits. Together, the cases make nationalization intelligible as a legitimacy correction while showing that ownership alone cannot supply capacity or stable international settlement.

## Key Claims
- Nationalization can increase state control over oil rents, strategy, and sovereignty symbolism.
- Formal state ownership does not automatically produce reinvestment, diversification, or operational competence.
- Contractor relationships can preserve foreign technical capacity while reducing foreign-company autonomy.
- Renationalization can respond to genuine distributional or sovereignty concerns while damaging investor confidence and technical capacity.
- The nationalization-reopening cycle becomes unstable when each phase solves one problem while worsening another.
- Nationalization pressure rises when foreign-company accounting is opaque and strategic benefits visibly flow abroad.

## Evidence
Formal state control:
- [[chevron-venezuela-and-the-paradox-of-plenty]] says Venezuela fully nationalized its oil industry in 1976 and created PDVSA while foreign firms continued under stricter contractor rules.

Continuing need for outside capacity:
- [[trump-drinks-venezuelas-milkshake]] describes the 1990s Apertura as a response to low prices, underinvestment, and technology needs, despite the prior nationalization.

Legitimacy and capacity tradeoff:
- [[trump-drinks-venezuelas-milkshake]] says Chávez criticized foreign contracts as unfair, then fired oil workers and reversed contracts in ways tied to later capacity loss.

Corporate continuity and exit:
- [[chevron-venezuela-and-the-paradox-of-plenty]] and [[trump-drinks-venezuelas-milkshake]] both contrast Chevron's negotiated continuity with the exit of other large firms.

Sovereignty and distributive legitimacy:
- [[259-iran-england-the-little-satan-glt8347276204]] connects Iranian nationalization to profit-based royalties without Iranian inspection rights, preferential British naval pricing, and the refusal of the oil-company board to revise the relationship.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Nationalization is not presented as inherently destructive; it can correct foreign-company overreach and increase public capture of resource value. The sources' warning is narrower: ownership does not substitute for investment discipline, institutional trust, or credible contract terms. The Iranian episode is a bilateral historical survey rather than a full account of the oil crisis, so its financial claims and allocation of British institutional responsibility remain source-scoped.

## What Changed
- Added Iran as a second nationalization case beyond Venezuela.
- Added opaque accounting and asymmetric strategic benefit as nationalization pressures.
- Distinguished the legitimacy case for control from the separate problem of operating capacity.
