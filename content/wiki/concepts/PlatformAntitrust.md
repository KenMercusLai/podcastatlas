# Platform Antitrust

## Definition
Platform antitrust is the question of when a dominant platform's rules, pricing, ranking, contracts, content use, licensing terms, or data advantages become abusive rather than merely efficient.

## Current Synthesis
The wiki treats platform antitrust as an infrastructure-versus-control problem. Useful coordination can lower friction, but the same control layer can exclude rivals, pressure suppliers, redirect traffic, or impose terms that weaker participants cannot realistically reject. The source set spans patent pools, online travel agencies, resale marketplaces, Google AI answer surfaces, and Google remedy outcomes. The September 2026 Google discussion adds a remedy-design layer: courts may acknowledge monopoly power while declining breakup-style remedies, especially when AI competition is argued to be changing the market quickly.

## Key Claims
- Platform concentration can have efficiency reasons, but those efficiencies do not remove the need for scrutiny.
- Abuse claims become stronger when grounded in transaction data, platform rules, content use, contractual pressure, or exclusionary conduct rather than generalized resentment.
- Antitrust boundaries now span licensing, merchant restrictions, ranking, trademark control, data access, AI answer surfaces, and advertising markets.
- AI answer surfaces create publisher and competition conflicts when dominant platforms summarize or reuse content while reducing outbound traffic.
- Remedy design can be weakened or narrowed by rapid-market-change arguments; lack of divestiture does not erase monopoly findings.
- Fines and narrow conduct remedies may be too small to change behavior by trillion-dollar platform companies.

## Evidence
- Infrastructure-versus-control evidence: [[the-invention-invention]] uses patent pools to show how shared standards infrastructure can become exclusionary market power without access, pricing, and behavior constraints.
- Travel-platform evidence: [[kunzai-xitong-li-de-jiudian-ni-buzhidao-de-xiecheng-longduan-lianchengshi-keji-luandun]] and [[ep91-dingfang-dingpiao-dingjiangshan-xiecheng-51-yi-wei-aoman-maidan-lovhfkz4rklv1ik-uqyeswrdf3uw]] ground the Ctrip branch in merchant-side exclusivity, price-parity pressure, platform concentration, and state investigation or penalties.
- Resale-market evidence: [[seven-allegedly-fake-chanel-bags-vs-the-realreal]] adds The RealReal's argument that trademark litigation and pressure on market intermediaries can function as anti-competitive control over secondary resale.
- AI-answer evidence: [[tech-20260220-0220-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260220-0220-mp-tech-pod-128]] says European regulators were investigating Google AI Overviews over possible competition-rule violations tied to publisher content use.
- Remedy evidence: [[tech-20260904-0904-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260904-0904-mp-tech-pod-128]] describes Google monopoly findings in digital advertising alongside no required ad-tech sale, and compares that result with the search case where Chrome was not spun off.
- AI-competition evidence: [[tech-20260904-0904-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260904-0904-mp-tech-pod-128]] says AI competition was cited as one reason for conservative search remedies, while Google remained much larger in advertising dollars.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources are source notes and podcast discussions, not full court records or regulator files. Some concentration reflects real coordination value, and AI competition may become a genuine constraint even if it is not yet a complete substitute for incumbent platform power. The synthesis therefore separates monopoly findings, remedy design, and long-run market change rather than treating any one as dispositive.
