## Definition

临终废储阻断 / Deathbed heir replacement resistance is a succession-stability pattern where ministers block a dying ruler's late preference for an alternative heir by stressing institutional cost, established designation, and likely court resistance.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-498-taizi-liu-ao-huangyin-wudu-han-yuandi-weihe-bu-fei-le-ta-lnnuw3h3c8tqlf5ystx58gi7vvjo|Hanji 498]] supplies the current source case. [[HanYuandi|汉元帝]] becomes dissatisfied with crown prince [[HanChengdi|刘骜 / 后来的汉成帝]] and emotionally closer to [[LiuKangDingtaoKingWesternHan|刘康]] and [[FuZhaoyiWesternHan|傅昭仪]] during illness. The risk is not simply that Yuan likes one son more; it is that late-life intimacy could overturn a long-standing crown-prince arrangement and turn succession into a public political crisis.

[[ShiDanWesternHan|史丹 / 施丹]] blocks the move by changing the decision frame. He does not try to prove Liu Kang unworthy. He argues that Liu Ao is the嫡长 crown prince, has held the position for many years, and cannot be replaced without provoking senior ministers to resist. The pattern therefore turns personal affection into a question of court order: when succession has already become institutionalized, changing it near death can be more destabilizing than tolerating an imperfect heir.

## Key Claims

- A dying ruler's bedside attachments can create late heir-replacement pressure even after a crown prince has been established.
- The strongest resistance may avoid attacking the favored alternative heir and instead emphasize the public cost of changing the succession.
- Long-standing designation,嫡长 status, and visible ministerial opposition can make a replacement politically infeasible.
- Court actors close enough to reach the sick ruler may matter more than formal debate once access is restricted.
- The pattern is a near-displacement case: it preserves the established heir while leaving the later quality of that heir unresolved.

## Evidence

- Late-life pressure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-498-taizi-liu-ao-huangyin-wudu-han-yuandi-weihe-bu-fei-le-ta-lnnuw3h3c8tqlf5ystx58gi7vvjo|Hanji 498]] says Yuan repeatedly asks about Emperor Jing's replacement of his crown prince with the future Han Wudi.
- Bedside access: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-498-taizi-liu-ao-huangyin-wudu-han-yuandi-weihe-bu-fei-le-ta-lnnuw3h3c8tqlf5ystx58gi7vvjo|Hanji 498]] says Fu Zhaoyi and Liu Kang attend Yuan during illness while Wang Zhengjun and Liu Ao have less access.
- Institutional reframing: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-498-taizi-liu-ao-huangyin-wudu-han-yuandi-weihe-bu-fei-le-ta-lnnuw3h3c8tqlf5ystx58gi7vvjo|Hanji 498]] says Shi Dan stresses Liu Ao's嫡长 status, long tenure as crown prince, and certain ministerial resistance.
- Outcome: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-498-taizi-liu-ao-huangyin-wudu-han-yuandi-weihe-bu-fei-le-ta-lnnuw3h3c8tqlf5ystx58gi7vvjo|Hanji 498]] says Yuan stops asking about the Emperor Jing precedent and entrusts Shi Dan with assisting Liu Ao.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Hanji 498 does not prove what Yuan would have done without Shi Dan's intervention. It also does not settle whether preserving Liu Ao was good for Western Han long-term; the source itself notes later criticism of Chengdi. The concept therefore covers the institutional blocking mechanism, not a claim that the preserved heir was substantively fit.

## What Changed

- Created a source-bounded succession concept from Shi Dan's Hanji 498 intervention.
