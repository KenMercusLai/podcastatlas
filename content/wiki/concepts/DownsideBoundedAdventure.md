## Definition

Downside-bounded adventure is a decision practice that identifies the worst plausible loss, compares it with physical and psychological capacity, prepares an exit, and then preserves openness rather than demanding complete information before acting.

## Current Synthesis

[[QilinPhotographer|麒麟]]'s account separates adventure from fearlessness. A route is acceptable when money, time, health, equipment loss, psychological exposure, survival probability, local support, and retreat remain within a personally bearable range. That logic led him to reject a Mali route, choose a Kenya route where loss of camel and camera still appeared survivable, and later end the journey when armed conflict changed the downside.

The same framework also opposes indefinite preparation. Once a credible floor and response plan exist, excessive guides and short videos can manufacture expectation, stereotype, or paralysis. The discipline therefore has two halves: conservative downside control and a willingness to take the first step before an opportunity disappears.

## Key Claims

- Risk tolerance depends on both the external event and the person's current physical and psychological capacity.
- A useful plan specifies unacceptable outcomes, survivable losses, retreat conditions, and available support.
- Choosing a route is part of risk management; courage does not require selecting the most dangerous option.
- Stopping after conditions change is evidence of calibration rather than failed commitment.
- Preparation should reduce catastrophic exposure without pretending to eliminate uncertainty.
- After the downside is bounded, excessive information can become avoidance rather than safety.
- First action can be trained through smaller journeys that reveal capability and limits.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The framework is a participant's decision model, not a validated probability tool. A self-judged survivable downside may omit risks to guides, residents, animals, rescuers, or partners; unfamiliar settings also limit calibration. Less information is not automatically better: maps, local intelligence, medical preparation, equipment, and conflict updates remain essential. Survival does not prove that the original estimate was accurate.
