# Music-Evoked Autobiographical Memory

## Definition
Music-evoked autobiographical memory is the involuntary or deliberate reinstatement of personally lived scenes, sensations, emotions, relationships, and earlier identities when music acts as a retrieval cue.

## Current Synthesis
The source distinguishes music-evoked reliving from bare factual recall. A song or composition can recover bodily feeling, place, weather, shame, freedom, peers, or an earlier self before the listener consciously names the music. Repeated pairing supplies the cue: music heard during emotionally charged or identity-forming periods accumulates contextual associations that can later reactivate a broader experience.

Adolescent and early-adult music may be especially durable because those years contain dense self-defining events and group affiliation, though the source also describes a secondary bump for parents' adolescent music. In dementia care, familiar music may sometimes preserve access to pleasure, performance, and life story when ordinary verbal recall is impaired. That possibility supports careful cueing, not a claim that music restores memory generally or treats neurodegeneration.

## Key Claims
- Music can reinstate sensory and emotional detail before conscious recognition of the cue.
- Repeated pairing with a context allows later hearing to retrieve place, relationships, bodily state, and identity together.
- Music from adolescence and early adulthood may persist because it becomes attached to self-defining memories and peer belonging.
- Parents' favored music can create a secondary exposure-based reminiscence pattern in children.
- Familiar music may sometimes support autobiographical access and participation in dementia despite impaired ordinary recall.
- A strong cue can also retrieve shame, grief, trauma, or overwhelming loss rather than comfort.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source supplies vivid cases and broad study descriptions rather than protocols, diagnostic detail, prevalence, effect sizes, or comparisons with other sensory cues. Autobiographical memory remains reconstructive, vividness does not certify historical accuracy, and the reminiscence-bump explanation is presented alongside biological and contextual alternatives. Dementia examples do not establish recovery of lost memory, slowed disease, or a universally safe cue.
