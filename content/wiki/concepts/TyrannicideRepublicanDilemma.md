# Tyrannicide Republican Dilemma / 弑君式共和困境

## Definition

Tyrannicide republican dilemma / 弑君式共和困境 is the problem in which killing a ruler presented as a tyrant can preserve republican refusal while remaining unlawful, politically unpredictable, and insufficient to rebuild the institutions, legitimacy, succession, and force needed for republican government.

## Current Synthesis

The Caesar source establishes the dilemma around [[CaesarAssassination|Caesar's assassination]]. [[MarcusBrutus]] limits the action to Caesar to preserve the language of tyrannicide, while [[GaiusCassiusLonginus]] and the wider conspiracy join principle to fear, resentment, and rivalry. That limitation has a strategic cost: [[MarkAntony]], Caesar's veterans, will, money, and [[OctavianAugustus|heir]] remain available to defeat the conspirators.

A second case comes from the Marat episode. [[CharlotteCorday]] acts as a moderate republican who believes killing [[JeanPaulMarat]] will stop massacre, yet the act neither restores lawful politics nor halts [[RevolutionaryTerrorPolitics|revolutionary terror]]. Across both cases, the killing can remain morally legible to later audiences without becoming an institutionally reparative act.

[[305-the-fall-of-the-roman-republic-glt6995332578]] sharpens the missing statecraft. The liberators fail to control the city, Senate, funeral, public emotion, succession, or armies. They later reproduce civil-war coercion while raising huge forces in the East. The result is not simply that assassination “backfires”; it is that negative unity against one ruler does not supply a governing coalition for the following day.

## Key Claims

- Violence against a would-be tyrant can be morally intelligible without being lawful or politically sufficient.
- Limiting an assassination may preserve a justice narrative while leaving the target's network able to retaliate.
- Removing one ruler does not recreate trust, office norms, military loyalty, succession rules, or public consent.
- Public ritual and material inheritance can reverse the assassins' intended meaning after the killing.
- Assassins may reproduce coercive politics when they later need armies and revenue to defend their act.
- Failed tyrannicide can preserve a memory of refusal even while accelerating the concentration of power.

## Evidence

- Roman plot dilemma: [[124-kaisa-zhisi-lishi-de-yunjiao-shifou-yi-shixiao-781894361]] joins republican symbolism, mixed motives, limited killing, and failure to restore the republic.
- French comparative case: [[125-mala-zhisi-beibi-de-ansha-huo-zhengyi-de-chujue-783972516]] presents Corday's act as politically conscious but unable to stop the wider terror system.
- Post-assassination control failure: [[305-the-fall-of-the-roman-republic-glt6995332578]] traces the Capitol retreat, funeral backlash, Octavian's rise, triumviral terror, and defeat at Philippi.
- Coercion on both sides: [[305-the-fall-of-the-roman-republic-glt6995332578]] qualifies the liberators' moral profile through their eastern war machine and atrocities.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not prove that every tyrannicide must fail or that preserving a refusal has no political value. These sources provide two morally and institutionally different cases, not a universal causal law. The Roman sources also leave open how much of the outcome follows from contingent mistakes rather than the republic's prior structural breakdown.

## What Changed

- Added post-assassination control of institutions, public emotion, succession, and armies as the missing dimensions of republican statecraft.
- Added the liberators' own later militarization as a qualification to a clean law-versus-force contrast.
