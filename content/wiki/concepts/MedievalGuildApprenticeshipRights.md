## Definition

中世纪行会学徒权利 / medieval guild apprenticeship rights is the comparative frame in which apprenticeship includes hierarchy and service but also gives the apprentice recognized claims to treatment, food, legal personhood, and sometimes mobility to another master.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-405-2-weihe-shuo-yiriweishi-zhongshenweifu-lsrigo7tzkp-ugt1mtbrud4etmko|Hanji 405-2]] uses a Western medieval guild example as a contrast case for Chinese master-apprentice dependence. The point is not that European apprenticeship was egalitarian, but that guild rules could make the apprentice's treatment and mobility legible as a regulated claim rather than only a matter of obedience to the master.

The episode's example says a master should provide food and treat the apprentice in a status-conscious way; if the master fails to follow the rule, the apprentice may seek another teacher. This gives the concept its narrow shape: the apprentice remains subordinate, but subordination is partly bounded by guild rule and legal personhood.

For the wiki, this concept mainly functions as a comparative boundary. It helps prevent "apprenticeship" from being treated as one universal institution, because similar master-apprentice language can sit inside very different legal and customary structures.

## Key Claims

- Guild apprenticeship can include hierarchy and service while still recognizing apprentice claims against the master.
- Food, treatment standards, and exit to another master are the episode's main indicators of bounded apprentice status.
- The contrast with Chinese apprenticeship is about legal personhood and contractual protection, not absence of discipline.
- Comparative apprenticeship analysis must distinguish moral hierarchy from enforceable rights.
- The source uses medieval guilds as a compressed contrast case rather than a full account of European craft law.

## Evidence

- Treatment standard: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-405-2-weihe-shuo-yiriweishi-zhongshenweifu-lsrigo7tzkp-ugt1mtbrud4etmko|Hanji 405-2]] cites a 13th-century Paris wool-weavers' guild rule saying masters should provide food and treat apprentices like sons of higher-status people.
- Mobility claim: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-405-2-weihe-shuo-yiriweishi-zhongshenweifu-lsrigo7tzkp-ugt1mtbrud4etmko|Hanji 405-2]] says apprentices could find another master if the master failed to follow the arrangement.
- China-West contrast: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-405-2-weihe-shuo-yiriweishi-zhongshenweifu-lsrigo7tzkp-ugt1mtbrud4etmko|Hanji 405-2]] contrasts this with Chinese master-servant style authority, punishment, meal hierarchy, and weak complaint rights.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is intentionally narrow because the episode offers only one public-history comparison. It should not imply that all medieval European apprentices were well protected, that guild rules were always enforced, or that Chinese apprenticeship never involved reciprocal care. The concept's job is to record the episode's rights-and-personhood contrast until more specialized sources add detail.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Hanji 405-2's medieval guild comparison.
- Framed the comparison as a boundary on apprenticeship generalization rather than a full China-West civilization judgment.
