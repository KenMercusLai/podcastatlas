## Definition
Vienna social housing scale is the use of broad government-supported, cooperative, nonprofit, and rent-controlled housing supply to shape a whole city's rental market rather than only a narrow safety-net segment.

## Current Synthesis
The episode treats [[Vienna]] as a large-supply housing case. About half of residents are described as living in supported, cooperative, or rent-controlled housing, and new supply can be built on reused public or infrastructure land. The source's strongest housing claim is that nonprofit supply can put downward pressure on for-profit rents, while the main qualification is fairness when higher-income residents keep low-rent central apartments over time.

## Key Claims
- Social housing can be a market-shaping institution when it reaches large scale.
- Building on old airport areas or unused train-station land makes land policy part of housing policy.
- Nonprofit housing supply can lower private rents by increasing alternatives and reducing pressure.
- Long-tenure security supports stability but can create fairness concerns when residents' incomes rise.
- The concept extends [[HousingAffordabilitySupplyMechanics]] by showing a nonprofit supply route rather than only zoning or private construction.

## Evidence
- Scale claim: [[the-continent-nobody-owns-everyone-benefits-from-summer-school]] says around half of Vienna residents live in government-supported, cooperative, or rent-controlled housing.
- Rent effect: [[the-continent-nobody-owns-everyone-benefits-from-summer-school]] cites a study saying a 10% increase in nonprofit housing lowers rents in for-profit buildings by 5%.
- Fairness qualification: [[the-continent-nobody-owns-everyone-benefits-from-summer-school]] says higher-earning residents can remain in social housing and pay very low central rents if they obtained apartments decades earlier.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source gives a concise listener example rather than a complete fiscal, architectural, eligibility, or political analysis. The reported rent effect should remain source-scoped until supported by additional housing research.
