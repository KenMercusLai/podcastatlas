## Definition

社会根基崩塌与精英叛乱之别 / social-base collapse versus elite rebellion distinguishes a crisis driven by widespread livelihood failure and loss of governing responsiveness from one led by powerful regional or elite actors whose formal resources are large but whose cause does not command broad popular alignment.

## Current Synthesis

The current evidence comes from [[XuLeWesternHan|徐乐]]'s reported “土崩／瓦解” memorial. [[ChenSheng|陈胜]] represents social-base collapse: a low-status initiator can trigger cascading revolt when poverty, resentment, corruption, official indifference, and policy immobility have already made large groups ready to respond. The Seven States represent elite rebellion: territory, rank, chariots, and troops do not guarantee systemic collapse when most people remain comparatively settled and the dynasty retains usable legitimacy.

The distinction changes what an early-warning system should measure. Elite plots and troop counts matter, but crop failure, household pressure, tax collection, war burdens, local concealment, and the government's capacity to hear and correct distress may be more important for regime survival. The two forms are not mutually exclusive: elite challengers can recruit social grievance, and bottom-up unrest can acquire elite organization after it begins.

## Key Claims

- The initiator's rank and resources do not measure a rebellion's systemic danger as reliably as the breadth and intensity of accumulated grievance.
- Widespread hardship becomes politically dangerous when governing institutions fail to perceive, relieve, or credibly answer it.
- Large elite-led forces can remain containable when everyday livelihood and dynastic legitimacy still bind most people to the existing order.
- Prevention requires policy correction before open revolt, because repression after social cascading begins addresses the symptom later than the cause.
- “Bottom-up” and “elite-led” are analytical poles; real crises may combine or move between them.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The page currently rests on one popular-history rendering of a classical memorial. The binary can simplify mixed crises: the Seven States may still have had local support, Chen Sheng's revolt required organization and coercion as well as grievance, and popular hardship does not mechanically produce rebellion. Exact troop totals, social conditions, and causal claims require primary-source comparison. The concept is best used as a diagnostic question about support breadth and feedback failure, not as a deterministic prediction or a claim that all elite rebellions are less dangerous.

## What Changed

- Established the concept from Xu Le's Chen Sheng and Seven States comparison.
- Reframed regime risk around support breadth, livelihood pressure, and responsiveness rather than rebel status alone.
- Added the guardrail that real crises can mix or transition between bottom-up and elite-led forms.
