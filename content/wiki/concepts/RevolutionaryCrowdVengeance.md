# Revolutionary Crowd Vengeance

## Definition

Revolutionary crowd vengeance is extra-legal punishment in which rumor, betrayal claims, bodily mutilation, and public display make enemies' deaths into immediate political theatre.

## Current Synthesis

The July 1789 cases show a form of violence earlier and less procedurally organized than [[RevolutionaryPopularJustice|revolutionary popular justice]] in the September Massacres. [[BernardReneDeLaunay|De Launay]] and Jacques de Flesselle were killed and decapitated after the Bastille's surrender; an invalid who had helped stop the fortress's destruction was killed after misidentification; Foulon and Berthier de Sauvigny were later hanged, mutilated, and displayed through Paris. These acts treated suspected treachery and elite cruelty as self-validating grounds for punishment, while severed heads and damaged bodies converted victory into warning, revenge, and sovereign spectacle.

## Key Claims

- Collapse of trusted custody allows surrender to pass directly into exemplary killing.
- Rumor and misidentification can outweigh conduct that should exculpate the accused.
- Decapitation and bodily display turn punishment into a public claim about who now holds power.
- Wax-head processions show that symbolic political theatre can precede and script actual violence.
- Early vengeance created a precedent for bloodshed without making later institutional terror inevitable.

### Foulon and Berthier

- [[479-the-french-revolution-the-storming-of-the-bastille-part-5-glt5485693441]] connects food-cruelty rumor, kinship, forced confrontation with a severed head, killing, mutilation, and parade.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The concept names a mechanism; it does not treat a heterogeneous Parisian crowd as one actor or motive.
- Real hunger, arbitrary power, and lethal combat explain anger but do not establish individual guilt or lawful punishment.
- Exact responsibility, dialogue, mutilation sequence, and the truth of claims attributed to victims remain source-scoped.
- Continuity with later terror is interpretive; institutions, war conditions, and political actors changed substantially.

## What Changed

- Distinguishes immediate retaliatory spectacle in July 1789 from the quasi-judicial tribunal logic of the September 1792 massacres.
