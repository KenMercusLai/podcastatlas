# Sales Velocity

## Definition
Sales velocity is the rate at which a product sells through a channel relative to the shelf space, inventory, time, and economics required to carry it.

## Current Synthesis
Across food, beauty, natural retail, marketplaces, and warehouse clubs, sales velocity is not merely sales growth. It is channel-specific proof that a product moves often and profitably enough to justify replenishment and scarce space. Price, purchase frequency, pack format, shelf placement, claim clarity, sampling, repeat purchase, retailer incrementality, and inventory turns all affect the result.

The bounded cases also show that velocity is actively built. [[JustinsNutButter]] changed format and placement; [[ELFCosmetics]] needed extreme unit throughput at a one-dollar price; [[CatalinaCrunch]] became clearer when its claim shifted to keto-friendly; [[MadeGood]] combined school-safe positioning, bright packs, demos, and retailer-specific formats; and [[GoodCulture]] used tasting, package correction, expiration monitoring, and store-level intervention to meet grocery thresholds. At retailer scale, [[Costco]] turns high per-SKU volume into supplier leverage and working-capital advantage.

## Key Claims
- Product liking is insufficient when purchase frequency, margin per unit, or replenishment does not support the channel.
- Velocity depends on contextual legibility: claim, package, shelf location, and use occasion must tell shoppers why and where to buy.
- Sampling is especially valuable for unfamiliar sensory products because it can create immediate sales and reusable customer language.
- Low-priced products need high unit throughput, while perishable products add expiration and reclamation pressure to the same metric.
- Early marketplace movement or retailer reorders are useful signals only when they indicate repeatable demand rather than launch novelty.
- Retailer-level velocity can create system advantages through limited SKUs, inventory turns, supplier leverage, and negative cash conversion.
- Velocity should be read alongside incrementality, repeat purchase, gross margin, stockouts, spoilage, and promotional dependence.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- Fast launch sales can reflect novelty, discounts, distribution loading, demos, or stockpiling rather than durable repeat demand.
- A high-velocity SKU can still destroy value if gross margin, spoilage, returns, trade spending, or working-capital needs are poor.
- Velocity thresholds differ by retailer, category, price, shelf life, space, and strategic role; the Good Culture weekly threshold is not universal.
- Store sellouts may indicate understocking rather than exceptional demand, while stockouts can suppress measured velocity and damage trust.
- The cases are founder and podcast accounts rather than standardized retailer datasets, so cross-case numerical comparison is limited.
