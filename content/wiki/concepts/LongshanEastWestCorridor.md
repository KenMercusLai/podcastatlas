## Definition
Longshan east-west corridor logic is the source's claim that [[LongshanGansuShaanxi|陇山 / 龙山]] should be read as a recurring axis of Chinese historical movement because it mediates passage between [[GuanzhongRegion|关中]], Longyou, Hexi, and the [[WesternRegionsProtectorate|Western Regions]].

## Current Synthesis
The concept turns a regional mountain system into a state-formation and frontier-governance explanation. In [[499-fanyue-longban-yanhaijun-tan-zuowei-gudai-zhongguo-dongxi-hudong-zhouxin-de-longshan-lrm9qbtncvuh2utckq7hogpoxhay|499 翻越陇坂]], [[YanHaijun|严海军 / 阎海军]] argues that early east-west movement underwrote later north-south and frontier relationships: Qin crossing east into Guanzhong, Western Han moving west toward Hexi and the Western Regions, and later Guanlong transport all depend on the same positional geography.

The concept is not only about armies. It also includes agricultural and pastoral complementarity, mountain-pass security, imperial infrastructure, indirect rule, route memory, and bodily experience. The source treats modern railways, expressways, highways, and pipelines as a continuity signal: changed technology has not eliminated the corridor's geographic convenience.

## Key Claims
- Longshan matters because of location and route concentration, not because it is exceptionally high.
- Qin and Western Han form the source's paired historical loops: Qin moves east through Longshan into Guanzhong and the central plains, while Han Wudi moves west from the Guanzhong-Huabei base toward Hexi and the Western Regions.
- The corridor makes Guanzhong security relational: Longshan can be a defensive screen in strong periods and a border condition when central capacity weakens.
- Agricultural highlands, plains, pastoral military capacity, and grain supply are treated as complementary resources for early expansion.
- Fieldwork, road traces, poems, local names, and walking difficulty are part of the source's evidence style, not decorative anecdotes.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source's corridor thesis is an interpretive frame, not a settled monocausal explanation for Chinese state formation. Qin-origin debates, Zhang Qian place memory, and the link between specific local legends and transmitted history remain source-scoped. The source also does not deny north-south conflict, steppe pressure, or maritime and southern routes; it argues that the east-west Longshan axis deserves more explanatory weight than it often receives.
