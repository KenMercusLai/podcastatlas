# Aviation Finance Leasing

## Definition

Aviation finance leasing is the system through which airlines obtain aircraft from specialist owners or financiers instead of purchasing every plane outright. A dry lease supplies the aircraft; a wet lease also bundles operating inputs such as crew and maintenance.

## Current Synthesis

Leasing converts a very large capital purchase into access over time. It can preserve airline cash, support young carriers with limited credit histories, match capacity more closely to route demand, and shift some aircraft remarketing or technology-cycle exposure to the lessor. The global market described in the newer source is therefore broader than emergency financing: it is an operating-capacity allocation system connecting manufacturers, lessors, airlines, and capital markets.

The earlier [[EastStarAirlines]] case keeps the downside visible. Aircraft access can let a carrier scale before internal cash flow is durable, but lease obligations persist when demand, fuel costs, weather, disasters, or credit conditions turn against it. Leasing changes the timing and ownership of capital exposure; it does not make aviation low-capital or eliminate fixed commitments.

Provider economics also matter. Manufacturing shortages and long delivery queues make lessors' existing fleets and order books more valuable. Large firms can seek manufacturer discounts, cheaper funding, better credit ratings, global customer reach, and geographic diversification, while specialists may compete in cargo, older aircraft, or wet leases.

## Key Claims

- Aircraft leasing can lower the entry barrier for a new airline but does not make aviation low-capital.
- Leasing can preserve cash and make fleet capacity more adjustable across routes and demand cycles.
- Dry and wet leases allocate aircraft and operating responsibilities differently.
- Leasing turns manufacturer access, financing capacity, creditworthiness, and remarketing reach into operating constraints and advantages.
- Fast fleet commitments can create visible growth before an airline has proven durable cash flow.
- When shocks arrive, lease obligations can accelerate [[PrivateAirlineFailureModes]], while defaults and repossession become lessor risks.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources are structured narrative summaries rather than lease contracts, audited fleet data, or a comparative financing study. Leasing may improve flexibility relative to ownership without making every lease easy to exit, and different contract terms allocate maintenance, residual value, insurance, currency, interest-rate, default, and repossession risks differently. The East Star case demonstrates one failure path, not the outcome of leasing in general; the industry source emphasizes benefits and scale while giving less attention to lessor leverage, aircraft-value declines, and geopolitical recovery risk.

## What Changed

- Broadened the concept from one young airline's financing mechanism to a global capacity-allocation system.
- Added dry-versus-wet lease distinctions and airline cash or fleet-flexibility benefits.
- Added lessor-side scale, delivery-slot scarcity, remarketing, and specialist strategy.
