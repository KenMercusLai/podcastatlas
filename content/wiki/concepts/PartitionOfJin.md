## Definition

Partition of Jin / 三家分晋 is the staged transfer of territorial power, public rank, and final sovereignty from [[JinState|晋国]] to [[HanState|韩国]], [[ZhaoState|赵国]], and [[WeiState|魏国]].

## Current Synthesis

The partition is best treated as a sequence rather than a single date. The 453 BCE destruction of the Zhi house changes the practical balance and lets Han, Zhao, and Wei divide [[ZhiYao|智瑶]]'s land. The 403 BCE act of [[ZhouWeilieWang|周威烈王]] converts that power into recognized vassal status through [[ThreeJinVassalRecognition|三晋受封]]. The 376 BCE deposition of [[JinJinggong|晋静公]] and division of the remaining Jin territory ends the residual polity.

The newest source sharpens why the middle threshold matters: formal recognition does not merely describe power already held. In the episode's Sima Guang-centered reading, the Zhou king's public naming authority ratifies ministers as peers of their former ruler and weakens the legitimacy rule that had constrained similar seizures. A later governance episode reuses the Jin case more abstractly as a warning about competing elite power centers.

## Key Claims

- The partition has military, legitimating, and terminal thresholds rather than one uncontested start date.
- The 453 BCE defeat of Zhi marks de facto redistribution among the surviving Jin houses.
- The 403 BCE Zhou recognition changes public rank even though it follows an earlier change in force.
- The 376 BCE deposition of Jin's last ruler ends the residual state and distributes its remaining territory.
- The case can serve both historical periodization and a reusable warning about rival elite power centers.

## Evidence

Practical redistribution and formal recognition:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-04-yurang-wei-zhiyao-fuchou-ln34tydha6iqwlrgmw7iugqfgfik|Zhouji 04]] distinguishes the post-Jinyang division of Zhi land from the later Zhou recognition of Han, Zhao, and Wei.
- [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-01-hanzhaowei-sanjiafenjin-lu9desx1v8h1wwfhvbqusoutukqm|Zhouji 01]] centers the 403 BCE recognition and interprets it as the legitimating breach that makes the event programmatic for the chronicle.

Final extinction:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-09-zhanguo-shidai-de-shijie-dazhan-losmum91vjpqfymywelj60o7-vbl|Zhouji 09]] records Jin Jing Gong's deposition and the division of Jin's remaining land in 376 BCE.

Later governance reuse:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-44-yi-ge-gongsi-weishenme-buneng-you-liang-wei-ceo-lsz0xk6-0wwznkc62k-xkoxgz-r|Zhouji 44]] uses Jin's internal elite rivalry as an analogy for the risk of competing chief ministers.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources use different dates for different meanings of “beginning”: 453 BCE for practical redistribution, 403 BCE for formal rank, and 376 BCE for final extinction. The causal claim that Zhou recognition itself produced wider social collapse is Sima Guang's normative interpretation as presented by the podcast, not a mechanism independently established by these notes. The Zhouji 44 analogy also does not prove that plural leadership always causes state breakup; that episode explicitly notes the danger of one unchecked minister.

## What Changed

- Made the three-threshold model explicit and added the 403 BCE legitimacy mechanism.
- Qualified the causal force of the episode's “ritual breach leads to collapse” interpretation.
