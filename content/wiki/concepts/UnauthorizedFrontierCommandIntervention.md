## Definition

边疆将领擅断干预 / Unauthorized Frontier Command Intervention is the pattern where a frontier official uses local force or delegated office to alter an allied frontier polity's leadership or submission order without sufficient central authorization, creating legality and legitimacy problems beyond the immediate tactical result.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 820 now supplies the earlier case. During the 140 CE [[SouthernXiongnu|Southern Xiongnu]] revolt, [[ChenGuiDuliaoGeneralLateHan|Chen Gui]] acts in the Xiongnu command role after [[LiangBingLateHan|Liang Bing]] is recalled for illness. Although [[XiuliChanyu|Xiuli]] is presented as uninvolved in [[WusiSouthernXiongnu|Wusi]] and [[CheNiuChanyu|Che Niu]]'s plot, Chen Gui forces Xiuli and the Left Wise King into suicide and wants to move close chanyu kin inward. The court later jails and removes him, and the surrounding narrative shows the intervention worsening surrender and succession conditions.

Hanji 917 supplies the later repeated case through [[ZhangXiuProtectorOfXiongnu|Zhang Xiu]]. As protector of Xiongnu, Zhang clashes with [[HuzhengChanyu|Huzheng]], kills him without prior court authorization, and installs [[QiangquChanyu|Qiangqu]] as Southern Xiongnu chanyu. The court punishes Zhang Xiu, showing that a frontier outcome may look useful and still violate the authority structure that gives allied rule legitimacy.

## Key Claims

- Frontier officials can create facts on the ground faster than the center can approve or legitimate them.
- Local discretion becomes politically dangerous when it changes chanyu succession rather than only battlefield tactics.
- A tactically assertive intervention can damage surrender prospects if loyal or neutral actors no longer trust Han promises.
- Court punishment in both cases marks the boundary between delegated frontier command and unauthorized regime alteration.
- The pattern differs from frontier-population backlash: here the trigger is unilateral Han-official intervention, not primarily requisition or supply pressure.

## Evidence

- Chen Gui's coercion: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-820-zhichang-jingshi-xiaoxin-lingdao-rang-ni-bei-heiguo-lqkejieiihmupfifuk8sxp3oadap|Hanji 820]] says Chen Gui forces the Southern Xiongnu chanyu and Left Wise King into suicide after Liang Bing's recall.
- Legitimacy fallout: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-820-zhichang-jingshi-xiaoxin-lingdao-rang-ni-bei-heiguo-lqkejieiihmupfifuk8sxp3oadap|Hanji 820]] follows Chen Gui's action with unsettled surrender conditions, Wusi installing Che Niu, and Chen Gui's imprisonment and removal.
- Zhang Xiu's killing and installation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-917-ruhe-xunsu-zhaodao-shengzhi-jiaxin-de-pojudian-lk2zt5c3onvui9a4ppbowshy66tg|Hanji 917]] says Zhang Xiu kills Huzheng without prior authorization and installs Qiangqu.
- Court boundary enforcement: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-917-ruhe-xunsu-zhaodao-shengzhi-jiaxin-de-pojudian-lk2zt5c3onvui9a4ppbowshy66tg|Hanji 917]] says the Han court punishes Zhang Xiu, preserving the idea that succession intervention exceeds local discretion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

These two cases do not prove that the center is always better informed than frontier commanders. Slow authorization can be dangerous in emergencies, and field officials may see conditions the capital cannot. The concept applies when local action rewrites leadership legitimacy or coerces submitted actors without clear mandate; it should not be used to condemn every battlefield decision made before formal approval arrives.
