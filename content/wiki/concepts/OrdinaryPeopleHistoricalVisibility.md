## Definition
Ordinary people historical visibility is the problem of how people without official rank, fame, or durable public archives become legible and consequential within historical explanation.

## Current Synthesis
The evidence now joins recovery method, interpretive commitment, and historiographical program. The Fuyou Tiandi case shows how rural girls become visible through letters, mission archives, maps, fieldwork, genealogy, descendants, and oral memory. [[XuZhuoyun|Xu Zhuoyun]] makes ordinary production, circulation, sacrifice, resilience, and moral judgment organize an account of how society works. [[MarcBloch|Marc Bloch]] and the [[AnnalesSchool|Annales tradition]] widen the program further: soldiers' perceptions, rumor, account books, medical texts, wills, ritual belief, prices, and material traces can reveal collective experience and long-term structure that official records and elite chronology leave incomplete. Visibility is therefore not merely finding forgotten names; it is giving non-elite action and belief explanatory weight without inventing private lives or romanticizing “the people.”

## Key Claims
- Ordinary people often enter history through accidental, hostile, or institutionally mediated traces rather than self-authored public archives.
- Visibility improves when historians combine archives, maps, family memory, genealogy, oral accounts, field sites, material evidence, and social structure.
- Everyday evidence becomes historically serious when a defined question connects it to work, migration, gender, belief, exchange, war, or institutions.
- Historical visibility is a matter of scale and causal emphasis: ordinary life can explain systems rather than merely illustrate elite decisions.
- Collective mentalities and anonymous social practices can be historical subjects even when individual biographies cannot be recovered.
- Recovery must preserve uncertainty and avoid treating ordinary people as morally uniform or representative by default.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Archives, memories, and close observation remain selective. Mission records preserve lives through unequal institutions, wartime recollection can overrepresent vivid scenes, and aggregate evidence may expose collective patterns without recovering individual intention. An ordinary-people focus should widen explanation without turning anecdote into complete proof, presuming that all non-elite actors share one perspective, or excluding elite institutions whose records and power shape the surviving evidence.

## What Changed
- Added Annales and Bloch as a movement-level program for making ordinary experience and collective mentality historically explanatory.
- Expanded the evidence model from named archival recovery to anonymous practices, material traces, perception, and rumor.
- Clarified that collective visibility does not automatically recover individual biography or intention.
