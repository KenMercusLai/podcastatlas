## Definition

Court women historiography is the pattern in which elite women inside the imperial household shape dynastic memory through court records, editing, teaching, continuation, or control over what becomes authoritative history.

## Current Synthesis

Court women historiography now has two Eastern Han source cases. [[EmpressMaMingdeEasternHan|Mingde Empress Ma]] represents the record-adjacent palace route: she is presented as learned, involved in editing `显宗起居注`, and source-scoped as a candidate for China's first female historian. [[BanZhaoLateHan|Ban Zhao]] represents the dynastic-history continuation route: after [[BanGuEasternHan|Ban Gu]] dies with `Han Shu` unfinished, [[EmperorHeOfHan|Emperor He]] orders her to work at Dongguan, continue the inherited Ban-family project, complete the eight tables, and teach the text.

The concept emphasizes court-mediated access rather than formal office parity. Women in this pattern need not hold a compiler's office in the same way as male historians; their position inside or near the palace gives them proximity to imperial memory, household evaluation, archival materials, and teaching channels. The key synthesis is that court women can affect what later becomes authoritative history through record work, continuation, and transmission even when their political legitimacy is expressed through family, virtue, or teacher language.

## Key Claims

- Court women can influence history through access to palace records and ruler-centered memory.
- Historical authority in this pattern is tied to household position as well as textual skill.
- Empress Ma's `起居注` work links maternal governance to record production.
- Ban Zhao provides a direct continuation case where a woman becomes responsible for rescuing and transmitting an unfinished dynastic history.
- Teaching is part of historiographical authority when court-backed explanation makes a difficult history text legible to scholars and palace women.
- The source's "first female historian" judgment is significant but needs corroboration before becoming a wiki-wide universal claim.

## Evidence

- Empress Ma's record work: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-750-jiemi-donghan-wanmei-nvxing-beihou-manshi-xisheng-lu-96lbdh7r0tsmiopbqqrj0q1gt|Hanji 750]] says she edited `显宗起居注` and links this to the early use of the `起居注` genre.
- Female historian framing: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-750-jiemi-donghan-wanmei-nvxing-beihou-manshi-xisheng-lu-96lbdh7r0tsmiopbqqrj0q1gt|Hanji 750]] presents Empress Ma as a candidate for China's first female historian.
- Comparative anchor: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-750-jiemi-donghan-wanmei-nvxing-beihou-manshi-xisheng-lu-96lbdh7r0tsmiopbqqrj0q1gt|Hanji 750]] contrasts Empress Ma's record role with Ban Zhao's later continuation of `Han Shu`.
- Dynastic-history continuation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-705-5-banzhao-xubian-hanshu-shibi-qianqiu-diyi-ren-ljueol3mogwfh-d-w4fs7jzp0h9e|Hanji 705-5]] says Ban Zhao is summoned to continue `Han Shu` after Ban Gu's death and works from the Dongguan archive.
- Textual transmission: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-705-5-banzhao-xubian-hanshu-shibi-qianqiu-diyi-ren-ljueol3mogwfh-d-w4fs7jzp0h9e|Hanji 705-5]] says Ma Rong and other scholars hear Ban Zhao explain `Han Shu`, while palace women are ordered to study with her.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The current evidence is podcast interpretation, not a dedicated historiographic source. The exact authorship, editorial scope, and genre history of `显宗起居注`, Ban Zhao's exact share of `Han Shu` completion beyond the source-described eight tables, and the source's comparative first-person labels all need further support before the wiki treats them as settled historiography.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 705-5 so Ban Zhao is no longer only a comparison case; she now directly grounds continuation and transmission of dynastic history.
- Reframed the concept around record work, continuation, and teaching as three court-mediated routes into authoritative history.
