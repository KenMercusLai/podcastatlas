# Supplements Good Manufacturing Practice / 保健品GMP

## Definition
Good manufacturing practice for supplements is the international production standard that assures a product matches its description, is produced consistently between batches, and can be traced and recalled - without certifying that it works.

## Current Synthesis
The source treats GMP as the component of the Australian system that genuinely deserves the word "strict." It requires the product to match its stated description, keeps quality stable across batches, tracks production conditions, processes, and operators, and enables batch-level tracing and recall when something goes wrong. A second effect is industrial: shared GMP discipline narrows quality differences between contract manufacturers, so a brand can change factory as it grows without losing compliance - the flexibility that [[SupplementContractManufacturing|contract manufacturing]] depends on.

The limit is equally important. GMP does not establish efficacy. It shows that the factory made the product to the specification the brand supplied; the brand files the efficacy claim with the regulator, and the regulator does not verify the benefit. This is the mechanism behind the episode's judgement that the system is strict in appearance and loose in practice, and it is why a GMP-certified origin country can be simultaneously a real quality signal and a poor proxy for whether a supplement does anything.

## Key Claims
- GMP's guarantees are procedural: description accuracy, batch-to-batch consistency, process traceability, and recall capability.
- Traceability has real consumer-protection value, especially when contamination or batch defects appear.
- Common manufacturing standards lower quality variance between contract manufacturers, which gives brands flexibility to switch suppliers.
- GMP says nothing about whether the product produces the benefit implied by its marketing.
- Efficacy is asserted by the brand in its regulatory filing rather than demonstrated to the manufacturer or the public.
- Consumers and marketers frequently read manufacturing certification as product certification, which is the misconception this concept exists to prevent.

## Evidence
- Standard content: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] defines GMP through description consistency, batch-to-batch stability, process and operator tracking, and post-hoc supervision.
- Recall and traceability: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] says GMP makes it possible to trace a problem to a batch and recall it.
- Manufacturing flexibility: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] says shared GMP standards reduce quality gaps between contract manufacturers and make switching factories easier for brands.
- Efficacy limit: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] states that GMP does not cover product effectiveness and that efficacy is declared by the brand with no effective pre-market verification.
- System impression: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] uses this gap to argue the Australian framework looks strict while remaining loose on the dimension consumers care about most.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source describes GMP in general terms and does not examine audit frequency, inspector capacity, or variation between jurisdictions, so the page should not be read as an assessment of any specific certificate. It also conflates the standard with its enforcement in places; a standard that is not audited rigorously provides weaker assurance. Separately, GMP's practical value is real - product-identity accuracy and traceability are not trivial - so the concept qualifies rather than dismisses manufacturing certification.
