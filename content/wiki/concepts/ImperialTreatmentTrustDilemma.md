## Definition

帝王诊疗信任困境 / Imperial Treatment-Trust Dilemma is the political problem in which a ruler cannot evaluate medical care independently of who selected the practitioner, who controls access, and who benefits from recovery or death. The concept describes a trust structure, not proof that a physician or court rival intended harm.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-192-liubang-zhongshang-jing-ganyuan-dengsi-lozkwavngz30qzynceptext1qurx|Hanji 192]] supplies a one-source case involving [[LiuBang|刘邦]]'s final wound. A physician sent by [[LuZhi|吕雉]] reportedly says the injury can be treated, but Liu Bang refuses care, invokes Heaven, rewards the physician, and dismisses him. Because Liu Bang's attempted replacement of [[LiuYing|刘盈]] with [[LiuRuyi|刘如意]] had made Lu Zhi both a household rival and the chief protector of the established succession, the host proposes that the treatment channel itself may have looked dangerous.

The useful synthesis is narrower than the episode's suggested motive. Severe political conflict can make even beneficial care noncredible when the patient lacks an independent way to verify the practitioner's loyalty, diagnosis, or intervention. In this case the refusal is reported, but fear of poisoning or deliberate mistreatment is not demonstrated; the same behavior could also reflect fatalism, pain, resignation, medical pessimism, or factors absent from the supplied account.

## Key Claims

- Court medicine is also an access-and-control relationship when another political actor selects the physician.
- Succession conflict can make the beneficiary of a ruler's death relevant to how treatment is perceived without proving hostile intent.
- A ruler may rationally distrust a channel under uncertainty even when the offered care is genuine, creating a harmful refusal loop.
- Reported refusal, asserted treatability, actual prognosis, and inferred motive are separate evidentiary claims.
- The Liu Bang case supports a political-trust hypothesis but does not establish medical conspiracy.

## Evidence

- Reported refusal: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-192-liubang-zhongshang-jing-ganyuan-dengsi-lozkwavngz30qzynceptext1qurx|Hanji 192]] says Liu Bang rejects treatment after a physician sent by Lu Zhi says the wound can be treated.
- Political context: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-192-liubang-zhongshang-jing-ganyuan-dengsi-lozkwavngz30qzynceptext1qurx|Hanji 192]] connects the refusal to the unresolved conflict over Liu Ying and Liu Ruyi.
- Evidence boundary: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-192-liubang-zhongshang-jing-ganyuan-dengsi-lozkwavngz30qzynceptext1qurx|Hanji 192]] explicitly describes suspicion of Lu Zhi and the physician as an unverified inference from historical clues and human behavior.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept currently rests on one popular-history episode, not a comparative set of court-medical cases. The source provides no direct statement that Liu Bang feared poisoning, no evidence that Lu Zhi or the physician intended harm, and no clinical basis for deciding whether treatment could have saved him. It also does not exclude fatalism, resignation, concern about painful intervention, or other medical and political explanations. The concept should therefore organize uncertainty around trust and access, not convert suspicion into an assassination claim.

## What Changed

- Established a bounded political-trust model for Liu Bang's reported refusal of treatment.
- Separated the observable refusal from the host's proposed succession-based motive.
