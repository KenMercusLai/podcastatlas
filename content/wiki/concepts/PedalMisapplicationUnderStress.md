# Pedal Misapplication Under Stress

## Definition

Pedal misapplication under stress is the unintended pressing of the accelerator when the driver means and believes they are pressing the brake, especially when panic, unfamiliar vehicle geometry, or disrupted learned movement makes intention diverge from bodily action.

## Current Synthesis

[[blame-game-blame-game|Blame Game]] treats pedal misapplication as the main explanation for many sudden-acceleration events. The proposed mechanism is not mere inattention: learned foot placement depends on expected geometry, and an unfamiliar vehicle or thick floor mat can shift that geometry. When unexpected acceleration triggers panic, a driver may press harder on the wrong pedal, interpret the resulting acceleration as brake failure, and remain sincerely convinced that the brake was applied.

The episode combines this mechanism with three evidence types: controlled demonstrations that functioning brakes overpowered the tested engines, cross-manufacturer incident patterns summarized by [[DickSchmidt]], and official event-recorder findings that brakes were often not applied. Together they make pedal error plausible and sometimes strongly supported, but they do not reconstruct every event or exclude mixed mechanical and human causes.

## Key Claims

- Intended movement and executed movement can diverge without conscious awareness.
- Unfamiliar pedal placement or changed footwell geometry can disrupt learned motor expectations.
- Panic can intensify the wrong movement when the driver interprets acceleration as failed braking.
- Cross-manufacturer and low-speed incident patterns are more consistent with a general human-factors mechanism than a defect unique to one model.
- Event-recorder evidence of no brake application can distinguish perceived braking from actual pedal input.
- Demonstrating brake dominance in tested vehicles narrows, but does not eliminate, alternative explanations.

## Evidence

### Motor-control mechanism

- [[blame-game-blame-game]] attributes “impulse variability” to [[DickSchmidt]] and uses it to explain how sincere intent can differ from executed pedal movement.

### Geometry and context

- [[blame-game-blame-game]] reports clustering around unfamiliar cars, low-speed or parking situations, driver stature or age, and floor mats that alter footwell geometry.

### Vehicle and recorder evidence

- [[blame-game-blame-game]] reports controlled stops under full throttle and official cases in which vehicle recorders showed that the brakes had not been applied.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode is argumentative and the supplied document is a secondary structured summary. It does not include Schmidt's publications, the NHTSA/NASA report, raw recorder data, software analysis, or a case-by-case denominator. A brake test in one Camry and one Mustang cannot establish the condition of every vehicle. Pedal error, trapped throttles, software faults, brake degradation, and institutional misconduct are not logically exclusive, and the mechanism should not be inferred solely because a driver cannot explain an event.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to connect motor-control variability, unfamiliar geometry, panic, and recorder evidence without treating every sudden-acceleration report as identical.
