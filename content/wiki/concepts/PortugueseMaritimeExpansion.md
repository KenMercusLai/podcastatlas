# Portuguese Maritime Expansion / 葡萄牙海上扩张

## Definition
Portuguese maritime expansion is the source's frame for Portugal's shift from mainland Iberian consolidation toward North Africa, Atlantic islands, African coasts, and eventually Indian Ocean routes.

## Current Synthesis
The Ceuta episode treats the 1415 capture of [[Ceuta]] as the conventional first station in Portugal's overseas expansion story, but it keeps that claim qualified. Ceuta marks a turn from mainland Reconquista to sea-facing conquest and trade imagination; it does not prove that Portuguese leaders already had a fully formed global empire plan in 1415.

The Columbus episode shows the system a generation later as a practical network of Atlantic islands, West African routes, trading posts, fortresses, plantation slavery, winds, and currents. It trained [[ChristopherColumbus]] but also gave [[JoaoIIOfPortugal|João II]] reason to reject his westward alternative once the African route was succeeding.

The Portugal episode carries that route around the Cape with [[BartolomeuDias]] and to India with [[VascoDaGama]]. Expansion then changes from exploratory passage into [[PortugueseFortifiedMaritimeNetwork|a fortified network of coastal nodes]] under [[AfonsoDeAlbuquerque]], driven by [[PortugueseCrusadingCommercialExpansion|interlocking commercial and crusading aims]]. The navigational achievement is inseparable from attrition, coercion, massacre, enslavement, and forced conversion.

## Key Claims
- Ceuta matters as a narrative opening because it redirected Portuguese ambition across the strait and toward maritime routes.
- Expansion followed from both opportunity and constraint: Portugal's mainland frontier had stabilized while North Africa and Atlantic islands remained available targets.
- The 1415 victory revealed wealth and commercial networks, but occupation also damaged the port economy it had sought to control.
- Later memory can make the expansion look more planned and linear than the source says it was at the time.
- By the late fifteenth century, accumulated route knowledge and African progress made Portuguese strategy more than a speculative discovery program.
- Dias and da Gama converted accumulated Atlantic knowledge into a demonstrated Cape route and an Indian Ocean crossing.
- Fortified ports and trading posts projected power without large inland conquest, while commercial, royal, and Christian-war aims made violence structural to the system described by the source.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is not a complete history of Portuguese exploration. Ceuta is a conventional and symbolic starting point, not proof of a fully designed global strategy. The later episode is a fast Portuguese-centered narrative; its casualty estimates, motive attributions, and claims about the model's influence on Dutch and British empire remain source-scoped, while African and Asian perspectives are underdeveloped.

## What Changed
- Extended the concept from Ceuta and the Columbus decision through the Cape route and Indian Ocean network.
- Made coercion and crusading violence part of the expansion model rather than an incidental qualification.
- Distinguished coastal-node empire from extensive inland territorial settlement.
