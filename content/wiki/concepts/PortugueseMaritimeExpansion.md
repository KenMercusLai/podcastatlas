# Portuguese Maritime Expansion / 葡萄牙海上扩张

## Definition
Portuguese maritime expansion is the source's frame for Portugal's shift from mainland Iberian consolidation toward North Africa, Atlantic islands, African coasts, and eventually Indian Ocean routes.

## Current Synthesis
The sources treat the 1415 capture of [[Ceuta]] as the conventional first station in Portugal's overseas expansion story, but keep that claim qualified. Ceuta marks a turn from mainland Reconquista to sea-facing conquest and trade imagination; its commercial disappointment also shows that military capture did not guarantee control of exchange networks, and it does not prove that Portuguese leaders already had a fully formed global empire plan.

Part 1 adds the technical and coercive transition from North Africa into the Atlantic. Sagres knowledge exchange, caravels, winds, islands, and experiments with African rivers developed beside militant Christianity, captive raiding, sugar, and [[EarlyPortugueseAtlanticPlantationSlavery]]. The result is not a clean exploration story but a route system in which knowledge and violence grew together.

The Columbus episode shows the system a generation later as a practical network of Atlantic islands, West African routes, trading posts, fortresses, plantation slavery, winds, and currents. It trained [[ChristopherColumbus]] but also gave [[JoaoIIOfPortugal|João II]] reason to reject his westward alternative once the African route was succeeding.

The Portugal episode carries that route around the Cape with [[BartolomeuDias]] and to India with [[VascoDaGama]]. Expansion then changes from exploratory passage into [[PortugueseFortifiedMaritimeNetwork|a fortified network of coastal nodes]] under [[AfonsoDeAlbuquerque]], driven by [[PortugueseCrusadingCommercialExpansion|interlocking commercial and crusading aims]]. The navigational achievement is inseparable from attrition, coercion, massacre, enslavement, and forced conversion.

## Key Claims
- Ceuta matters as a narrative opening because it redirected Portuguese ambition across the strait and toward maritime routes.
- Expansion followed from both opportunity and constraint: Portugal's mainland frontier had stabilized while North Africa and Atlantic islands remained available targets.
- The 1415 victory revealed wealth and commercial networks, but occupation also damaged the port economy it had sought to control.
- Maritime knowledge, ship design, island bases, and route learning accumulated incrementally rather than appearing in one breakthrough.
- Sugar plantations and African captive trading were early structural components of Atlantic expansion.
- By the late fifteenth century, accumulated route knowledge and African progress made Portuguese strategy more than a speculative discovery program.
- Dias and da Gama converted accumulated Atlantic knowledge into a demonstrated Cape route and an Indian Ocean crossing.

- Commercial evidence: [[496-yiminchao-beihou-de-xiuda-cong-gudian-shidai-manzu-duhai-yibiliya-shoufu-shidi-yundong-dao-putaoya-dahanghai-lu8pawnkiselmmrgubrey4pq-4fw]] says Portuguese forces were struck by Ceuta's shops, luxury goods, and wealth.
- Qualification evidence: [[496-yiminchao-beihou-de-xiuda-cong-gudian-shidai-manzu-duhai-yibiliya-shoufu-shidi-yundong-dao-putaoya-dahanghai-lu8pawnkiselmmrgubrey4pq-4fw]] says the hosts reserve judgment on whether 1415 already contained a conscious global conquest design.
- Mature network evidence: [[306-columbus-the-adventure-begins-part-1-glt6089901915]] connects Atlantic islands, African trade, plantation slavery, navigation, and Dias's Cape route to João II's rejection of Columbus.
- Route-completion evidence: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] follows Dias around the Cape and da Gama from East Africa to Calicut.
- Network evidence: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] connects Cannanore, Cochin, Goa, and Malacca to a sea-based system of fortified coastal positions.
- Violence evidence: [[228-portugal-the-golden-age-of-discovery-part-2-glt7949934800]] describes attacks on pilgrims, fishermen, and Muslim populations alongside trade and navigation.
- Early maritime evidence: [[227-portugal-on-the-edge-of-the-world-part-1-glt1834337589]] links Sagres, caravels, Atlantic islands, and African-route experimentation.
- Early coercion evidence: [[227-portugal-on-the-edge-of-the-world-part-1-glt1834337589]] connects papal authorization, Arguin captive raiding, Lisbon sales, sugar, and island plantation labor.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept is not a complete history of Portuguese exploration. Ceuta is a conventional and symbolic starting point, not proof of a fully designed global strategy. Sagres's institutional character, individual sponsorship, captive totals, and participant motives remain source-scoped. The episodes are fast Portuguese-centered narratives whose African and Asian perspectives are underdeveloped; later casualty estimates and claims about Dutch and British imitation also remain bounded.

## What Changed
- Added the Sagres-caravel-island knowledge sequence before the Cape route.
- Integrated early African captive trading and Atlantic plantation labor into the expansion model.
- Added Ceuta's commercial disappointment as evidence against a simple conquest-equals-control story.
