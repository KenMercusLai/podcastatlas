# Massacre of the Innocents

## Definition

The Massacre of the Innocents is Matthew's account of [[HerodTheGreat|Herod the Great]] ordering the killing of young boys around Bethlehem after the visiting wise men fail to identify the infant [[Jesus]], later commemorated by the church on Childermas as the death of early Christian martyrs.

## Current Synthesis

The episode distinguishes narrative meaning from historical confidence. The account appears in Matthew but not the other canonical Gospels or Josephus, and its December 28 commemoration sits awkwardly before the traditional January 6 visit of the wise men. These problems support doubt about the event as recoverable history.

At the same time, the story is intelligible as sacred narrative. A threatened child, a murderous ruler, Egypt, escape, and return echo Pharaoh, [[Moses]], and Exodus, placing Jesus inside Israel's scriptural pattern. Herod's documented reputation for killing dynastic threats makes the characterization plausible, but plausibility is not corroboration. Later martyr commemoration and the Coventry Carol show how the narrative acquired meanings beyond the question of original occurrence.

## Key Claims

- The episode judges the massacre historically doubtful because it lacks canonical-Gospel and Josephus corroboration outside Matthew.
- Liturgical dates organize commemoration rather than supplying a coherent event chronology.
- Matthew's Moses and Exodus echoes provide a strong literary-theological function for the story.
- Herod's violent dynastic reputation explains narrative plausibility but cannot prove the specific killings.
- The Innocents' status as martyrs turns victimhood before conscious confession into a theological claim.
- The Coventry Carol demonstrates the story's later capacity to carry grief about endangered children and wartime destruction.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Silence in Josephus and the other Gospels is strong negative evidence in the episode, not direct proof that no local killing occurred. The source is a short conversational treatment rather than a comprehensive study of Matthew, Herodian chronology, Bethlehem's population, patristic tradition, liturgy, or modern biblical scholarship. The Moses parallel is a literary interpretation; theological significance and secular historical recoverability remain separate questions.

## What Changed

- Established the massacre as a case where low historical confidence can coexist with clear scriptural and commemorative function.
- Distinguished Herod's character plausibility from independent evidence for an event.
- Connected martyr memory to later cultural mourning through the Coventry Carol.
