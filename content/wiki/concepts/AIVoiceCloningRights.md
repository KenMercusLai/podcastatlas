# AI Voice Cloning Rights

## Definition
AI voice cloning rights are the consent, contract, attribution, disclosure, evidence, and enforcement questions raised when a person's voice can be learned, cloned, blended, licensed, localized, or reused by AI systems.

## Current Synthesis
The current synthesis separates four layers that often blur together. Performers and speakers need explicit contractual control over whether recordings can be used for learning, cloning, training, or downstream synthetic performance. Audiences still need disclosure when synthetic voices are used, because an authorized clone can still change trust in a podcast, film, ecommerce, or interactive media experience. Platforms then need technical and commercial infrastructure: authentication, tracing, moderation, detection, blocked abuse cases, and revenue sharing are what make voice licensing plausible without turning voice archives into open impersonation material.

In commerce, voice misuse can carry endorsement even when a face is masked, cropped, altered, or absent, and it can steer consumers toward goods associated with the copied celebrity or creator. Voice-rights governance therefore has to connect consent and proof to platform product listings, livestreams, account identity, and authorization records. The assistive-speech source adds that voice rights are also a continuity and portability problem: a person who records a voice before surgery should retain control over storage, provider transfer, model training, and later recreation rather than becoming locked to the company holding the archive.

## Key Claims
- Voice consent should be explicit and scoped, not inferred from ordinary recording, performance, or podcast participation.
- Blended or transformed voices create attribution and evidence problems because similarity alone may not identify the source voice or percentage contribution.
- Audience disclosure matters even when the voice owner has consented, because synthetic speech changes the listener relationship.
- Voice marketplaces can turn synthetic voice into licensed commercial inventory only if authentication, compensation, usage limits, and takedown rules are credible.
- Detection and provenance are part of rights enforcement, not optional technical extras.
- Voice can become a fallback infringement signal when visual likeness is degraded or deliberately hidden.
- Commerce platforms need voice-rights data and authorization scope to avoid impersonation-based selling and mistaken takedowns, while voice archives should remain portable across providers with voluntary rather than presumed training participation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Platform safety claims are source-scoped and do not prove that detection, tracing, or moderation catches downstream misuse after audio leaves the platform. Voice law also remains unsettled across jurisdictions, especially for blended voices, estate-controlled voices, parody, multilingual localization, archival training, ecommerce endorsement, provider portability, and audience disclosure standards. More shared data can improve models, but assistive need does not erase consent, security, or the sensitivity of conversations captured in voice archives.

## What Changed
- Added ownership and provider portability as assistive continuity requirements for voice recorded before speech loss.
- Made voluntary training participation and secure storage explicit even when pooled data could improve restoration models.
