## Definition

Moral licensing is the tendency to use a good identity, mission, or prior moral action as permission to behave less ethically, excuse misconduct, or reduce scrutiny elsewhere.

## Current Synthesis

[[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-8ba2ac9ba457581786d577922ac762fc-50949639|When Doing Right Feels Wrong]] uses [[AdamWaytz]]'s explanation of moral licensing to interpret mission-driven organizations. Members may reason that because the organization does valuable work, exposing misconduct would harm the greater good, or that existing virtue makes a particular violation less important.

The danger is not mission itself but conversion of moral credit into exemption. A police department, public-service body, charity, or values-led company can protect misconduct when loyalty to its good identity outranks evidence about actual conduct. [[WhistleblowingAsMissionLoyalty]] supplies the corrective frame: the mission should increase the duty to address betrayal of that mission, not decrease scrutiny.

The source provides an explanatory idea rather than a complete evidence base. Claims about particular people or institutions still require direct evidence; a virtuous identity does not prove licensing, and a single failure does not erase all legitimate good work.

## Key Claims

- Prior virtue or a good self-concept can become a rationale for relaxing ethical scrutiny.
- Mission-driven organizations are vulnerable when members treat disclosure as damage to the greater good.
- Moral identity is not reliable evidence that a specific action is ethical.
- Loyalty to mission should increase accountability for conduct that undermines the mission.
- Licensing is a hypothesis about rationalization, not proof of motive in any individual case.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The supplied source is a structured episode summary rather than the underlying moral-licensing literature, an organizational dataset, or evidence about prevalence and effect size. Good actions and missions can be sincere and consequential; they should not automatically be dismissed as reputation cover. Inferring licensing from inconsistency alone risks mind-reading unless the rationalization process is supported by evidence.
