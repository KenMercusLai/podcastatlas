# Confederate Cost-Imposition Strategy

## Definition

Confederate cost-imposition strategy is the route to independence through making continued Union war so costly in casualties, morale, and politics that Northern voters and leaders would accept negotiation before Confederate military resistance collapsed.

## Current Synthesis

[[202-american-civil-war-gettysburg-part-3-glt5256515778]] uses this framework to explain why Confederate victory still appeared possible in 1863 despite Union material superiority. [[RobertELee|Robert E. Lee]]'s Pennsylvania invasion could relieve Virginia, gather resources, threaten Northern places, and magnify the political damage of another Union defeat.

[[BattleOfGettysburg|Gettysburg]] and [[SiegeOfVicksburg|Vicksburg]] narrowed that route, but the 1864 election kept it open. The episode treats a potential Lincoln defeat and negotiated armistice as the Confederacy's final plausible political path; Atlanta's fall then strengthened Lincoln and made that outcome less likely.

## Key Claims

- Material inferiority does not preclude victory when the stronger side's political willingness is limited.
- Invasion can target morale and electoral legitimacy as well as territory and armies.
- Battlefield defeat matters asymmetrically when the weaker side cannot replace losses as readily.
- Strategic turning points narrow options without making later political outcomes inevitable.
- The 1864 election linked battlefield developments directly to the survival of emancipation policy and Confederate hopes.

## Evidence

### Political route to victory

- [[202-american-civil-war-gettysburg-part-3-glt5256515778]] attributes Confederate hope to raising the war's price beyond what the North would continue to pay.

### Northern invasion

- [[202-american-civil-war-gettysburg-part-3-glt5256515778]] connects Lee's campaign to Northern morale, relief of Virginia, resource gathering, and possible pressure on major cities.

### Election and battlefield feedback

- [[202-american-civil-war-gettysburg-part-3-glt5256515778]] presents Atlanta's fall as a major boost to Lincoln after he had feared electoral defeat and treats a McClellan victory as threatening emancipation policy.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The framework explains a plausible Confederate route rather than proving that independence was likely. Lee's precise objectives in Pennsylvania, the effect of a Gettysburg victory, McClellan's possible conduct in office, and Atlanta's exact electoral weight are counterfactual or interpretive. Union resource advantage, Confederate internal strain, emancipation, and territorial losses constrain but do not by themselves settle the political analysis.

## What Changed

- Established cost imposition as the link among Lee's invasion, unequal resources, battlefield setbacks, Northern morale, and the 1864 election.
