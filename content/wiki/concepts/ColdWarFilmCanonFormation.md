## Definition
Cold War film canon formation is the process by which geopolitical blocs, state selection, festival programming, distribution access, and later scholarship made some national films internationally canonical while rendering others difficult to see or write into world-film history.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded source treats the world-film canon as a reception outcome rather than a transparent ranking of artistic merit. Western festivals offered crucial access to Polish work, while socialist cultural agreements created a separate circulation network. Both systems selected. Films withheld from export, not submitted, censored, or unavailable to scholars could disappear from comparative histories even when locally significant; later restoration and curation can partially revise, but not erase, that inherited map.

## Key Claims
- Canonical visibility reflects selection and access as well as artistic achievement.
- Festival prestige and bloc exchange created distinct but overlapping reception pathways.
- Absence from foreign scholarship may reflect non-circulation rather than low domestic importance.
- Restoration and retrospective work can expose historical blind spots without producing a neutral final canon.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Selection bias does not imply that festival-recognized films lack merit, nor that every unexported film is an overlooked masterpiece. The relative causal weight of politics, taste, subtitling, print availability, scholarship, and audience demand requires case-specific evidence. The concept is grounded here in one national example.
