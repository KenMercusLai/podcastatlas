# Supplement Label Accuracy

## Definition
Supplement label accuracy is the requirement that a product's actual ingredients, identity, and amounts match its package before efficacy or safety claims can be meaningfully assessed.

## Current Synthesis
Label accuracy is a first-order consumer and experiment-design problem. If a capsule contains too little, too much, the wrong botanical, or an undeclared contaminant, a user cannot reliably attribute benefit, absence of effect, or harm to the stated ingredient or dose.

The Planet Money source documents broad variation in tested turmeric, echinacea, and elderberry products and distinguishes content verification from efficacy. The Huberman Lab episode applies the same concern to melatonin: reported mismatch between labels and contents weakens routine self-experimentation even before questions about sleep maintenance, hormone exposure, or childhood use are considered.

Independent testing can reduce identity, dose, and contamination uncertainty, but a verification mark does not prove that the ingredient improves health or fits a person's needs. Label confidence therefore belongs alongside [[TargetedSupplementNeedAssessment]], [[ThirdPartySupplementTesting]], and clinical context rather than substituting for them.

## Key Claims
- Label accuracy is necessary for interpreting efficacy, dose, adverse effects, and interactions.
- Missing, substituted, under-dosed, over-dosed, or contaminated contents can convert uncertain benefit into direct risk.
- Product-content uncertainty is especially consequential for hormones or potent bioactive compounds such as melatonin.
- Independent testing can reduce content uncertainty but cannot establish health benefit or personal need.
- Single-ingredient trials are only interpretable when the product reliably contains the named ingredient at the stated amount.

## Evidence
- Cross-product variation - [[why-is-there-a-supplement-craze-if-they-dont-even-work]] cites ConsumerLab testing that reportedly found large differences across turmeric, echinacea, and elderberry products, including products without authentic elderberry.
- Regulatory and verification boundary - [[why-is-there-a-supplement-craze-if-they-dont-even-work]] distinguishes weaker supplement assurance from food-label spot checks and describes NSF or USP verification as content protection rather than proof of efficacy.
- Melatonin relevance - [[developing-a-rational-approach-to-supplementation-for-health-performance-scim5857890354]] reports concern that melatonin products may contain materially different amounts from their labels, compounding routine-use and pediatric caution.
- Experiment attribution - [[developing-a-rational-approach-to-supplementation-for-health-performance-scim5857890354]] favors one-ingredient-at-a-time testing and minimum effective doses, a method that depends on accurate product identity and quantity.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source notes do not reproduce laboratory reports, sampling frames, brand-level results, assay methods, or current market-wide failure rates. Reported product variation should not be generalized to every supplement or manufacturer. Certification scope varies, and even accurate identity and contaminant testing do not establish bioavailability, effectiveness, appropriate dosing, interaction safety, or suitability for an individual.
