## Definition
Procedureless political purge is the pattern in which a regime removes a defeated power network through criminal punishment without producing visible proof, charge specificity, interrogation, or proportional settlement. The core problem is not that political cleanup is always illegitimate, but that punishment presented as law becomes unstable when it cannot show how guilt was tested.

## Current Synthesis
[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-795-dengjia-miewang-hou-weihe-juchao-wei-tamen-mingyuan-lqvmbqrrookphb4becaqbp7h-wi1|Hanji 795]] makes the concept visible through the fall of [[EmpressDowagerDengLateHan|Empress Dowager Deng]]'s household. [[EmperorAnOfHan|Emperor An]] and anti-Deng actors have an intelligible reason to reduce [[OuterRelativeCourtPower|outer-relative power]] after a regency, but [[ZhuChongLateHan|Zhu Chong]] argues that the actual process rests on one-sided palace testimony, no confrontation, unclear evidence, and excessive household destruction.

The result is a partial legitimacy failure. The purge is not overturned, but public mourning and Zhu Chong's coffin memorial force Emperor An to limit its aftermath by permitting burial and survivor return. The same episode then shows a second failure: the Deng vacuum is filled by Geng, Song, Yan, eunuch, wet-nurse, and favorite networks, so procedureless cleanup does not restore public appointment order.

## Key Claims
- Political cleanup after a regency can be structurally understandable while still failing as lawful punishment.
- Evidence defects become politically dangerous when the accused household is punished through death, burial disruption, and kinship ruin.
- Public remonstrance can force limited correction without producing full rehabilitation.
- Procedureless purge is especially unstable when the winners immediately reproduce similar access problems through new kin and favorites.
- The concept links legal-process critique to court-feedback failure: evidence problems surface only after costly, symbolic, and risky protest.

## Evidence
- Deng purge as defective adjudication: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-795-dengjia-miewang-hou-weihe-juchao-wei-tamen-mingyuan-lqvmbqrrookphb4becaqbp7h-wi1|Hanji 795]] reports that Zhu Chong challenges the anti-Deng case because it relies on palace testimony without confrontation or clear proof.
- Household punishment and ritual damage: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-795-dengjia-miewang-hou-weihe-juchao-wei-tamen-mingyuan-lqvmbqrrookphb4becaqbp7h-wi1|Hanji 795]] says multiple Deng family members die and burial/survivor arrangements become part of the remonstrance.
- Partial correction under pressure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-795-dengjia-miewang-hou-weihe-juchao-wei-tamen-mingyuan-lqvmbqrrookphb4becaqbp7h-wi1|Hanji 795]] links public mourning and Zhu Chong's memorial to Emperor An's limited retreat.
- New favorite replacement: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-795-dengjia-miewang-hou-weihe-juchao-wei-tamen-mingyuan-lqvmbqrrookphb4becaqbp7h-wi1|Hanji 795]] shows Geng, Song, Yan, Jiang Jing, Li Run, Wang Sheng, Bo Rong, and Fan Feng gaining access after the Deng purge.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The current evidence is one case. It does not prove that every purge lacking transparent process is identical, nor does it show that the Deng household had no political liability at all. The concept should be applied narrowly: the marker is the combination of power-transfer punishment, weak visible evidence, blocked confrontation, and disproportionate kinship or ritual consequences.

## What Changed
- Created this concept from Hanji 795 to separate evidentiary/procedural purge failure from broader outer-relative politics.
- Added the Deng purge as the initial case where public remonstrance wins partial correction but not full vindication.
