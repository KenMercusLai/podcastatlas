# Insect Bite Dermatitis

## Definition
Insect bite dermatitis is a broad dermatology frame for inflammatory, allergic, irritant, or toxin-mediated skin reactions caused by mosquitoes, midges, caterpillars, rove beetles, mites, ticks, or similar bite and contact exposures.

## Current Synthesis
“Bug bite” is too blunt. The same exposure label can cover ordinary mosquito saliva reactions, stronger midge-like allergy, retained caterpillar hairs, rove-beetle contact injury, tick attachment, and mite or chigger bites that raise infection-risk questions.

The combined sources sharpen the rove-beetle branch. Injury can occur when an insect is crushed, flicked, or pressured against skin and releases irritating fluid; the safer response is to blow or shake it off, avoid spreading residue with the hands, and wash exposed skin promptly. Rapid burning, pain, linear or irregular redness, blisters, erosion, eye-area involvement, infection, or deeper tissue loss moves the problem beyond casual itch relief and may leave pigmentation or scarring.

## Key Claims
- Insect-related dermatitis is heterogeneous; organism and exposure mechanism matter more than the generic label “bite.”
- Ordinary swelling is often allergic or inflammatory, while rove-beetle injury is presented as irritant or toxin contact rather than biting.
- Rove beetles should be blown or shaken off rather than crushed or flicked; contacted skin should be washed promptly without transferring residue to the face or eyes.
- Blistering, persistent pain, broken skin, eye-area involvement, infection, pigmentation, or scarring can make the consequences more serious than ordinary itch.
- Individual response and exposure intensity affect severity, while fever, eschar, retained tick parts, or neurological symptoms raise different infectious or procedural concerns.
- Prevention depends on organism and context, including screens, lighting, clothing, bed checks, and exposure history rather than one universal repellent strategy.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not turn insect-related dermatitis into one diagnosis or treatment. They differ in their simplified biochemical description of rove-beetle toxin, so the precise mechanism remains source-scoped even though both support a contact-injury model. Organism, exposure route, severity, skin breakage, eye involvement, fever, eschar, tick attachment, and patient context remain reasons to seek clinical judgment rather than rely on a general anti-itch routine.

## What Changed
- Rove-beetle dermatitis is now separated more clearly from biting and ordinary allergic itch.
- Added blow-or-shake removal, prompt washing, household prevention, and eye, infection, pigmentation, scar, and tissue-loss escalation boundaries.
- Preserved disagreement in the sources' simplified toxin-mechanism descriptions instead of flattening it.
