# Roman Conquest of Britain

## Definition

The Roman conquest of Britain is the prolonged process beginning with the Claudian invasion of AD 43, in which Rome joined military superiority, client politics, elite collaboration, colonization, extraction, terror, and public spectacle to create [[RomanBritain]].

## Current Synthesis

Episode 500 rejects a simple picture of Rome suddenly attacking an isolated island. Southeastern Britain already had trade, coinage, rulers in diplomatic contact with Rome, and competing tribal powers. [[Caratacus]]'s displacement of the Roman-backed [[Verica]] supplied [[Claudius]] with a usable grievance, while preparations inherited from Caligula supplied much of the expedition's capacity.

[[AulusPlautius]] won the operational campaign at the Medway and Thames, but Claudius's arrival shows that conquest was also political theater. Victory allowed an insecure emperor to receive submissions, stage Britannia's domination, and claim the identity of imperator. Yet conquest did not end at Camulodunon: collaboration brought rewards to some elites, colonies and garrisons imposed new burdens, Caratacus continued resistance in Wales, and the Anglesey campaign flowed directly into the [[BoudiccanRevolt]].

## Key Claims

- Invasion grew from an already connected frontier of trade, diplomacy, client kings, and tribal competition.
- Protection of a displaced client supplied a public justification that aligned with Claudius's private legitimacy needs.
- Professional command, logistics, auxiliaries, armor, and discipline gave Rome operational advantages over formidable British forces.
- Imperial victory required staging and narration as well as battlefield success.
- Collaboration and resistance were structured choices under unequal power, not fixed ethnic dispositions.
- The conquest was a long, uneven process rather than a single AD 43 event.

- Trigger and motive: [[500-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-the-empire-strikes-back-part-2-glt3137178317]] joins Verica's exile to Claudius's need for military legitimacy.
- Operational conquest: [[500-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-the-empire-strikes-back-part-2-glt3137178317]] describes the crossing, Medway and Thames battles, Batavian auxiliaries, and advance on Camulodunon.
- Victory spectacle: [[500-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-the-empire-strikes-back-part-2-glt3137178317]] distinguishes Plautius's command from Claudius's submissions, triumph, arch, title, and public reenactment.
- Unequal settlement: [[500-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-the-empire-strikes-back-part-2-glt3137178317]] contrasts rewards for Togidubnus and urbanizing elites with occupation abuse and colonia settlement.
- Prolonged resistance: [[500-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-the-empire-strikes-back-part-2-glt3137178317]] follows Caratacus, the Silures, Ordovices, and Anglesey into AD 60.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The narrative is reconstructed largely from Roman texts, coinage, and archaeology rather than British written testimony. Roman claims about barbarism, Druids, sacrifice, submission, and civilization could justify conquest; force totals, routes, landing sites, battle locations, and individual motives remain uncertain. Collaboration could be strategic or coerced, and Romanization should not be treated as passive cultural replacement.

## What Changed

- Created a conquest framework joining pre-invasion entanglement, military operations, legitimacy, and prolonged resistance.
- Distinguished the AD 43 victory claim from the longer process of occupation.
- Added collaboration and imperial spectacle as mechanisms of conquest.
