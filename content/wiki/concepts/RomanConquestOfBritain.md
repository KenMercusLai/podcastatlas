# Roman Conquest of Britain

## Definition

The Roman conquest of Britain is the prolonged process in which Caesar's 55–54 BC interventions created precedent and client leverage before the Claudian invasion of AD 43 joined military superiority, collaboration, colonization, extraction, terror, and public spectacle to create [[RomanBritain]].

## Current Synthesis

Episode 499 rejects a clean starting point in AD 43. [[JuliusCaesar]] crossed to an island already linked to Gaul through trade and politics. His first campaign established only a precarious beachhead; the second used [[Mandubracius]], the [[Trinovantes]], and [[Commius]] against [[Cassivellaunus]]. Caesar gained hostages, promised tribute, captives, and prestige without permanent occupation, showing that intervention and conquest were not the same achievement.

Episode 500 then rejects a picture of Rome suddenly attacking an isolated island. Southeastern Britain had nearly another century of trade, coinage, diplomacy, client rulers, and competing powers. [[Caratacus]]'s displacement of the Roman-backed [[Verica]] supplied [[Claudius]] with a usable grievance, while preparations inherited from Caligula supplied much of the expedition's capacity.

[[AulusPlautius]] won the operational campaign at the Medway and Thames, but Claudius's arrival shows that conquest was also political theater. Victory allowed an insecure emperor to receive submissions, stage Britannia's domination, and claim the identity of imperator. Yet conquest did not end at Camulodunon: collaboration brought rewards to some elites, colonies and garrisons imposed new burdens, Caratacus continued resistance in Wales, and the Anglesey campaign flowed directly into the [[BoudiccanRevolt]].

## Key Claims

- Invasion grew from an already connected frontier of trade, diplomacy, client kings, and tribal competition.
- Caesar's campaigns created coercive and diplomatic precedent but lacked the continuity required for provincial conquest.
- Protection of a displaced client supplied a public justification that aligned with Claudius's private legitimacy needs.
- Professional command, logistics, auxiliaries, armor, and discipline gave Rome operational advantages over formidable British forces.
- Imperial victory required staging and narration as well as battlefield success.
- Collaboration and resistance were structured choices under unequal power, not fixed ethnic dispositions.
- The conquest was a long, uneven process rather than a single AD 43 event.

- Trigger and motive: [[500-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-the-empire-strikes-back-part-2-glt3137178317]] joins Verica's exile to Claudius's need for military legitimacy.
- Operational conquest: [[500-the-roman-conquest-of-britain-the-empire-strikes-back-part-2-glt3137178317]] describes the crossing, Medway and Thames battles, Batavian auxiliaries, and advance on Camulodunon.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The narrative is reconstructed largely from Roman texts, coinage, and archaeology rather than British written testimony. Caesar's own account makes his expeditions especially vulnerable to self-promotional distortion. Roman claims about barbarism, Druids, sacrifice, submission, and civilization could justify conquest; force totals, routes, landing sites, battle locations, tribute, and individual motives remain uncertain. Collaboration could be strategic or coerced, and Romanization should not be treated as passive cultural replacement. Caesar's southern interventions and Agricola's northern campaigns both show why military penetration should not be equated automatically with durable rule.

## What Changed

- Extended the framework backward to Caesar's 55 and 54 BC expeditions.
- Distinguished intervention, hostage-taking, tribute promises, and prestige from provincial occupation.
- Added Mandubracius, Cassivellaunus, Commius, and the Trinovantes as an earlier client-politics case.
