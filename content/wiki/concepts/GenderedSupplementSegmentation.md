# Gendered Supplement Segmentation / 性别细分保健品

## Definition
Gendered supplement segmentation is the practice of splitting a standard multivitamin into women's, men's, and life-stage variants so that an industrial product feels individually targeted.

## Current Synthesis
The source uses [[Swisse|Swisse]]'s 1991 women's multivitamin and 1995 men's multivitamin as the category's model case, and argues that the formulation difference between men's and women's versions may not be large while the psychological effect is: a segmented product feels more specific and therefore more effective. The hosts place the idea in a longer U.S. lineage, where a 1940s industrial brand advertised a single daily tablet and successors kept adding age, life-stage, and health-goal variants, creating a consumer expectation that a supplement should be both effective and convenient.

The pattern matters for the wiki because it shows how a standardised product can be re-differentiated without new evidence. Segmentation supplies the novelty, natural-sounding ingredients supply the credibility, and the underlying product architecture stays largely American and industrial - which the episode reads as Swisse splicing a U.S. product structure onto a more European, natural-sounding brand tone.

## Key Claims
- Sex-based product lines are primarily a positioning device, with the source sceptical that the formulations differ meaningfully.
- Perceived targeting raises the sense of personal relevance, which is what the segmentation is designed to create.
- The base product architecture predates the brand: the U.S. "one a day" multivitamin already existed in the 1940s.
- Segmentation keeps refreshing demand through age, life-stage, and health-goal variants rather than through new efficacy evidence.
- Natural-source ingredients and extracts such as grapeseed are the complementary differentiation claim.
- Together, segmentation and naturalness let an industrial product carry a personalised, quasi-natural brand story.

## Evidence
- Product case: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] dates Swisse's women's multivitamin to 1991 and the men's version to 1995.
- Positioning judgement: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] argues gender differentiation may not matter much at the formulation level while making the product feel more targeted and effective.
- U.S. lineage: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] links the pattern to 1940s industrial multivitamins and later age-, life-stage-, and goal-based variants.
- Differentiation claim: [[vol-275-aozhou-baojianpin-shi-zenme-huoqilai-de-1015773861]] describes natural vitamin sources and grapeseed extract as Swisse's relative distinctiveness.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode's judgement that gendered formulations are largely cosmetic is a podcast opinion, not a product-by-product comparison; some nutrient requirements genuinely differ by sex and life stage, so the honest claim is that segmentation is a marketing architecture whose physiological justification varies by nutrient and must be checked individually. The source also gives no price, margin, or sales-by-variant data, so the commercial effect of segmentation is inferred rather than measured.

## What Changed
- Created the product-segmentation concept behind the multivitamin brand story.
