# College Ranking Privilege

## Definition

College ranking privilege is the tendency of a ranking system to treat institutional wealth, student advantage, selectivity, completion, and inherited reputation as educational quality, thereby rewarding colleges for whom they enroll and what they already possess more than for what students learn or become.

## Current Synthesis

[[project-dillard-project-dillard|Project Dillard]] makes the mechanism visible through [[DillardUniversity]]. Peer reputation, endowment, tuition, spending, alumni giving, debt, graduation, selectivity, and test scores are not interchangeable, but many share a common relationship to institutional and family resources. Counting correlated advantages across several categories can make privilege appear repeatedly as independent evidence of quality.

The reputation input itself has an additional circularity problem. The [[ReedCollege]] reconstruction treats peer assessment as highly influential, while administrators acknowledge limited knowledge of many schools they score. Prior rankings, familiarity, age, scenery, athletics, anecdotes, and publicity can therefore help reproduce status inside a number that appears to measure academic quality. [[lord-of-the-rankings-lord-of-the-rankings]]

The resulting incentives can conflict with an access mission. A college may improve rank by admitting fewer, wealthier, more conventionally prepared students, spending on prestige signals, and avoiding applicants whose material circumstances make uninterrupted completion harder. The rank can then rise without demonstrating better teaching, while an institution that expands access can be penalized for serving students with fewer prior advantages.

The alternative is not to ignore resources or outcomes. It is to separate inputs, context, process, and contribution: what students bring, what constraints they face, what support the institution supplies, and how capability changes. Dillard's reported physics graduates and close support culture are suggestive evidence for that developmental view, not a complete replacement score.

## Key Claims

- Rankings can count the same underlying wealth advantage through several correlated variables.
- Peer reputation may reproduce visible wealth, demographic, and prestige signals rather than independent knowledge of teaching.
- Raw completion rates mix institutional performance with students' financial, family, employment, health, and housing conditions.
- Selectivity rewards excluding applicants, which can directly conflict with an access-oriented mission.
- A rank can change substantially through institutional finance and student composition without demonstrated classroom improvement.
- Educational quality should distinguish starting inputs from support, learning, attainment, and value added.
- Quantifying administrator reputation does not remove circularity or shared bias when raters rely on prior status and weak proxies.

## Evidence

- Repeated wealth signals - [[project-dillard-project-dillard]] identifies faculty resources, spending, alumni giving, indebtedness, tuition, and endowment as related ranking inputs.
- Reputation prediction - [[project-dillard-project-dillard]] reports that endowment predicted about half of peer assessment and that a wider institutional model predicted 91.3%.
- Completion context - [[project-dillard-project-dillard]] connects graduation interruption to housing, work, income, illness, and family instability.
- Incentive simulation - [[project-dillard-project-dillard]] reports large simulated ranking gains after substituting wealth, test scores, ratios, and faculty resources while shrinking access.
- Developmental counterexample - [[project-dillard-project-dillard]] contrasts Dillard's reported Black-physics production and relational support with its low rank.
- Limited peer knowledge - [[lord-of-the-rankings-lord-of-the-rankings]] reports that administrators rate hundreds of institutions despite sparse direct knowledge.
- Ranking circularity - [[lord-of-the-rankings-lord-of-the-rankings]] raises prior ranks and publicity as possible inputs to peer scores and reports no U.S. News research resolving the concern.
- Independent material analogy - [[lord-of-the-rankings-lord-of-the-rankings]] cites higher HBCU bond spreads as a separate example of racialized institutional reputation carrying cost.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Resources, completion, staffing, preparation, and informed professional reputation can affect students' experiences and should not be dismissed simply because they correlate with wealth. The sources do not provide the full ranking model, regression specifications, causal identification, uncertainty, rater-level data, or balanced comparison with alternative systems. Reed's mismatch does not prove retaliation, and the HBCU bond study is structurally analogous rather than direct evidence about peer scores. Value-added measurement also faces selection, data, time-horizon, comparability, gaming, and mission-difference problems. The episodes therefore support a construct-validity and incentive critique, not a definitive universal ranking formula.

## What Changed

- Created a focused concept separating institutional advantage from educational contribution.
- Identified access-versus-selectivity conflict and repeated counting of correlated wealth signals.
- Added circular, weakly informed peer reputation as a distinct route by which privilege becomes quantified quality.
