# 同姓王分封 / Same-Surname King Enfeoffment

同姓王分封 / same-surname king enfeoffment enters the wiki through [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-174-liubang-weihe-dafeng-zijide-chouren-1-lnzzvpieqtgatmvgogz3msyhs60u|Hanji 174]] as [[LiuBang|刘邦]]'s answer to the early Western Han regional-control problem. The source says Liu Bang's sons are young and his brothers are few, so he expands the usable Liu-family circle and grants large kingdoms to Liu relatives.

The immediate target is not abstract kin preference. Han Xin's former Chu kingdom is split into [[JingKingdomEarlyHan|荆国]] for [[LiuJia|刘贾]] and [[ChuKingdomEarlyHan|楚国]] for [[LiuJiao|刘交]]. [[LiuXiDaiKing|刘喜]] receives [[DaiKingdomEarlyHan|代国]], while [[LiuFeiQiKing|刘肥]] receives [[QiKingdomEarlyHan|齐国]]. The grants place family members over strategic regions just after [[DifferentSurnameKingSuspicion|异姓诸侯王猜忌]] has become visible.

The concept sits in tension with [[CommanderyCountyAntiEnfeoffment|郡县制反分封定局]], but it is not recorded here as a simple contradiction. Qin's anti-enfeoffment decision rejected hereditary royal fiefs after conquest; Hanji 174 shows early Han reintroducing royal kingdoms as a postwar control compromise while the imperial center is still managing contributors, borders, and former enemy regions.

## Key Claims
- Same-surname kingship is used as a trust substitute when different-surname kings look unsafe after victory.
- The early Han grant map combines family politics with territorial control over former wartime bases.
- Kinship does not remove regional autonomy risk; it only changes the ruler's first-order trust assumption.
- The move contrasts with Qin direct-rule ideology but arises from a different postwar settlement problem.
