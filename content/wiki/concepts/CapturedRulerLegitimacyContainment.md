# Captured Ruler Legitimacy Containment / 生俘亡君的合法性钳制

## Definition

Captured ruler legitimacy containment / 生俘亡君的合法性钳制 is the use of a defeated sovereign's surviving title, command authority, or symbolic position to constrain succession, immobilize resistance, collect resources, or govern indirectly.

## Current Synthesis

The mechanism has a title-containment form. After [[QinState|秦国]] captures [[Handan|邯郸]], [[ZhaoYoumiuWang|赵迁 / 赵幽缪王]] is moved away and kept alive. The host argues that his survival makes it harder for [[ZhaoJia|赵嘉]] to claim the Zhao kingship, helping explain why the remnant ruler calls himself king of Dai instead. [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-114-1-jintian-liaoliao-yingzheng-dou-you-naxie-canren-shouduan-lmqky6g3qi9nklpdnye4lk5rrbz6]]

The mechanism also has a command-leverage form. Captive [[Atahualpa]] can still order armies not to resist, mobilize gold and silver, direct Spanish expeditions toward rival centers, and draw [[Chalcuchima]] into custody. His office residue does not only block an alternative title; it actively transmits commands that help a much smaller force survive and expand. [[646-the-fall-of-the-incas-death-to-the-emperor-part-3-glt8131543734]]

The cases also expose the tactic's limit. Once the Spaniards treat Atahualpa's remaining authority as a rescue risk rather than a useful control mechanism, they execute him. The resulting succession vacuum then requires puppet rulers and leaves northern commanders active. Captivity can concentrate leverage, but killing the captive may exchange that leverage for fragmentation and legitimacy costs.

## Key Claims
- A conquered ruler can be more useful alive than dead when survival weakens rival claims or transmits commands.
- Captivity separates bodily control from institutional authority: the person is confined while the office still shapes armies, resources, ritual, and succession.
- The mechanism can support passive title containment or active indirect rule.
- The tactic is distinct from mercy because conquerors preserve authority residue for control rather than restoration.
- Execution ends the captive's leverage but can create succession fragmentation, resistance, and legitimacy costs.

## Evidence

- Blocked succession: [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-114-1-jintian-liaoliao-yingzheng-dou-you-naxie-canren-shouduan-lmqky6g3qi9nklpdnye4lk5rrbz6]] connects Zhao Qian's survival to Zhao Jia's use of a Dai title.
- Resource and military control: [[646-the-fall-of-the-incas-death-to-the-emperor-part-3-glt8131543734]] follows Atahualpa's ransom orders and commands restraining major generals.
- Command-mediated capture: [[646-the-fall-of-the-incas-death-to-the-emperor-part-3-glt8131543734]] links Atahualpa's authority to Chalcuchima entering custody despite superior forces.
- Leverage collapse: [[646-the-fall-of-the-incas-death-to-the-emperor-part-3-glt8131543734]] connects execution to a leaderless empire, royal criticism, and the search for a puppet ruler.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Both cases are source interpretations rather than direct proof of conquerors' complete intentions. Zhao Jia may have had several reasons to choose the Dai title, while Atahualpa's generals faced unfamiliar weapons, information gaps, civil-war division, and sacred hierarchy as well as explicit commands. The framework does not imply that captured rulers are fully autonomous or that preservation is humane. Nor does the Atahualpa case prove that continued captivity would have produced stable Spanish control.
