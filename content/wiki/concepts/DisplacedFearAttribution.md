# Displaced Fear Attribution

## Definition

Displaced fear attribution is the episode's framework for anxiety surviving the crisis that produced it and attaching to a new target without adequate causal evidence. The fear or symptoms can be genuine even when the attributed agent is not their demonstrated cause.

## Current Synthesis

The Belgian Coca-Cola scare supplies the clearest physical-symptom case. A recent dioxin contamination crisis had made food danger vivid; an unusual Coke odor then became the focus of nausea, headaches, trembling, and dizziness even though investigators reportedly found no toxic exposure sufficient to explain them. Mass sociogenic illness names the symptom-spread pattern, while displaced attribution explains why this product became the object of fear.

The [[MargitHamosh]] comparison extends the mechanism from public health to institutional accusation. AIDS-era suffering and distrust of medical authority created justified anxiety, but the episode argues that investigators and journalists redirected some of that demand for accountability toward weak scientific-fraud cases. The analogy is useful only at the level of misattributed threat: it does not make illness and misconduct procedure identical.

## Key Claims

- Real fear does not establish that the feared target is the true cause.
- Socially connected people can experience genuine physical symptoms without a toxic agent sufficient to explain them.
- A prior crisis can prime which later anomaly a public interprets as dangerous.
- Authorities and media can convert uncertainty into premature causal certainty through recall, headlines, leaks, and repetition.
- Correcting the attribution does not erase the bodily, reputational, or institutional harm already produced.

## Evidence

### Symptoms without demonstrated poisoning

- [[the-imaginary-crimes-of-margit-hamosh-the-imaginary-crimes-of-margit-hamosh]] reports genuine illness among Belgian students, trace odor-producing sulfur compounds, no toxin concentration sufficient to explain the symptoms, and a toxicologist's classification of mass sociogenic illness.

### Fear migrating between targets

- [[the-imaginary-crimes-of-margit-hamosh-the-imaginary-crimes-of-margit-hamosh]] places the Coke scare shortly after Belgium's dioxin crisis and places Hamosh's prosecution within AIDS-era distrust of scientists and medical institutions.

### Harm after weak attribution

- [[the-imaginary-crimes-of-margit-hamosh-the-imaginary-crimes-of-margit-hamosh]] connects the two cases to a vast product recall, public alarm, leaked accusations, lasting stigma, and research time consumed by defense.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Temporal sequence and thematic resemblance do not independently prove fear displacement. Unusual odors can justify precaution, genuine misconduct warrants investigation, and absence of a demonstrated toxin does not mean symptoms were imagined. The supplied source gives limited space to Belgian officials, Hamosh's accuser, investigators, or alternative causal accounts, and it does not provide epidemiological data or a comparative sample showing how often one crisis redirects fear toward another target.

## What Changed

- Established a framework separating genuine fear and harm from correct causal attribution.
- Connected mass sociogenic illness with institutional and media-driven accusation while preserving their differences.
- Identified prior crisis, salient anomaly, amplification, and persistent harm as the episode's proposed sequence.
