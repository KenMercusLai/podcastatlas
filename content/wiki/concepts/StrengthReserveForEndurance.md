# Strength Reserve for Endurance

## Definition
Strength reserve for endurance is the episode's training principle that increasing maximal force capacity can make each repeated submaximal movement a smaller percentage of what the athlete can produce.

## Current Synthesis
Edgley applies the principle to swimming: pure-strength work may lower the relative cost of each stroke and support connective-tissue robustness during very high repetition. The relevant comparison is not strength versus endurance but whether a carefully selected strength dose improves capacity without creating excessive hypertrophy, fatigue, soreness, mass, or recovery interference.

The source offers a rationale and athlete example rather than a tested prescription. Transfer depends on movement, technique, programming, injury history, recovery, and sport constraints. More maximal strength does not remove cardiovascular, metabolic, thermoregulatory, or skill limits.

## Key Claims
- The relative effort of a repeated movement can fall when maximal force capacity rises.
- Strength work can complement endurance training rather than belonging to a separate athletic category.
- Connective-tissue robustness is part of the proposed benefit for very high repetition.
- Low-repetition pure-strength work may create less endurance interference than higher-volume hypertrophy-oriented lifting.
- Transfer requires sport-relevant technique and does not erase other physiological bottlenecks.
- Programming must balance adaptation against fatigue, injury risk, body-mass effects, and recovery demand.

## Evidence

- Swimming application - [[scim9489463983-scim9489463983]] reports Edgley's continued strength practice to reduce the relative demand of each stroke.
- Tissue and recovery boundary - [[scim9489463983-scim9489463983]] links strength to connective-tissue robustness and contrasts focused strength work with more interfering hypertrophy volume.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The supplied summary provides no training program, study design, dose-response evidence, injury data, stroke-economy measurement, or comparison group. The Eddie Hall example is illustrative rather than swimming-specific evidence. Heavy lifting can increase injury risk and recovery demand, and sport-specific coaching or clinical guidance may be needed.

## What Changed
- Established a relative-capacity rationale for combining maximal strength with endurance preparation.
- Preserved recovery interference and transfer as programming constraints.
- Kept the principle distinct from a universal lifting prescription.
