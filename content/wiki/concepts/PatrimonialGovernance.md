# Patrimonial Governance

Patrimonial governance is the Weberian frame [[vol-123-telangpu-de-duideng-guanshui-an-buzhi-shi-yi-chang-daxing-fucongxing-ceshi-lm6bkiqgyyefwompxon7lo6cnqp6]] uses to interpret [[DonaldTrump]]'s governing style. Drawing on a [[TheAtlantic]] article and [[MaxWeber]], the episode contrasts rational-legal bureaucracy with a patrimonial style in which the state is treated like an extension of the ruler's household or personal domain.

The concept helps the source connect tariffs, bureaucracy, and loyalty politics. If agencies and officials are expected to serve the person of the ruler rather than an impersonal office, then anti-bureaucracy rhetoric, loyalty tests, personnel replacement, and market-access bargaining all fit a broader pattern of personal rule. The episode treats institutional constraint as possible, but more likely after economic damage becomes severe enough for other actors to push back.

## Key Claims
- Patrimonial governance is not just corruption or informality; it is a different legitimacy logic from rational-legal bureaucracy.
- The source uses it to explain why loyalty to [[DonaldTrump]] personally can matter more than loyalty to constitutional or administrative procedure.
- The concept overlaps with [[AdministrativeStateDismantling]], but it is not identical: dismantling bureaucracy can be an ideological project, while patrimonialism describes personal control over institutions.
- The tariff branch shows patrimonial governance in external policy: access to the U.S. market becomes a personal bargaining lever.
- The concept extends [[ExecutivePowerPrecedent]] and [[AmericanDemocraticResilience]] by asking whether temporary personal rule habits become reusable institutional precedent.
