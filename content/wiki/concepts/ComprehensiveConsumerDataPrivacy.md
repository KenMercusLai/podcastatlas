# Comprehensive Consumer Data Privacy

## Definition
Comprehensive consumer data privacy is a privacy-law approach that constrains collection, targeting, retention, sharing, and user control across data systems rather than relying only on narrow product bans, sector-specific statutes, or after-the-fact remedies.

## Current Synthesis
The concept began as an alternative to broad youth social-media bans: [[AaronMackey]] argues that platform harms should be addressed through limits on invasive data practices rather than by making lawful speech access depend on age verification. Later sources widen the frame to child-bedroom monitoring, federal data trust, and DHS/ICE DNA collection, showing the need for purpose, retention, and accountability limits where people cannot realistically opt out. The [[VideoPrivacyProtectionAct]] dispute adds an institutional reason for comprehensiveness: when no general federal framework exists, plaintiffs and courts press older sectoral statutes into modern analytics and advertising disputes, producing both coverage opportunities and category mismatch.

## Key Claims
- Collection and targeting limits can address platform harms without turning speech access into an identity-check system.
- Transparency and user control are incomplete unless paired with purpose limits, retention limits, accountability, and workable recourse.
- Sensitive data includes ad profiles, child-bedroom and health-adjacent signals, benefit data, viewing activity, and bodily identity data.
- Public-sector privacy belongs in the synthesis when agency data use can chill benefits, immigration safety, or civil liberties.
- Comprehensive rules can reduce dependence on case-by-case product-liability claims and narrow legacy statutes whose categories fit the modern internet unevenly.
- Consent remains one control rather than a complete privacy regime, especially when repeated prompts impede access or delegation obscures who authorized an action.

## Evidence
- Platform regulation alternative - [[tech-20260406-0406-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260406-0406-mp-tech-pod-128]] presents comprehensive privacy as EFF's preferred route over under-16 social-media bans and parental-consent gates.
- Public-sector trust - [[tech-20260409-0409-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260409-0409-mp-tech-pod-128]] says federal data practices and enforcement-agency sharing can discourage public-benefit use.
- Child-bedroom data - [[tech-20260811-0811-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260811-0811-mp-tech-pod-128]] shows subscription baby tech collecting and interpreting intimate household and child data beyond ordinary ad targeting.
- Bodily identity data - [[tech-20260831-0831-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260831-0831-mp-tech-pod-128]] extends the sensitivity ladder to compelled DNA collection, indefinite CODIS retention concerns, and relatives' exposure.
- Sectoral-law gap - [[tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261116-1116-mp-tech-pod-128]] shows plaintiffs using a 1988 video statute against contemporary analytics and advertising amid the absence of comprehensive federal data-privacy law.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept's title remains consumer-focused, while some evidence is public-sector or law-enforcement centered. That does not make every government biometric practice a consumer privacy issue; it shows that general privacy principles are incomplete if they exclude non-optional data relationships. Comprehensive legislation would not automatically resolve constitutional limits, enforcement quality, technical security, sector-specific harms, or the meaning of valid consent. The VPPA source also cautions that analytics and targeting may support functionality users value, so a general framework still needs proportional rules rather than assuming every data flow is equivalent.

## What Changed
- Added reliance on legacy sectoral statutes as evidence of the federal privacy-policy gap.
- Added consent friction and delegated agent consent as limits on notice-and-consent alone.
