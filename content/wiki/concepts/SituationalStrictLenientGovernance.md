## Definition

Situational strict-lenient governance is the statecraft pattern in which strict law, leniency, moral teaching, and coercive correction are treated as conditional tools whose legitimacy depends on the disorder, actors, timing, and institutional condition they are meant to address.

## Current Synthesis

The concept enters the wiki through [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] and its presentation of [[CuiShiLateHan|Cui Shi]]'s [[ZhenglunCuiShi|Zhenglun]]. The source rejects rule by inherited formula: a ruler should not copy an ancient model or prefer harshness or leniency in the abstract, because the governing method has to fit the actual condition. Confucius' different answers to different rulers become the explanatory model for this flexibility.

The current synthesis has two sides. When order is decayed and powerful actors evade consequences, strict enforcement may function as medicine that restores accountability. When society is peaceful or already overburdened by harsh statutes, moral teaching and lenient repair may be the better nourishment. Sima Guang's qualification is central: Han law was already severe, so the late-Han failure was not simply insufficient cruelty but weak enforcement against privileged people and a ruler class unable to correct them.

A Western Han succession example shows the same principle from the opposite angle. [[LiuJuCrownPrinceWesternHan|刘据]]'s leniency is not framed as inherently wrong: [[HanWudi|汉武帝]] reportedly sees him as a humane stabilizer for the post-expansion order. The risk comes from mismatch and audience: severe-law officials resent the crown prince's lighter punishments, while [[EmpressWeiZifuWesternHan|卫子夫]] fears that mercy against Wudi's harsh administrative climate will be read as disobedience. The source therefore reinforces that strictness and leniency are political tools whose effect depends on institutional moment, ruler judgment, and surrounding incentives.

Hanji 1127 turns the theory into an order-interpretation problem. Cao Cao's earlier rule denies pardon to enemies who surrender only after encirclement, but once more than one thousand Hejian surrenderers are controlled, advisers argue that a wartime deterrent should not be mechanically carried into a more settled condition. The source preserves two boundaries: changed circumstances can justify leniency, yet subordinate discretion over military operations does not necessarily include authority for irreversible mass punishment. [[CaoPi|曹丕]] therefore asks Cao Cao, who pardons the captives.

Hanji 1148 applies the framework to regime transition. It presents [[ZhugeLiang|诸葛亮]]'s strict administration of Yizhou as a correction to the lax discipline and weak authority attributed to [[LiuZhang|刘璋]], while insisting that severity should include explicit rules, punishment of actual offenses, and positive recognition. The same episode also supplies a counterexample from inside the new regime: [[FaZheng|法正]] allegedly uses office for private retaliation. The synthesis therefore adds an impartiality condition—strictness is corrective only when rules are public and conduct-based, not when protected insiders can redirect them toward personal enemies.

## Key Claims

- Strictness and leniency are not permanent moral identities; they are tools whose value depends on condition and target.
- Political diagnosis precedes policy choice, because the same instrument can heal, fail, or worsen the disease under different circumstances.
- Harsh law can be legitimate as corrective medicine when disorder and elite impunity have made ordinary instruction ineffective.
- Leniency and moral teaching remain necessary where order is stable or where excessive punishment has become part of the problem.
- Mechanical imitation of ancient models is dangerous when it ignores time, person, institutional capacity, and enforcement reality.
- The concept does not authorize domination: severity remains a proportional, temporary correction, while valid leniency can still become politically exposed when harsh-law officials control the evaluation environment.
- Temporary severity should not become a context-free standing rule after the emergency, target, and decision authority have changed; corrective strictness also requires legible standards, positive as well as negative incentives, and equal constraints on politically valuable insiders.

## Evidence

- Diagnostic principle: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] uses "对症下药" to frame governance as matching remedy to political disease.
- Anti-formula argument: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] uses Confucius' different answers and the Emperor Xuan versus Emperor Yuan contrast to reject fixed imitation.
- Medicine and nourishment: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] compares punishment and law to strong medicine for disorder and moral teaching to nourishing food for peace.
- Enforcement qualification: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] includes Sima Guang's warning that late-Han legal harshness already existed, while enforcement against powerful actors was weak.
- Balance rule: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-837-weiren-chushi-weihe-buneng-xinci-shouruan-luznyzqldwdrbkfm7ce-1huuxm7b|Hanji 837]] closes by saying laxity should be corrected with strictness and oppressive strictness should be repaired with leniency.
- Succession-stabilizing leniency: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-384-2-you-duo-shanliang-jiu-you-duo-can-jiemi-bei-xianqi-de-taizi-liuju-lsu-7qabf34o5xza0axewim1quf0|Hanji 384-2]] says Han Wudi sees Liu Ju's humane steadiness as suitable for settling the realm after Wudi's own harsher expansionary work.
- Evaluation mismatch: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-384-2-you-duo-shanliang-jiu-you-duo-can-jiemi-bei-xianqi-de-taizi-liuju-lsu-7qabf34o5xza0axewim1quf0|Hanji 384-2]] says Liu Ju's lighter case handling pleases commoners but angers severe-law officials, making leniency a court-politics liability.
- Post-capture reassessment: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1127-caocao-cuanwei-sanjian-ta-zhide-shi-shenme-livrh3obobkcfhj_ngnhe-kgv2ls|Hanji 1127]] distinguishes Cao Cao's wartime no-pardon order from a later decision over secured surrenderers and has Cao Pi seek authorization before irreversible punishment.
- Regime-transition correction: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1148-fazheng-zuowei-zuofu-zhugeliang-weishenme-buguan-ljdigbpovfw6rh6oy30drk1idks9|Hanji 1148]] presents strict law as a response to Liu Zhang's lax order and joins punishment to clear standards and recognition of good performance.
- Impartiality boundary: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1148-fazheng-zuowei-zuofu-zhugeliang-weishenme-buguan-ljdigbpovfw6rh6oy30drk1idks9|Hanji 1148]] says Fa Zheng persecutes former enemies, showing how formally strict administration loses corrective legitimacy when private retaliation or insider protection overrides conduct-based rules.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept is grounded in four podcast sources with different emphases. Hanji 837 treats Cui Shi and Sima Guang as a political-theory frame; Hanji 384-2 adds a Liu Ju example but does not prove that leniency alone would have solved late-Wudi succession politics; Hanji 1127 does not supply the full legal wording, rebel identities, captive count, or scope of delegated authority. Hanji 1148 does not independently establish Liu Zhang's administrative condition, the content and enforcement of Zhuge Liang's laws, the speed of stabilization, or the exact Fa Zheng abuses. The concept should not be used as a general claim that harsh punishment is good or that every later actor may suspend an earlier order. Its point is diagnostic fit, proportionality, accountable authority, and impartial enforcement—not always choosing a middle quantity of punishment.

## What Changed

- Added Yizhou regime transition as a case for strict correction after lax rule.
- Added clear standards, positive recognition, and insider equality as conditions of legitimate corrective severity.
- Marked Fa Zheng's private retaliation as a counterexample rather than treating all strict administration as one coherent policy.
