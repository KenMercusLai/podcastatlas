## Definition

Continuous DJ mixing uses selection, cueing, beat alignment, and transitions to sustain an uninterrupted dance-floor experience across separate recordings.

## Current Synthesis

In [[439-disco-sex-and-race-in-seventies-america-glt8206137792|the source]], [[FrancisGrasso]] is associated with beat mixing and slip cueing at the Sanctuary, while [[DavidMancuso]] is associated with eclectic selection and continuous sound at the Loft. Recorded tracks become material sequenced into a longer environment rather than fillers between live performances.

Continuity changes the relationship among music, technology, and audience. The DJ reads room energy, searches beyond charts, controls transitions, and lets dancers experience a whole-night arc. Mixing is therefore social architecture as well as technique.

## Key Claims

- Continuous mixing minimizes silence and disruptive breaks.
- Beat mixing and slip cueing help preserve dance-floor momentum.
- Obscure or eclectic selection makes curation part of authorship.
- The unit of experience becomes the set and crowd energy, not only a track.
- These practices helped establish the DJ as organizer of a club night.

## Evidence

- Technique: [[439-disco-sex-and-race-in-seventies-america-glt8206137792|the source]] credits Grasso with beat mixing and slip cueing that removed silence.
- Curation: [[439-disco-sex-and-race-in-seventies-america-glt8206137792|the source]] describes Grasso ignoring charts and Mancuso combining eclectic records.
- Social effect: [[439-disco-sex-and-race-in-seventies-america-glt8206137792|the source]] treats immersive, continuous dancing and shared release as defining early disco features.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The innovation story is selective and does not prove that one DJ invented every technique. It does not fully compare parallel scenes, document equipment chronology, or distinguish later standardized beatmatching from all early transitions. Technical priority remains source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Established continuous mixing as a technical and social early-disco practice.
- Separated DJ sequencing authorship from authorship of recordings.
