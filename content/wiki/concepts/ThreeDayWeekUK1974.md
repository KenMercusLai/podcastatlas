# UK Three-Day Week of 1974

## Definition
The UK three-day week was the Heath government's emergency rationing of commercial electricity from New Year's Eve 1973 during the miners' overtime ban and wider energy crisis.

## Current Synthesis
[[120-the-oil-weapon-glt6620754143]] supplies the international and psychological prehistory: the [[OilCrisis1973|1973 oil shock]] intensified inflation and scarcity fears before the miners' dispute converted coal supply into domestic leverage. [[417-britain-in-1974-state-of-emergency-part-1-glt6761791949]] traces the measure from that collision through a November state of emergency to limits on industrial, shop, and office power, reduced lighting, restricted floodlit sport, and early television closure. [[418-britain-in-1974-the-crisis-election-part-2-glt9690615062]] shows the political paradox: mild weather and successful adaptation limited disruption, weakening [[EdwardHeath|Edward Heath]]'s attempt to turn hardship into a mandate against union power.

## Key Claims
- The three-day week was planned rationing, not a period defined by random unexpected blackouts.
- It emerged from the interaction of the 1973 oil shock, coal dependence, and miners' industrial action.
- Government restrictions reached factories, shops, offices, street lighting, sport, and broadcasting.
- Businesses and households adapted more effectively than expected under relatively mild weather.
- Contemporary fear and international ridicule exceeded some of the disruption ultimately experienced.
- Limited hardship reduced the political force of Heath's “who governs?” election framing.
- The measure converted a wage dispute into a visible test of governing authority.

## Evidence
- Escalation and design: [[417-britain-in-1974-state-of-emergency-part-1-glt6761791949]] connects the overtime ban, 13 November emergency, Barber's warning, and New Year's Eve restrictions.
- Lived experience: [[417-britain-in-1974-state-of-emergency-part-1-glt6761791949]] stresses orderly allocation, adaptation, and the difference between memory of power cuts and the three-day week itself.
- International shock and public mood: [[120-the-oil-weapon-glt6620754143]] connects oil-price fear, inflation, miners' pay, Christmas pessimism, panic buying, foreign mockery, and unexpectedly high workplace output.
- Electoral effect: [[418-britain-in-1974-the-crisis-election-part-2-glt9690615062]] says mild weather and lower-than-expected disruption weakened the government's case.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The three podcast sources emphasize political narrative and public mood rather than measuring output losses across sectors. The source-reported estimate of roughly 80% productivity is not an economy-wide audited result. Less disruption than forecast does not mean that restrictions imposed no economic or household cost, and the oil shock did not produce the measure without the coal dispute and wage-policy confrontation.

## What Changed
- Created the missing link between the energy confrontation and February election.
- Distinguished scheduled restriction from popular memories of random blackout.
- Added the gap between apocalyptic expectation, foreign perception, and practical adaptation.
