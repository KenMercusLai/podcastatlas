## Definition
Success as maintained coordination is the view that large-scale wealth, authority, or organizational achievement is not a permanent personal possession but a continuing state reproduced through relationships among investors, lenders, partners, customers, workers, political actors, and costly assets.

## Current Synthesis
The source uses [[LarryEllison|Larry Ellison]]'s island and commercial network to challenge the image of success as something an individual simply acquires. An island, a company, a family office, and an infrastructure strategy keep consuming money, attention, legitimacy, and cooperation. Expansion increases personal reach while also creating obligations that cannot be abandoned without changing the person's life, identity, and power.

This relational account also explains fragility. A system can appear controlled by one central will yet still depend on the continued participation of many actors. A breakdown in financing, profitability, partnership, political support, or operating discipline can affect the whole network. Success is therefore closer to a maintained equilibrium than a stored attribute.

## Key Claims
- Large-scale success depends on repeated coordination rather than a one-time act of acquisition.
- Assets and organizations become obligations because they require continuing capital, labor, legitimacy, and attention.
- Expansion can reduce exit freedom by tying identity and daily life to the system being maintained.
- Centralized control does not remove dependence on partners, lenders, investors, workers, customers, or political conditions.
- Visible power and structural fragility can coexist in the same organization or personal empire.

### Identity and exit cost
- [[zong-di-074-qi-tamen-ceng-jianguo-weilai-yushi-chuangzaole-xianzai-cmvkvb75u00q001xaevgt4t42]] argues that once an empire becomes personal life and identity, simple contraction no longer restores the earlier condition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source derives the framework from a journalistic portrait summarized by the podcast; it does not provide a general measure of success, dependence, or fragility. Some assets are easier to sell, delegate, or unwind than others, and concentrated ownership can create real unilateral power. Calling success relational should not erase individual skill or responsibility, nor should dependence on others imply equal bargaining power among them.

## What Changed
- Established success as a maintained stakeholder relationship rather than a durable personal attribute.
- Added exit cost and simultaneous power-fragility as central features of large institutional systems.
