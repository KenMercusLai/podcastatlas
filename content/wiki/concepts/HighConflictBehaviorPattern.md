# High-Conflict Behavior Pattern

## Definition
A high-conflict behavior pattern is [[BillEddy]]'s practical description of recurring blame, unmanaged emotion, all-or-nothing thinking, extreme conduct, and disputes that escalate or migrate instead of resolving. It is an observation frame, not a clinical diagnosis.

## Current Synthesis
The source's useful distinction is between ordinary difficult behavior and a durable conflict pattern. A single angry exchange, dramatic personality, diagnosis, occupation, or political view is insufficient. The pattern becomes more concerning when blame remains externalized, emotional intensity recruits others, conduct becomes disproportionate, and similar unresolved disputes recur across close relationships, work, housing, or legal settings.

Eddy's WEB method keeps recognition tied to Words, the observer's Emotions, and Behavior. Blaming or absolute words are signals; sudden fear, obligation, or pressure in the observer is information; and extreme acts deserve more weight than charm or explanation. These channels support slower vetting and boundary decisions, but none proves motive or diagnosis.

## Key Claims
- Persistent externalized blame is the source's central distinction between high-conflict behavior and conflict that can be jointly examined and repaired.
- All-or-nothing language, unmanaged affect, and disproportionate behavior matter most as a repeated cluster across time and settings.
- Charm, credentials, calm presentation, or public success do not establish how someone behaves in close relationships.
- WEB organizes observation through Words, the observer's Emotions, and Behavior without licensing amateur diagnosis.
- Emotional recruitment can turn friends, professionals, audiences, or groups into “negative advocates” who feel certain before learning the whole situation.
- Time and observation of stable long-term relationships are more informative than irreversible commitment made during early intensity.
- Recognition should guide one's own contact and safety decisions, not public rejection, humiliation, or group labeling.

## Evidence
- Recurring pattern and blame - [[how-to-deal-with-high-conflict-people-bill-eddy-scim8877999828]] connects stuck family-court disputes to repeated blame and escalation across life domains.
- WEB recognition - [[how-to-deal-with-high-conflict-people-bill-eddy-scim8877999828]] asks observers to integrate language, their own affective response, and conduct rather than relying on one impression.
- Close-relationship vetting - [[how-to-deal-with-high-conflict-people-bill-eddy-scim8877999828]] recommends time, observation of family and long-term friendships, and caution around rapid irreversible commitment.
- Advocate recruitment - [[how-to-deal-with-high-conflict-people-bill-eddy-scim8877999828]] describes emotionally hooked but incompletely informed allies and professionals as escalation pathways.
- Label boundary - [[how-to-deal-with-high-conflict-people-bill-eddy-scim8877999828]] repeatedly separates the practical pattern from formal personality-disorder diagnosis.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
“High-conflict personality” is not established here as a formal diagnostic category, and the episode acknowledges limited direct research. Gut feelings can warn but can also reflect bias, trauma, stereotype, or misunderstanding; they require behavioral corroboration. Personality-disorder prevalence and overlap figures are reported from older research, and the claim that roughly half of people with personality disorders show this conflict pattern is Eddy's source-scoped estimate. The framework should not turn ordinary disagreement, neurodivergence, mental illness, cultural difference, or assertiveness into a stigmatizing label.

## What Changed
- Created a pattern-first concept that separates repeated conflict escalation from casual personality diagnosis.
