## Definition
Farmers-market urbanism is the way a recurring public market can organize food access, producer or vendor livelihoods, neighborhood life, and shared urban space at once.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources show two distinct models. New York's [[Greenmarket]] is a managed network connecting regional farms directly to city customers through producer vetting, neighborhood expansion, benefit-payment infrastructure, and restaurant relationships. Northern Chinese morning markets are shorter-duration systems in which produce, meat, prepared food, and household goods temporarily occupy streets or multi-use sites. Across both, a market becomes urban infrastructure when food supply, livelihoods, access rules, place management, and repeated social routines reinforce one another.

## Key Claims
- A recurring market can function as public-space infrastructure rather than only a collection of stalls.
- Direct city demand can support producers or low-cost vendors, but participation depends on governance and affordable access.
- Market institutions include vendor selection, payment access, spatial allocation, opening hours, sanitation, and conflict management.
- Repetition builds neighborhood knowledge and social contact while also creating obligations and possible resident friction.
- Restaurant sourcing and ready-to-eat tourism are different market extensions: one can strengthen regional supply, while the other may displace everyday provisioning.

## Evidence
- Producer-city connection: [[a-firm-andy-what-are-new-british-pms-plans-6a5deeca76f8ee25bb4bbf9c]] describes [[Greenmarket]], [[GrowNYC]], and [[UnionSquare]] as a direct regional-farm network with vetting, food-benefit payments, and restaurant ties.
- Reversible public space: [[ep287-yiqi-guang-zaoshi-xunzhao-beifang-zui-xianhuo-de-zaochen-lpuox4b-b02k-awt-hsjuph-froi]] describes Shenyang roads and underpasses and Taiyuan parking or antique-market sites returning to other uses after morning trade.
- Livelihood and access: [[a-firm-andy-what-are-new-british-pms-plans-6a5deeca76f8ee25bb4bbf9c]] shows city demand changing farm viability; [[ep287-yiqi-guang-zaoshi-xunzhao-beifang-zui-xianhuo-de-zaochen-lpuox4b-b02k-awt-hsjuph-froi]] links low-cost selling space to vendor survival and Shenyang's post-layoff re-employment history.
- Governance and conflict: [[a-firm-andy-what-are-new-british-pms-plans-6a5deeca76f8ee25bb4bbf9c]] and [[ep287-yiqi-guang-zaoshi-xunzhao-beifang-zui-xianhuo-de-zaochen-lpuox4b-b02k-awt-hsjuph-froi]] make management constitutive through vendor rules, payment systems, site selection, timed closure, noise mitigation, waste removal, and resident complaints.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources describe different institutions and should not be collapsed into one universal market form. Greenmarket emphasizes verified regional producers and formal network infrastructure; the northern China cases mix produce, food service, and reselling in tightly timed spaces. Neither source provides a comparative measure of prices, farmer or vendor income, displacement, neighborhood welfare, or long-run survival. Public popularity can strengthen a market while tourism-driven stall replication makes it less useful to residents.

## What Changed
- Expanded the synthesis from one New York producer-market network to a qualified comparison with northern Chinese morning markets.
- Added time-bounded space reuse, sanitation, noise, and resident conflict to market governance.
- Distinguished regional supply-chain extension from tourism-led prepared-food displacement.
