## Definition
K-shaped consumer spending is the pattern where aggregate consumption remains resilient because asset-rich or higher-income households keep spending while price-sensitive households cut, defer, borrow, or optimize basics.

## Current Synthesis
The concept now spans market wealth effects, household debt stress, premium-brand resilience, and lived budgeting strain. U.S. stock gains, retirement accounts, home values, and top-income spending can hold aggregate demand up, while households like [[LindsayCole]]'s ration snacks, hot water, electricity, and children's activities. The result is not simply "strong" or "weak" consumption; it is a distributional split where hard spending data can look better than the experience of many households.

## Key Claims
- Consumer spending can stay strong when high-income households carry a large share of spending.
- Stock-market and housing gains can support asset-owning households while increasing vulnerability to market corrections.
- Retirement-account exposure can make some middle-class households indirectly market-sensitive even when wages and prices feel weak.
- Price-sensitive households show the lower branch through debt stress, car affordability pressure, utility optimization, food budgeting, and delayed purchases.
- Multi-price-band brands can outperform narrow discretionary categories when consumers trade down within the brand instead of exiting.
- The concept is a distributional interpretation of consumption data, not a claim that aggregate spending figures are wrong.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
K-shaped spending is a frame for uneven demand, not a replacement for measured consumption. Some lower-income or middle-income households may still spend, and some asset-rich households may cut back after market losses or confidence shocks. The source-specific examples should not be treated as full population measurement.
