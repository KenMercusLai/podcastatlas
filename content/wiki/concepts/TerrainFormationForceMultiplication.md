## Definition

Terrain and formation force multiplication / 地形阵型战力放大 is the tactical pattern in which a smaller force combines terrain selection, field defenses, weapon layering, discipline, information, and role allocation to impose losses beyond what raw troop numbers predict.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 377-2 supplies a bounded Western Han case through [[LiLingWesternHan|李陵]]'s five thousand infantry against much larger [[Xiongnu|匈奴]] cavalry forces. Carts form a defensive enclosure; shield-and-halberd troops protect crossbowmen; mountains, woods, reeds, and narrow ground limit cavalry use; volleys and pursuit convert defensive cohesion into local counterattack; and wounded soldiers are reassigned by remaining capability rather than removed from the system altogether.

The case also exposes the concept's limits. Terrain is not intrinsically favorable: reeds invite fire, and the final valley lets the Xiongnu cut the road, shoot from height, and roll stones downward. Force multiplication depends on ammunition, equipment, command integrity, and informational uncertainty. When [[GuanGanWesternHan|管敢]] reveals that reinforcement is absent and arrows are nearly exhausted, the opponent can keep attacking until the system collapses.

## Key Claims

- Formation and weapon complementarity can let infantry survive an otherwise unfavorable numerical and mobility matchup.
- Terrain matters through interaction with troop type: mountains and woods can restrict cavalry, while valleys and heights can trap the retreating force.
- Field-expedient defenses such as carts and cleared firebreaks can convert transport or destructive fire into protection.
- Injury-based role reassignment preserves scarce manpower when withdrawal cannot safely evacuate everyone.
- Local victories do not remove cumulative depletion; ammunition, equipment, fatigue, casualties, and route access remain hard constraints.
- Information can be a force multiplier or force-divider because an opponent's uncertainty may restrain attack until an insider reveals actual limits.

## Evidence

- Layered formation and firepower: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-377-2-yi-yi-di-shi-zhanli-baobiao-de-shashen-liling-likohxfnnybzg6l-q_dadkjeujri|Hanji 377-2]] describes cart defenses, front-rank shields and halberds, rear-rank bows and crossbows, massed volleys, and counter-pursuit.
- Terrain interaction: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-377-2-yi-yi-di-shi-zhanli-baobiao-de-shashen-liling-likohxfnnybzg6l-q_dadkjeujri|Hanji 377-2]] describes combat between mountains, in forest, through reeds, and finally inside a valley where high ground favors the Xiongnu.
- Adaptive manpower and fire defense: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-377-2-yi-yi-di-shi-zhanli-baobiao-de-shashen-liling-likohxfnnybzg6l-q_dadkjeujri|Hanji 377-2]] says soldiers are assigned tasks by wound count and that the Han force burns nearby reeds first to survive an upwind fire attack.
- Depletion and intelligence limit: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-377-2-yi-yi-di-shi-zhanli-baobiao-de-shashen-liling-likohxfnnybzg6l-q_dadkjeujri|Hanji 377-2]] connects Guan Gan's disclosure, arrow exhaustion, abandoned carts, improvised weapons, blocked retreat, and final breakout failure.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is currently grounded in one popular-history episode and does not independently verify its force totals, casualty counts, ammunition figure, speeches, or exact sequence. It should not be reduced to a universal claim that infantry defeats cavalry in confined terrain: training, weapon range, resupply, route control, enemy adaptation, weather, morale, and command cohesion all matter. The case ends in defeat, showing delay and disproportionate losses rather than strategic victory.
