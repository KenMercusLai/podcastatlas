# Atrocity Narrative Reframing

## Definition

Atrocity narrative reframing is the historical process by which an event first represented as justified victory, battle, or security action is reinterpreted around unequal force, civilian victims, suppressed testimony, and the institutions that produced violence.

## Current Synthesis

The [[WoundedKneeMassacre]] provides the episode's clearest case. Early newspapers said the Lakota attacked first and praised the Seventh Cavalry; the Secretary of War emphasized discipline and restraint; Colonel Forsyth was cleared. Against this stood General Nelson Miles's contemporary condemnation and the event's material pattern of encirclement, disarmament, civilian killing, pursuit, heavy weapons, and friendly fire.

Later works such as *Bury My Heart at Wounded Knee* helped shift public sympathy toward Native perspectives and changed the dominant moral language from battle to massacre. Yet reframing is not restitution. The episode stresses that symbolic sympathy can coexist with continuing deprivation and with [[FrontierViolenceSpectacle]] that still asks Native communities to perform the past for outsiders.

## Key Claims

- Official and press labels can translate an atrocity into a story of justified victory.
- Event classification should test force asymmetry, victim status, disarmament, flight, weapon use, and conduct after the initiating incident.
- Contemporary dissent can survive beneath a dominant official narrative and later anchor reinterpretation.
- Cultural works can change public memory even when they do not change material distributions of land, wealth, or power.
- Victim-centered sympathy can itself become romanticizing or extractive if it fixes living people inside a heroic-loss image.

## Evidence

- Competing contemporary accounts: [[456-fall-of-the-sioux-the-massacre-at-wounded-knee-part-3-glt7996658277]] contrasts early heroic reporting and official praise with Miles's condemnation.
- Material classification: [[456-fall-of-the-sioux-the-massacre-at-wounded-knee-part-3-glt7996658277]] emphasizes surrounding troops, disarmament, Hotchkiss guns, women and children, flight, ravine fire, and friendly fire.
- Later memory: [[456-fall-of-the-sioux-the-massacre-at-wounded-knee-part-3-glt7996658277]] credits later books and cultural changes with increasing the salience of Native perspectives.
- Limits of sympathy: [[456-fall-of-the-sioux-the-massacre-at-wounded-knee-part-3-glt7996658277]] distinguishes moral or symbolic victory from material recovery and links representation to tourism.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- Reframing should not erase that some Lakota resisted after fire began or that U.S. soldiers were killed.
- A later narrative is not automatically accurate because it reverses an earlier official one; casualty figures and specific participant motives still require source criticism.
- The episode's broad account of changing American attitudes is interpretive and does not establish a uniform public consensus.
- Greater attention to one atrocity can still leave Native history less central than other national memory debates.

## What Changed

- Established a framework for comparing official battle language with evidence of mass killing.
- Added contemporary dissent as a bridge between the event and later reinterpretation.
- Distinguished symbolic moral reversal from material restitution and representational control.
