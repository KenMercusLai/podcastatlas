# Parliamentary System

## Definition

A parliamentary system is a form of government in which the executive emerges from, and remains politically dependent on, legislative majority or confidence arrangements rather than holding a separately elected fixed presidential mandate.

## Current Synthesis

[[173-tanhe-ruhe-bamian-yiwei-zongtong-963141809]] uses parliamentary government as the contrast that clarifies why U.S. impeachment is a rare constitutional repair rather than an ordinary no-confidence mechanism. Cabinets can often be changed through confidence politics, although constructive no-confidence rules, dissolution powers, coalition bargaining, and party discipline qualify that flexibility. [[205-the-last-days-of-boris-johnson-glt2329301983]] adds the British leadership case: a prime minister may invoke a general-election victory, but does not thereby acquire a separate personal presidential mandate. Ministerial and party withdrawal can make formal continuation politically unsustainable even before every legal power expires.

## Key Claims

- Parliamentary government can be more flexible because a cabinet depends on legislative confidence.
- That flexibility can also mean more bargaining, coalition fragility, or frequent conflict.
- No-confidence and dissolution mechanisms show that checks on power themselves require checks.
- Impeachment should not be read as a U.S. version of ordinary no-confidence politics.
- A party's election victory does not necessarily create a direct personal mandate independent of parliamentary support.

## Evidence

### System comparison

- [[173-tanhe-ruhe-bamian-yiwei-zongtong-963141809]] contrasts cabinet dependence on legislative confidence with a separately elected president's fixed term and high-threshold impeachment remedy.

### British leadership enforcement

- [[205-the-last-days-of-boris-johnson-glt2329301983]] uses Johnson's loss of ministers and Conservative support to show how parliamentary authority can collapse despite personal-mandate rhetoric and temporary formal tenure.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Parliamentary systems vary substantially in electoral rules, head-of-state powers, party discipline, confidence procedures, coalition practice, and constitutional codification. The U.S.-focused source simplifies that diversity for comparison, while the Johnson episode is immediate commentary on one British crisis rather than a general institutional test. Informal withdrawal of support can be delayed, strategically manipulated, or fail to prevent damage.

## What Changed

- Added the distinction between a party's electoral victory and a prime minister's claimed personal mandate.
- Connected formal tenure to the separate practical requirement of sustained ministerial and parliamentary support.
