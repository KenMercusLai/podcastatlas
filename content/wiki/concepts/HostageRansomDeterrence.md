## Definition

Hostage-ransom deterrence is the public-order rule pattern in which officials refuse ransom, private buyback, or hostage-first delay because making hostage-taking profitable would endanger future victims even when the immediate hostage may die.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 709 now supplies the direct Guangwu-era precedent that Hanji 915 had preserved only as a comparison. In the earlier case, [[YinLihua|阴丽华]]'s mother and brother are seized by hostage-takers, and the state attacks under the "不得顾质" principle rather than prioritizing negotiation or ransom. Both hostages are killed, but the episode presents the policy as a deliberate refusal to let criminals convert elite family attachment into repeatable leverage.

Hanji 915 extends the pattern through [[QiaoXuanLateHan|桥玄]]. Qiao refuses ransom when his youngest son is abducted, and the later memorialized rule forbids families from using private wealth to buy hostages back. The combined synthesis is severe but coherent: the state sacrifices individualized bargaining room to make hostage-taking an unrewarding crime, and the rule only has public credibility if high-status families bear the same cost.

The wiki keeps the deterrence-effect claim bounded. These sources show the rule logic and the elite credibility signal; they do not independently prove that hostage-taking disappeared everywhere or that every real hostage case should be handled without regard to immediate life.

## Key Claims

- Ransom and delay can create future hostage-taking incentives when criminals learn that family attachment reliably produces payment.
- A no-ransom rule works only if powerful households cannot buy exceptions for themselves.
- The rule is ethically costly because protecting future potential victims can require accepting the death of the current hostage.
- The Guangwu-Yin case and Qiao Xuan case make elite suffering part of the deterrence signal.
- The concept describes a state public-order logic, not a private moral command for all families in all hostage situations.
- Claims about long-run disappearance of kidnapping remain source-scoped until broader evidence is ingested.

## Evidence

- Direct Guangwu precedent: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-709-mian-dui-bangjia-an-guren-weihe-buguan-renzhi-sihuo-luknor8yw839qwxogeyzwluo6qe2|Hanji 709]] says Yin Lihua's mother and brother are taken hostage and the government attacks under "不得顾质" rather than paying or yielding.
- Immediate human cost: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-709-mian-dui-bangjia-an-guren-weihe-buguan-renzhi-sihuo-luknor8yw839qwxogeyzwluo6qe2|Hanji 709]] says both hostages are killed, making the deterrence policy visible as a current-life sacrifice rather than a painless rule.
- Later approval frame: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-709-mian-dui-bangjia-an-guren-weihe-buguan-renzhi-sihuo-luknor8yw839qwxogeyzwluo6qe2|Hanji 709]] reports Cao Cao's later approval of the principle as a rule usable for later generations.
- Qiao Xuan case: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-915-shishang-meiyou-zhenzheng-de-kouwu-zhehua-dui-ma-lgxcodjxnaozvyegxqurgzji5gus|Hanji 915]] says Qiao Xuan refuses ransom for his abducted son and later supports a ban on private ransom payment.
- Elite credibility: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-915-shishang-meiyou-zhenzheng-de-kouwu-zhehua-dui-ma-lgxcodjxnaozvyegxqurgzji5gus|Hanji 915]] explicitly compares Qiao Xuan's late-Han case with the earlier Liu Xiu and Yin Lihua family precedent.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The two source notes document a deterrence rule and its moral cost, not a complete empirical history of kidnapping. The host's claim that the rule reduced or ended the practice remains source-scoped. The principle also sits near a legal-moral boundary: a public order rule can be strategically intelligible while still being cruel to the immediate hostage and family. The wiki therefore treats the pattern as a historically attested deterrence logic, not as advice for modern crisis handling.
