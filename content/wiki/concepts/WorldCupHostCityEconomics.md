# World Cup Host-City Economics

World Cup host-city economics is the cost and revenue split created when [[FIFA]] owns the central commercial rights to the [[FIFAWorldCup|FIFA World Cup]] while cities and host committees handle much of the local operating burden. [[vol-267-ruhe-xianchang-kan-shijiebei-bing-da-kui-yi-bi-feat-yingyan-shijian-1002986145]] uses the 2026 tournament to show this split through venue upgrades, security, transportation, fan festivals, city operations, government subsidies, hotel-tax mechanisms, and local sponsorship.

The source's core point is not that every host city loses money. It is that the hard revenue streams - tickets, media rights, global sponsorship, and licensed merchandise - belong mainly to FIFA, while many visible and politically sensitive costs sit closer to the city. Host committees then need public funding, venue rent, [[HostCitySupporterSponsorship]], and on-site consumption to close the gap.

## Key Claims
- Existing North American stadiums reduce new-build cost but do not eliminate football-specific retrofits such as natural grass, field dimensions, temporary seating, dressing rooms, display systems, and broadcast facilities.
- Host committees can look like temporary coalitions among government, sports associations, venue operators, teams, tourism boards, chambers of commerce, and local business groups.
- Public subsidy becomes structurally important when the host city cannot directly share the largest tournament revenue streams.
- Chicago's withdrawal from the 2026 host-city process is treated as a warning that unknown FIFA obligations can become taxpayer-risk questions.
- [[SportsMegaEventWelfareAccounting]] needs a city-budget layer because local happiness, tourism, and visibility may coexist with public spending and uneven fan affordability.
