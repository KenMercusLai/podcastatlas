# British EC Referendum 1975

## Definition
British EC Referendum 1975 refers to the United Kingdom's first national referendum, held on 5 June 1975, asking whether Britain should remain in the European Community after [[EdwardHeath|Ted Heath]]'s 1973 entry and [[HaroldWilson|Harold Wilson]]'s renegotiation.

## Current Synthesis
[[418-britain-in-1974-the-crisis-election-part-2-glt9690615062]] supplies the referendum's electoral prehistory: Labour's pledge let [[EnochPowell|Enoch Powell]] urge an anti-Conservative vote in February 1974 and helped return [[HaroldWilson|Harold Wilson]] to office. [[663-britain-in-the-70s-the-brexit-that-never-was-part-2-glt2237203377]] then frames the vote as a party-management solution inside a broader national crisis. Wilson converted a Labour split into a public choice, recommended Yes after renegotiation, and allowed ministers to campaign on both sides; voters chose the safer status quo while the economic crisis continued.

## Key Claims
- The referendum was primarily a Labour-management device before it was a settled constitutional norm.
- Labour's referendum pledge shaped the February 1974 election before it became government policy.
- The 1975 Yes coalition joined most Conservatives, business, newspapers, and pro-European Labour figures rather than matching later Brexit alignments.
- The No campaign fused Labour-left, democratic-sovereignty, nationalist, communist, anarchist, and right-Conservative strands.
- The result reflected crisis aversion and status-quo preference more than popular European identity.
- The referendum temporarily settled the Europe question for Wilson while leaving inflation, sterling pressure, and public-finance weakness in place.

## Evidence
- Electoral prehistory: [[418-britain-in-1974-the-crisis-election-part-2-glt9690615062]] says Powell urged voters to support Labour because it promised a referendum on European Community membership.
- Party-management mechanism: [[663-britain-in-the-70s-the-brexit-that-never-was-part-2-glt2237203377]] says Wilson promised renegotiation and a referendum to prevent Labour's Europe split from breaking his tiny majority.
- Pro-Yes coalition: [[663-britain-in-the-70s-the-brexit-that-never-was-part-2-glt2237203377]] identifies [[MargaretThatcher|Margaret Thatcher]], Heath, major businesses, and most newspapers as backing continued membership.
- Anti-EC coalition: [[663-britain-in-the-70s-the-brexit-that-never-was-part-2-glt2237203377]] places [[TonyBenn]], Michael Foot, Enoch Powell, Labour leftists, communists, anarchists, and right-wing Conservatives in the Leave camp.
- Status-quo logic: [[663-britain-in-the-70s-the-brexit-that-never-was-part-2-glt2237203377]] argues that Yes won decisively because voters feared adding European exit to inflation, borrowing, and national uncertainty.
- Unresolved economic crisis: [[663-britain-in-the-70s-the-brexit-that-never-was-part-2-glt2237203377]] ends with inflation near 30%, a sliding pound, and the "economic crisis" still looming after the referendum.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
These sources are narrative-history episodes rather than technical accounts of EC law, campaign finance, opinion polling, or regional voting returns. The February source does not quantify Powell's electoral effect, while the comparison with 2016 remains interpretive rather than proof that the two referendums are unrelated.

## What Changed
- Added Labour's February 1974 pledge and Powell's intervention as the referendum's electoral prehistory.
- Preserved the distinction between the 1975 vote and later [[Brexit]].
