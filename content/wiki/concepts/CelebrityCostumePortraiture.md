# Celebrity Costume Portraiture

## Definition

Celebrity costume portraiture is the design of performance clothing as a person-specific extension of the wearer's body, character, movement, and public identity.

## Current Synthesis

The concept emerges from [[BobMackie]]'s method. Glitter, fit, transparency, color, and silhouette are not treated as independent fashion statements; they help make a particular star legible under lights. A successful costume therefore belongs partly to a relationship among designer, wearer, performance, and moment, which explains Mackie's resistance to treating Marilyn Monroe's original dress as a transferable red-carpet object.

## Key Claims

- The performer, rather than the garment alone, is the central work of costume design.
- Stage light, movement, body, and audience distance make theatrical visibility a functional design problem.
- Person-specific fit and symbolism can turn clothing into a public-character portrait.
- Re-wearing an original costume can detach it from the body, occasion, and persona that gave it meaning.
- Institutional fashion prestige and cultural impact do not necessarily coincide.

## Evidence

- Wearer-first method: [[big-meet-little-meat-the-trump-xi-summit-6ab6401bbd67ce4b534215db]] says Mackie's guiding question was who would wear a costume and why.
- Light and spectacle: [[big-meet-little-meat-the-trump-xi-summit-6ab6401bbd67ce4b534215db]] describes his aim of making the star shimmer and appear uniquely special.
- Person-specific ownership: [[big-meet-little-meat-the-trump-xi-summit-6ab6401bbd67ce4b534215db]] connects his objection to [[KimKardashian]] wearing Monroe's dress with the belief that it had been made around Monroe's body and persona.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source supplies Mackie's philosophy, not a general theory accepted across costume conservation, fashion history, or performance studies. Costumes can acquire new cultural meanings after their original performance, and legal ownership does not necessarily follow artistic person-specificity.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to capture Mackie's wearer-centered design philosophy and its limits on costume transferability.
