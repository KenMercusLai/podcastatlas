# Correlated Catastrophe Insurance Risk

## Definition
Correlated catastrophe insurance risk arises when one event or common hazard regime can damage many insured properties at once, making geographic accumulation and shared community vulnerability matter in addition to each property's construction quality.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources connect physical and financial correlation. [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-9de180c7bff8bba31ae5f11fce51f1e1-34185790]] shows why a fortified home can still burn through a neighboring structure or remain unattractive when one carrier has too much local exposure. [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-043ffe0bc2c7516bac64132229755e93-43026861-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-043ffe0bc2c7516bac64132229755e93-43026861]] extends the judgment across time and geography: more frequent severe wildfires, hurricanes, and hail can produce simultaneous losses that historical pricing, ordinary diversification, and house-level mitigation do not fully absorb.

## Key Claims
- Catastrophe risk is portfolio-level because one wildfire, hurricane, or hail event can trigger many claims at once.
- Individual resilience is valuable but remains dependent on neighboring structures, vegetation, and community conditions.
- Geographic diversification weakens when severe hazards become more frequent across multiple regions or when one carrier accumulates too much local exposure.
- Insurers can withdraw, restrict, or reprice coverage even for mitigated homes when expected aggregate loss exceeds available capacity.
- Forward-looking models may improve recognition of changing correlation while creating affordability and governance tradeoffs.
- Engineering, community mitigation, pricing, regulation, residual coverage, and portfolio management address different layers of the same risk.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
- Neither source provides insurer portfolio data, reinsurance terms, capital models, or a causal decomposition of premium increases and exits.
- Reported losses, premium shocks, discounts, and policy counts are episode examples rather than population estimates.
- Better construction and collective mitigation can materially reduce loss even when they cannot guarantee insurance availability.
- Forward-looking models may improve risk estimates, but their accuracy, transparency, distributional effects, and regulatory use are not established here.

## What Changed
- Extended correlation from one-event geographic accumulation to a changing multi-region climate-risk regime.
- Added regulatory modeling, residual-market pressure, and community mitigation as distinct responses.
- Added limited evidence that better local risk recognition can restore some capacity without resolving affordability.
