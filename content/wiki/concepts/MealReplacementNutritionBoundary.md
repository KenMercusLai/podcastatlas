# Meal Replacement Nutrition Boundary / 代餐营养边界

## Definition
Meal replacement nutrition boundary is the distinction between filling a nutrient gap and replacing an actual meal with a product whose energy, protein, carbohydrate, fiber, micronutrient, and satiety profile must be checked.

## Current Synthesis
The sources treat meal replacements as a market category that can easily be confused with protein powder, collagen, vegetable powder, cereal bars, supplements, or outdoor energy products. The decision hinge is whether a product is replacing a meal, supplementing a missing nutrient, or solving a temporary constraint such as travel, hiking, camping, overtime, night work, or lack of meal access.

This makes label reading central. A meal replacement is not safer because it looks lighter, has "zero sugar" language, is high in protein, or is sold in health packaging. Some powders, bars, cereal products, or energy bars can be calorie-dense, carbohydrate-heavy, or poorly suited to blood-sugar control. VOL.161 adds the bodily and behavioral side: ordinary food also supplies texture, chewing, meal structure, and varied intake. The sources therefore frame replacement products as occasional context support rather than routine substitutes for ordinary meals.

VOL.160 sharpens the food-form boundary through juice and liquid salad. Blending or powdering several low-GI fruits and vegetables does not preserve every practical property of eating them intact, and a drink should not acquire full-meal status from ingredient count alone. The episode nevertheless keeps a bounded role for travel, postoperative recovery, or other periods when ordinary food access or eating capacity is disrupted.

## Key Claims
- Meal replacement products should not be treated as interchangeable with protein powder or collagen because their purposes and nutrient profiles differ.
- Replacing a meal requires checking energy, protein, carbohydrate, fat, fiber, micronutrients, and satiety rather than only checking one headline nutrient.
- "Zero sugar" or healthy-looking packaging does not prove low carbohydrate, low energy, or suitability for blood-sugar control.
- Energy bars and meal bars may be designed for outdoor or high-demand scenarios and can be counterproductive as casual snacks.
- Vegetable powders and meal replacements can help in abnormal travel or outdoor contexts but should not become default substitutes for regular meals.
- Collagen is protein after digestion, but the source does not treat it as guaranteed face, skin, or joint rebuilding.
- Fresh, varied food and chewing remain the default when ordinary meal access is available; a long ingredient list or low-GI positioning does not by itself make a liquid salad equivalent to an intact meal.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not provide a formal standard, full product comparison, or individualized meal-replacement plan. Claims about oral digestion, glycemic response, fiber change, stomach burden, and postoperative suitability are conversational and should not be overgeneralized. The concept is a public-literacy boundary: read the label, ask what meal or nutrient gap the product is replacing and for how long, and keep diabetes, kidney disease, pregnancy, eating disorders, weight-management distress, surgery recovery, and clinical nutrition inside qualified care.

## What Changed
- Added juice and liquid salad to the food-form boundary and clarified that ingredient count does not establish meal equivalence.
- Added postoperative recovery as a qualified temporary-use case rather than a general product endorsement.
