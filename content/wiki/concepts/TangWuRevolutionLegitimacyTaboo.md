## Definition

Tang-Wu Revolution Legitimacy Taboo / 汤武革命合法性禁区 is the political boundary that appears when a dynasty needs anti-tyrant revolution to justify its own founding but fears that the same logic can be turned against the current ruler.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-392-3-daodi-zenme-zuo-caishi-yige-hao-diwang-lgrcpyv1mf14axsq6hnqxaymrpu0|Hanji 392-3]] turns a doctrine question into a court-risk problem. [[YuanGusheng|袁固生]] defends [[ShangTang|商汤]] and [[ZhouWuwang|周武王]] as legitimate replacers of tyrants because popular support reveals [[MandateOfHeavenLegitimacy|Heaven's mandate]]. [[HuangShengWesternHan|黄生]] rejects that logic through fixed ruler-minister hierarchy. Yuan's reply about whether [[LiuBang|刘邦]] was also illegitimate for replacing Qin makes the question dangerous for [[HanJingdi|汉景帝]], because condemning revolution can condemn Han's founding, while endorsing revolution can authorize future rebellion.

The taboo is therefore not anti-learning in the abstract. It is a self-protection move around a legitimacy paradox: dynasties founded by rupture often need memory of righteous replacement, but stable monarchy prefers a doctrine of obedience once it is in power.

## Key Claims

- Tang-Wu revolution supplies a classical precedent for replacing a ruler who has lost moral and popular legitimacy.
- Fixed ruler-minister hierarchy tries to prevent that precedent from becoming permission for rebellion.
- The Han case is self-referential because Liu Bang's anti-Qin founding depends on a similar replacement logic.
- A court can resolve the paradox by discouraging public inquiry rather than settling the doctrine openly.
- The concept connects people-based mandate language with dynastic anxiety about repeatable overthrow.

## Evidence

Classical precedent as legitimacy test:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-392-3-daodi-zenme-zuo-caishi-yige-hao-diwang-lgrcpyv1mf14axsq6hnqxaymrpu0|Hanji 392-3]] presents Yuan Gusheng as defending Tang's overthrow of Jie through popular support and Heaven's mandate.

Hierarchy as anti-revolution boundary:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-392-3-daodi-zenme-zuo-caishi-yige-hao-diwang-lgrcpyv1mf14axsq6hnqxaymrpu0|Hanji 392-3]] presents Huang Sheng's hat-and-shoe analogy as a claim that ruler and minister positions cannot be reversed.

Han self-reference and taboo:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-392-3-daodi-zenme-zuo-caishi-yige-hao-diwang-lgrcpyv1mf14axsq6hnqxaymrpu0|Hanji 392-3]] says Yuan Gusheng's Liu Bang question makes the debate too awkward, after which Han Jingdi marks Tang-Wu mandate transfer as unnecessary for scholarship.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This page currently rests on one short episode. It records the host's reading of a Han court debate and does not yet establish how consistently Tang-Wu revolution was treated as taboo across Western Han, Eastern Han, or later dynastic intellectual history.
