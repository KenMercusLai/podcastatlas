# Suicide-Risk Recognition and Support / 轻生风险识别与支持

## Definition
Suicide-risk recognition and support is the practice of noticing self-directed death talk, preparatory behavior, self-harm, marked state change, or explicit help-seeking, then responding with direct concern, calm presence, safety protection, and qualified escalation rather than blame, taboo, or amateur diagnosis.

## Current Synthesis
Death curiosity and suicide risk overlap in vocabulary but are not equivalent. General childhood questions about mortality can be developmentally ordinary. Concern rises when attention persistently turns toward one's own death, how others would cope, unusual farewells, giving away valued possessions, self-harm, abrupt deterioration, preparation, or direct crisis contact.

Support has two linked tasks: reduce isolation by acknowledging pain and inviting direct conversation, and protect safety through current individualized assessment and appropriate professional, crisis, or emergency help. A person may be trying to end unbearable pain or communicate an unmet need, but that interpretation must not minimize lethality or turn one explanation into certainty.

The funeral interview adds practitioner observations of bereaved and self-harming families, including a direct question that the guest believes interrupted one mother's suicidal trajectory. It also describes discussing methods and bodily consequences with her own daughter. These accounts support taking death talk seriously and asking directly about safety; they do not validate graphic method discussion or funeral expertise as a substitute for clinical crisis care.

## Key Claims
- Self-directed death talk, preparation, unusual farewells, valued-item disposal, self-harm, deterioration, or help-seeking warrants active attention.
- General mortality curiosity is not equivalent to intent; pattern, personal reference, preparation, distress, means, and current safety matter.
- Direct, nonjudgmental questions can surface risk and unmet need more safely than taboo or dismissal.
- Quiet presence can reduce isolation, but severe or immediate danger requires qualified crisis or emergency escalation.
- Suicidal behavior may express unbearable pain or a need to be seen without becoming merely symbolic or nonlethal.
- Diagnosis labels, one behavior, or one successful anecdote cannot replace individualized assessment.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
No single sign proves intent, and absence of listed signs does not prove safety. The sources are public podcast summaries, not validated screening tools or complete safety plans. The funeral interview's method-specific family conversation and claimed rescue outcome are anecdotes and should not be generalized. Immediate danger, a plan or means, severe agitation or psychosis, an attempt, or inability to maintain safety requires urgent local crisis or emergency help.

## What Changed
- Added funeral-practice observations about direct inquiry after suicide bereavement and youth self-harm.
- Explicitly bounded method-focused discussion as personal testimony rather than a reusable intervention.
- Preserved the distinction between communicative function and potentially lethal risk.
