# Perpetrator Humanization–Responsibility Boundary / 加害者人性化与责任边界

## Definition

The perpetrator humanization–responsibility boundary separates making an agent's motives, shame, fear, ignorance, ordinary life, or capacity for change intelligible from using those qualities to erase public harm and responsibility.

## Current Synthesis

[[20-shiji-zhuanzhi-shi-dianying-xian-qingsuan-yi-bufen-gkwriaiosoyxcck-awttzvx8]] treats humanization as an ethical risk and a necessary analytical tool. A perpetrator who appears only as a monster can make mass violence seem external to ordinary institutions and people. Yet a story centered on private suffering, illiteracy, romance, charisma, or conscience can redirect sympathy away from victims and turn explanation into excuse.

The boundary is maintained by holding different questions together: what made participation possible, what choices remained, what harm resulted, what institutions authorized or rewarded it, and what public responsibility persists. Reenacted self-presentation can be especially revealing when perpetrators remain powerful, because boastful performance documents impunity; it can also reproduce their spectacle and expose subjects to risks the filmmaker cannot fully control.

## Key Claims

- Understanding a perpetrator's humanity does not by itself diminish responsibility.
- Pure monstrosity narratives can conceal the ordinary institutions, incentives, and choices that enable mass violence.
- Private shame or suffering must not displace the public and victim-centered dimensions of wrongdoing.
- Claims of conscience or moral awakening require scrutiny for plausibility, self-exculpation, and narrative convenience.
- Perpetrator self-performance can reveal continuing impunity while also granting renewed attention and control.
- Responsibility remains differentiated across leaders, institutions, direct actors, beneficiaries, bystanders, and resisters without becoming optional.

## Evidence

### Humanization without exculpation

- [[20-shiji-zhuanzhi-shi-dianying-xian-qingsuan-yi-bufen-gkwriaiosoyxcck-awttzvx8]] uses *The Reader* to ask whether shame, ignorance, and intimacy can deepen characterization without obscuring legal and historical responsibility.

### Conscience and narrative convenience

- [[20-shiji-zhuanzhi-shi-dianying-xian-qingsuan-yi-bufen-gkwriaiosoyxcck-awttzvx8]] treats the listener's moral turn in *The Lives of Others* as dramatically possible but ethically contestable when it risks beautifying a surveillance-system operator.

### Perpetrator performance under impunity

- [[20-shiji-zhuanzhi-shi-dianying-xian-qingsuan-yi-bufen-gkwriaiosoyxcck-awttzvx8]] reads *The Act of Killing* as evidence that perpetrators' confident genre performance can expose the continued power of both killers and their public narrative.

### Distributed responsibility

- [[20-shiji-zhuanzhi-shi-dianying-xian-qingsuan-yi-bufen-gkwriaiosoyxcck-awttzvx8]] repeatedly asks how ordinary people, informers, police, bureaucrats, professionals, and national audiences enter systems of violence without assigning identical culpability to every role.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not supply a universal test for when sympathy becomes excuse. Audience response can differ from authorial intention, and a morally complex portrayal may be interpreted as condemnation, understanding, fascination, or rehabilitation. The source's film readings remain interpretive, and legal guilt must be established through evidence and procedure rather than narrative force.

## What Changed

- Created a responsibility-preserving framework for humanized depictions of perpetrators.
- Added self-performance under continuing impunity as both evidence and an ethical hazard.
