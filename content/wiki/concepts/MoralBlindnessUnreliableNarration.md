# Moral Blindness Through Unreliable Narration

## Definition

Moral blindness through unreliable narration is a storytelling method in which a loyal, limited, or self-protective narrator misreads events whose darker significance is increasingly visible to the audience.

## Current Synthesis

[[BubblesFilm|Bubbles]] builds unreliability from character rather than arbitrary concealment. The imagined chimpanzee reads social life through rank, succession, territory, and loyalty; takes “King of Pop” literally; and transforms cosmetic procedures, celebrity behavior, family power, and children at Neverland into a royal story he can emotionally survive.

The gap between narrator and audience produces comedy, dignity, and pathos at once. Bubbles is funny because his explanations are grandiose and mistaken, but tragic because they protect his attachment to someone who abandons and may have harmed him. As children displace him, jealousy supplies personal stakes before moral understanding catches up.

Revision exposes the device's ethical limit. After *Leaving Neverland*, Adamson and the producers made Bubbles less ambiguous about alleged abuse and more decisive in rejecting Jackson. That change increases explicit moral clarity but narrows the interpretive gap that made loyalty resemble collective public denial.

## Key Claims

- Effective unreliability follows a stable worldview, emotional need, and pattern of interpretation.
- Audience knowledge can exceed narrator knowledge without stripping the narrator of dignity.
- Comedy can coexist with harm when mistaken interpretation reveals attachment rather than treating suffering as disposable.
- Personal displacement can make a distant moral conflict emotionally legible before a narrator understands it fully.
- Loyal blindness can implicate audiences who share the narrator's desire not to revise their image of a beloved figure.
- Greater explicit clarity can improve moral legibility while weakening ambiguity, pathos, and dramatic participation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Unreliable narration can create productive distance, but distance can also aestheticize, obscure, or trivialize harm. The bounded source provides the writer and host's interpretation rather than audience research, the screenplay, testimony from alleged victims, or comparative evidence about alternate drafts. It therefore demonstrates a proposed mechanism and revision dilemma, not a general rule that ambiguity is ethically or artistically superior.

## What Changed
