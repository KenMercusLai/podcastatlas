## Definition
Psychiatric everyday language is the spread of clinical or quasi-clinical mental-health vocabulary into ordinary self-description, relationship conflict, workplace talk, and social media interpretation.

## Current Synthesis
The episode treats psychiatric language as real progress and real risk at the same time. Naming anxiety, depression, ADHD, bipolar disorder, and other conditions can reduce stigma, make suffering visible, and route people toward help. But when diagnostic and therapy vocabulary becomes a fast label for ordinary relational difficulty, it can replace curiosity with control.

The strongest synthesis is that naming is double-edged. It can open care when it identifies a real condition, but it can close relationship when words such as NPD, PUA, gaslighting, or PTSD become preemptive verdicts. In the episode's [[TechnologyPhilosophy|technology-philosophy]] frame, this is part of a broader technical mindset: life becomes easier to manage once named, but less able to surprise, touch, or transform the person.

## Key Claims
- Wider mental-health vocabulary is an important social gain when it reduces stigma and helps people identify real distress.
- Clinical labels become risky when they are used too quickly as relationship shortcuts.
- Fast naming can give a feeling of control while reducing patience for complexity, ambiguity, and repair.
- Over-professionalized responses can weaken ordinary support from friends, classmates, teachers, and daily communities.
- The concept connects language, therapy culture, AI advice, and technical thinking because all can turn open-ended life into manageable objects.

- Ordinary-support loss - [[zai-keji-shidai-zhongxin-lijie-womende-ai-gongzuo-yu-shenghuo-ljjrm6-ovwduouaecvymkbeyuvuqu]] contrasts immediate recourse to counseling or institutional mediation with talking through pain among friends, classmates, or teachers.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
This concept should not be read as anti-psychiatry or anti-therapy. The source explicitly recognizes the importance of clinical recognition and stigma reduction. The risk concerns overextension and premature labeling in ordinary life.
