# Revolutionary Iconoclasm

## Definition

Revolutionary iconoclasm is the attempt to make political renewal possible by breaking inherited ideas, practices, objects, institutions, and historical memory rather than reforming them incrementally.

## Current Synthesis

In the source's account of the [[CulturalRevolution]], attacks on the “Four Olds” extended from street names, clothes, hair, gardens, pets, books, paintings, and monuments to temples and other material traces of the past. The objective was not only to defeat political opponents but to make an old social world illegitimate, unavailable, and nearly invisible.

The episode traces this impulse beyond 1966. May Fourth and New Culture thinkers treated Confucian inheritance as implicated in weakness, foreign domination, forced marriage, hierarchy, and national failure. Mao's image of tradition as an iron cage and Lu Xun's language of cannibalism expressed the belief that escape required rupture. That longer lineage explains a vocabulary of destruction without making the Cultural Revolution inevitable or excusing its violence.

## Key Claims

- Iconoclasm targets everyday practice and material memory as well as formal doctrine and elite authority.
- Destruction can function as a theory of liberation when inherited order is imagined as a closed cage.
- China's ancient civilizational weight could intensify radical anti-traditionalism rather than protect heritage.
- Earlier New Culture critique supplied a language of rupture that Maoist mobilization radicalized.
- Cultural erasure and interpersonal persecution were joined parts of the same purification campaign.

## Evidence

- Four Olds campaign: [[173-chairman-mao-the-cultural-revolution-glt2886812821]] lists attacks on names, grooming, clothes, gardens, animals, monuments, literature, art, and temples.
- Erasure of memory: [[173-chairman-mao-the-cultural-revolution-glt2886812821]] uses dismantled Tibetan temples to illustrate how the past could be made materially invisible.
- Intellectual lineage: [[173-chairman-mao-the-cultural-revolution-glt2886812821]] connects Cultural Revolution destruction to May Fourth, New Culture, Mao's Miss Zhao essay, and Lu Xun's anti-Confucian metaphors.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Intellectual precedent is not a direct causal chain. May Fourth and New Culture movements were internally diverse, and critique of coercive tradition does not imply support for Cultural Revolution persecution. The episode's selected examples cannot measure destruction uniformly across regions or distinguish every central order from local initiative.

## What Changed

- Established iconoclasm as a bridge between cultural destruction, political purification, and an older language of anti-traditional rupture.
- Qualified intellectual continuity so that precedent does not become inevitability.
