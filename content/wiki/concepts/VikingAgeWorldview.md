## Definition

Viking Age worldview is a reconstruction of how Scandinavian people may have experienced gods, local beings, dead people, animals, landscape, magic, danger, law, and social practice as interacting parts of reality rather than as modern separate categories.

## Current Synthesis

[[358-viking-sorcery-glt1200485419]] argues that modern “religion,” “mythology,” and “supernatural” categories can distort the evidence. Stories varied locally, children could learn practical warnings about forests and unseen beings, and everyday negotiation with elves or local powers may have mattered more frequently than formal attention to elite gods. Ritual evidence also includes sacrifice, gendered magic, battle fate, and morally troubling violence.

The Icelandic case adds landscape and conversion to that reconstruction. Volcanic forms, smoke, glaciers, and rocks can sustain story-shaped attention to place without proving unchanged pagan belief, while [[103-the-norse-sagas-glt8280648151]] presents official Christianisation around 1000 as a pragmatic political settlement that accommodated some older private practice. The surviving account is nevertheless mediated through later Christian manuscripts and must not be treated as direct access to pre-Christian consciousness.

## Key Claims

- Belief is better approached as practical orientation to a populated world than as assent to doctrine.
- Gods, local beings, animals, landscape, magic, law, and the dead could belong to one lived field.
- Oral stories were variable, local, and sometimes contradictory rather than one fixed canon.
- Christianisation could alter official structures faster than it erased everyday practices, fears, or respect for place.
- Landscape can shape supernatural imagination without proving simple religious continuity.
- Surviving reconstructions depend on late, uneven, and often Christian evidence.

## Evidence

- Category boundary: [[358-viking-sorcery-glt1200485419]] compares belief in Odin with belief in a mountain and describes practical negotiation with local beings.
- Ritual and moral range: [[358-viking-sorcery-glt1200485419]] joins seiðr, sacrifice, animals, Valkyries, warfare, and the dead within the lived field.
- Conversion and persistence: [[358-viking-sorcery-glt1200485419]] notes formal displacement alongside folkloric survival, while [[103-the-norse-sagas-glt8280648151]] presents Icelandic conversion as a pragmatic settlement with temporary accommodation.
- Landscape imagination: [[103-the-norse-sagas-glt8280648151]] links volcanic and uncanny physical forms to continuing respect for non-human presences without claiming an unchanged pagan survival.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The evidence is regionally uneven and often late, Christian, literary, archaeological, or analogical. “Viking Age worldview” is itself a modern synthesis and should not imply one uniform mentality across Scandinavia, Iceland, centuries, classes, or local traditions. Modern Icelandic landscape stories are not direct proof of Viking Age belief.

## What Changed

- Added Icelandic Christianisation as negotiated institutional change rather than instant replacement.
- Added landscape as a qualified source of continuing story and respect.
- Strengthened the boundary against treating modern folklore as unchanged pagan survival.
