## Definition

证据切断式宗室妥协 / evidence-suppression dynastic compromise is a ruler-centered settlement in which officials acknowledge a protected royal's likely guilt but deliberately remove or disable the evidentiary path to final punishment, allowing law, kinship, and regime stability to coexist as partially incompatible public stories.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-241-liangxiaowang-liuwu-kan-wo-ruhe-huayang-zuosi-lqbllfh9nph-vvu9emzhsxdfj1le|Hanji 241]] supplies the current case. Investigation of [[YuanAngWesternHan|袁盎]]'s assassination reaches Liang, the principal advisers die, and [[TianShuHan|田叔]] returns with testimony and records. He tells [[HanJingdi|汉景帝]] that [[LiuWuLiangKingWesternHan|梁孝王刘武]] has incurred a capital offense, so the settlement does not depend on a sincere finding of innocence.

Tian then burns the collected material and urges Jingdi not to press the factual question. If the prince survives after formal proof, Han law loses credibility; if he is executed, [[EmpressDowagerDouWesternHan|窦太后]] may collapse under grief and the emperor faces a dynastic-family crisis. Removing the record changes the decision space: responsibility can be shifted publicly onto dead advisers while the court privately retains a judgment of princely guilt.

The compromise is therefore not ordinary reasonable doubt. It creates doubt or non-justiciability on purpose. It also does not fully restore the offender: Liu Wu performs submission and regains a public relationship with Jingdi, but loses the trust signaled by sharing the imperial carriage. Evidence suppression avoids the terminal sanction while political suspicion and relational penalty remain.

## Key Claims

- A court can preserve a private judgment of guilt while disabling the public proof required for final punishment.
- Deliberate evidentiary loss differs from ordinary uncertainty because the gap is produced as a governance instrument.
- The mechanism protects the ruler from an impossible binary between visible legal surrender and catastrophic family enforcement.
- Dead or already punished subordinates can become the public endpoint of responsibility even when suspicion reaches their patron.
- Avoiding formal punishment does not restore trust, office boundaries, or political intimacy.
- The mechanism sacrifices archival truth and equal enforcement for short-term dynastic stability.

## Evidence

Acknowledged guilt and destroyed proof:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-241-liangxiaowang-liuwu-kan-wo-ruhe-huayang-zuosi-lqbllfh9nph-vvu9emzhsxdfj1le|Hanji 241]] says Tian Shu calls Liu Wu capital-guilty, burns the Liang testimony and records, and advises Jingdi not to pursue the facts further.

Public blame transfer:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-241-liangxiaowang-liuwu-kan-wo-ruhe-huayang-zuosi-lqbllfh9nph-vvu9emzhsxdfj1le|Hanji 241]] says Tian tells Dowager Dou that Liu Wu did not know of the plot and that the guilty advisers are dead, enabling her to accept the outcome.

Residual political penalty:
- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-241-liangxiaowang-liuwu-kan-wo-ruhe-huayang-zuosi-lqbllfh9nph-vvu9emzhsxdfj1le|Hanji 241]] says Jingdi and Liu Wu reunite publicly but no longer share the imperial carriage.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

One narrated case cannot establish a general Western Han doctrine or show that evidence destruction was lawful, routine, or institutionally accepted. The episode does not preserve the destroyed file, so the precise strength of the case, Liu Wu's instructions, Tian Shu's authority, Jingdi's knowledge, and the legal effect of burning the material remain source-scoped. The concept names the political logic attributed to the act; it does not endorse suppression, equate it with due process, or assume that every incomplete case is manufactured compromise.

## What Changed

- Created a distinction between naturally missing proof and proof deliberately removed to make a royal-family settlement governable.
- Identified lost trust as a residual sanction even when formal prosecution stops.
