# Frequency-First Outbreak Screening

## Definition

Frequency-first outbreak screening is the use of fast, inexpensive, repeatable triage to identify enough potentially infectious people quickly enough to reduce transmission, while routing alerts to a more specific confirmation process.

## Current Synthesis

[[the-dog-will-see-you-now-the-dog-will-see-you-now]] contrasts slow, costly, site-centered COVID testing with a proposed dog-screening workflow at schools and public venues. Its core systems claim is that outbreak control does not require every first-line screen to be perfect: speed, access, price, and repetition can matter because a result received after further transmission has less preventive value than a timely flag.

That insight does not make accuracy optional. A useful system must specify sensitivity, specificity, prevalence, screening interval, infectious-stage performance, confirmation, isolation consequences, access, and the harms of false results. The source's reproduction-number arithmetic and cost estimates illustrate the hypothesis but do not establish that canine screening would actually push transmission below replacement in a real population.

## Key Claims

- Timeliness and repetition can affect outbreak control independently of maximum analytical precision.
- Low-friction screening can reach people who would not repeatedly use expensive or centralized testing.
- A first-line alert should trigger defined confirmation rather than be treated as a diagnosis.
- False negatives matter because they can license continued transmission, while false positives can impose exclusion, anxiety, and confirmatory burden.
- Throughput, staffing, fatigue, queue design, access, and turnaround time are part of test-system performance.
- Population impact must be measured in deployment rather than inferred from per-screen accuracy or simple reproduction-number arithmetic alone.

## Evidence

### Timeliness and access

- [[the-dog-will-see-you-now-the-dog-will-see-you-now]] criticizes travel, discomfort, cost, and delayed results in PCR-centered screening and argues that people can remain infectious while waiting.

### Layered workflow

- [[the-dog-will-see-you-now-the-dog-will-see-you-now]] proposes rapid canine triage at a school entrance followed by a secondary rapid test for anyone indicated by a dog.

### Transmission hypothesis

- [[the-dog-will-see-you-now-the-dog-will-see-you-now]] uses an episode-reported reproduction number near 1.3 to argue that preventing a minority of transmissions could move an outbreak below replacement.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source offers a proposal, selected expert claims, and rough cost comparisons rather than a field evaluation. Frequent screening can fail when participation is low, infectiousness precedes detection, confirmation is delayed, isolation is unsupported, operators fatigue, performance drifts, or consequences deter attendance. Cost per screen is not total program cost, and a lower reproduction number cannot be attributed to screening without accounting for behavior, immunity, season, variants, other interventions, and measurement uncertainty.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to separate the general timing-and-frequency argument from the specific use of dogs.
- Made confirmation, false-result harms, and measured population outcomes explicit constraints on the proposal.
