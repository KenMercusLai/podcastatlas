# Hostage Mission Dependency Trap

## Definition

Hostage mission dependency trap names a crisis pattern in which the people sent to rescue, negotiate for, or support hostages become additional leverage because the detaining ruler can convert each new mission into a new dependency.

## Current Synthesis

The Abyssinian hostage crisis in [[705-queen-victorias-revenge-the-mad-emperor-of-abyssinia-part-1-glt8608697296]] shows the trap in stages. Captain Cameron and missionaries are detained while Teodros waits for a British answer; [[HormuzdRassam]] is sent to repair the relationship and briefly succeeds; then Rassam, Blanc, and Prideaux are also seized, while Reverend Martin Flad is sent to London with demands for technicians and equipment, leaving his family behind in Abyssinia.

[[706-queen-victorias-revenge-storming-the-citadel-part-2-glt3285474568]] shows how the trap ends. It is not dissolved by a better mission but by the [[AbyssinianExpedition1868|expedition]]: the prisoners are freed only after [[TeodrosII]]'s army is destroyed at [[Magdala]], and the emperor's own attempt to settle - 1,000 cows and 500 sheep and the question "surely it is peace now" - comes after the British already hold the hostages and is refused on grounds of local custom. Rassam, who spent the crisis as envoy, confidant and hostage, is the one who then arranges the emperor's burial.

## Key Claims

- A rescue envoy can become part of the hostage set if the detaining ruler sees continued possession as the only reliable way to command attention.
- Each new negotiator or technician request can widen the hostage field rather than narrowing it.
- Humanitarian caution can prolong the trap when the outside government fears that force will trigger hostage killing.
- The trap ends diplomatic ambiguity by making the outside power choose between continued bargaining and coercive rescue.
- Once the hostages are in the rescuing army's hands, the detaining ruler loses his leverage, and his later offers can be refused without cost.
- The rescue force's own logic takes over the ending: the emperor is killed less because the hostages still need freeing than because the army's withdrawal would otherwise be unsafe.
- The people inside the trap can end up as its archivists, as when the former hostage buries the ruler who held him.

## Evidence

- Envoy capture: [[705-queen-victorias-revenge-the-mad-emperor-of-abyssinia-part-1-glt8608697296]] says Rassam, Blanc, and Prideaux are seized after Rassam's apparently successful audience with [[TeodrosII]].
- Dependency demand: [[705-queen-victorias-revenge-the-mad-emperor-of-abyssinia-part-1-glt8608697296]] says Teodros keeps envoys and others while demanding blacksmiths, gunsmiths, artillery specialists, and equipment.
- Bargaining limit: [[705-queen-victorias-revenge-the-mad-emperor-of-abyssinia-part-1-glt8608697296]] records Flad's warning that troops could cause Teodros to slaughter hostages, followed by hostage messages saying Teodros is unlikely to release anyone.
- Force as the exit: [[706-queen-victorias-revenge-storming-the-citadel-part-2-glt3285474568]] says the hostages were released only after the Battle of Magdala and the emperor's failed suicide attempt.
- Refused settlement: [[706-queen-victorias-revenge-storming-the-citadel-part-2-glt3285474568]] says Theodros sent 1,000 cows and 500 sheep asking whether it was peace now, and that Napier refused the animals.
- Post-release role of the envoy: [[706-queen-victorias-revenge-storming-the-citadel-part-2-glt3285474568]] says Rassam rejoined the hostages, celebrated in Napier's tent, and later organised the emperor's funeral.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources leave Teodros's exact motive unresolved: deception, hatred of missionaries, emotional dependency on Rassam, and strategic leverage are all presented as possible explanations. The concept should therefore be used for the structural trap, not as a settled psychological diagnosis, and its resolution here is specific to a case where the rescuing state was willing to spend an enormous sum and march hundreds of miles rather than bargain further.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from the Abyssinian hostage-rescue sequence.
- Extended the concept to its resolution, which came through military victory rather than negotiation, and added the envoy's afterlife as the emperor's undertaker.
