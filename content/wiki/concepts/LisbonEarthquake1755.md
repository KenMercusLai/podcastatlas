# 1755 Lisbon Earthquake

## Definition

The 1755 Lisbon earthquake was a compound urban catastrophe of earthquake, tsunami, and fire whose destruction reshaped Lisbon and became a European reference point for debates about providence, suffering, reason, and state capacity.

## Current Synthesis

The sources treat the disaster as compound hazard, intellectual shock, knowledge event, and governing test. Lisbon's religious calendar, built environment, waterfront exposure, tsunami, and fire magnified destruction. [[Voltaire]] used the event against easy optimism, [[JeanJacquesRousseau|Rousseau]] emphasized urban concentration, and [[ImmanuelKant|Kant]] attempted a natural explanation. [[MarquisDePombal|Pombal]] joined emergency control to planned reconstruction and a nationwide parish questionnaire, making recovery an example of centralized administrative capacity without removing its coercive edge.

## Key Claims

- Earthquake, tsunami, and fire interacted rather than operating as separate disasters.
- Lisbon's wealth and prominence made the catastrophe a Europe-wide intellectual event.
- The disaster challenged confident providential and optimistic explanations of suffering.
- Emergency response joined practical relief to severe coercive control.
- Rebuilding the Baixa as a planned grid converted disaster recovery into state-led urban redesign.
- Standardized parish questions converted local observers into a distributed disaster-information network.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Mortality, destruction, GDP-loss, distant-effect, and reconstruction-speed estimates vary and remain episode-level claims. The “first scientific/systematic earthquake study” wording requires specialist qualification. The sources' contrast between an older city and rational Enlightenment rebuilding is analytically useful but compresses social costs, engineering history, property politics, forced movement, and the longer recovery process.

## What Changed

- Added parish questionnaires and archives as part of the disaster's knowledge legacy.
- Distinguished Voltaire's, Rousseau's, and Kant's intellectual responses.
- Deepened the qualification that fast response and planned rebuilding also relied on coercion.
