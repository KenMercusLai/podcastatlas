# Pull the Goalie Decision Rule

## Definition

The pull-the-goalie decision rule is the practice of choosing the action that best improves the probability of recovering a losing position, even when it increases the chance of a conspicuous, humiliating, or convention-defying failure.

## Current Synthesis

The rule separates the outcome that matters from the appearance of competent loss. [[CliffAsness]] and [[AaronBrownFinance|Aaron Brown's]] hockey model asks when an extra attacker raises the chance of tying a game enough to justify an undefended net. Its episode-reported answers—5:40 remaining when down one and 11:40 when down two—are far earlier than convention, making the coach's institutional problem as important as the calculation: spectators and players may punish a lopsided loss even when the decision improved the chance of recovery.

The episode extends that structure beyond hockey, but the analogy is safest as a decision-audit question: are reputation, honor, or loss appearance silently replacing the actual objective? It does not establish that every unconventional high-variance action is rational. The objective, model, evidence, downside constraints, reversibility, affected parties, and decision-maker's incentives must still be examined.

## Key Claims

- Decision quality depends on the objective being maximized; recovery probability is different from expected score margin or entertainment value.
- Visible downside can make a better modeled strategy institutionally unattractive even when decision-makers understand the calculation.
- Conventional failure may protect reputation because observers punish an unusual action more readily than an ordinary loss.
- The rule is most useful as a diagnostic for hidden approval and honor costs, not as a command to maximize risk.
- Ethical and safety constraints can override a narrow probability calculation, especially when the model assigns risk to other people.

## Evidence

### Objective mismatch and modeled timing

- [[malcolm-gladwell-s-12-rules-for-life-malcolm-gladwell-s-12-rules-for-life]] reports that Asness and Brown optimize the chance of recovering the game and recommend much earlier goalie pulls than customary practice.

### Reputation-sensitive implementation

- [[malcolm-gladwell-s-12-rules-for-life-malcolm-gladwell-s-12-rules-for-life]] explains that an early pull can transform a close-looking loss into a humiliating score, creating audience, player, and career pressure against the model.

### Cross-domain stress tests

- [[malcolm-gladwell-s-12-rules-for-life-malcolm-gladwell-s-12-rules-for-life]] applies the structure to a fictional home invasion and duty-to-retreat law, showing how visible abandonment or cowardice can compete with survival probability while leaving both analogies contested.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The supplied source is a structured summary rather than the hockey paper, assumptions, code, or empirical evaluation. Optimal timing changes with scoring rates, rules, game state, opponent quality, information, and the selected utility function. Coaches may also have legitimate objectives beyond tie probability, including player safety, season incentives, morale, entertainment, and uncertainty about the model.

The home-invasion analogy is not general safety advice and illustrates why transferring a clean sports model into life-or-death conditions is dangerous. Real cases contain unknown attacker behavior, multiple lives, asymmetric information, moral duties, legal constraints, and irreversible harm. A decision can reject reputation management yet still be reckless, unethical, or based on false precision.

## What Changed

- Established the distinction between maximizing recovery probability and preserving the appearance of a close loss.
- Added reputation, honor, and conventional competence as implementation costs rather than treating the problem as calculation alone.
- Limited the rule to an objective-and-incentive audit rather than a generic endorsement of high variance.
