# Postwar Keynesian Retreat

## Definition
Postwar Keynesian retreat is the wiki's label for the political move away from the post-war assumption that governments could reliably use deficit spending to escape recession without triggering deeper confidence, inflation, or currency problems.

## Current Synthesis
[[665-britain-in-the-70s-the-bailout-from-hell-part-4-glt9598017819]] makes [[JimCallaghan|Callaghan]]'s 1976 Labour conference speech the key moment. Written with help from Peter Jay, the speech told Labour that Britain had lived on borrowed time and could no longer spend its way out of recession. The episode treats this as a major shift because it came from a Labour prime minister before [[MargaretThatcher]] consolidated Thatcherism.

## Key Claims
- The retreat is political as much as technical: it matters that a Labour leader stated the limit publicly to his own party.
- The shift was driven by inflation, sterling weakness, unemployment, and external-credit constraints rather than by ideology alone.
- Callaghan's speech did not instantly make Labour Thatcherite, but it narrowed the plausible range of governing economics.
- Praise from Conservative-leaning papers and Milton Friedman shows how the speech crossed old partisan expectations.

## Evidence
- Public break: [[665-britain-in-the-70s-the-bailout-from-hell-part-4-glt9598017819]] says Callaghan told Labour that Britain could no longer spend its way out of recession.
- Crisis constraint: [[665-britain-in-the-70s-the-bailout-from-hell-part-4-glt9598017819]] embeds the speech in sterling weakness, inflation, and IMF pressure.
- Cross-partisan reception: [[665-britain-in-the-70s-the-bailout-from-hell-part-4-glt9598017819]] notes limited Labour enthusiasm but praise from Conservative-leaning papers and Milton Friedman.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept should not be flattened into a claim that Keynesianism vanished in 1976 or that Callaghan and Thatcher had identical programs. The source presents a turning point in assumptions, not a complete ideological conversion.
