# Idiosyncratic Attraction

## Definition
Idiosyncratic attraction is the relationship pattern where desirability becomes pair-specific as people interact, so one person can become unusually attractive to a particular counterpart even if broader social consensus rates them as ordinary.

## Current Synthesis
The source uses idiosyncratic attraction to qualify marketplace models of romance. When people meet as strangers or evaluate profiles, visible desirability and group consensus can dominate; after repeated contact, private moments, support, reciprocity, and shared stories can make one person's evaluation diverge from the crowd.

The concept does not deny first impressions or broad attractiveness effects. Its narrower claim is that compatibility often emerges through a dyadic history that cannot be predicted well from universal rankings, stated preferences, or trait checklists alone.

## Key Claims
- Attraction can begin with social consensus, but that consensus weakens as people know one another.
- Pair-specific positive impressions can make someone especially desirable to one person without making them generally high-ranked.
- Responsiveness, support, reciprocity, banter, and private moments help explain why attraction can diverge from group opinion.
- Idiosyncratic attraction makes [[RepeatedContactDating]] more informative than profile-only or interview-like dating.
- The concept qualifies [[DatingAppMarketplaceDynamics]] because app rankings describe one context, not the whole relationship process.

## Evidence
- Consensus decay: [[science-of-attraction-compatibility-romance-dr-paul-eastwick-scim9984287085]] says agreement about who is highly desirable tends to weaken as people get to know each other.
- Pair-specific fit: [[science-of-attraction-compatibility-romance-dr-paul-eastwick-scim9984287085]] describes compatibility as emerging when two people develop uniquely positive impressions of each other.
- Mechanisms of divergence: [[science-of-attraction-compatibility-romance-dr-paul-eastwick-scim9984287085]] points to support, reciprocity, unique experiences, banter, and private moments as reasons one person's opinion can depart from group consensus.
- Context boundary: [[science-of-attraction-compatibility-romance-dr-paul-eastwick-scim9984287085]] treats marketplace models as partly useful for first-impression settings but incomplete once people interact repeatedly.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source still allows marketplace dynamics in app, stranger, and first-impression contexts. It also does not prove that looks, status, age, or other broad traits never matter; it argues that those traits lose explanatory power when attraction develops inside a shared history. Because the source note summarizes research without full methods, the empirical strength of the claim remains source-scoped.
