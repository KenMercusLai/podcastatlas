## Definition

Greed as moral poison is a literary pattern in which coveted wealth does more than motivate acquisition: it narrows what characters can imagine about other people, converts cooperation into suspicion, and makes self-destruction appear as the rational defense of value.

## Current Synthesis

In [[LianChengJue|《连城诀》]], the treasure does not create every vice from nothing. 戚长发 and his fellow disciples already know betrayal, concealment, and violence, but their commitment to the treasure makes generosity and indifference to wealth unintelligible. After 狄云 saves him, 戚长发 still anticipates rivalry and tries to remove it; his own desire supplies the model through which he reads another person's conduct.

The poisoned jewels turn this distortion into a physical allegory. Jianghu figures and officials rush into the temple, refuse durable cooperation, and become animal-like while fighting over wealth. The episode's “贪毒” interpretation therefore joins motive, perception, and consequence: greed is poisonous before the toxin acts because it has already reorganized trust and judgment. The physical poison intensifies that pattern rather than proving a general psychology of crowds.

## Key Claims

- A prize can reveal existing moral commitments by showing what actors will sacrifice to possess or defend it.
- Greed distorts social inference when a person assumes that everyone else must value wealth in the same way.
- Projection can make rescue, restraint, and noncompetition appear deceptive rather than credible.
- Competitive possession can dissolve family, teaching, and Jianghu obligations before any literal punishment occurs.
- A physical toxin can externalize an already active ethical or psychological poison.
- Allegorical force and realistic plausibility remain separate judgments.

## Evidence

### Greed as a model of other minds

- [[lh2xjiu48gisboa7esabpaejxisk]] recounts that 戚长发 tries to kill 狄云 after being rescued because he cannot believe anyone could face treasure without wanting it.
- [[lh2xjiu48gisboa7esabpaejxisk]] reads the three disciples' concealment and mutual suspicion as projection from their own betrayal onto pupils, children, and allies.

### Literal poison and allegorical consequence

- [[lh2xjiu48gisboa7esabpaejxisk]] presents the poisoned jewels and the temple frenzy as a literal mechanism that also symbolizes Buddhist “贪毒.”
- [[lh2xjiu48gisboa7esabpaejxisk]] argues that the competitors' desire has already made them behave destructively even before the physical toxin takes effect.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is derived from one podcast interpretation of a fictional novel, not empirical evidence that greed always produces violence or that wealth is intrinsically corrupting. The characters also act through prior betrayal, fear, institutional position, incomplete information, and genre convention. The hosts themselves question the crowd's refusal to cooperate and other plot mechanics, so the finale's allegorical clarity should not be converted into realistic social prediction.

## What Changed

- Created the concept by separating greed's perceptual and relational effects from the treasure's literal poison.
- Preserved the distinction between an effective moral allegory and disputed causal realism.
