# Constitutionalist Military Norm

## Definition
A constitutionalist military norm is the expectation that armed forces obey lawful civilian authority and do not decide political succession through force.

## Current Synthesis
The Chilean cases show both the value and fragility of the norm across two command crises. [[370-the-1973-chilean-coup-allende-nixon-and-the-cia-part-1-glt6405070034]] presents [[ReneSchneider|René Schneider]] as the obstacle to using the army against [[SalvadorAllende]]'s 1970 accession; the failed kidnapping and his death initially strengthened constitutional ratification. [[371-the-1973-chilean-coup-general-pinochet-seizes-power-part-2-glt3740766328]] shows [[CarlosPrats]] suppressing the Tanquetazo and obstructing later coup planning until political isolation, personal error, and resignation allowed [[AugustoPinochet]] to replace him. A professional tradition mattered, but it depended on commanders, networks, and political support strong enough to survive repeated pressure.

## Key Claims
- Constitutional restraint can depend heavily on command appointments, succession, and the protection of commanders who embody it.
- A failed attack on a constitutionalist commander can temporarily reinforce legal succession through public reaction.
- A failed coup can reveal loyal capacity while teaching plotters how to organize a stronger attempt.
- Professional tradition is vulnerable when political polarization enters officer networks and families.
- Personal confidence in an officer is not a substitute for resilient institutional controls.
- Replacing successive constitutionalist commanders can change feasible action even when formal rules remain unchanged.

## Evidence
- Early constitutional barrier: [[370-the-1973-chilean-coup-allende-nixon-and-the-cia-part-1-glt6405070034]] presents Schneider's non-intervention stance as an obstacle to preventing Allende's ratification.
- Failed coercion: [[370-the-1973-chilean-coup-allende-nixon-and-the-cia-part-1-glt6405070034]] connects Schneider's kidnapping and death to stronger support for constitutional succession.
- Active restraint: [[371-the-1973-chilean-coup-general-pinochet-seizes-power-part-2-glt3740766328]] describes Prats suppressing the June revolt and being viewed as a coup obstacle.
- Norm fragility: [[371-the-1973-chilean-coup-general-pinochet-seizes-power-part-2-glt3740766328]] follows reputational pressure, Prats's resignation, Pinochet's appointment, and the successful coup less than three weeks later.
- Misplaced reliance: [[371-the-1973-chilean-coup-general-pinochet-seizes-power-part-2-glt3740766328]] records Allende's circle trusting both Chile's non-intervention tradition and Pinochet's perceived loyalty.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Two narrative episodes cannot isolate how much causal weight belongs to command succession versus economic crisis, institutional conflict, foreign pressure, or deeper officer alignment. Schneider's and Prats's importance does not mean the norm rested only on individuals, nor does it imply that civilian legality was uncontested or every officer shared the same motives.

## What Changed
- Extended the model back to Schneider's defense of Allende's 1970 constitutional accession.
- Added the distinction between failed coercion that temporarily reinforces a norm and failed revolt that teaches later plotters.
