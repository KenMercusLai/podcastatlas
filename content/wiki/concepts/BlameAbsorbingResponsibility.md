## Definition

揽过式责任承担 / Blame-Absorbing Responsibility is the practice of voluntarily taking visible fault or procedural liability in order to reduce harm, protect a morally important relationship, or stabilize a dangerous power situation. Its boundary is principle and legal risk: responsibility can be chosen only when the actor can genuinely own the act without hiding unlawful, coercive, or unauthorized harm.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 832 gives the positive case through [[ChenShiLateHan|Chen Shi]]. [[HouLanLateHan|Hou Lan]] pressures [[GaoLunLateHan|Gao Lun]] into an unsuitable appointment, and Chen recognizes both sides of the trap: the person should not hold office, but Hou Lan's power cannot be ignored without consequence. Chen privately absorbs responsibility for the appointment document, and Gao later tells the public that Chen gave the good name to his superior and took the fault onto himself.

Hanji 820 adds the hard boundary. The episode opens with a workplace warning that small responsibility can sometimes preserve relationships, but principle and legal-risk responsibility should not be accepted for a superior. Its historical warning case is [[ChenGuiDuliaoGeneralLateHan|Chen Gui]]: the court later jails and removes him after he coerces [[XiuliChanyu|Xiuli]] and the Left Wise King into suicide without proper authority. That may look like a "backing the leadership" or scapegoat story, but the page treats it as a failed responsibility case because the underlying action was unauthorized and harmful.

## Key Claims

- Blame absorption differs from passive scapegoating when the actor knowingly chooses responsibility to reduce harm under constraint.
- The practice depends on moral credibility and later intelligibility; without trust or disclosure, it can look like corruption, cowardice, or opaque favoritism.
- Responsibility cannot be ethically absorbed when it hides principle violations, legal exposure, or unauthorized coercion.
- Protecting a superior's reputation is not itself enough; the protected act must still be bounded and defensible.
- Chen Gui's Hanji 820 case shows that being punished after taking or receiving blame is not the same as morally clean responsibility-taking.
- The practice should remain exceptional, not a general workplace tactic for covering bad decisions.

## Evidence

- Positive chosen-responsibility case: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-832-wei-lingdao-beiguo-ta-shi-sha-haishi-zhi-lmfpuixmi4odrf6lu31rf6-66-lq|Hanji 832]] says Chen Shi proposes handling the appointment responsibility himself after Hou Lan's pressure traps Gao Lun.
- Moral credibility and public explanation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-832-wei-lingdao-beiguo-ta-shi-sha-haishi-zhi-lmfpuixmi4odrf6lu31rf6-66-lq|Hanji 832]] says local people misunderstand Chen, Gao later explains him publicly, and Chen still treats the matter as his own fault.
- Workplace boundary statement: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-820-zhichang-jingshi-xiaoxin-lingdao-rang-ni-bei-heiguo-lqkejieiihmupfifuk8sxp3oadap|Hanji 820]] explicitly warns against carrying blame for principle and legal risks.
- Negative frontier boundary: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-820-zhichang-jingshi-xiaoxin-lingdao-rang-ni-bei-heiguo-lqkejieiihmupfifuk8sxp3oadap|Hanji 820]] says Chen Gui coerces the Southern Xiongnu chanyu and Left Wise King, is jailed and removed, and worsens surrender conditions.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept is morally narrow. Hanji 832 is persuasive because the source emphasizes coercive pressure, later explanation, and Chen Shi's broader moral reputation; without those conditions, blame absorption can become accountability evasion. Hanji 820 does not prove that every punished official is culpable, but it does show that a person cannot make unauthorized or legally risky action virtuous merely by accepting the consequences.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 820 as a negative boundary case for principle and legal-risk responsibility.
- Reframed the concept so Chen Shi's positive case is not generalized into a duty to carry a superior's bad or unauthorized decisions.
