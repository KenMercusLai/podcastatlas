## Definition

Legitimacy narrative attack is the strategy of weakening an opponent by first damaging the moral, procedural, or public-reason basis on which the opponent's actions can stand, so that later coercion or removal appears "名正言顺" rather than merely self-interested force.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-520-daji-duishou-weishenme-yao-xian-faqi-yulunzhan-lh1ibhc9xsihtrh6mhzznqq9ioso|Hanji 520]] introduces the concept through the episode's opening explanation of why conflict often begins with public-opinion work. The host's frame is that attacking a rival is not only material; it also targets the rival's moral and symbolic footing. If a rival can be marked as lacking private virtue, violating public duty, or acting without proper name and reason, then subsequent pressure becomes easier to justify.

The Western Han case adds a qualification. The same public-reason field can also constrain the attacker. [[WangFengWesternHan|王凤]] can move against [[FengYewangWesternHan|冯野王]], but [[DuQingWesternHan|杜清]] warns that unclear punishment and secret charges after [[WangZhangWesternHan|王章]]'s death may make the court itself look illegitimate. Narrative attack therefore depends on credibility: once the public believes punishment is retaliation against honest speech, the attack can damage the powerholder's own standing.

## Key Claims

- Conflict actors seek public and moral reasons because force without accepted justification is harder to stabilize.
- Attacking an opponent's private virtue or public duty can make the opponent's later actions look invalid.
- Procedural credibility matters: punishment framed as lawful can backfire when rules are unclear or applied retroactively.
- Secret charges create interpretive risk because observers may fill the silence with a retaliation story.
- Reopening speech channels can be a legitimacy-repair move after a punished-speaker case.

- Opening frame: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-520-daji-duishou-weishenme-yao-xian-faqi-yulunzhan-lh1ibhc9xsihtrh6mhzznqq9ioso|Hanji 520]] explains "舆论战" through "顺德者昌," "兵出无名," and the need to make action publicly justifiable.
- Moral weakening: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-520-daji-duishou-weishenme-yao-xian-faqi-yulunzhan-lh1ibhc9xsihtrh6mhzznqq9ioso|Hanji 520]] says attackers often portray the opponent as unfilial or morally flawed so the opponent's work will not "stand."
- Procedural backfire: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-520-daji-duishou-weishenme-yao-xian-faqi-yulunzhan-lh1ibhc9xsihtrh6mhzznqq9ioso|Hanji 520]] says Du Qing objects to punishing Feng Yewang under a rule not clearly present when the conduct occurred.
- Secret-charge risk: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-520-daji-duishou-weishenme-yao-xian-faqi-yulunzhan-lh1ibhc9xsihtrh6mhzznqq9ioso|Hanji 520]] says Wang Zhang's charges were not public, so people might believe he was punished for direct remonstrance.
- Repair through speech: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-520-daji-duishou-weishenme-yao-xian-faqi-yulunzhan-lh1ibhc9xsihtrh6mhzznqq9ioso|Hanji 520]] says Du Qing recommends opening remonstrance so the court can visibly show it does not punish honest advice.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is created from a single episode and should stay bounded. The source gives a broad strategy frame and one Western Han case, but it does not prove that every public-opinion campaign is cynical or that Wang Feng's full motives in the Feng Yewang impeachment are completely recoverable. The concept also distinguishes legitimacy attack from simple propaganda: its effect depends on whether the public reason remains credible.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Hanji 520's opening "舆论战" frame and its Wang Feng, Feng Yewang, Wang Zhang, and Du Qing case evidence.
