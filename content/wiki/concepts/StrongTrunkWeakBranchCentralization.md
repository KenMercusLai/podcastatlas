## Definition

Strong-trunk weak-branch centralization / 强干弱枝式集权 is the imperial governance logic of concentrating practical capacity at the center while reducing the military, demographic, infrastructural, or elite-network capacity of regions that could resist central command.

## Current Synthesis

The Qin version appears through [[QinShiHuang|嬴政 / 秦始皇]]'s order to remove large city walls and river levees after the [[Jieshi|碣石]] tour. The point is not only construction policy: walls and defensive hydraulic works can preserve local autonomy and make regional actors harder to command.

The Western Han version works through [[GuanzhongEliteRelocation|关中豪强迁徙]] and [[MausoleumCountyEliteRelocation|陵县式豪强迁徙]]. Instead of dismantling physical defenses, the state moves powerful households and senior officials' families toward the imperial capital and mausoleum zone, making the center stronger while thinning regional power bases.

Hanji 1134 adds a late-Han administrative-map version. The episode presents the consolidation of fourteen provinces into a classicizing Nine Provinces arrangement as an imperial edict driven by [[CaoCao|曹操]]. Because Cao Cao remains governor of Ji Province while the reorganization absorbs additional territory into Ji and removes Sili, Liang, You, and Bing as separate units, redrawing the map strengthens the powerholder's direct base without requiring a new battlefield conquest.

Across the three branches, the stable mechanism is asymmetric capacity movement. The center or its dominant officeholder gains people, territory, supervision, or defensibility while regional units lose independent resources or status. The institutional surface differs, and formal redesign must not be equated automatically with effective control.

## Key Claims

- Regional capacity can reside in walls, levees, armed resources, population, elite networks, and household registration, not only formal offices.
- Qin's version weakens local defensive infrastructure after conquest.
- Western Han's version strengthens Guanzhong and mausoleum settlements by moving wealthy and powerful households toward the capital region.
- Late Han's version can work through provincial consolidation that enlarges the dominant officeholder's directly governed territory.
- The same centralization logic can look like security policy, population policy, ritual-mausoleum policy, or elite supervision depending on the institutional surface.
- Strong-trunk policy creates legitimacy and consent costs when local families lose ancestral, property, or livelihood continuity.
- Formal boundary change does not by itself prove uniform administrative capacity or uncontested local control.

## Evidence

- Built-environment control: [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-123-1-shen-zhi-yuyan-wangqin-zhe-hu-jing-shi-zhi-lg7m3vfdbcweb4joxe4jvhxjx6y8|Qinji 123-1]] reads Qin's removal of large walls and levees as reducing local ability to resist the center.
- Precedent logic: [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-123-1-shen-zhi-yuyan-wangqin-zhe-hu-jing-shi-zhi-lg7m3vfdbcweb4joxe4jvhxjx6y8|Qinji 123-1]] links the logic to [[Confucius|孔子]]'s "堕三都" precedent in [[LuState|鲁国]].
- Han relocation logic: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-526-hanchao-qipa-zhidu-huangdi-si-le-jiu-neng-qianhukou-lkht_jo0z7je4gbyk10ckuxv4cub|Hanji 526]] says moving Guandong wealthy households and senior officials' families into mausoleum counties strengthens the capital and weakens local magnates.
- Policy revival: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-526-hanchao-qipa-zhidu-huangdi-si-le-jiu-neng-qianhukou-lkht_jo0z7je4gbyk10ckuxv4cub|Hanji 526]] says Chen Tang argues for reviving Changlingyi under Chengdi to address powerful-household growth and fiscal strain.
- Administrative-map concentration: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1134-caocao-meiyou-cuanhan-chengdi-zhi-xin-llg1ubbjxxuwjyuoallhd8mkecod|Hanji 1134]] says a Nine Provinces reorganization removes several separate units and expands Ji Province while Cao Cao remains its governor.
- Classical cover and practical effect: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1134-caocao-meiyou-cuanhan-chengdi-zhi-xin-llg1ubbjxxuwjyuoallhd8mkecod|Hanji 1134]] interprets restoration of an ancient territorial name as the legitimating language for contemporary concentration.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept should not be flattened into one institution. Qinji 123-1 concerns walls, levees, and post-conquest local capacity; Hanji 526 concerns capital-region population concentration and household relocation; Hanji 1134 concerns a popular-history interpretation of provincial reorganization. Hanji 526 records moral and political resistance to forced migration, especially ancestral-grave separation, livelihood disruption, and elite unease. Hanji 1134 does not by itself verify every boundary, commandery transfer, legal consequence, implementation date, or Cao Cao's private motive, and formal incorporation should not be treated as complete local control.

## What Changed

- Added provincial consolidation as a late-Han administrative-map form of strong-trunk centralization.
- Distinguished formal boundary enlargement from effective and uncontested administration.
- Added classical restoration language as a possible legitimacy wrapper for contemporary concentration.
