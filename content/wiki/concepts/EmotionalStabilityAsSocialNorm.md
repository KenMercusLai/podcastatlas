## Definition
Emotional stability as social norm / 情绪稳定作为社会规范 is the idea that adults are expected to keep behavior controlled even when inner emotion is unsettled, while the demand to be "always stable" can itself become a questionable form of social discipline.

## Current Synthesis
The sources separate emotional stability into several layers: internal feeling, outward behavior, scene-specific role control, delayed release, recovery, and post-conflict interpretation. The comedy panel broadly accepts that some settings, such as commercial negotiation or public performance, require controlled behavior. It does not treat that requirement as proof that a person has no anger or should never express it.

The newer monologue supplies a more prescriptive version: sleep and restart after injury, do not let hatred ferment, control anger, avoid indiscriminate complaint, and sometimes replace speech with observation or a smile. It also says deliberate anger can be instrumental rather than uncontrolled. These claims sharpen the difference between regulating action and suppressing feeling, but they do not explain when disclosure, protest, or help-seeking is the safer response.

The contested part is whether “adults should always stay stable” is a useful responsibility norm or an excessive expectation. Luo Yonghao treats permanent stability as possible adult PUA or social pressure, while the newer source attaches the norm specifically to an ideal of female capability. The useful synthesis is therefore not stable versus unstable, but whether a person can fit expression to consequence, scene, safety, and relationship without turning composure into compulsory silence.

## Key Claims
- Emotional stability should distinguish inner feeling from outward behavior.
- Scene-specific composure can be necessary without requiring permanent emotional flatness.
- Adult stability norms are useful when they prevent harm, but suspect when they demand total suppression.
- Recovery after injury and refusal to ruminate can preserve agency without requiring forgiveness.
- Retaliation, sleep, argument, gaming, cleaning, and withdrawal are different regulation strategies rather than one moral scale.
- Gendered expectations can make emotional control a measure of female worth rather than a context-dependent skill.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Neither source is clinical evidence or workplace training. The panel does not settle whether restraint is maturity, cowardice, politeness, or strategy in every case; the same outward behavior can be interpreted differently by actor and observer. The monologue's advice can help interrupt rumination and impulsive action, but smiling, silence, and concealed distress can also hide abuse, discrimination, grief, illness, or unsafe work. “Controlled anger” may be strategic, but it can still intimidate or harm other people.

## What Changed
- Added recovery after injury, rumination limits, selective silence, and the idea of deliberate rather than uncontrolled anger.
- Added gendered composure as a social-pressure risk and made safety, disclosure, and help-seeking explicit boundary conditions.
