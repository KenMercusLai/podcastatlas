# Fugitive Slave Act Federalism

## Definition

Fugitive Slave Act federalism is the reversal in which slaveholding interests used stronger federal enforcement to recover alleged fugitives while Northern states invoked local rights against that enforcement.

## Current Synthesis

[[200-american-civil-war-the-causes-part-1-glt1124511401]] uses the 1850 law to show why a generic “states' rights” explanation cannot displace slavery. Federal authority carried slave-catching into Northern communities, while free-state resistance appealed to state and local protections. The constitutional principle changed sides according to whether federal power protected or obstructed slave property.

The [[AnthonyBurns|Anthony Burns]] rendition supplied the episode's clearest public example. A chained man escorted through Boston to a federal ship turned legal obligation into witnessed coercion, bringing slavery into a city that identified with revolutionary liberty.

## Key Claims

- The 1850 act expanded federal enforcement in service of slave-property claims.
- Northern resistance could use states' rights language against federal power.
- The conflict shows that federalism arguments were inseparable from the institution being protected.
- Public renditions changed opinion by making distant slavery locally visible.
- Legal enforcement and abolitionist media worked through different but reinforcing routes.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not imply that every Southern politician always favored federal power or every Northern state consistently resisted it. It identifies a specific policy conflict demonstrating that “states' rights” was applied instrumentally around slavery. The statute's legal details, enforcement frequency, public reaction, and ranking among causes remain source-scoped.
