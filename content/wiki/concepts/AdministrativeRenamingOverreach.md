## Definition

Administrative renaming overreach / 行政改名过度 is the governance failure in which rulers repeatedly rename offices, places, divisions, or fiefs to express ideological order or auspicious intent, but create operational confusion for officials and commoners.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-647-shisanchao-gudu-luoyang-weihe-zhiguanzhongyao-ljjcpxlri4dq2zoa9oonyauhcwfv|Hanji 647]] grounds the concept in [[WangMang|王莽]]'s [[XinDynasty|新朝]] reforms. Wang Mang imitates ancient institutional language, creates offices such as zuzheng, lianshuai, and dawei, redraws commanderies and counties, divides large commanderies, and uses Zhou-style categories to imagine ten thousand fiefs.

The source's key point is legibility cost. Wang Mang's new names often carry good wishes, such as peace, stability, loyalty, and virtue, but repeated changes make administration harder to operate. The episode says some places are renamed repeatedly and then returned to their old names, forcing edicts to include old names under new names so officials and commoners can understand them.

## Key Claims

- Administrative names can be instruments of ideology, not only neutral labels.
- Renaming becomes overreach when symbolic improvement increases practical confusion.
- A reform can weaken state legibility when offices and jurisdictions change faster than users can learn them.
- Auspicious language may reveal ruler hopes while doing little to solve material disorder.
- Repeated renaming fits the broader Wang Mang pattern of archaizing reform outrunning implementation capacity.

## Evidence

- Ancient-office imitation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-647-shisanchao-gudu-luoyang-weihe-zhiguanzhongyao-ljjcpxlri4dq2zoa9oonyauhcwfv|Hanji 647]] says Wang Mang uses texts such as Zhouguan and Wangzhi to set new office titles equivalent to commandery administrators.
- Territorial redesign: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-647-shisanchao-gudu-luoyang-weihe-zhiguanzhongyao-ljjcpxlri4dq2zoa9oonyauhcwfv|Hanji 647]] says he creates new state-pastor, monitor, commandery, township, and suburban-county arrangements.
- Repeated place-name churn: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-647-shisanchao-gudu-luoyang-weihe-zhiguanzhongyao-ljjcpxlri4dq2zoa9oonyauhcwfv|Hanji 647]] says some local names changed several times before reverting.
- Old-name annotations: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-647-shisanchao-gudu-luoyang-weihe-zhiguanzhongyao-ljjcpxlri4dq2zoa9oonyauhcwfv|Hanji 647]] says edicts had to annotate new place names with former names so people could identify them.
- Auspicious naming motive: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-647-shisanchao-gudu-luoyang-weihe-zhiguanzhongyao-ljjcpxlri4dq2zoa9oonyauhcwfv|Hanji 647]] reads names such as Danning, Fengmei, Pingbao, Shanyang, and Jianping as attempts to encode hopes of stability and order.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not reject administrative standardization as such. The bounded failure is rapid, repeated, and ideologically loaded renaming that creates confusion. Exact office equivalences, commandery counts, county totals, fief geography, and philological readings of each new name remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Hanji 647's account of Wang Mang's office, commandery, county, and fief renaming.
