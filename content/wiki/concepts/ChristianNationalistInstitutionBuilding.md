# Christian-Nationalist Institution Building

## Definition
Christian-nationalist institution building is the strategy of linking worship, family formation, education, publishing, business, housing, and political aspiration so that a local religious community can function as a prototype for a preferred national order.

## Current Synthesis
The source presents [[DougWilson]]'s Moscow, Idaho, network as more than a growing congregation. Churches, Logos School, New Saint Andrews College, [[CanonPress]], aligned businesses, housing, and large families create an ecosystem able to socialize members, employ and house newcomers, reproduce doctrine, and export media. Wilson explicitly connects this local prototype to a Christian America.

Institutional density can produce belonging, certainty, mutual support, and reduced dependence on hostile outside institutions. The same density can raise exit costs and concentrate authority when family, schooling, work, belief, friendship, and residence overlap. The episode therefore records community flourishing claimed by adherents alongside patriarchal hierarchy, anti-LGBTQ teaching, abuse allegations, civic separation, and family estrangement after departure.

## Key Claims
- A movement becomes socially durable when doctrine is embedded across multiple everyday institutions.
- Schools, colleges, publishers, businesses, and housing can turn local concentration into reproduction and expansion capacity.
- Owned media lets a place-based community recruit and influence audiences far beyond its geography.
- Dense community can provide genuine support and certainty while also increasing conformity pressure and exit costs.
- Democratic participation does not by itself make the intended end state religiously equal.
- Civic backlash may slow integration while also strengthening internal solidarity and attracting sympathetic newcomers.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The concept does not establish centralized control over every linked institution, unanimous member belief, or a completed national political program. The supplied source gives no independent census of Kirkers, ownership map, longitudinal recruitment data, complete abuse record, or comparison with other religious communities. Dense institutions are not inherently abusive or nationalist; the concept applies here because the source explicitly joins Wilson's institutional ecosystem to his preferred confessional state.

## What Changed
- Established a mechanism connecting local institutional density to national religious-political ambition.
- Made support, conformity, expansion, and exit costs simultaneous rather than mutually exclusive outcomes.
