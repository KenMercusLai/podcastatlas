## Definition
Control-recall self-efficacy is the use of a remembered episode of power, competence, or control to restore confidence and reduce stress when the present situation feels uncontrollable.

## Current Synthesis
[[AdamGalinsky]] uses experimental work with Joris Lammers to argue that recalling earlier control can change performance in a later stressful task. In the episode's narrative application, [[EkkapolChantawong]] draws on monastery experience with fasting, meditation, calm, and acceptance to reassure the boys and regulate himself. The experimental mechanism and the biographical case are related but not identical: one is a prompted memory manipulation, while the other is practiced experience available under crisis.

## Key Claims
- Present helplessness does not erase access to memories of earlier competence.
- Recalling control may increase perceived confidence and self-efficacy in a subsequent task.
- Related research summarized in the episode links control recall to lower physiological arousal during stressful speech.
- Prior practiced experience may provide both factual reassurance and emotional regulation.
- Memory recall supplements rather than replaces current skills, resources, and realistic assessment.

- Cave application - [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-7d7aecc147a16363380202f9d2d200df-53688783-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-7d7aecc147a16363380202f9d2d200df-53688783]] connects Chantawong's monastery experience to fasting knowledge, meditation, calm, and acceptance.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The supplied episode omits methods, sample sizes, effect sizes, and replication context, so the experimental claim remains source-scoped. Recalling competence can also produce overconfidence if earlier success is poorly matched to the present task. In the cave case, long-practiced skills and factual experience cannot be reduced to a brief recall prompt.

## What Changed
- Established the distinction between experimentally prompted control recall and crisis use of practiced prior experience.
