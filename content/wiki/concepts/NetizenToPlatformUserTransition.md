# Netizen-to-Platform-User Transition / 从网民到平台用户

## Definition

The netizen-to-platform-user transition is a shift in political and social role from membership in an online community with some expectation of rule-making, moderation, and exit-by-rebuilding toward operation inside a platform whose agreement, account system, ranking, moderation, and available actions are largely predefined.

## Current Synthesis

[[lpunzmmsbu-bkekxyllalgjz-sdd]] uses the contrast between “netizen” and “user” as an ideal-type history rather than a complete chronology. Forums, personal sites, and blogs are presented as spaces where members could sometimes set rules, choose moderators, manage sections, or establish a rival forum. Large platforms instead organize participation through accounts, standardized features, recommendation, moderation, and compulsory terms.

The key loss is not choice altogether but participation in setting the choice architecture. A user may optimize behavior, demand consistent enforcement, appeal a penalty, or move among available functions while rarely reaching the question of who made the rule and how it might be changed. Operational competence can therefore grow while governance capacity and political imagination contract.

## Key Claims

- “Netizen” can name community membership, while “user” can reduce the relationship to use of an already governed system.
- Platform scale and convenience can coexist with weaker participant access to rule-making and accountable decision-makers.
- Exit changes when leaving a forum to build another community is replaced by dependence on platform-held audiences, identity, and distribution.
- Appeals tend to frame disputes as execution errors or compliance problems rather than questions about the legitimacy of the underlying rule.
- Fair rule execution and fair rule-making are distinct governance demands.
- Increasing skill at operating a system does not necessarily increase the ability to govern or contest it.

## Evidence

- Community role contrast: [[lpunzmmsbu-bkekxyllalgjz-sdd]] describes early forum participants setting rules, choosing moderators, managing sections, and forming new forums.
- Bounded platform action: [[lpunzmmsbu-bkekxyllalgjz-sdd]] describes users accepting existing agreements and acting through predefined functions and options.
- Appeal reframing: [[lpunzmmsbu-bkekxyllalgjz-sdd]] argues that account and moderation disputes redirect attention toward compliant operation.
- Governance distinction: [[lpunzmmsbu-bkekxyllalgjz-sdd]] separates fairness in enforcement from participation in making the rules.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source provides an interpretive contrast, not a representative history of the internet. Early communities could be exclusionary, privately controlled, technically inaccessible, or dominated by administrators, while contemporary platforms can include elected moderators, community rule-setting, interoperability, creator governance, or meaningful appeal. “Netizen” and “user” overlap in ordinary language, and exit has never been equally available to all participants. The concept should therefore identify a governance tendency, not a universal before-and-after periodization.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to distinguish platform use from membership with governance capacity.
- Added the rule-execution versus rule-making fairness distinction.
- Identified operational competence and political participation as potentially divergent.
