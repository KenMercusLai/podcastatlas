# Presidential Impeachment

## Definition

Presidential impeachment is a constitutional process for confronting abuse of office, betrayal of public trust, or threats to constitutional government when elections, criticism, and ordinary administration are insufficient.

## Current Synthesis

The bounded sources treat impeachment as exceptional system repair rather than punishment for every illegality, policy error, private wrong, or unpopular act. The House accuses, the Senate tries, the Chief Justice presides over a presidential trial, and conviction requires a two-thirds Senate vote. Those high barriers preserve a fixed-term presidential system while leaving a remedy for abuse of the office itself.

Watergate adds the mechanism's political operation before formal completion. Investigation, documentary evidence, House Judiciary votes, and defection by members of the president's own party made conviction increasingly plausible, so [[RichardNixon|Nixon]] resigned without being impeached by the full House or tried by the Senate. Impeachment can therefore deter and signal constitutional judgment without reaching a final verdict, but its legitimacy depends on evidence and a standard distinguishable from partisan no-confidence politics.

## Key Claims

- Impeachment is constitutional repair, not an ordinary criminal prosecution or parliamentary confidence vote.
- The relevant threshold concerns abuse of presidential office and public trust, not merely bad policy, incompetence, or private wrongdoing.
- Separate House and Senate roles and a two-thirds conviction requirement keep removal exceptional.
- The credible advance of proceedings can force resignation before a completed House vote or Senate trial.
- Documentary evidence and cross-party judgment strengthen impeachment's claim to represent constitutional rather than purely partisan accountability.
- Repeated use inside polarized politics can blur the boundary between emergency repair and party retaliation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Nixon's resignation did not itself complete the constitutional impeachment process, so Watergate demonstrates political and deterrent force rather than a Senate conviction precedent. The source's claim that modern presidential politics has become criminalized is interpretive, and comparisons among Nixon, Reagan, Clinton, and Trump do not make their conduct legally equivalent. High thresholds can protect stability while also making valid removal difficult; the bounded sources do not resolve that structural tradeoff for every case.
