# Climate-Disease Ecology

## Definition

Climate-disease ecology is the historical pathway through which volcanic, climatic, and other environmental disturbances alter pathogen, host, vector, food, or mobility conditions and thereby change disease risk.

## Current Synthesis

[[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] argues that environmental shocks often matter through what they trigger rather than through temperature or rainfall alone. Its sixth-century discussion links volcanic cooling to the conditions surrounding the Justinianic plague, and it offers Santorini-smallpox and Tambora-cholera comparisons as further examples.

The concept sits between environmental and pandemic history. It does not claim that an eruption mechanically causes an outbreak; it asks which biological and social channels connect the disturbance to pathogen behavior, exposure, spread, and institutional response.

## Key Claims

- Disease can be an indirect transmission channel for environmental shocks.
- Volcanic effects matter through ecological and social consequences, not eruption chronology alone.
- Pathogen behavior must be analyzed with host vulnerability, mobility, food stress, and institutions.
- Historical disease attribution requires tighter evidence than temporal proximity.
- Preparedness for changing disease environments is part of climate resilience.

## Evidence

- Sixth-century case: [[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] says eruptions in the 530s and 540s matter partly through what they triggered around the Justinianic plague.
- Comparative examples: [[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] associates Santorini with smallpox dynamics and Tambora with cholera spreading from Bengal.
- Present priority: [[313-climate-apocalypse-glt8815959934]] names resistance to disease environments alongside food and energy as a basic preparedness goal.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode presents large causal comparisons without detailing the underlying epidemiological evidence. The Santorini, Tambora, and sixth-century links remain source-scoped, and temporal association alone cannot distinguish climatic influence from war, trade, sanitation, demography, or other disease drivers.
