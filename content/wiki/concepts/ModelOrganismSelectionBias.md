## Definition

Model organism selection bias is the risk that a research field builds general theory around species chosen for cost, availability, breeding speed, docility, housing, and experimental control rather than demonstrated representativeness of the target phenomenon.

## Current Synthesis

[[rat-vs-raccoon-rat-vs-raccoon|Rat vs. Raccoon]] traces psychology's “century of the rat” to an institutional fit. Rats were small, inexpensive, fast-breeding, already available to biomedical researchers, and capable of producing large, repeatable samples. Raccoons escaped, damaged facilities, resisted standard conditioning, and made clean experiments harder.

The bias is not that rat evidence is automatically false. It arises when success inside a tractable model becomes an unstated claim about humans, or when alternative species and behaviors disappear from theory because they are methodologically inconvenient. Selection, breeding, and theory then reinforce one another: the field prefers controllable animals, creates more controllable strains, and interprets controllable behavior as the general form of behavior.

## Key Claims

- Model-organism centrality can reflect institutional convenience as much as target similarity.
- Existing infrastructure and training create path dependence by making the incumbent model appear to be the only practical option.
- Species that resist conditioning or standard housing can be excluded even when their behavior is theoretically informative.
- Selective breeding changes the model itself, so laboratory animals may represent the experimental system better than their wild population.
- Generalization becomes risky when the reasons for model selection are forgotten or mistaken for evidence of representativeness.

## Evidence

### Convenience and scale

- [[rat-vs-raccoon-rat-vs-raccoon|Rat vs. Raccoon]] says rats were cheaper, smaller, easier to house and feed, faster to reproduce, and suitable for larger samples than raccoons.

### Behavioral and institutional fit

- [[rat-vs-raccoon-rat-vs-raccoon|Rat vs. Raccoon]] contrasts predictable conditioning with raccoon escape, destruction, and species-typical coin rubbing.

### Path dependence

- [[rat-vs-raccoon-rat-vs-raccoon|Rat vs. Raccoon]] has [[KellyLambert]] describe rats as effectively the inherited model available during her training.

### Theory-shaping selection

- [[rat-vs-raccoon-rat-vs-raccoon|Rat vs. Raccoon]] connects rat controllability to behaviorist claims that incentives and punishments could predict and control human conduct.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source offers a historical critique, not a systematic review of comparative psychology or biomedical translation. Practical tractability is scientifically valuable because it enables controls, replication, and cumulative work. A biased selection process does not invalidate every result from the selected organism, and a difficult or behaviorally varied species is not automatically a better human model. Fitness must be judged claim by claim.
