# Manufactured War Pretext

## Definition

A manufactured war pretext is a policy sequence that creates the appearance of noncompliance or threat by selectively enforcing rules, imposing conditions that the target cannot realistically meet, and publicizing a justificatory narrative before using the resulting failure as grounds for force.

## Current Synthesis

The Black Hills case begins before the formal ultimatum. Military exploration and [[GeorgeArmstrongCuster]]'s press cultivation turned a small gold find into a rush that officials initially tried but failed to contain. Purchase talks then failed because federal authority and Lakota leaders valued the [[BlackHills]] on incompatible terms. Commissioners recommended a congressionally imposed price and ration pressure, establishing coercive options before the administration changed enforcement policy.

The later episode then joins three moves. The Grant administration reportedly stopped enforcing restrictions on settlers entering land protected for the [[LakotaPeople|Lakota]] while leaving those restrictions formally intact. It ordered non-treaty Lakota to reach reservation agencies by 31 January 1876, despite winter travel conditions that made compliance effectively impossible. Finally, the Watkins report portrayed [[SittingBull]] and his followers as violent threats deserving punishment.

After the deadline passed, [[PhilipSheridan]] received authority to prepare for war. The concept does not require proving that every participant shared one secret intention; it identifies how asymmetric enforcement, an infeasible ultimatum, and threat amplification can make coercion appear reactive even when policy has structured the failure in advance.

## Key Claims

- Formal rules can conceal selective enforcement that benefits one side's encroachment.
- State-sponsored exploration and publicity can create settlement pressure that later policy treats as an accomplished fact.
- Failed negotiation becomes coercive when one side can impose price, supplies, or military consequences.
- An ultimatum is not a meaningful compliance test when its timing or conditions make compliance infeasible.
- Threat reports can convert structured noncompliance into public evidence of hostility.
- The transition from failed demand to military authorization should be analyzed as a sequence.
- Institutional participation can manufacture a pretext without requiring identical motives among all officials.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The episode supplies a strong intentional interpretation, but proving a unified conspiracy would require direct comparison of meeting records, orders, correspondence, and implementation.
- Governments may defend deadlines as administrative tools even when effects are coercive; stated purpose and structural function can diverge.
- Some contemporaneous security concerns could have been sincere without making every reported claim accurate.
- This concept should not make targeted people passive: Lakota leaders made strategic choices under the coercive conditions.
- The current synthesis is grounded in two adjacent popular-history reconstructions of one campaign.
- Failed purchase talks do not alone prove a manufactured pretext; the concept depends on the later sequence of non-enforcement, infeasible demand, publicity, and military authorization.

## What Changed

- Extended the sequence backward through gold publicity, uncontrollable intrusion, failed purchase, and proposed ration coercion.
