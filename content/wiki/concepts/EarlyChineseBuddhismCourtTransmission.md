## Definition

早期中国佛教宫廷传播 / early Chinese Buddhism court transmission is the pattern where Buddhist ideas move from limited foreign contact and elite practice into a court-legible narrative of imperial dream, envoy mission, scriptures, monks, and temple institution.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 737 gives two layers of Buddhist arrival. First, it says a Western Han Ai-era boshi disciple learned Buddhist scripture from a Great Yuezhi envoy, but the knowledge did not broadly spread. Second, it presents the familiar Eastern Han [[EmperorMingOfHan|Mingdi]] story: a dream of a golden figure leads to inquiry, envoys to Tianzhu, returned scriptures and monks, and the building of [[WhiteHorseTemple|白马寺]] in Luoyang.

The same source links this court-transmission story to [[LiuYingChuKingEasternHan|楚王刘英]], who reveres Huang-Lao, worships Buddha, fasts, and is initially praised rather than suspected in Mingdi's退赎诏. The current concept therefore separates early Buddhist practice from later political suspicion: Buddhism is not inherently treated as rebellion in Hanji 737, even though later episodes make private religious organization politically dangerous.

## Key Claims

- Early Buddhist transmission can appear first as limited foreign-contact learning before broader elite or court uptake.
- The Mingdi dream story turns foreign teaching into a court-authorized narrative of search, return, and institution-building.
- Liu Ying's early Buddhist/Huang-Lao practice is initially tolerated in Hanji 737, complicating later treason-case readings.
- White Horse Temple functions as an institutional memory node rather than the only possible beginning of Buddhism in China.
- Religious practice becomes politically legible differently depending on context: devotion, court curiosity, and covert organization are not the same thing.

- Earlier-contact layer: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-737-huanghou-shihao-zhidu-diyiren-liuxiu-laopo-bu-jiandan-lsdhr-gdx-yep3tzprjg9wtjbul2|Hanji 737]] says an Ai-era boshi disciple learned Buddhist scripture from a Great Yuezhi envoy but the teaching did not spread widely.
- Liu Ying tolerance: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-737-huanghou-shihao-zhidu-diyiren-liuxiu-laopo-bu-jiandan-lsdhr-gdx-yep3tzprjg9wtjbul2|Hanji 737]] says Mingdi returns Liu Ying's redemption silk and describes his Huang-Lao and Buddhist fasting without suspicion.
- Dream and mission narrative: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-737-huanghou-shihao-zhidu-diyiren-liuxiu-laopo-bu-jiandan-lsdhr-gdx-yep3tzprjg9wtjbul2|Hanji 737]] says Mingdi dreams of a golden figure and sends envoys to Tianzhu.
- Institutional node: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-737-huanghou-shihao-zhidu-diyiren-liuxiu-laopo-bu-jiandan-lsdhr-gdx-yep3tzprjg9wtjbul2|Hanji 737]] says scriptures, monks, and White Horse Temple follow the mission.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept records the episode's account and does not settle modern Buddhist-history chronology. It also does not erase later suspicion: Hanji 739-740 make Liu Ying's case politically dangerous in a different context. The current source supports a narrower distinction between tolerated religious practice and later covert-power anxiety.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Hanji 737's Liu Ying, Ai-era, Mingdi dream, and White Horse Temple material.
- Added an explicit qualification separating early court transmission from later religious-political suspicion.
