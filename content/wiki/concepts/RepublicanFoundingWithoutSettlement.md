# Republican Founding Without Settlement

## Definition

Republican founding without settlement describes a regime transition in which the old sovereign form is abolished before the new order has achieved a clear constitutional definition, shared legitimacy, or single accepted founding moment.

## Current Synthesis

The French and English cases share a gap between abolition and settlement but expose different failure modes. In France, the [[NationalConvention|National Convention]] abolished monarchy and instituted “Year One” amid low turnout, coercive electoral conditions, war, undefined institutions, and the continued presence of [[LouisXVI|Louis XVI]]; [[BattleOfValmy|Valmy]] supplied military credibility, and the king's later trial became a candidate for the irreversible act abolition had not provided.

In England, the execution of [[CharlesIOfEngland|Charles I]] preceded agreement over whether one king, the Stuart dynasty, or monarchy itself had ended. Kingship and the Lords were abolished only afterward, while a purged Commons and the army retained rival claims to authority. Regicide removed the old sovereign but neither defined civilian supremacy nor created durable succession, allowing [[OliverCromwell]]'s personal rule to postpone rather than complete the settlement.

## Key Claims

- Abolishing an old regime does not by itself specify the institutions or limits of its successor.
- Calendar reform and unanimous declarations can create symbolic rupture without producing broad political consent.
- Electoral mandate is qualified when turnout, intimidation, public voting, and wartime disruption constrain participation.
- Military survival can make an otherwise fragile constitutional order appear viable.
- A deposed ruler's continued existence can concentrate unresolved questions of sovereignty and legitimacy.
- Later founding violence may acquire meaning partly because the original transition remained symbolically incomplete.
- Military force and regicide can make abolition irreversible while weakening representative legitimacy and leaving authority or succession unresolved.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The source's claim that France lacked a clear proclamation is interpretive; abolition and “Year One” were themselves explicit acts of foundation.
- Institutional ambiguity does not mean the Republic had no legal authority or popular support.
- Louis's trial was not the only possible means of resolving the new regime's legitimacy.
- The French and English sequences should not be collapsed: their wars, representative institutions, religious conflicts, and successor regimes differed materially.
- The English Restoration does not prove the republic was doomed from the moment of regicide; the source emphasizes contingent leadership and succession choices.

## What Changed

- Extended the concept from the French abolition-before-regicide sequence to the English regicide-before-settlement sequence.
- Added military–legislative authority and succession as distinct dimensions of an incomplete founding.
