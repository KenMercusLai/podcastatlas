# AI-Assisted Writing Disclosure

## Definition
AI-assisted writing disclosure is the norm question of when a writer, publication, or public figure should tell readers that generative AI helped draft, edit, or shape a signed text.

## Current Synthesis
[[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-nvidias-historic-quarter-saas-comeback-bessent-vs-druck-americas-debt-crisis-cancer-vaccine-42597345]] creates this concept from the discussion of [[StanleyDruckenmiller]]'s reported AI-assisted op-ed. The hosts do not agree that AI use itself invalidates the argument. [[ChamathPalihapitiya|Chamath Palihapitiya]] and [[DavidSacks|David Sacks]] emphasize substance, while [[JasonCalacanis|Jason Calacanis]] argues that readers of a famous investor's signed op-ed expect that person's own voice unless assistance is disclosed.

The source's useful distinction is between tool use and byline expectation. [[DavidFriedberg|David Friedberg]] compares AI to [[AdobePhotoshop|Photoshop]], synthesizers, Excel, and calculators: tools can expand capability without making the output fake. Jason's objection is narrower: when the attraction is the named author's judgment and voice, undisclosed AI assistance can feel misleading even if the facts and thesis are acceptable.

## Key Claims
- The disclosure problem is not simply whether AI touched the text; it depends on what readers believe the byline promises.
- Substantive argument quality and authorship authenticity are related but separable questions.
- AI assistance can be ordinary tooling for drafting, editing, analysis, or translation without automatically undermining the author's responsibility.
- Famous-person op-eds carry a higher voice-and-judgment expectation than low-stakes internal writing.
- Disclosure norms may need to distinguish light editing, heavy drafting, ghostwriting-like assistance, and AI-generated argument structure.

## Evidence
- Byline expectation claim: [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-nvidias-historic-quarter-saas-comeback-bessent-vs-druck-americas-debt-crisis-cancer-vaccine-42597345]] records Jason's view that readers expected Druckenmiller's own voice and that undisclosed AI assistance felt like lip-syncing.
- Substance-first claim: [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-nvidias-historic-quarter-saas-comeback-bessent-vs-druck-americas-debt-crisis-cancer-vaccine-42597345]] records Chamath and Sacks treating the op-ed's argument as more important than the drafting tool.
- Tool analogy claim: [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-nvidias-historic-quarter-saas-comeback-bessent-vs-druck-americas-debt-crisis-cancer-vaccine-42597345]] records Friedberg's comparisons to Photoshop, synthesizers, Excel, and calculators as examples of tools that augment production.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode does not independently establish how much AI assistance was used in the op-ed or whether the publication had a disclosure policy. It also does not settle a universal rule for disclosure. The concept should stay norm-focused: the key issue is reader expectation under a signed public byline, not a blanket claim that AI-assisted writing is deceptive.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to preserve the episode's distinction between AI as useful writing tool and AI as an undisclosed substitute for expected authorial voice.
