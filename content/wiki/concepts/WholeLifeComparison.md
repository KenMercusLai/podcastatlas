# Whole-Life Comparison

## Definition

Whole-life comparison is an anti-envy rule that tests a desired advantage only as part of the complete life, costs, relationships, history, and vulnerabilities that accompany it.

## Current Synthesis

[[LeeCCamp]] first proposes that a happy life may be one a person can accept in its entirety, including difficulty. [[MalcolmGladwell]] then adds a friend's comparison rule: a person cannot coherently desire one isolated advantage from somebody else's life while refusing the rest of that life.

Celebrity supplies the episode's example. Attractiveness, wealth, fame, or admiration may look desirable in isolation, but the comparison changes when it includes lost privacy, scrutiny, conflict, and other unseen burdens. The rule does not claim that everyone should prefer their present circumstances or that injustice deserves acceptance. It corrects selective comparison by restoring hidden costs and incomplete information.

## Key Claims

- Envy often compares one's whole life with a selectively visible advantage from another person's life.
- A fairer comparison restores the costs, relationships, constraints, and history attached to the desired trait.
- Hidden burdens make confident judgments about another person's total wellbeing unreliable.
- Whole-life acceptance can reduce envy without requiring denial of grief, injustice, or changeable harm.
- The rule is a cognitive correction, not evidence that every current life is preferable to every alternative.

## Evidence

- Whole-life framing: [[malcolm-on-no-small-endeavor-malcolm-on-no-small-endeavor]] has Camp ask whether happiness includes accepting one's life as a whole.
- Anti-envy rule: [[malcolm-on-no-small-endeavor-malcolm-on-no-small-endeavor]] reports Gladwell's friend's rule against choosing only one advantage from another person's life.
- Celebrity example: [[malcolm-on-no-small-endeavor-malcolm-on-no-small-endeavor]] contrasts visible fame and attractiveness with privacy loss, scrutiny, and personal conflict.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The thought experiment does not reveal the actual quality of another person's life, and people may reasonably prefer many feasible alternative lives. It should not be used to discourage escape from abuse, poverty, discrimination, illness, or preventable suffering. Structural injustice remains open to comparison and change even when a person's complete life contains goods worth preserving.

## What Changed

- Created the concept as a bounded correction to selective envy and incomplete social comparison.
