# Static Stretching Dose Protocol

## Definition
Static stretching dose protocol is the source-scoped practice frame that lasting range-of-motion gains are best pursued through repeated low-intensity static holds, enough weekly volume per muscle group, warmup, and attention to the body's current end range.

## Current Synthesis
[[essentials-improve-flexibility-with-research-supported-stretching-protocols-scim2453607831]] presents static stretching as the clearest practical starting point for durable flexibility changes. The episode distinguishes static holds from dynamic, ballistic, and PNF methods, then emphasizes weekly dose: at least five total minutes per week per muscle group, often accumulated through 30-second holds across five or more days.

The protocol is deliberately modest. Stretching should begin warm, use the day's actual range of motion, and often stay around 30-40% of the point of pain rather than treating stronger pain as better. This makes the concept a dose-and-intensity companion to [[StretchingMobilitySafety]], not a universal sport, rehabilitation, or pain-treatment prescription.

The Cavaliere interview sharpens the immediate-performance distinction: it favors dynamic movement before training and generally places passive holds away from the session because a temporary change in force production or length-tension behavior may be undesirable. The later Essentials source is more conditional, allowing static work before training when restricted range blocks safe form. The combined judgment is therefore goal- and deficit-dependent, not a categorical ban.

## Key Claims
- Static stretching is favored for long-term range-of-motion improvement in the evidence discussed by the source.
- Dynamic, ballistic, static, and PNF stretching are distinct categories with different momentum, assistance, and end-range profiles.
- A practical benchmark is at least five total minutes per week of stretching for each targeted muscle group.
- Thirty-second holds are treated as a useful unit, with three 30-second sets repeated about five days per week as one sample structure.
- Stretching should usually be low intensity, with the cited low-intensity condition around 30-40% of the point of pain.
- A general warmup or prior exercise bout should raise body temperature before static stretching.
- Pre-training stretching should follow the immediate goal: dynamic movement usually fits readiness and skill preparation, while restricted range that blocks safe form may justify targeted static holds despite possible short-term performance tradeoffs.

## Evidence
- Stretching-type claim: [[essentials-improve-flexibility-with-research-supported-stretching-protocols-scim2453607831]] distinguishes dynamic, ballistic, static, and PNF stretching and says static protocols produced significant long-term range-of-motion gains in the review discussed.
- Weekly-dose claim: [[essentials-improve-flexibility-with-research-supported-stretching-protocols-scim2453607831]] highlights at least five minutes per week per muscle group and gives a hamstring example using repeated 30-second static holds.
- Intensity claim: [[essentials-improve-flexibility-with-research-supported-stretching-protocols-scim2453607831]] cites a six-week dancer study where low-intensity micro-stretching outperformed a higher-intensity comparison on lower-limb range of motion.
- Warmup claim: [[essentials-improve-flexibility-with-research-supported-stretching-protocols-scim2453607831]] advises stretching after another warm activity or after five to ten minutes of easy movement if not already warm.
- Goal-dependent timing claim: [[essentials-improve-flexibility-with-research-supported-stretching-protocols-scim2453607831]] says static stretching before weight training can help when tightness blocks form, while dynamic or ballistic movement can serve warmup purposes.
- Performance-timing claim: [[optimize-your-exercise-program-with-science-based-tools-jeff-cavaliere-scim5122820076]] generally places passive stretching away from workouts and uses leg swings, lunges, kicks, and rotation as dynamic preparation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources differ in emphasis rather than establishing a settled contradiction: Cavaliere generally separates passive holds from performance, while the Essentials episode allows targeted pre-training static work when mobility limits form. Neither source note quantifies the size or duration of any acute performance effect across activities. The dancer study is one specific source case, not proof that one dose fits every sport, injury, age, or mobility restriction. Pain, neurological symptoms, acute injury, swelling, or persistent functional limits should not be managed by self-directed stretching alone.

## What Changed
- Added dynamic warmup versus passive-stretch timing as an immediate-performance decision.
- Reconciled the sources by making pre-training static work goal- and restriction-dependent rather than universally required or forbidden.
