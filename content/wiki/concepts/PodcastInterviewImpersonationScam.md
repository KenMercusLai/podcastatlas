# Podcast Interview Impersonation Scam

## Definition
A podcast interview impersonation scam is a social-engineering attack in which criminals pose as recognizable journalists or shows, invite a target to appear as a guest, and use the apparent production workflow to obtain software installation, remote access, payment, credentials, or digital assets.

## Current Synthesis
The [[PlanetMoney]] warning shows how an attractive media opportunity can lower skepticism. Fake staff identities, plausible profiles, lookalike URLs, social accounts, supposed recording software, and video-call distraction combine institutional trust with technical access. The immediate case targets people likely to hold cryptocurrency, but the durable defense is broader: verify the sender through an independently located institutional channel and reject unexpected remote-access, payment, or installation requests.

## Key Claims
- The scam borrows credibility from a real show, journalist, and guest-booking process.
- Lookalike domains, social profiles, and video interaction can create mutually reinforcing false proof.
- Supposed recording software or remote-access requests turn the interview premise into device and asset compromise.
- Opportunity and flattery can function like financial FOMO by making verification feel costly or rude.
- Independent-domain and cross-channel checks should happen before installing software, granting access, or moving money.

## Evidence
- **Credibility construction** - [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b5a6e3798d93ba5a123766ba4019c696-28183476-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b5a6e3798d93ba5a123766ba4019c696-28183476]] reports impostors using convincing profiles, fake URLs, and social accounts while claiming to represent Planet Money or The Indicator.
- **Technical conversion** - [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b5a6e3798d93ba5a123766ba4019c696-28183476-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-b5a6e3798d93ba5a123766ba4019c696-28183476]] says targets may be asked to install supposed recording software and then distracted during a call while cryptocurrency is stolen. It states that authentic NPR addresses end in `npr.org` and that NPR journalists do not request remote computer access or interview payments.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a warning from the impersonated media organization, not a complete incident-response report. It does not establish one universal malware path or show that every unsolicited interview request is fraudulent. Domain appearance alone is also insufficient if an account has been compromised, so verification should use an independently sourced contact route.

## What Changed
- Added podcast guest recruitment as a distinct institutional-impersonation pathway.
- Connected attractive opportunity, fake production software, and cryptocurrency theft in one attack chain.
