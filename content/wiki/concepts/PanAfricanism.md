## Definition

Pan-Africanism is a transnational political and intellectual tradition that treats African and African-diasporic struggles as connected rather than confined to individual colonial territories or nation states.

## Current Synthesis

[[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] presents Pan-Africanism as a network built across Africa, the Caribbean, Europe, and the United States. Fanon's 1958 Accra appeal joins Algeria to Rhodesia and Angola, while [[KwameNkrumah]] provides the Ghanaian political center around which continental solidarity could be staged.

Its history in metropolitan congresses and through figures such as Henry Sylvester Williams, C. L. R. James, Marcus Garvey, and [[FrantzFanon]] makes the tradition geographically hybrid. That hybridity does not make it inauthentically African; it shows how diaspora, imperial mobility, education, race, religion, Marxism, and universal rights furnished shared languages for opposing colonial rule.

## Key Claims

- Pan-Africanism links geographically separate liberation struggles into a shared political field.
- Caribbean and diasporic thinkers were central to its formation and circulation.
- Early congresses in London, Paris, New York, and Manchester show the importance of imperial and metropolitan networks.
- Ghana under Nkrumah became an African center for a tradition partly organized abroad.
- The tradition used Western-derived vocabularies without being reducible to Western political control.

## Evidence

- Continental solidarity: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] uses Fanon's Accra speech to connect Algeria, Rhodesia, and Angola.
- Diasporic formation: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] names West Indian and Caribbean thinkers as major contributors to Pan-African thought.
- Metropolitan networks: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] notes that several major congresses occurred outside Africa before the Accra gathering.
- Political center: [[100-decolonising-africa-glt2427735251]] connects Nkrumah's Ghanaian leadership to continental organization and immediate-independence politics.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode sketches an intellectual genealogy rather than a comprehensive history of Pan-African organizations, ideological divisions, popular participation, gender, labor, or state practice. Calling the tradition “Western” can illuminate where networks and vocabularies formed, but it can also obscure African agendas and agency if treated as a claim of ownership or derivation.

## What Changed

- Created the concept as a diasporic and continental network rather than a purely territorial African ideology.
- Qualified the episode's “Western roots” claim by separating geographic and intellectual hybridity from political authorship.
