# Quantum Cryptographic Migration

## Definition
Quantum cryptographic migration is the coordinated replacement of encryption, signature, wallet, node, and internet-security systems that could become vulnerable if practical quantum computers can break widely used public-key cryptography.

## Current Synthesis
The episode frames quantum risk as both a [[Bitcoin]] governance problem and a broader internet infrastructure problem. [[ChamathPalihapitiya|Chamath Palihapitiya]] warns that quantum timelines may be shorter than many crypto holders assume, while [[DavidFriedberg|David Friedberg]] explains that Shor-style attacks threaten integer-factorization-based schemes and that post-quantum standards already exist. The main synthesis is organizational: the hard part may be migrating wallets, transaction flows, nodes, certificates, hardware, software, and user behavior before an adversary has usable capability.

## Key Claims
- Quantum risk is not only a chip problem; algorithmic improvements can reduce the resources needed for attacks.
- Bitcoin and crypto systems may be obvious targets because financial payoff is direct and visible.
- Decentralized governance makes urgent cryptographic migration harder than in centrally managed systems.
- Post-quantum standards reduce technical uncertainty but do not solve adoption, compatibility, and operational rollout.
- The same repricing logic applies beyond crypto: breakthrough capability can invalidate assumptions about asset durability.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The timeline is uncertain and the episode cites research direction rather than a demonstrated cryptographically relevant quantum computer. Not all deployed cryptography is equally exposed, and migration urgency depends on key type, exposure model, address reuse, adversary incentives, standards readiness, and whether systems face "harvest now, decrypt later" risk.
