## Definition

Conquest captivity narrative uncertainty is the difficulty of deciding who controlled whom when hospitality, surveillance, armed dependence, restricted movement, translation, and later self-justifying testimony overlap during an imperial encounter.

## Current Synthesis

The Tenochtitlan encounter cannot be reduced to a clean switch from Mexica sovereignty to Spanish control. [[Moctezuma]] housed, fed, toured, and observed the Spaniards while retaining enough apparent freedom to hunt and appear in public. Those same facts can describe hospitality, strategic study, incorporation of useful outsiders, containment, or captivity. They sit uneasily beside the later Spanish story that [[HernanCortes|Cortes]] quickly took Moctezuma prisoner and received a voluntary transfer of empire.

The episode therefore treats chronology as part of the political argument. Matthew Restall's interpretation places the Spaniards initially inside Moctezuma's system of control, perhaps as curiosities or armed dependents, while a later-hostage chronology places Cortes's seizure of Moctezuma only when [[PanfiloDeNarvaez|Narvaez]] forced him to leave the capital. Neither revision turns ambiguity into certainty; each tests whether Spanish accounts projected mastery backward to legitimate conquest.

## Key Claims

- Hospitality and captivity are not clean opposites when one side controls lodging, movement, information, provisioning, and armed access.
- Continued hunting, processions, tours, games, and courtly exchange weaken a simple account of early total imprisonment without proving that Moctezuma was fully free.
- The Spaniards' dependence on [[Malinche]] means every reported negotiation was mediated across language and political categories.
- A later hostage date better explains why Cortes would seize Moctezuma when he divided his force, but it remains a reconstruction rather than a settled event.
- Claims of voluntary surrender served Spanish legal and narrative interests and therefore require scrutiny alongside the conduct described in the same sources.

## Evidence

- Ambiguous accommodation: [[387-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-prisoners-of-montezuma-part-4-glt8017930427]] places hospitality, tours, hunting, games, shipbuilding, surveillance, and Spanish unease inside the same relationship.
- Conflicting control signals: [[387-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-prisoners-of-montezuma-part-4-glt8017930427]] contrasts the early-hostage story with Moctezuma's continued public and recreational activity.
- Alternative interpretation: [[387-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-prisoners-of-montezuma-part-4-glt8017930427]] presents Restall's proposal that the Spaniards were contained curiosities or useful armed dependents within Moctezuma's order.
- Chronology and motive: [[387-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-prisoners-of-montezuma-part-4-glt8017930427]] links the plausible later seizure to Cortes's departure to confront Narvaez.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

- The source is a retrospective popular-history discussion relying heavily on Spanish testimony even as it criticizes that testimony.
- Freedom of movement can coexist with coercive custody, and formal courtesy does not establish equal power or voluntary consent.
- The zoo-proximity argument does not prove that Moctezuma classified the Spaniards as specimens or curiosities.
- Reported threats, Malinche's advice, dialogue, and the exact moment of hostage-taking cannot be independently established from this episode.
- Questioning a voluntary surrender narrative does not by itself prove the inverse claim that the Spaniards were prisoners in every relevant sense.

## What Changed

- Established a conquest-specific framework for separating hospitality, containment, dependency, and captivity.
- Preserved the later-hostage chronology as a plausible revision rather than a settled replacement narrative.
