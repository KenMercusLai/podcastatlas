## Definition

Qilin Pavilion merit honors / 麒麟阁功臣荣典 are a ruler-sponsored political-memory practice in which meritorious ministers are portrayed, titled, and named in a palace setting so that frontier, administrative, and succession achievements become a visible honor order.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-463-caicaikan-zai-hanxuandi-yanzhong-shui-shi-zuidade-gongchen-lqblzdg7sdkzln4vxhtnnye92292|Hanji 463]] grounds the concept in [[EmperorXuanOfHan|汉宣帝]]'s Ganlu 3 order to paint eleven meritorious ministers in the Qilin Pavilion. The episode emphasizes that the list follows Xuan's successes in the Western Regions, Western Qiang, and Xiongnu fields. The portraits do not merely praise individuals; they convert the reign's restored order into court memory.

The practice is narrower than general reward. It is retrospective, selective, visual, and tied to public historical ranking. The episode compares it with Tang Taizong's Lingyan Pavilion honors, but the wiki keeps this page centered on the Western Han case: Xuan's honor roll marks a late-reign statement that "middle restoration" depended on a ministerial coalition as well as imperial judgment.

## Key Claims

- The honor roll is political memory, not only personal reward.
- Portrait, office title, noble title, and name turn service into a durable palace record.
- The list links frontier achievement, administrative repair, and succession service into one restored-reign narrative.
- Huo Guang's special labeling by office and surname rather than direct personal name shows that honor can also preserve hierarchy and sensitivity.
- The Qilin Pavilion case makes ministerial merit part of imperial self-definition: Xuan's restoration is narrated through the people who made it workable.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept is based on one compact episode. It does not settle the original physical arrangement of the Qilin Pavilion images, exact court ceremony, how later historical memory transmitted the list, or each minister's individual claim to merit. The Tang Lingyan Pavilion comparison is useful as a listening bridge, not evidence that the two practices were institutionally identical.
