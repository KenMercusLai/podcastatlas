# Interest Reframing Persuasion / 利益重构式说服

## Definition
Interest reframing is an attributed pattern in historical diplomatic storytelling: rather than demand sympathy, a persuader redescribes the desired choice as safer or more profitable for the person holding power, often supplying an alternative that protects the listener's standing.

## Current Synthesis
The cases are not one replicable formula. Huang Xie addresses Qin's state strategy; Su Dai addresses a minister's rank; Dong Zhao offers complementary grain and troops; envoys to Yan and Qi alter perceptions of a third party. The strength of the argument depends on a credible substitute and coordinated conduct after assent. A vivid precedent or private fear can persuade without being historically sound or beneficial to the target.

## Key Claims
- Strategic-risk appeals translate a vulnerable state's survival into the stronger state's route security and future advantage.
- Ministerial rank, family grievance and personal safety can redirect decisions that would otherwise look obvious at state level.
- Face-preserving exchanges and third-party incentives can make cooperation preferable to humiliation or hostage-taking.
- A proposed alternative needs credible partners and resources; rhetoric does not itself supply them.
- A diplomatic promise fails when the principal's army or envoy makes the offered bargain incredible.
- Coercive commitment or a tragic outcome should not be mistaken for mutually beneficial persuasion.

## Evidence
- [[Chunshenjun|黄歇]] writes [[QinZhaoxiangwang|秦昭襄王]] as [[BaiQi|白起]] prepares to attack [[ChuState|楚国]]: [[QinState|秦国]] could expose its rear; hostile [[HanState|韩国]] and [[WeiState|魏国]] are unsafe corridors; direct war could strengthen [[QiState|齐国]] and [[ZhaoState|赵国]]. Alliance with Chu, pressure near Xinzheng and a better eastern sequence offer a replacement for invasion. The speaker invokes [[Fuchai|夫差]], [[Goujian|勾践]] and [[ZhiYao|智瑶]], using elite allusion and [[PoetryAsElitePoliticalLanguage]] as political language rather than proof of exact strategic equivalence. - After [[ChangpingBattle|长平]], [[SuDai|苏代]] warns [[FanJu|范雎]] that Bai Qi's destruction of Zhao and capture of [[Handan|邯郸]] could elevate a rival above him; conquered land might not yield subjects if people flee. Qin's reported pause and six-city settlement followed military exhaustion as well as the pitch. [[YuQing|虞卿]] then proposes sending six cities to Qi instead of Qin to signal [[HezongAlliance|合纵]], against [[LouHuan|楼缓]]'s accommodation advice; the show doubts [[QiWangJian|齐王建]]'s willingness to back that bargain. - [[LuZhonglian|鲁仲连]] argues to [[XinyuanYan|新垣衍]] that elevating Qin to emperor exposes the Wei ruler, ministers and envoy himself, joining civilizational shame to self-preservation. The show's challenge to his Shang–Zhou analogy and its contrast with [[FanJu]]'s mistreatment in Wei are reminders that [[HistoricalPrecedentWeaponization]] can succeed despite weak evidence. In [[LiaochengWarringStates|辽城]], his arrow letter to the commander trapped by [[YanWangXi|燕王喜]] and [[TianDan|田单]] changes the perceived choices; the commander kills himself and the siege ends, not a safe surrender. - When [[WuChen|武臣]] is detained by [[YanState|燕国]], a low-status [[ZhaoServantEnvoy|赵厮养卒]] persuades [[HanGuang|韩广]] that killing him would furnish [[ZhangEr|张耳]] and [[ChenYu|陈馀]] a pretext to attack; prior Zhao envoys had failed. [[DongZhaoLateHan|董昭]] instead writes in [[CaoCao|曹操]]'s name to [[YangFengLateHan|杨奉]], praising his rescue of [[EmperorXianOfHan|汉献帝]] and proposing his troops plus Cao's grain as cooperation rather than capitulation. [[zizhi-tongjian-qinji-128-8-yi-ming-zhaoguo-chuishibing-nixi-chengwei-waijiao-dachen-lhfwk-hjge5iuqet6astmbclhyri]] [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1003-sanguo-zui-bei-digu-de-moushi-dongzhao-llc7q-loennxw26v3u5cbhitikw1]]
- [[SunJianLateHan|孙坚]] reportedly restores [[YuanShu|袁术]]'s grain support by connecting the fight against [[DongZhuo|董卓]] to Han duty and Yuan-family revenge, invoking [[WuQi|吴起]] and [[LeYi|乐毅]] as warnings about mistreated commanders. [[LyuBu|吕布]] draws [[HanXianLateHan|韩暹]] and Yang Feng away from Yuan Shu by combining imperial legitimacy and promised supplies. These late-Han appeals join moral name and material advantage without independently proving their speakers' sincerity. Ying Bu subsequently loses to Chu and flees with Sui He. [[LiShiqi|郦食其]] similarly persuades [[TianGuangQiKing|田广]] at [[Linzi|临淄]] to align Qi with [[LiuBang|刘邦]] by contrasting his rewards with Xiang Yu's; but [[HanXin|韩信]], prompted by [[KuaiChe|蒯彻]]'s merit calculation, attacks Qi without a synchronized stop order. Qi executes the envoy, and the [[Aocang|敖仓]]–[[Chenggao|成皋]] front remains a military, not merely rhetorical, contest. ## Counterevidence & Qualifications
All twelve notes come from the same [[RuiqiJiangtouZizhiTongjian|《资治通鉴》讲述系列]], not independent source criticism. The host disputes Lu Zhonglian's precedent and Li Shiqi's claim of secure control of Aocang and Chenggao; Yu Qing's hoped-for Qi backing is doubtful. Sui He's tactic included lethal coercion. The Liaocheng commander did not survive the letter. The Hanji 955 episode's reconstruction should not be upgraded into an independently checked “official history” claim. Persuasion may change a choice while failing to improve the listener's welfare.

## What Changed
- Replaced episode-arrival paragraphs with six distinct decision mechanisms and their concrete consequences.
- Put the doubtful precedent, unreliable coalition and command failure beside the claimed successes.
