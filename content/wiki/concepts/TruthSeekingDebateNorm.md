## Definition

求明理的辩论规范 / truth-seeking debate norm is the standard that debate should clarify categories, evidence, reasons, and shared understanding rather than merely generate verbal victory, ritualized deference, or punishable embarrassment.

## Current Synthesis

Zhouji 64 defines the philosophical version through [[KongChuan|Kong Chuan]] and [[ZouYan|Zou Yan]] against [[GongsunLong|Gongsun Long]]. Kong Chuan gives the fact-facing test: an argument that is difficult and false is worse than one that is easy and true. Zou Yan gives the public-purpose test: debate should separate categories, keep concepts from invading each other, make principles clear, and leave both winner and loser oriented toward truth.

Hanji 772 extends the norm into an Eastern Han court-institutional setting. In the classics debate, [[LuPiEasternHan|Lu Pi]] argues that questions and answers should explain their reasons rather than merely perform mutual concession, and that errors in such debate should not be converted into punishments. The result broadens the concept from philosophical critique of wordplay to a governance norm for structured disagreement inside court learning.

## Key Claims

- A strong argument is not only hard to answer; it should be true, clarifying, and worth the attention it demands.
- Debate should help the audience and participants distinguish categories rather than scramble them.
- Winning by exhausting or silencing others is treated as a failure of purpose, not as intellectual success.
- The concept turns [[MingjiaDisputation|名辩]] from a talent display into an object of ethical and epistemic evaluation.
- In an institutional setting, reason-giving matters more than polite non-disagreement because deference without explanation does not clarify doctrine.
- Non-punitive debate norms protect correction: error in reasoned discussion should be answerable by argument before it becomes an offense.

## Evidence

- Easy-truth test: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-64-gudai-gangjing-gongsunlong-yangchengji-lkrchesllfi9ipgfapft7av2mnh5|Zhouji 64]] says Kong Chuan rejects "three ears" cleverness by contrasting difficult falsehood with easy truth.
- Category clarification: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-64-gudai-gangjing-gongsunlong-yangchengji-lkrchesllfi9ipgfapft7av2mnh5|Zhouji 64]] says Zou Yan refuses Gongsun Long's "white horse is not horse" terms and defines debate as separating categories and clarifying principles.
- Public purpose: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-64-gudai-gangjing-gongsunlong-yangchengji-lkrchesllfi9ipgfapft7av2mnh5|Zhouji 64]] treats verbal agility that forces silence without understanding as a misuse of argument.
- Court classics extension: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-772-ta-weihe-ningyuan-chengwei-taofan-ye-buyuan-jicheng-jiaye-lrou5naye7opb5favwuz6bqbggae|Hanji 772]] has Lu Pi argue that classics debate should include reasons, not only reciprocal yielding.
- Non-punitive correction: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-772-ta-weihe-ningyuan-chengwei-taofan-ye-buyuan-jicheng-jiaye-lrou5naye7opb5favwuz6bqbggae|Hanji 772]] uses the Lu Pi debate to separate reasoned disagreement from punishable wrongdoing.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The norm does not mean endless antagonism, contempt for ritual decorum, or indifference to office hierarchy. Zhouji 64 is a Warring States philosophical anecdote about disputation, while Hanji 772 is an Eastern Han court/classics setting; the shared synthesis is reason-giving for clarification, not a claim that both episodes operate under the same institutions. Hanji 772 also does not prove a general free-speech regime, only that this episode values reasoned, non-punitive correction inside a bounded learned debate.
