# Native-Alien Species Boundary / 原生外來邊界

## Definition
Native-alien species boundary is the ecological and historical line used to decide whether a population belongs to a place by evolution or natural dispersal, or arrived outside its natural range through human action.

## Current Synthesis
The Duanwen episode uses Taiwan mynas to show that this boundary is necessary but not self-executing. Classification requires a time baseline, a spatial unit, a route of movement, and sometimes a judgment about how far historical human activity matters.

The boundary becomes politically and ethically important because it can be mistaken for a moral division. The source distinguishes classification from harm: alien status describes origin, while invasive status requires evidence of negative ecological, economic, health, or safety effects.

## Key Claims
- Native and alien status are relative to chosen time and space boundaries.
- Human-mediated movement is central to alien-species classification, but historical records and migration baselines can be ambiguous.
- Invasive status requires demonstrated negative effects, not alien origin alone.
- Classification should support concrete risk assessment rather than enemy-making.

- Boundary evidence: [[wailai-bage-yeying-zhibo-yu-taiwan-wailai-wuzhong-nanti-db02e7aa04c129b49b1f36f750155b42]] notes that baselines can vary from pre-human modification to pre-colonial or longer ecological timescales.
- Taiwan evidence: [[wailai-bage-yeying-zhibo-yu-taiwan-wailai-wuzhong-nanti-db02e7aa04c129b49b1f36f750155b42]] contrasts clearly introduced common and white-vented mynas with the more historically debated crested myna.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Some cases are much clearer than others. The episode treats common myna and white-vented myna in Taiwan as clear alien species, while using crested myna and regional white-headed bulbul movement to show boundary complexity.
