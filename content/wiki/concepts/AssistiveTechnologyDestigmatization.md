## Definition
Assistive technology destigmatization is the reduction of social marking, embarrassment, or exclusion around an aid through usable design, user choice, public familiarity, and adoption beyond the population for whom the device is medically necessary.

## Current Synthesis
The source argues that assistive speech devices can follow a path similar to glasses: a device becomes less defining when it is attractive, ordinary, and sometimes chosen by people without the underlying impairment. Mainstream adoption can widen markets and normalize visible augmentation, but destigmatization is not achieved merely by selling the same technology to a broader public. The device must still meet disability-specific needs, remain affordable, and avoid making early users subsidize a lifestyle repositioning that weakens access.

The stronger principle is choice. A user should be seen as a person selecting technology, not reduced to disease or necessity. That requires reliable performance in ordinary social settings, control over appearance and voice, reimbursement or affordable tiers, and public norms that accept augmentation without demanding concealment.

## Key Claims
- Social stigma can persist even when an assistive device technically works.
- Ordinary wearable form and broader public familiarity can reduce visible disability marking.
- Mainstream adoption should complement rather than dilute disability-specific design.
- Choice over whether and how to use augmentation supports dignity and identity.
- Pricing, reimbursement, appearance, latency, and conversational usability all affect normalization.
- Destigmatization does not require pretending the underlying impairment or access need does not exist.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Mainstream adoption does not automatically reduce ableism and can redirect design, marketing, data, or supply toward more profitable non-disabled users. The glasses analogy is suggestive rather than demonstrated evidence for speech devices. Some users may prefer a visibly assistive identity, non-use, surgery, or another communication mode, and normalization should not become pressure to adopt augmentation.

## What Changed
- Established broader elective adoption as one possible destigmatization path while preserving access and user-choice qualifications.
