# Oceanic Voyage Attrition

Oceanic voyage attrition is the source's frame for the cumulative loss of ships, crews, health, command capacity, and navigational options during early modern long-distance voyages. In [[14-wushi-weilian-dahanghai-shidai-de-riben-he-xifang-555771285]], [[WilliamAdams|William Adams / 三浦按针]] begins in a five-ship expedition, but the route is degraded by fever, hunger, failed raids, storms, separation, capture, attacks on landing parties, scurvy, and desertion before one damaged ship reaches [[Japan]].

The concept extends [[ScurvyNutritionInsight]] from a disease-specific page into the wider system of maritime survival. Scurvy matters, but it is only one layer: political hostility, route choice, cargo mismatch, local reputation left by earlier Europeans, and weather all compound into mortality.

[[693-elizabeth-i-vs-the-catholics-englands-greatest-hero-part-3-glt5069845329]] adds the [[GoldenHind]] as a success case that still shows the same risk field. [[FrancisDrake]] loses contact with companion ships after storms beyond the Strait of Magellan, continues alone in the Pacific, loses [[DiegoDrakeAlly|Diego]] after wounds off Chile, sails long stretches without sighting land, grounds on a reef, and still returns without scurvy or dehydration deaths in the final long passage.

## Key Claims
- Long-distance maritime risk is cumulative rather than a single disaster event.
- Strategic route choice can be driven by commercial constraints, such as avoiding Iberian-controlled routes or seeking markets for unsuitable cargo.
- Health failure reduces seamanship and decision quality, which then increases further voyage risk.
- [[ConfessionalTradeConflict]] and imperial reputation can shape how stranded sailors are treated when they seek food or safety.
- A successful circumnavigation can still be evidence of attrition pressure: success depends on ship survival, command continuity, route choice, water, food, and luck as much as boldness.
