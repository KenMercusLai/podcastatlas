# Self-Explanation Knowledge Calibration

## Definition
Self-explanation knowledge calibration is the practice of explaining a mechanism, belief, or policy in enough detail to reveal the boundary between genuine understanding and familiarity-based confidence.

## Current Synthesis
[[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-d08500083dda91b814b053f30bd7b557-87666361-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-d08500083dda91b814b053f30bd7b557-87666361|The Story of Stories]] presents the self-explanation effect as learning produced without new external information: attempting to make steps explicit can expose gaps, connect pieces, and generate insight. The calibration branch is especially important for familiar objects and political policies, where recognition can masquerade as mechanistic understanding.

This is a diagnostic and learning practice, not a guarantee of truth. A person can invent a fluent post-hoc story, retain false premises, or lack the external evidence needed to choose among explanations. Calibration improves when detailed explanation is paired with source checking and [[ConsiderTheOppositeBiasCorrection|a deliberately generated rival account]].

## Key Claims
- Attempted explanation can reorganize existing information even when no new facts are supplied.
- Familiarity and successful use can create an [[IllusionOfExplanatoryDepth|illusion of explanatory depth]].
- Mechanistic detail exposes missing links more effectively than a global confidence rating.
- Explaining a policy can reduce certainty when a person cannot specify how it works.
- Self-explanation needs verification and rival hypotheses because fluency can generate plausible error.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The supplied note omits study designs, effect sizes, replication evidence, and backfire conditions. Detailed articulation can produce persuasive confabulation rather than knowledge, especially without feedback, causal access, or reliable sources. The method should calibrate confidence, not reward verbosity.

## What Changed
- Established self-explanation as both a learning mechanism and a test of overclaimed understanding.
