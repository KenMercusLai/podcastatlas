# British Two-Party Realignment of 1922

## Definition

The British two-party realignment of 1922 is the shift from a Conservative-Liberal coalition and divided Liberal politics toward a national contest led by Conservatives and Labour.

## Current Synthesis

The episode locates the turning point at the Carlton Club meeting of 19 October 1922, where Conservatives rejected continued coalition under Lloyd George despite Austin Chamberlain's support. Bonar Law then led the party into the November election; Conservatives won a large majority, the two Liberal factions remained divided, and Labour became the second-largest party. The result is treated as an institutional birth of modern British party competition rather than merely a change of prime minister.

## Key Claims

- A governing coalition can collapse when a major party decides organizational independence is worth the electoral risk.
- Internal party meetings can become constitutional turning points without changing formal constitutional rules.
- Vote and seat competition are shaped by opposition fragmentation as well as governing-party strength.
- Labour's rise depended partly on Liberal division, but represented a durable shift in the social and ideological structure of opposition.
- Political modernity in 1922 included institutional realignment alongside media and geopolitical change.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source uses “death of Liberal England” as a broad interpretive frame, not a claim that Liberal voters, institutions, or ideas disappeared in 1922. It does not provide a full electoral analysis of class, constituency geography, franchise change, organization, or later minority governments.
