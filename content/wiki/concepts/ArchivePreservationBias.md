# Archive Preservation Bias / 档案保存偏差

Archive preservation bias is the source's reminder that historical visibility depends on which institutions saved which records. In [[168-yin-yu-xin-yibu-riben-laogong-yimin-de-kuayang-huikuan-shi-lmgfmmgkawoa-6xyucnm1ljebzwu]], Japanese foreign-ministry files and [[YokohamaSpecieBank|Yokohama Specie Bank]] records make [[JapaneseLaborMigrationRemittances]] unusually legible, while Chinese [[QiaopiRemittanceNetworks]] are more scattered across private, local, museum, and family holdings.

The concept is adjacent to [[ArchiveAccessTradeoff]], but the emphasis differs. [[ArchiveAccessTradeoff]] asks who may access preserved materials; archive preservation bias asks why those materials, and not others, became preserved, cataloged, searchable, or publicly narratable in the first place.

## Key Claims
- State and bank archives can make ordinary people visible, but usually because those people passed through official processes.
- Decentralized private systems may leave fewer centralized records even when they were socially and economically large.
- Museums and state archives preserve within narrative and institutional priorities, so researchers must read both content and selection.
- Privacy limits can protect individuals while also keeping rich personal data from broader public digitization.
