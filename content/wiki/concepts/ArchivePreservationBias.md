# Archive Preservation Bias / 档案保存偏差

## Definition

Archive preservation bias is the dependence of historical visibility on which institutions, victors, families, and accidents preserved, destroyed, centralized, cataloged, translated, or dispersed records.

## Current Synthesis

The remittance source shows how preservation selects visibility even without wholesale destruction. Japanese foreign-ministry and [[YokohamaSpecieBank|Yokohama Specie Bank]] files make [[JapaneseLaborMigrationRemittances]] unusually legible because migrant families passed through state and bank procedures. Chinese [[QiaopiRemittanceNetworks]] were socially extensive but left records scattered across private, local, museum, and family holdings. Centralization therefore changes what can be counted and narrated without proving that the centralized system was larger or more important.

The Carthage source shows the stronger case of archival defeat. Most Carthaginian writing was destroyed or dispersed, while Greek and Roman enemies supplied the surviving literary frame. Archaeology, institutional comparison, and the preservation of Mago's agricultural treatise can correct or complicate that frame, but they cannot restore a complete Carthaginian voice. Claims about cruelty, luxury, sacrifice, constitutional office, and motive therefore require confidence calibrated to both content and survival history.

The concept is adjacent to [[ArchiveAccessTradeoff]], but the emphasis differs. Access asks who may use surviving material; preservation bias asks why this material, and not something else, survived to become searchable or publicly narratable.

## Key Claims

- State and bank archives can make ordinary people visible, but usually because those people passed through official processes.
- Decentralized private systems may leave fewer centralized records even when they were socially and economically large.
- Museums and state archives preserve within narrative and institutional priorities, so researchers must read both content and selection.
- Conquest and destruction can leave a society known mainly through rivals whose categories and moral interests shape the record.
- Surviving technical, archaeological, or external evidence can qualify a hostile archive without fully recovering lost self-representation.
- Privacy limits can protect individuals while also keeping rich personal data from broader public digitization.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Preserved official records are not automatically false, and hostile sources may contain accurate information. Dispersed records are not necessarily absent, while archaeology and comparison can recover structures that literary archives omit. The bias is a reason to grade and triangulate evidence, not to reverse every judgment or treat the lost voice as recoverable by speculation.

## What Changed

- Extended the concept from unequal administrative centralization to conquest-driven archival destruction.
- Added enemy narration, translation, archaeology, and surviving technical writing as distinct evidence layers.
