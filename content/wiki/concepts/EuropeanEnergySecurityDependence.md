# European Energy Security Dependence

## Definition

European energy security dependence is the strategic exposure created when European industry, households, and foreign policy rely on imported oil or gas from suppliers whose interests can conflict with European security goals.

## Current Synthesis

[[290-2022-a-history-glt4625765030]] treats energy dependence as more than a price problem. Europe's long movement from domestic coal toward imported oil and gas increased exposure to external producers; after Russia invaded [[Ukraine]], sanctions and supply disruption therefore imposed uneven costs across the Atlantic. The [[UnitedStates]] could act with less self-damage because it was less dependent on Russian supply and able to export gas.

The historical comparison with the 1973 oil shock suggests a recurring mechanism: energy systems that appear efficient in normal conditions can become geopolitical constraints during conflict. [[168-oil-conflict-chaos-and-climate-change-glt4572551005]] extends that history to Western Europe's purchase of Soviet oil through the Friendship Pipeline and recurring U.S.-European disputes over energy links. It also sharpens the German case: [[NordStream]] did not merely purchase Russian gas but changed the route, reducing Ukraine's transit role and leverage.

The German political sequence adds domestic policy interaction. [[GerhardSchroder]] is linked to Nord Stream and later Russian corporate roles, while [[AngelaMerkel]]'s post-Fukushima nuclear closure is presented as increasing fossil-fuel reliance before the full security cost of Russian gas became visible. The same exposure has an industrial pathway: if European energy remains substantially more expensive than energy in the United States or Russia, it may shift production and investment as well as constrain sanctions. Alternative energy can therefore serve a strategic as well as environmental goal, although [[EnergyTransitionGeopolitics]] can create new manufacturing and mineral dependencies rather than instantly ending fossil-fuel exposure. These are plausible mechanisms, not proof that continent-wide deindustrialization or clean strategic autonomy has occurred.

## Key Claims

- Energy sourcing can constrain sanctions, diplomacy, industry, and alliance bargaining as well as household prices.
- Dependence is asymmetric: import exposure and domestic production shape how much economic pain different allies absorb.
- The 1973 oil shock and the 2022 Russian-energy shock reveal a recurring gap between peacetime efficiency and crisis resilience.
- Pipeline routes distribute transit revenue and bargaining power, so infrastructure design matters independently of the identity of the supplier.
- Long political stability can conceal energy vulnerability, and moral opposition to fossil fuels does not remove present material dependence.
- Diversification, domestic capacity, storage, LNG access, infrastructure, and demand adaptation are security questions, though the sources do not compare specific remedies.
- Persistent energy-price divergence can become an industrial-competitiveness risk as well as a household and diplomatic burden.

## Evidence

- Structural exposure: [[290-2022-a-history-glt4625765030]] connects Europe's move from coal toward imported oil with its lack of the reserves held by Russia and the United States.
- Sanctions asymmetry: [[290-2022-a-history-glt4625765030]] says the United States could sanction Russia at lower self-cost because it was less dependent and could export gas.
- Historical recurrence: [[290-2022-a-history-glt4625765030]] compares the 2022 shock with 1973's revelation of dependence on imported Middle Eastern oil.
- Route-specific leverage: [[168-oil-conflict-chaos-and-climate-change-glt4572551005]] connects the Soviet-era Friendship Pipeline, Ukraine's transit role, and Nord Stream's Baltic route to recurring disputes over European energy autonomy.
- German case: [[290-2022-a-history-glt4625765030]] connects Schröder, Nord Stream, and Merkel-era gas policy to accumulated strategic exposure; [[168-oil-conflict-chaos-and-climate-change-glt4572551005]] adds that dependence persisted immediately after the invasion shattered the policy assumptions behind it.
- Domestic policy interaction: [[102-germany-from-adenauer-to-angela-glt6085378369]] connects nuclear closure, fossil-fuel reliance, Nord Stream, and German-Russian commercial ties while presenting their long-term effect as a warning rather than a settled outcome.
- Uneven alternatives: [[168-oil-conflict-chaos-and-climate-change-glt4572551005]] contrasts Germany with Britain's North Sea production and LNG capacity while noting the difficulty of substitutes such as Azerbaijan and Iran.
- Industrial pathway: [[246-the-fall-of-liz-truss-glt3484252092]] connects expensive European energy to a possible divergence from energy-rich America and potential industrial relocation.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episodes offer high-level historical arguments, not measured comparisons of national energy mixes, carbon intensity, contract structures, infrastructure, prices, firm relocation, or policy alternatives. Europe is not a single energy system: Britain and Germany illustrate materially different production and import positions. The contribution of nuclear closure, industrial demand, Russian coercion, Schröder-era policy, Merkel-era policy, and allied choices is not isolated. Claims about deindustrialization, U.S.-European divergence, alternative suppliers, and future competition for the German gas market remain source-scoped. The 2021 and March 2022 sources predate later changes to Nord Stream and European gas trade.

## What Changed

- Added pipeline routing and transit-state leverage as mechanisms distinct from supplier dependence alone.
- Distinguished national exposure through Britain's production and LNG capacity versus Germany's Russian-gas position.
- Added transition as a strategic response that may create new dependencies rather than immediate autonomy.
- Added nuclear closure and chancellor-level policy continuity as interacting parts of the German exposure case.
