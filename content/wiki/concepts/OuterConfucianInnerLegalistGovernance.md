## Definition

Outer Confucian Inner Legalist Governance / 外儒内法式帝国治理 is the episode-pair's formula for an imperial order that can draw on Confucian morality, Heaven, ritual, and benevolent language while muting early Confucian anti-ruler sharpness and retaining a Legalist core of hierarchy, obedience, centralized rule, and ruler-centered control.

## Current Synthesis

The concept begins from a tension inside Han political ideology. Early Confucian "法先王" and people-first language can judge rulers, defend Tang-Wu revolution, and even make Han's own anti-Qin founding dependent on anti-tyrant legitimacy. That sharpness is useful for legitimating a founder but dangerous for a settled monarchy.

"独尊儒术" is then treated as an ideological transformation rather than a simple return to Kong-Meng teaching. In the host's account, [[DongZhongshu|董仲舒]] fuses Confucian moral language with [[HanFei|韩非]]-style hierarchy, allowing [[HanWudi|汉武帝]]'s state to look morally and cosmically justified while preserving the core operating structure of centralized autocracy.

Hanji 248(2) and Hanji 249 preserve a useful tension rather than one flat origin story. The former attributes to Dong a proposal to prevent teachings outside the Six Classics or opposed to Confucius from developing, joining ideological unity to clear law and public direction. The latter argues that elevating Confucianism did not mean literally deleting every other school and attributes an explicit personnel screen to [[WeiWanWesternHan|魏绾]]: non-Confucian ideas and methods could instead be subordinated, renamed, or used inside a Confucian public frame. The contrast supports predominance and exclusion pressure while leaving the formula's exact authorship, reach, and institutional implementation unsettled.

The concept is ambivalent rather than purely decorative. Heaven, disasters, astrology, qi observation, and "罪己诏" can pressure the ruler to reflect and restrain himself. Yet the same theological frame elevates the emperor as the mediator of Heaven, Earth, and humanity, turning moralized language into support for absolute status. The episode's house metaphor makes the continuity claim concrete: [[QinShiHuang|秦始皇]] builds the autocratic frame, and Han Wudi renovates it with Confucian ideology.

## Key Claims

- Official Han Confucianism is treated as a remade ideology rather than simply original Kong-Meng teaching: Hanji 248(2) supplies stronger exclusionary language, while Hanji 249 distinguishes predominance from literal erasure and separates Dong's argument from Wei Wan's explicit candidate screen.
- Hanji 392-3 makes the remaking problem visible by showing how early Confucian anti-tyrant doctrine can threaten an established dynasty.
- One-way ruler-minister, father-son, and husband-wife hierarchy is attributed by the episode to Legalist influence rather than to early Confucianism alone.
- Dong Zhongshu is the key synthesizer who makes Confucian language usable for imperial monarchy.
- Heaven-response and disaster-omen reasoning can admonish the emperor while keeping politics inside a sacred imperial frame.
- The same system strengthens imperial supremacy by presenting the ruler as cosmically central.
- Qin-Han continuity is structural: Han ideology beautifies and stabilizes the Qin-style centralized house more than it replaces it.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept currently rests on four short podcast episodes from the same show. It should not be treated as a full intellectual history of Confucianism, Legalism, Dong Zhongshu, Tang-Wu revolution theory, or the "三纲" vocabulary. Hanji 248(2)'s elimination language and Hanji 249's absorption boundary are not necessarily mutually exclusive, but the sources do not establish their textual relation, authorship, administrative reach, or which texts, offices, and practices survived. “Outer Confucian, inner Legalist” remains a retrospective compression rather than proof of one consistent hidden program, and the claimed two-millennium causal arc requires fuller textual corroboration.

## What Changed

- Preserved the tension between Hanji 248(2)'s exclusionary “大一统” language and Hanji 249's claim of predominance without literal intellectual erasure.
- Kept Dong Zhongshu's policy argument distinct from Wei Wan's explicit candidate-screening proposal.
