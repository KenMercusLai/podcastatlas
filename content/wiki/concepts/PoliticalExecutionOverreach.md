## Definition

政治诛杀过度 / political execution overreach is the pattern where a ruler punishes real or alleged misconduct so severely that the sentence damages legitimacy, public conviction, or the ruler's own otherwise positive governing record.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-441-gaikuanrao-bei-hanxuandi-bisi-beihou-de-zhichang-zhenxiang-tai-fengci-lm7iqjl6m638ulcpvir1jv7gxho0|Hanji 441]] now supplies a direct [[GaiKuanraoWesternHan|盖宽饶]] case behind a name that earlier appeared only in [[SimaGuang|司马光]]'s broader criticism. Gai's clean but sharp censorial speech is not treated as harmless: he uses classical language to criticize Xuan-era law, palace trust, and governance direction. The overreach problem emerges when hostile officials recode a classical distinction about public and hereditary rule as a demand that [[EmperorXuanOfHan|汉宣帝]] abdicate to Gai, [[ZhengChangRemonstratorWesternHan|郑昌]]'s defense fails, and Gai kills himself before judgment.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-457-hanxuandi-xinxiong-xiaai-shiji-gongchen-yangyun-yinhe-er-si-lpcfrhewqmmexb3ofg18iqbdsslm|Hanji 457]] grounds the Yang Yun branch. The episode does not exonerate Yang Yun: it stresses his resentment, high-profile conduct, and dangerous letter after losing office. But it also follows Sima Guang in treating the execution as disproportionate and politically damaging.

The Han Yanshou branch supplies a more detailed case behind one name in that Sima Guang critique. Han is not portrayed as spotless: his East Commandery spending and lending violations are treated as real. The overreach concern comes from escalation: [[BingJiWesternHan|丙吉]] initially wants to stop under the amnesty, [[XiaoWangzhiWesternHan|萧望之]] presses forward and reframes Han's counteraccusation, and Xuan approves death despite public grief over a capable local official. [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-450-qingguan-bixu-si-jiemi-hanchen-hanyanshou-bisi-zhi-mi-lmdsplur02fn29qllfhnz3fhinkq|Hanji 450]] adds that overreach can also be driven by political-security fear: popular local virtue and scholar-official ties become suspicious capital after Huo Guang.

The concept is therefore not the claim that punished officials are always innocent. Its core distinction is between guilt and death-worthiness. A ruler can be broadly effective and still create a stain when punishment turns imprudence, bluntness, or offense against superiors into irreversible public killing.

## Key Claims

- Excessive execution can damage an otherwise strong reign by making punishment look like severity rather than justice.
- The judgment depends on proportionality: a target may be culpable, or a remonstrance may be hostilely interpreted, without becoming death-worthy.
- Public conviction matters; punishment that people cannot accept weakens the moral force of law.
- Capable or upright officials create especially costly cases because killing them can look like eliminating useful criticism.
- The concept preserves both sides of the Xuan-era cases: Gai's sharp and risky memorial language, Yang Yun's imprudence, and Xuan's punitive overreach.
- The Han Yanshou case extends the concept from dangerous speech to administrative old-account exposure, failed counteraccusation, and discretionary escalation.
- Political-security fear can intensify overreach when public affection and moral reputation are read as rival legitimacy.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept does not deny that Gai Kuanrao's wording was politically risky, that Yang Yun's behavior was dangerous, or that Han Yanshou had real old-account exposure. The sources explicitly present real causes of exposure in all three cases. The overreach judgment is a proportionality claim, not an innocence claim. Hanji 441's reading of Gai's intended meaning and Hanji 450's post-Huo-Guang motive reading are interpretive, not independently settled proof of Xuan's inner reasoning. Zhao Guanghan remains a name in the critique until direct source notes are integrated.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 441's Gai Kuanrao case, extending the concept from Sima Guang's named criticism into a direct remonstrance-recoding and suicide case.
- Preserved Hanji 450's post-Huo-Guang security reading, where popular legitimacy and scholar-official reputation become suspicious capital.
- Added Hanji 451's Han Yanshou case, extending the concept from Yang Yun's dangerous speech to a capable official's old-account case and politically shaped death sentence.
- Preserved Hanji 457's Sima Guang-backed critique of Emperor Xuan's punishment severity.
