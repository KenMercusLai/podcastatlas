## Definition
Domestication and social-network behavior is the hypothesis that ancestry shaped by human selection can alter how an animal responds to members of its own species and how socially central individuals transmit behavior through a group.

## Current Synthesis
[[08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5-08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5mp3aid6af5967413c30781a320173dchidd556eb54-6160-4]] uses contagious yawning as an observable social-response marker. In a captive sample, wolves without dog ancestry copied a witnessed yawn more often than wolves with dog ancestry. The source interprets this as evidence that dog genes may weaken some wolf-to-wolf responsiveness even as domestication made dogs unusually attentive to humans.

Within the wolf groups, yawns from broadly connected animals spread more readily than yawns from dominant individuals or close relatives. The result makes network integration, rather than hierarchy or pairwise closeness alone, a candidate mechanism for behavioral contagion. It does not show that yawning itself is ecologically decisive; its value is as a possible indicator of wider changes in coordination and pack sociality.

## Key Claims
- Domestication may redistribute social attention between conspecifics and humans rather than simply increasing or decreasing sociability overall.
- Contagious yawning can be used as a behavioral indicator without assuming that it directly measures empathy.
- Social centrality across a pack may predict behavioral spread better than dominance or close kinship.
- Hybrid ancestry can be associated with group-behavior differences before the responsible genetic or developmental mechanism is known.
- A visible contagion difference may signal wider coordination changes, but ecological consequences require separate evidence.

## Evidence
- Ancestry association - [[08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5-08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5mp3aid6af5967413c30781a320173dchidd556eb54-6160-4]] reports a 41% two-minute copying likelihood among 20 wolves without dog ancestry and 24% among 13 wolves with dog ancestry.
- Network position - [[08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5-08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5mp3aid6af5967413c30781a320173dchidd556eb54-6160-4]] reports that wolves interacting broadly across the pack were more likely to trigger yawn waves than dominant or closely related animals.
- Comparative interpretation - [[08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5-08cf7dbf07d967235ec2004c6e8b93f5mp3aid6af5967413c30781a320173dchidd556eb54-6160-4]] contrasts dogs' responsiveness to human yawns with their reported lack of dog-to-dog yawn contagion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The study as summarized includes only 33 captive animals and does not provide full methods, effect uncertainty, housing histories, ancestry proportions, or independent replication. Yawn contagion is not a direct readout of empathy, pack fitness, or ecosystem function. Genetic ancestry may correlate with developmental, social, or environmental differences, and the source's conservation implications remain hypotheses rather than demonstrated population effects.

## What Changed
- Created an ancestry-and-network framework for interpreting contagious yawning without equating it with empathy.
- Added social centrality as a possible pathway for behavior to spread through a wolf pack.
- Preserved the gap between captive behavioral association and conservation consequence.
