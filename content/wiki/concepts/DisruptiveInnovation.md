# Disruptive Innovation

## Definition
Disruptive innovation is an adoption pattern in which a technology that is weaker on incumbent performance measures becomes strategically important because a different advantage is good enough for a new market and can compound through iteration, cost, integration, distribution, or ecosystem growth.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded sources join a component-history case to a company case. Early [[MOSFET|MOS]] devices looked slower and less stable than [[BipolarJunctionTransistor|BJT]] devices for [[ATT|AT&T]]'s immediate switching needs, but simpler structure, lower cost, and integrability became more valuable as density scaled. [[Nvidia]]'s history adds two boundaries: its NV1 architecture was not productive disruption merely because it differed from the standard and had to be abandoned, while RIVA 128 and early compute GPUs became useful when they were good enough for gaming or research and could improve through distribution, software, and developer learning.

Disruption therefore does not mean that every flawed outsider wins. The route needs a scalable advantage, a reachable entry market, enough runway for learning, and an organization willing both to persist through weak early demand and to abandon a genuinely wrong architecture.

## Key Claims
- A new route can be weak by incumbent metrics and still matter if a different scalable advantage opens a reachable market.
- “Good enough” is an entry condition, not proof of eventual dominance; distribution, learning, manufacturing, and ecosystem support must compound.
- Technical difference alone is not disruption: NV1 shows that an incompatible architecture can require abandonment rather than persistence.
- Incumbents are vulnerable when the new route threatens existing revenue, evaluation systems, or organizational identity.
- Protected teams, alternate metrics, gaming-subsidized distribution, and deliberate self-replacement can preserve a route long enough to test it.

### Persistence-versus-abandonment boundary
- [[vmp8419655880-vmp8419655880]] shows Nvidia abandoning NV1 after DirectX made its architectural mismatch commercially dangerous, then using gaming to distribute the more adaptable CUDA platform while research demand matured.

### Organizational self-disruption

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Retrospective winners create survivor bias: many weak technologies remain weak, and later success can make early conviction look more informed than it was. “Good enough” is market-specific, standards can close a route, and runway can be consumed without learning. The Nvidia account is Huang's retrospective rather than an independent product history, while the semiconductor-history source uses analogies that do not prove current technologies will follow MOS adoption.
