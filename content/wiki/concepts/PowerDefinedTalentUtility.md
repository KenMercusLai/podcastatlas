## Definition

Power-defined talent utility is a personnel pattern in which a concentrated authority decides whether ability counts as talent by judging how readily the person serves the authority's priorities, preferred style, and political safety.

## Current Synthesis

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-319-2-kelian-caocao-bei-yi-ju-hua-ma-san-dai-lu4xhsvlcfpcbarplwyobxymqm4k|Hanji 319-2]] frames [[HanWudi|汉武帝]]'s court as a field where the emperor controls recognition as well as appointment. In that frame, a person's self-understood intelligence or achievement does not secure meaningful office; ability becomes politically consequential only after the ruler judges it usable.

The source contrasts [[DongfangShuo|东方朔]] and [[GongsunHong|公孙弘]]. Dongfang Shuo's *Da Ke Nan* is presented as a response to underuse: he explains earlier persuaders' success partly through the opportunities of a divided age. Gongsun Hong is the host's counterexample because he is described as capable while also understanding imperial intention. The concept therefore separates ability, recognition, and ruler-fit rather than treating “talent” as one independent quality.

This is a descriptive account of concentrated personnel power, not an endorsement of obedience as merit. A ruler may gain coordination by selecting people who understand priorities, but can also suppress corrective disagreement, mistake comfort for competence, or leave valuable ability unused.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-319-1-gen-zhe-gu-dai-huang-di-xue-xi-qiang-ren-cai-lmf6r8vlx2vukliuepeaygu-qm-g|Hanji 319-1]] sharpens the concept from appointment preference into an explicit doctrine of use. Its quoted Wudi says talent can arise anywhere and need not be scarce when recognized, yet calls the worthy person a useful instrument and equates refusal to serve fully with lack of talent. Recruitment breadth and personal autonomy therefore become separate variables: a system can search widely while still denying the recruit an independent claim to judgment or refusal.

## Key Claims

- Ability and political opportunity are separate when one authority controls recognition and appointment.
- Ruler-fit can include timing, framing, trust, and understanding priorities, not only technical skill.
- A system that defines talent through ease of use may reward coordination while filtering out useful dissent.
- Broad access beyond lineage does not prevent instrumentalization when obedience remains the final test of worth.
- Claims of being underused can reflect both real gatekeeping and a candidate's own incomplete judgment of role fit.
- The current evidence establishes one podcast interpretation of Han Wudi's court, not a general law of all imperial personnel systems.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Both sources are short popular-history commentaries and supply no systematic reconstruction of Han personnel institutions. Hanji 319-1 leaves the dissenting minister unnamed, while its lethal implication is broad and unsupported by a named victim or event-level case. Dongfang Shuo's *Da Ke Nan* can be read as literary self-positioning rather than transparent proof of Wudi's private judgment, while Gongsun Hong's effectiveness requires event-level evidence from other sources. Understanding a ruler's priorities can improve implementation, but agreement, flattery, fear, and genuine strategic translation must not be collapsed into one trait.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 319-1's explicit instrument metaphor and full-use test, moving the concept beyond appointment preference into a sharper account of denied refusal.
- Distinguished broad recruitment origins from respect for autonomy: the former does not guarantee the latter.
- Kept the killing implication source-scoped because no named case is supplied.
