# Golf-Course Property-Tax Preference

## Definition

Golf-course property-tax preference is the combined effect of rules that value qualifying course land by its current recreational use rather than its potentially more valuable development use and preserve an older assessment until a qualifying ownership change or new construction occurs.

## Current Synthesis

The bounded source describes two layers in California. A 1960 constitutional amendment exempted golf courses from ordinary “highest and best use” valuation, and Proposition 13 later anchored already reduced assessments near pre-1978 levels unless reassessment was triggered.

For member-owned clubs, the meaning of ownership change becomes decisive. If individual equity-share transfers are evaluated separately and none crosses a control threshold, complete membership turnover can occur without resetting the assessment. The defensible policy question is therefore not only whether open space merits protection, but whether the size, duration, beneficiaries, and reciprocal public value of this particular preference remain justified.

## Key Claims

- Current-use valuation can protect recreational open space from development pressure while also creating a large private tax advantage.
- Proposition 13 can compound an earlier valuation preference by preserving its historical assessment base.
- Transaction-by-transaction ownership tests may let the advantage persist through complete cumulative member replacement.
- Fiscal size and public benefit require primary valuation, assessment, legal, and land-use evidence rather than episode estimates alone.
- Preferential valuation does not by itself determine whether public access, alternative taxation, or another reciprocal benefit should follow.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source does not supply the constitutional text, implementing law, assessor methodology, full Proposition 13 doctrine, later amendments, litigation, or verified parcel records. Preferential treatment may preserve open space, habitat, recreation, or historic landscapes, and ordinary highest-and-best-use taxation could create development pressure; those benefits and risks must be compared with exclusion, foregone revenue, and feasible public alternatives.
