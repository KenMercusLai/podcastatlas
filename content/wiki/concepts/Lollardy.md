# Lollardy

## Definition

Lollardy is the English reform movement associated with John Wycliffe that the episode uses to connect biblical emphasis, criticism of clerical and papal authority, persecution, and the religious politics around [[HenryIVOfEngland|Henry IV]] and [[HenryVOfEngland|Henry V]].

## Current Synthesis

The source presents Lollardy as a varied challenge to late-medieval religious authority rather than simply a later Protestant movement in finished form. It associates Lollards with anti-papal and anti-clerical positions, opposition to some church practices, and emphasis on the Bible. Under Henry IV, repression hardened through executions such as the 1401 burning of William Sawtrey.

The movement also reveals ambiguity inside Lancastrian networks. John of Gaunt had patronized Wycliffe, Henry IV's confessor had earlier Wycliffite ties, Prince Henry owned an English Bible and supported academic freedom at Oxford, and [[JohnOldcastle]] was his friend. These connections complicate Henry V's later image as an intensely orthodox ruler but do not establish that he was secretly Lollard.

## Key Claims

- Lollardy drew on Wycliffite criticism of papal, clerical, and sacramental authority.
- Biblical access and religious reform were central concerns in the episode's account.
- Henry IV's reign marked a harsher persecutory environment, including execution by burning.
- Oldcastle joined religious dissent to political rebellion under Henry V.
- Lancastrian contact with Wycliffite people and texts complicates a simple orthodoxy-versus-heresy division.
- Prince Henry's earlier relationships and interests do not by themselves establish private heterodoxy.

## Evidence

- Belief profile: [[486-henry-iv-warrior-princes-and-fat-knights-part-2-glt8391310611]] links Lollardy to Wycliffe, anti-papal and anti-clerical positions, opposition to church practices, and biblical emphasis.
- Persecution: [[486-henry-iv-warrior-princes-and-fat-knights-part-2-glt8391310611]] discusses the burnings of William Sawtrey and John Badby.
- Lancastrian proximity: [[486-henry-iv-warrior-princes-and-fat-knights-part-2-glt8391310611]] notes John of Gaunt's patronage, the royal confessor's earlier affiliation, Henry's English Bible, and his friendship with Oldcastle.
- Orthodox reaction: [[486-henry-iv-warrior-princes-and-fat-knights-part-2-glt8391310611]] presents Henry V's later reputation as strongly orthodox and hostile to Lollard rebellion.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode compresses a diverse movement into a short narrative and sometimes describes it through later proto-Protestant comparison. An English Bible, concern for church reform, Oxford academic freedom, or friendship with Oldcastle does not prove Lollard adherence. The precise beliefs of individuals, the prince's motives in offering John Badby a chance to recant, and the evolution of Henry's private faith remain unresolved.

## What Changed

- Established Lollardy as both a persecuted reform movement and a network that complicates the religious biography of Henry V.
