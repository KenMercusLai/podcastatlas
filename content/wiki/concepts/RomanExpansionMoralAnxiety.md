# Roman Expansion Moral Anxiety / 罗马扩张的道德焦虑

## Definition

Roman expansion moral anxiety / 罗马扩张的道德焦虑 is the Roman Civil War sequence's mechanism for how Mediterranean success created fears that wealth, luxury, provincial empire, publicani extraction, and anti-Roman resentment were weakening the moral and social basis of the [[RomanRepublic|Roman Republic]].

## Current Synthesis

Episode 701 presents Rome after 146 BC as a superpower that had destroyed Carthage and Corinth, held major provinces, and received Pergamum's wealth in 133 BC. That success did not feel simply triumphant inside Rome. Asian luxury goods, celebrity chefs, sex manuals, and the influx of wealth allowed critics such as Pliny the Elder to treat conquest as moral danger, while poor urban Romans saw elite enrichment as evidence that the nobility no longer cared for ordinary citizens.

Episode 702 turns that anxiety into a more concrete political economy. Campania and the Bay of Naples appear as a dense zone of villas, Greek culture, luxury consumption, Puteoli trade, underwater concrete, and global goods. Italian elites can profit from the same imperial system, especially through Asia, while Roman and Italian publicani heavily exploit the province. Expansion therefore feeds both domestic resentment and the [[RomanAlliedCitizenshipCrisis]].

Episode 703 adds the perspective from outside Rome. [[MithridatesVI]] weaponizes resentment of Roman and Italian exploitation by presenting himself through prophecies as an eastern king punishing Roman overreach. His execution of [[ManiusAquillius]] with molten gold dramatizes Roman greed, his order to massacre Romans and Italians in Asia shocks Italy, and [[Athens]] joins the anti-Roman wave under [[AristionAthens]]. The concept therefore now includes both Roman moral anxiety about what empire is doing to Rome and provincial/Greek anger at what Rome is doing abroad.

## Key Claims

- Rome's Mediterranean dominance created internal pressure as well as external power.
- Provincial wealth and Asian luxury became symbols of moral decline for Roman critics.
- Poor citizens' resentment grew when elite enrichment looked detached from common welfare.
- Moral anxiety gave outsider soldiers such as [[GaiusMarius]] a public contrast against aristocratic softness.
- Campanian luxury and Asian trade show expansion as lived consumption, port infrastructure, and private profit.
- Publicani extraction and allied participation connect moral anxiety to the citizenship crisis as well as elite luxury.
- Anti-Roman movements can turn Roman greed into propaganda, massacre justification, and revolt language.

## Evidence

- Superpower setting: [[701-roman-civil-war-rise-of-the-general-part-1-glt1262857909]] notes Rome's destruction of Carthage and Corinth by 146 BC and its province network across Sicily, Africa, Spain, southern Gaul, Macedon, and Asia.
- Asian wealth and luxury: [[701-roman-civil-war-rise-of-the-general-part-1-glt1262857909]] describes Pergamum's 133 BC bequest and Roman exposure to luxury goods and pleasures as morally charged.
- Moral critique: [[701-roman-civil-war-rise-of-the-general-part-1-glt1262857909]] cites Pliny the Elder's view that Macedon and Asia harmed Roman morals.
- Class resentment: [[701-roman-civil-war-rise-of-the-general-part-1-glt1262857909]] connects the influx of wealth to urban poor resentment and suspicion of noble indifference.
- Campanian luxury economy: [[702-roman-civil-war-the-barbarian-invasion-part-2-glt3336491089]] describes the Bay of Naples, Puteoli, villas, spas, marinas, oysters, and high-value imports as signs of extraordinary Roman and Italian wealth.
- Asian extraction and allied resentment: [[702-roman-civil-war-the-barbarian-invasion-part-2-glt3336491089]] says Roman and Italian publicani exploited Asia heavily while Italian elites profited abroad but lacked full Roman status.
- Anti-Roman propaganda: [[703-roman-civil-war-marching-on-the-senate-part-3-glt3125075511]] says Mithridates uses eastern prophecies and Aquillius's molten-gold execution to present himself as punishing Roman greed.
- Provincial violence and Greek revolt: [[703-roman-civil-war-marching-on-the-senate-part-3-glt3125075511]] says Asian cities massacre Romans and Italians and that Athens revolts against pro-Roman oligarchs under Mithridatic inspiration.
- Punitive restoration: [[703-roman-civil-war-marching-on-the-senate-part-3-glt3125075511]] says Greek cities in Asia later suffer five years of back taxes, war costs, and Roman garrison costs after Sulla restores Roman rule.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The page records the sources' Roman moral-anxiety and political-economy frame rather than independently proving that luxury caused republican decline. Episode 703 also treats Mithridates's propaganda as politically effective, not as neutral moral truth; the massacre figure of 80,000 is reported as inflated but believed in Italy. Italian elites remain morally mixed because some profit from empire even while citizenship exclusion remains destabilizing.

## What Changed

- Added Mithridates's anti-Roman propaganda, Aquillius's execution, Asian massacres, Athenian revolt, and Sulla's punitive restoration of Asia.
- Expanded the concept from Roman anxiety about luxury and corruption to include provincial resentment against Roman and Italian exploitation.
- Preserved the distinction between propaganda power and settled causal proof.
