# Royal Execution Legitimacy

## Definition

Royal execution legitimacy is the problem of making a sovereign or plausible royal claimant's killing legally, theologically, and politically tolerable. It explains why [[MaryI|Mary I]] can regard [[LadyJaneGrey|Lady Jane Grey]] as dangerous yet initially spare her, and why [[ElizabethI|Elizabeth I]] can see [[MaryQueenOfScots|Mary, Queen of Scots]] as a security threat while resisting her execution.

## Current Synthesis

The Jane case adds a claimant-status version before the Mary Stuart sequence. After July 1553, Mary distinguishes Jane from the adult architects of the usurpation and can hold her under a suspended death sentence while her claim appears containable. The [[WyattRebellion|Wyatt Rebellion]] and [[HenryGrey|Henry Grey]]'s participation change the risk calculation: Jane can anchor Protestant opposition even without directing it herself.

The legitimacy problem then reappears more sharply around an anointed foreign queen. Mary's forced abdication creates the upstream question before execution: [[ElizabethI|Elizabeth I]] is appalled that subjects can depose a sovereign, but she will not pay the military and security cost of restoring [[MaryQueenOfScots|Mary, Queen of Scots]]. After Mary's arrival in England and the first conspiracy scares, [[WilliamCecil|William Cecil / Lord Burleigh]] wants Elizabeth to authorize execution, but Elizabeth resists because Mary remains an anointed monarch despite lost rule. The later Babington episode supplies stronger evidence and legal machinery, yet the same problem remains: executing Mary removes a claimant but advertises that a sovereign can be killed through procedure.

The concept therefore sits between security necessity and monarchical self-protection. Law can make execution possible, but it can also make the precedent clearer and give enemies symbolic material.

## Key Claims

- A royal execution requires more than proof of danger because sovereign status creates a legitimacy barrier.
- Mercy remains sustainable only while a defeated claimant appears politically containable.
- Elizabeth's reluctance is a defense of monarchy as an institution, not only personal mercy toward Mary.
- Deposition does not erase anointing or automatically make a captive former ruler safe to judge as an ordinary subject.
- Ministers try to convert execution from private vengeance into public law, but legal process also formalizes the precedent.
- Removing a claimant can intensify confessional war by producing martyr symbolism or foreign justification.

## Evidence

- Defeated-claimant mercy: [[294-lady-jane-grey-the-axe-falls-part-2-glt7286066638]] says Mary initially believed Jane had not truly consented to the usurpation and resisted killing her.
- Renewed security risk: [[294-lady-jane-grey-the-axe-falls-part-2-glt7286066638]] connects Wyatt's rebellion and Henry Grey's participation to the decision that Jane could no longer safely live.
- Early execution dilemma: [[691-elizabeth-i-vs-the-catholics-the-queens-spymaster-part-1-glt8312620561]] shows Cecil pressing for Mary's execution while Elizabeth resists because Mary is an anointed monarch.
- Deposition-to-execution boundary: [[589-mary-queen-of-scots-downfall-part-6-glt8999516788]] shows Elizabeth opposing Mary's coerced deposition in principle while refusing restoration and resisting a common tribunal's judgment of a monarch.
- Later legal mechanism: [[696-elizabeth-i-vs-the-catholics-killing-the-queen-of-scots-part-6-glt2334035496]] grounds the Act for the Queen's Safety, Fotheringhay trial, signed warrant, and council action around Mary.
- Precedent risk: [[691-elizabeth-i-vs-the-catholics-the-queens-spymaster-part-1-glt8312620561]] and [[696-elizabeth-i-vs-the-catholics-killing-the-queen-of-scots-part-6-glt2334035496]] both show Elizabeth fearing the precedent of killing an anointed queen.
- Escalation aftereffect: [[696-elizabeth-i-vs-the-catholics-killing-the-queen-of-scots-part-6-glt2334035496]] links Mary's execution to martyr staging and the wider Armada path.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not imply that mercy is permanent or that every claimant has equal sovereign status. Mary eventually authorizes Jane's execution after renewed rebellion, and Elizabeth eventually signs Mary Stuart's warrant after the Babington evidence path. Jane is a disputed ruler under an existing sentence; Mary Stuart is an anointed queen deposed under coercion and later tried under new English machinery. Elizabeth's objection to deposition did not oblige her to restore Mary. The shared point is that danger alone does not explain timing: execution requires a changed security, legal, and symbolic judgment.

## What Changed

- Added forced deposition as the upstream legitimacy problem that Mary's English captivity did not resolve.
- Clarified that Elizabeth could reject subject-led removal in principle while refusing restoration in practice.
