# Schlieffen Plan

## Definition

The Schlieffen Plan is the German opening-war design, as presented in [[594-the-first-world-war-the-invasion-of-belgium-part-1-glt4855256129]], for defeating France rapidly through [[Belgium]] before shifting forces east against Russia.

## Current Synthesis

The plan joined offensive ambition to perceived weakness. Germany and Austria-Hungary faced an Entente with greater combined population, economic output, manpower, and soldiers, so German leaders feared that a prolonged two-front war would become unwinnable. Speed in the west was therefore meant to compensate for unfavorable long-run arithmetic.

Operationally, the plan sought to bypass French frontier fortresses, move through Belgium, encircle Paris, and defeat France before slower Russian mobilization fully matured. That design made neutral territory, railway capacity, march rates, and the rapid reduction of Liège and Namur structural requirements rather than side issues.

The plan's central qualification is that felt necessity did not remove agency. Routing the attack through Belgium widened the war politically, invited British intervention, and placed commanders under a timetable that made resistance and delay appear existential. The result linked strategic fear to illegal invasion, operational rigidity, and violent responses to disruption.

## Key Claims

- The plan attempted to solve Germany's two-front problem through sequential rather than simultaneous concentration.
- It relied on defeating France quickly before shifting major forces against Russia.
- Movement through Belgium was central because it bypassed French frontier defenses and opened a wider approach toward Paris.
- Rail capacity, daily marching targets, and fortress reduction made time an operational resource.
- The plan expressed strategic anxiety as well as confidence in offensive maneuver.
- Its violation of Belgian neutrality created political and legal costs that military planning did not contain.

## Evidence

- Strategic premise: [[594-the-first-world-war-the-invasion-of-belgium-part-1-glt4855256129]] contrasts German fear of a long war with the Entente's larger combined resources.
- Operational route: [[594-the-first-world-war-the-invasion-of-belgium-part-1-glt4855256129]] describes the intended movement through Belgium, bypass of frontier fortresses, encirclement of Paris, and later transfer east.
- Time compression: [[594-the-first-world-war-the-invasion-of-belgium-part-1-glt4855256129]] connects more than 500 trains a day, three armies, and rapid march targets to pressure on the advance.
- Political consequence: [[594-the-first-world-war-the-invasion-of-belgium-part-1-glt4855256129]] connects the ultimatum and invasion to Britain's treaty, legal, and liberal-intervention case.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This page reflects one episode's high-level account rather than a full study of Schlieffen's original memorandum, Moltke's modifications, force allocation, logistics, or the plan-versus-deployment historiography. Calling the design a response to strategic weakness does not show that invasion was unavoidable or lawful. The source also does not prove that any one delay caused the later defeat at the Marne.

## What Changed

- Created the plan as a synthesis of two-front fear, rapid western concentration, Belgian violation, and timetable pressure.
