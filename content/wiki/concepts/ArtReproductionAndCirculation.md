# Art Reproduction and Circulation

## Definition
Art reproduction and circulation is the process by which copies, prints, photographs, tours, parodies, and mass-media reuse move an artwork beyond its original physical location and reshape its public meaning.

## Current Synthesis
[[667-the-mystery-of-the-mona-lisa-glt1735270133]] uses [[MonaLisa|the Mona Lisa]] to show that fame depends on circulation as well as original presence. Poor reproductions once failed to capture the painting's effect, but improved photography in the 1880s helped spread the image. Later theft coverage, parody, and touring made the reproduced image part of the painting's identity rather than a mere substitute.

## Key Claims
- Reproduction can create recognition at a scale museum display alone cannot.
- Better reproduction technology can change an artwork's cultural rank.
- Circulation can make viewers numb to an image even while increasing its fame.
- Parody depends on prior recognizability and can reinforce iconic status.
- Mass circulation can shift attention from the object alone to its accumulated story.

## Evidence
- Photography - [[667-the-mystery-of-the-mona-lisa-glt1735270133]] says improved photography in the 1880s allowed the image to circulate more effectively.
- Ubiquity - [[667-the-mystery-of-the-mona-lisa-glt1735270133]] says the painting's ubiquity has made some viewers numb to its beauty.
- Parody - [[667-the-mystery-of-the-mona-lisa-glt1735270133]] cites Duchamp's 1919 moustache/goatee parody and Warhol's 1963 reproductions after the American tour.
- Theft and news - [[667-the-mystery-of-the-mona-lisa-glt1735270133]] shows how news circulation around the 1911 theft made absence itself widely visible.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source is a single case study, not a general theory of every artwork. It also implies that reproduction may spread an image while dulling direct aesthetic encounter.

## What Changed
- Created a media-art concept grounded in the Mona Lisa's reproduction history.
