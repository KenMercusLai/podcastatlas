# July Crisis

## Definition

The July Crisis was the 1914 diplomatic and military escalation that turned the conflict surrounding Austria-Hungary and Serbia into a general European war; this page currently covers its final phase from Russian general mobilisation to Britain's declaration of war on Germany.

## Current Synthesis

The episode treats the final crisis as constrained agency rather than either innocent accident or a fully pre-scripted conspiracy. Leaders anticipated catastrophe and sometimes sought narrower outcomes, but alliances and informal commitments, mobilisation timetables, military control of transport, fear of strategic encirclement, domestic political pressures, miscommunication, honour, and fatalistic assumptions made reversal progressively harder.

Britain's path shows why no single cause is sufficient. Informal naval coordination created an obligation to [[France]]; German power on the Channel threatened British interests; a divided cabinet faced opposition pressure; and the ultimatum and invasion of [[Belgium]] supplied the legal-moral frame that joined those motives into action. Public justification then simplified this layered decision into a more durable story of treaty, honour, and defence of a small state.

## Key Claims

- General mobilisation converted diplomatic signals into operational pressures that political leaders struggled to reverse.
- Germany's westward plan made a politically desired limited eastern war difficult to execute once mobilisation began.
- Informal commitments can become morally and strategically binding even without a formal alliance treaty.
- Miscommunication briefly created a false belief that Britain would guarantee French neutrality, exposing the gap between diplomatic improvisation and military planning.
- Belgium joined treaty obligation, international law, Channel strategy, Liberal principle, and public persuasion in one decisive issue.
- Decision-makers' tears, dread, and breakdowns show awareness of catastrophe without cancelling their agency or responsibility.
- Wartime moral narratives compress multi-causal entry decisions into publicly usable stories.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source covers only the final days and assumes earlier stages of the road-to-war sequence; it is not a complete causal history of Sarajevo, Austria-Hungary's ultimatum to Serbia, alliance development, or prewar arms competition. Rejecting a literal sleepwalking metaphor does not imply complete information or unconstrained choice. Belgian neutrality was crucial to Britain's final decision and public narrative, but the episode explicitly retains French obligations, Channel security, party politics, and national honour as prior causes.
