# Mass Broadcast Culture

## Definition

Mass broadcast culture is the institutional production of large shared audiences through radio, film, news, and cultural gatekeepers.

## Current Synthesis

A decisive expansion of this system appears in the 1922 case: American cinema, jazz, celebrity, and commercial radio spread cultural influence beyond formal diplomacy, while Britain's licensed [[BBC]] joined technical broadcasting to an explicit public-cultural mission. Tutankhamun news shows the same attention system working through newspapers and exclusivity.

Comedy supplies a second test of synchronization. British radio and television created common characters, lines, and wartime reference points across large audiences, whereas earlier music hall could remain regional and later media abundance fragments the national comic repertoire. Broadcast scarcity thus produced unusual commonality, not an ahistorical default.

## Key Claims

- Mass media can expand a country's cultural power even when its government retreats from international institutions.
- Radio's technical possibility did not determine one institutional model: American commercial proliferation and British licensed coordination differed.
- Newspapers could turn an archaeological discovery into a worldwide craze through speed, exclusivity, personality, and myth-making.
- Shared broadcasters and studios created common national or transnational reference points, but did not guarantee equal understanding or cultural inclusion.
- Platform abundance can extend mass communication while fragmenting the common audience produced by broadcast scarcity.
- Shared comic culture depends on distribution infrastructure as well as creative quality or national character.

## Evidence

- **Cultural power and shared audiences:** [[137-1922-the-birth-of-the-modern-world-part-2-glt5281126494]] connects Hollywood, jazz, Chaplin, Disney, and radio to the “Americanization” of culture.
- **Institutional alternatives:** The source contrasts rapid American station growth with coordinated British company formation, licensing, and the early [[BBC]]. [[137-1922-the-birth-of-the-modern-world-part-2-glt5281126494]]
- **Media event and fragmentation:** The episode pairs Tutankhamun's worldwide news cycle with a closing argument that later media choice weakened shared canons and gatekeepers. [[137-1922-the-birth-of-the-modern-world-part-2-glt5281126494]]
- **Comic synchronization:** [[28-the-kings-of-comedy-glt5677879972]] contrasts regionally bounded music hall with radio- and television-era common references and later audience fragmentation.
- **Shared experience as material:** [[28-the-kings-of-comedy-glt5677879972]] attributes postwar comedy's common vocabulary partly to widely shared wartime experience as well as national broadcasting.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources do not measure audience size, reception, or cultural homogenization directly. “First global media event” and the loss of a collective British comic culture are attributed interpretations, and the evidence is weighted toward British and American institutions. Fragmentation may weaken shared culture while widening access, regional expression, and choice; neither source resolves that tradeoff.

## What Changed

- Added comedy as evidence that broadcasting synchronized national reference points only during a historically unusual media period.
- Distinguished shared distribution from shared interpretation or inclusion.
