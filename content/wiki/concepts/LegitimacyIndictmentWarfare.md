# Legitimacy Indictment Warfare / 正当性控诉战

## Definition
Legitimacy Indictment Warfare / 正当性控诉战 is the tactic of publicly assembling an opponent's alleged crimes into a right-to-punish frame, so a conflict is heard as legitimate prosecution of public wrongdoing rather than as private rivalry or naked force competition.

## Current Synthesis
The concept now covers both spoken battlefield indictment and written campaign proclamation. In the Chu-Han case, [[LiuBang|刘邦]] refuses [[XiangYu|项羽]]'s single-combat frame at [[GuangwuChuHan|广武]] by reciting broken covenants, settlement violence, and the killing of Yi Emperor [[MiXin|芈心 / 楚怀王]]. In the late-Han Guandu case, [[ChenLinLateHan|陈琳]] writes [[YuanShao|袁绍]]'s anti-[[CaoCao|曹操]] proclamation, listing Cao's alleged injuries to loyal officials, emperor-control, Xuzhou violence, tomb-robbing offices, and lineage-based ingratitude.

The shared pattern is audience conversion. The indictment moves attention away from the enemy's preferred measure, whether duel courage or battlefield scale, and toward the question of who has authority to mobilize punishment. The limit is equally important: indictment can sharpen morale and reputation pressure, but Cao Cao's response in the White Horse source insists that strong writing still has to be matched by talent, strategy, and command.

## Key Claims
- A commander or patron can reject the enemy's preferred contest by changing the audience and issue from personal strength to public right.
- Accusations work politically when they assemble remembered grievances into a coherent right-to-punish frame.
- The tactic depends on an opponent who can be made to look like a breaker of covenants, public order, or accepted legitimacy.
- Written proclamations extend the same logic beyond battlefield speech into pre-campaign public-opinion and morale work.
- Rhetorical force has limits: legitimacy indictment can wound reputation, but it does not by itself solve command, logistics, or execution.

## Evidence
- Duel-frame refusal: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-164-1-liubang-geqiang-chaofeng-qi-de-xiangyu-shejian-touxi-lhs6miq2aa3avnny-prxtvkfxgvy|Hanji 164-1]] has Liu Bang refuse Xiang Yu's personal combat demand by publicly reciting Xiang Yu's crimes and broken promises.
- Indictment material: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-164-1-liubang-geqiang-chaofeng-qi-de-xiangyu-shejian-touxi-lhs6miq2aa3avnny-prxtvkfxgvy|Hanji 164-1]] uses the Huaiwang agreement, entry into Qin, killing of surrendered people, unequal partition, and Yi Emperor's death as legitimacy anchors against Xiang Yu.
- Campaign proclamation: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1043-yuanshao-tongma-caocao-jing-zhihaole-tade-piantouteng-ltibjj6xizsrynpgr9oyf-q3ydna|Hanji 1043]] says Yuan Shao orders Chen Lin to write and circulate a proclamation accusing Cao Cao of five major categories of wrongdoing.
- Rhetorical impact and limit: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1043-yuanshao-tongma-caocao-jing-zhihaole-tade-piantouteng-ltibjj6xizsrynpgr9oyf-q3ydna|Hanji 1043]] frames the proclamation as powerful enough to shock Cao Cao, but also has Cao Cao answer that writing must be backed by real ability and military command.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Legitimacy indictment is not neutral fact-finding. The source claims may be selective, exaggerated, or polemical, so the concept tracks the political function of accusation rather than verifying every charge. The tactic also differs from ordinary slander because it needs a public authority frame and a usable audience. Xiang Yu's crossbow response and Cao Cao's command critique both show that force can interrupt or outlast the rhetorical moment.

## What Changed
- Migrated the concept to synthesis-v1 and broadened it from Liu Bang's spoken Guangwu indictment to include Chen Lin's written anti-Cao proclamation.
- Added the rhetoric-versus-command qualification: indictment can attack legitimacy but cannot replace operational capacity.
