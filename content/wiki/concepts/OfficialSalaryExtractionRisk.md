## Definition

官吏俸禄不足抽取风险 / Official Salary Extraction Risk is the governance pattern where low, cut, delayed, or unreliable official pay increases the incentive for officials with coercive authority to recover income through fees, bribery, favoritism, or informal local extraction.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 782 supplies the immediate fiscal prelude. Under Empress Dowager Deng, Qiang war, military defeat, grain transport, famine, and revenue exhaustion push the court toward selling ranks or court-adjacent identities for money and grain. That same budget field makes later salary reduction intelligible: it is not just moral austerity, but a sign that normal fiscal channels are failing.

Hanji 783 gives the explicit salary-cut layer. The court reduces salaries for officials by rank, and the host emphasizes the tradeoff: the state saves money quickly, but underpaid officials still hold administrative power over people who cannot easily refuse demands. The concept therefore sits between fiscal relief and corruption theory; the harm is not automatic, but the incentive gradient changes.

Hanji 650 adds an earlier [[WangMang|王莽]] case where the problem is not a simple uniform cut but overcomplex variable salary design under fiscal stress. Wang Mang first limits pay to small cloth allotments, then creates a fifteen-rank system tied to harvests, disasters, and palace supply conditions. The host's warning is the same incentive mechanism: if calculation complexity means officials still cannot receive pay, some officials convert office power into bribery and abuse.

The positive Western Han version appears under [[EmperorXuanOfHan|汉宣帝]] in [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-444-xihan-chehui-le-heqin-gongzhu-xiongnu-lqvcsgep3fsjtru13s694vqkr08r|Hanji 444]]. Xuan's edict says low-ranking officials have heavy duties and low pay, making it hard to prevent extortion and bribery; he therefore raises salaries for officials below the hundred-shi level. The episode's "high salary to nourish integrity" analogy is useful but qualified: higher pay can reduce temptation and stabilize grassroots administration, but it cannot by itself eliminate land concentration, office markets, hidden rules, rent seeking, or human desire.

## Key Claims

- Salary reduction is a fiscal tool, not just a virtue signal, when war, famine, and disaster exhaust normal revenue.
- The risk arises because officials keep coercive and administrative authority even when formal compensation falls.
- Pay pressure can shift public fiscal stress downward into private fees, bribery, requisitions, or favoritism.
- Rank sale and salary cuts belong to the same emergency extraction toolkit when ordinary revenue fails.
- Overcomplex variable pay can create the same extraction risk as direct salary reduction if officials remain unpaid in practice.
- Raising low-level salaries can be an anti-extraction policy when the court recognizes pay insufficiency as an incentive problem.
- The concept qualifies austerity: visible court thrift can be real while still creating harmful implementation incentives.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The current sources do not prove a direct measured rise in bribery after salary cuts or variable-pay arrears, or a measured fall in corruption after salary increases. The claim is incentive-based: lower or unreliable pay under unchanged authority makes extraction more likely and more tempting, especially when local society is already strained by war, famine, transport demands, disaster, and tax pressure. Hanji 444's "high salary to nourish integrity" and Hanji 650's "performance pay" labels are modern analogies, not proof of modern compensation systems.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 444 as the positive Western Han repair case: Xuan raises low-level pay because low salaries make extraction harder to prevent.
- Preserved Hanji 650 as an earlier Wang Mang case where complex variable salary design produces the same unpaid-official extraction risk.
- Preserved Hanji 782 and Hanji 783 as the Eastern Han fiscal-prelude and salary-cut layers.
