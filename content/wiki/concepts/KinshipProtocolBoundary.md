## Definition

亲情名分边界 / kinship protocol boundary is the court-governance principle that royal affection remains politically safe only when it stays inside public hierarchy, ritual form, fiscal limits, office boundaries, guest-network restrictions, and territorial distance.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 728 adds the earliest Guangwu-era distance-and-guest layer. When [[LiuXiu|Liu Xiu]] asks how to preserve the imperial enterprise, [[ZhaoXiEasternHan|赵熙]] advises that princes should return to their fiefs. The episode later shows why distance matters: princes in the capital compete to gather guests, [[LiuLiShouguangMarquisEasternHan|刘李]] uses guests to kill Liu Gong while favored by [[LiuFuPeiKingEasternHan|沛王刘辅]], Liu Fu is briefly imprisoned, thousands are killed in the national guest purge, and adult princes return to their fiefs after the disaster. Royal favor is therefore not safe merely because it is familial; it needs residence and guest boundaries.

Hanji 736 adds the early positive Mingdi version through [[LiuCangDongpingKing|Liu Cang]]. Liu Cang is trusted by [[EmperorMingOfHan|Mingdi]] and assists central government, but he reads sustained proximity to imperial power as unsafe for a royal kinsman. His repeated request to return the piaoji general's seal and go back to his fief turns withdrawal into a way to preserve both service and trust; Mingdi lets him return to Dongping while retaining his honor through title, staff transfer, and gifts.

Hanji 758 supplies the later excess version through [[EmperorZhangOfHan|Emperor Zhang of Han]]'s late-reign treatment of royal kin. The episode does not condemn kinship affection or generosity as such. It argues that affection becomes politically dangerous when relatives in the capital receive treatment that weakens the visible distinction between family intimacy and the emperor-subject order.

The source gives two complementary objections. [[HeChangEasternHan|He Chang]] makes the fiscal objection: disasters, Qiang pressure, exhausted local finance, and depleted reserves mean that lavish rewards and extended royal stays consume public capacity at the wrong time. [[SongYiEasternHan|Song Yi]] makes the protocol objection: uncles and brothers may be kin, but in the court setting they are still subjects, so palace entry, seating, bowing, food, gifts, residence, and fief return have to preserve rank.

The concept therefore marks a boundary between private emotion and public form. A ruler can remain humane and loyal while still needing rules that tell kin when to approach, when to withdraw, who may gather guests, and when their status must be treated as official rather than familial.

## Key Claims

- Royal kinship does not cancel sovereign-subject hierarchy inside the court.
- Adult princes require territorial distance when capital residence and guest recruitment create factional or violent capacity.
- Affection becomes safer when office, residence, access, and guest boundaries are reset before suspicion forms.
- Affection becomes a governance risk when it changes access, seating, meals, gifts, residence, fief boundaries, or private retainer networks.
- Fiscal circumstance is part of protocol: generosity is harder to justify when disasters, war, or public exhaustion define the moment.
- Ritual form is not empty ceremony; it helps keep private intimacy from becoming public privilege.
- The concept is a proportion test, not an anti-family rule.

- Voluntary royal withdrawal: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-736-shengzai-huangquan-xia-ta-pingshenme-neng-andu-wannian-lisegz1ia5najewghxaqirkafvup|Hanji 736]] says Liu Cang grows uneasy after assisting central government and repeatedly asks to return his piaoji general seal and leave for Dongping.
- Honor-preserving boundary: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-736-shengzai-huangquan-xia-ta-pingshenme-neng-andu-wannian-lisegz1ia5najewghxaqirkafvup|Hanji 736]] says Mingdi permits Liu Cang's return to his fief but keeps the seal and sash honor in place, transfers staff into Dongping posts, and gives gifts.
- Royal-kin privilege and fiscal objection: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-758-suoyou-gaoqingshang-beihou-doushi-fencungan-lr-6tzffmwdtzidkf3y8ule35qf7|Hanji 758]] says Zhangdi gives visiting royal relatives unusually high treatment, while He Chang argues that disasters, Qiang harm, public-private exhaustion, and depleted reserves make excessive gifts improper.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

This concept should not be flattened into hostility toward relatives or court ritualism for its own sake. Hanji 728 includes exceptional honor for [[LiuQiangDonghaiKing|Liu Qiang]] before the fief-return order, and Hanji 736 shows affection preserved through honor, staffing, and gifts after Liu Cang accepts distance from central power. Hanji 758 praises Zhangdi's humane temperament and simple deathbed order, and it presents kinship feeling as good before it becomes excessive. The concept applies when affection alters public resources, capital residence, office proximity, guest networks, or visible hierarchy enough that actors have to restore distance.

## What Changed

- Added Hanji 728 as the earliest Guangwu-era fief-return and princely-guest disaster layer.
- Reframed Liu Cang's Hanji 736 withdrawal and Hanji 758's remonstrances as later variations on the same kinship-boundary problem.
- Expanded the definition to include guest-network restrictions alongside office, ritual, fiscal, and territorial boundaries.
