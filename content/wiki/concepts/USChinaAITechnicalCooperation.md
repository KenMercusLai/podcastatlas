## Definition
U.S.-China AI technical cooperation is narrow, issue-specific work by experts, researchers, engineers, or firms to reduce shared AI and digital-system risks despite wider strategic rivalry between the two countries.

## Current Synthesis
The bounded source treats practical technical cooperation as more plausible than a grand interstate bargain. Both countries can recognize cross-border risks while still fearing that broad restraint would advantage the other. Track-two dialogue, technical standards work, and coordinated vulnerability remediation offer smaller cooperation units whose success does not require agreement on political systems, censorship, industrial policy, or the desired pace of frontier development.

## Key Claims
- Shared exposure to uncontrolled agents, labor disruption, youth harm, and system vulnerabilities creates a real common-interest layer.
- Strategic competition makes broad reciprocal restraint difficult even when both governments recognize the same risks.
- Informal academic and industry dialogue can preserve communication when official relations are strained.
- Engineers and firms can sometimes solve bounded security problems without first resolving diplomatic conflict.
- High-level political endorsement can help protect expert channels without pretending that they amount to a comprehensive AI treaty.

## Evidence
- Rivalry and shared risk: [[tech-20260924-0924-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260924-0924-mp-tech-pod-128]] pairs common AI vulnerabilities with fear of surrendering strategic advantage.
- Track-two pathway: [[tech-20260924-0924-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260924-0924-mp-tech-pod-128]] says academics and industry practitioners already discuss shared concerns and policy outside formal negotiations.
- Private remediation: [[tech-20260924-0924-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20260924-0924-mp-tech-pod-128]] describes a U.S. security company and [[Tencent]] coordinating to patch a major [[WeChat]] vulnerability affecting devices across borders.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Technical channels can be disrupted by export controls, sanctions, espionage fears, censorship, or broader diplomatic deterioration. The WeChat case is an analogy from cybersecurity rather than proof that frontier-AI safety cooperation will scale. The source supplies no verification regime, enforcement mechanism, or evidence that expert recommendations will change national policy.
