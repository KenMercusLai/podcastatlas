# Racial Triangulation / 种族三角关系

## Definition

Racial triangulation is a relational ordering in which Asian Americans can be relatively valorized against Black Americans through success narratives while simultaneously excluded from full national belonging as foreign, culturally separate, or politically marginal.

## Current Synthesis

The episode presents racial position as more than a white/non-white line. The [[ModelMinorityStereotype]] can elevate selected Asian outcomes as supposed proof that opportunity is already equal, converting histories of slavery, segregation, migration selection, and unequal institutions into a moral comparison with Black Americans. Yet the same Asian populations can be treated as newcomers or permanent outsiders. Relative praise therefore does not equal equal power or secure belonging; it can help preserve a hierarchy while making coalition harder.

## Key Claims

- Relative praise and civic exclusion can operate on the same group at the same time.
- Aggregate Asian success claims can be mobilized to deny structural explanations for Black inequality.
- Immigration selection and migration timing make simple outcome comparisons historically misleading.
- Treating Asian Americans as a single successful bloc hides subgroup, class, and generational differences.
- Triangulation can intensify mistrust between minorities while leaving the dominant institutional order less visible.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept does not assign one fixed position to every Asian or Black community. National origin, immigration channel, class, neighborhood, generation, gender, and political context alter the relationship. The source offers a conceptual podcast discussion rather than comparative demographic or causal evidence, and relative advantage in one domain does not establish immunity from discrimination in another.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to separate relative valorization from equal citizenship or institutional power.
- Connected immigration selection and historical timing to the limits of cross-group outcome comparison.
