# Rebel Royalism

## Definition

Rebel royalism is the political pattern in which insurgents affirm a monarch’s legitimacy while attacking ministers, magnates, landlords, officials, or institutions said to obstruct the just relationship between king and subject.

## Current Synthesis

The 1381 rebels did not generally frame themselves as enemies of kingship. They sent assurances that [[RichardIIOfEngland|Richard II]] remained their king and sought direct dependence on him rather than servitude under magnates or abbeys. Their demand for “traitors” in the Tower and faith in royal concessions allowed attacks on government personnel to coexist with loyalty to the crown.

This structure gave Richard unusual room to act. At Mile End he could promise sweeping reform as an anointed mediator above the hated intermediaries. At Smithfield, after [[WatTyler|Wat Tyler]] fell, he could tell the armed crowd that he would be their captain and lead them away. The revolt thus demonstrated both the strength and danger of personal monarchy: royal legitimacy could defuse rebellion even while institutions later revoked the promises made in the king’s name.

## Key Claims

- Loyalty to a ruler can coexist with violent rejection of the ruler’s agents and governing machinery.
- Direct dependence on the crown may appear emancipatory when intermediate lordship is experienced as servitude.
- Naming ministers as traitors preserves an idealized king by assigning injustice to corrupt mediation.
- Anointed charisma can create crisis-management capacity that institutional force alone does not possess.
- Royalist rebellion remains politically unstable because the crown can absorb the movement’s loyalty and later reverse its concessions.

## Evidence

- Allegiance and demands: [[414-the-peasants-revolt-londons-burning-part-2-glt6726049231]] records rebel assurances to Richard alongside demands for freedom and the surrender of Tower “traitors.”
- Direct kingship: [[414-the-peasants-revolt-londons-burning-part-2-glt6726049231]] explains the preference for royal dependence over subjection to magnates and abbeys.
- Crisis performance: [[414-the-peasants-revolt-londons-burning-part-2-glt6726049231]] traces Richard’s concessions at Mile End and assumption of leadership after Tyler’s fall.
- Political limit: [[414-the-peasants-revolt-londons-burning-part-2-glt6726049231]] records later revocation, punishment, and failure of the rebels’ institutional demands.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Royalist language did not make the movement peaceful, unified, or politically conservative. Rebels killed royal officers, violated sanctuary, and pressed demands that would have transformed social and ecclesiastical order. The episode does not establish that every participant understood kingship alike or that Richard sincerely accepted the role assigned to him.

## What Changed

- Added 1381 as a case where royal legitimacy enabled both rebellion against intermediaries and the eventual containment of that rebellion.
