# Academic Freedom

Academic freedom is the ability of faculty and students to teach, study, discuss, and research contested material without coercive political or administrative control. [[snap-judgement-japan-pms-electoral-landslide-6989c0fc61be18927058bb7d]] adds a public-university case through [[TexasAMUniversity]] and [[MartinPeterson]], where Plato's *Symposium* was removed from a philosophy syllabus because it discusses gender and sexuality.

The source presents academic freedom as vulnerable not only to individual classroom disputes, but also to state law, administrative risk management, course reviews, speaker rules, and preemptive self-censorship. Its important nuance is that the campus-speech fight is not static: the episode says earlier censorship pressure often came from the left, while recent cases increasingly come from the right through legislation and university governance.

[[19-huangye-shang-de-dashi-wo-bei-yu-luanshi-qiu-yanjiu-ben-wei-niliu-zhi-wangju-565412437]] adds a modern Chinese scholarly-institution version. [[ChenYinke|陈寅恪]]'s defense of the [[TsinghuaGuoxueInstitute|清华国学研究院]] and his memorial language around [[WangGuowei|王国维]] make academic freedom a condition for keeping a real research desk, not only for managing classroom controversy.

## Key Claims
- Canonical material can become restricted when policy rules define topics rather than educational purpose.
- Faculty can self-censor before formal punishment if course review, reassignment, or firing risks are visible.
- Academic freedom depends on institutional governance, not only on individual speech rights.
- Public-university status creates a recurring tension between state accountability and independent scholarly judgment.
- Academic freedom can also mean protecting a fragile scholarly institution from internal politics, reputational attack, and administrative capture.
