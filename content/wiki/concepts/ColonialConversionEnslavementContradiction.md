# Colonial Conversion-Enslavement Contradiction

## Definition

The colonial conversion-enslavement contradiction is the conflict between treating Indigenous people as royal subjects and potential Christians entitled to protection while simultaneously capturing, exploiting, transporting, and coercing them for colonial return.

## Current Synthesis

[[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] places this contradiction inside Spanish policy rather than between a uniformly religious Europe and a later secular critique. [[IsabellaIOfCastile]] instructed [[ChristopherColumbus]] to treat Indigenous people well and understood them as subjects capable of conversion. Columbus and other settlers nevertheless kidnapped people, seized women, imposed tribute, compelled labor, and shipped captives to Castile.

The tension had institutional consequences. Catholic legal and theological claims about human rights, clerical reports, and royal concern helped make enslavement contestable, but conversionary subjecthood still accompanied territorial conquest and paternal rule. The concept therefore describes a live constraint and a structural hypocrisy, not a clean humanitarian alternative to empire.

## Key Claims

- Subjecthood and capacity for Christian conversion supplied a period-specific argument against enslavement.
- Conversion language could protect Indigenous people while also legitimizing conquest and paternal authority.
- Colonial scarcity and return-on-investment pressure pushed practice toward captivity and labor extraction.
- Religious and royal criticism was historically real but unevenly enforced.
- The contradiction weakens claims that severe moral criticism is purely anachronistic.

## Evidence

- Royal instruction: [[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] reports Isabella's direction to treat Indigenous people well and lovingly and to prioritize conversion.
- Colonial practice: [[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] documents kidnapping, rape, mass captivity, slave shipment, tribute, and compelled labor.
- Period criticism: [[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] describes Catholic legal-theological arguments, Isabella's alarm, clerical reporting, and [[BartolomeDeLasCasas|Las Casas's]] later opposition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Royal and theological objections did not reject territorial claim, settlement, extraction, or conversion as such, and protective language could coexist with coercion. The source does not establish a single Catholic position or the exact legal status of every captive. Recognizing internal criticism does not imply that it reliably protected Taíno communities.

## What Changed

- Established conversion and enslavement as competing claims within the same colonial-religious order.
- Connected moral argument to royal oversight without treating oversight as decolonization.
