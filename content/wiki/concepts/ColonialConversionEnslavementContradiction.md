# Colonial Conversion-Enslavement Contradiction

## Definition

The colonial conversion-enslavement contradiction is the conflict between treating Indigenous people as subjects and potential Christians entitled to protection while simultaneously capturing, exploiting, transporting, and coercing them for colonial return.

## Current Synthesis

[[307-columbus-a-new-world-part-2-glt7219741357]] shows the contradiction before Spain developed a stable colonial administration. No priest sailed on the first voyage, and gold, spices, finance, and proof of return dominate its practical design. Yet Columbus used Christian providential language, described Taíno people as prospective subjects, seized captives as interpreters, and returned survivors to Spain for baptism under royal sponsorship. Conversion, possession, and captivity were therefore joined from the first encounter.

[[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] turns that unstable mixture into an explicit policy conflict. [[IsabellaIOfCastile]] instructed [[ChristopherColumbus]] to treat Indigenous people well as subjects capable of conversion, while Columbus and other settlers kidnapped people, seized women, imposed tribute, compelled labor, and shipped captives to Castile. Catholic legal and theological claims, clerical reports, and royal concern made enslavement contestable without ending territorial appropriation or paternal rule.

## Key Claims

- Christian rhetoric and captive-taking coexisted before clergy or a settled conversion program accompanied the voyage.
- Baptism could be presented as a gift while occurring within forced transport and profound power asymmetry.
- Subjecthood and capacity for conversion supplied a period-specific argument against enslavement.
- Conversion language could protect Indigenous people while also legitimizing conquest and paternal authority.
- Return-on-investment pressure pushed practice toward captivity and labor extraction.
- Religious and royal criticism was historically real but unevenly enforced.

## Evidence

- First-voyage contradiction: [[307-columbus-a-new-world-part-2-glt7219741357]] contrasts the absence of priests with providential language, captive-taking, prospective subjecthood, forced transport, and royal baptism.
- Royal instruction: [[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] reports Isabella's direction to treat Indigenous people well and lovingly and to prioritize conversion.
- Colonial practice: both sources document kidnapping; [[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] extends the record through rape, mass captivity, slave shipment, tribute, and compelled labor.
- Period criticism: [[308-columbus-death-in-the-caribbean-part-3-glt3250512968]] describes Catholic legal-theological arguments, Isabella's alarm, clerical reporting, and [[BartolomeDeLasCasas|Las Casas's]] later opposition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The absence of clergy does not prove that religious motives were insincere, and royal baptism does not reveal captive experience directly. Royal and theological objections did not reject territorial claim, settlement, extraction, or conversion as such. The sources do not establish a single Catholic position or the exact legal status of every captive; recognizing internal criticism does not imply reliable protection.

## What Changed

- Extended the contradiction to the first voyage, when captive transport and baptism preceded a stable colonial policy.
- Distinguished sincere religious language from the expedition's clergy-free and commercially pressured design without reducing either to a disguise.
