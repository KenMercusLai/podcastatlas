# Household Misconduct Political Liability / 家门失范政治连带

## Definition

Household Misconduct Political Liability is the pattern where a public official's domestic failure becomes actionable political evidence against his office, especially when household governance, moral reputation, and opportunistic impeachment channels converge.

## Current Synthesis

The concept is currently grounded in the 133 CE [[PangCanLateHan|Pang Can]] case from [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-815-kebei-gudai-zhongchen-de-jiafeng-zhishang-llkhbxo-63pjw-gk9pihnbfr94v|Hanji 815]]. Pang is a highly reputed taiwei defended by [[DuanGongchengLateHan|Duan Gongcheng]] as loyal and publicly praised, yet his current wife's killing of a son from his former wife gives [[ZhuLiangLuoyangLateHan|Zhu Liang]] a concrete impeachment path. The household event does not merely embarrass Pang; it changes the evidentiary terrain on which [[EmperorShunOfHan|Emperor Shun]] can dismiss him.

The pattern matters because it shows a limit of [[MoralReputationPoliticalCapital|moral reputation political capital]]. Reputation can draw defense and imperial sympathy, but a domestic scandal can become easier to act on than a general claim of loyalty. In Shun's court, the same environment that permits slander also rewards a tangible scandal that converts private disorder into public accountability.

## Key Claims

- Elite household governance can become part of public-office legitimacy when family misconduct signals failure of order close to the official.
- Domestic scandal is politically powerful because it supplies concrete, narratable evidence to enemies or inspectors.
- Moral reputation can slow or complicate attack, but it does not neutralize a household event that can be framed as legal and moral failure.
- The pattern is especially dangerous in courts already prone to slander because domestic facts give accusation a less abstract handle.
- Pang Can's case shows household failure interacting with disaster-and-epidemic accountability language rather than replacing it entirely.

## Evidence

- Pang Can case: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-815-kebei-gudai-zhongchen-de-jiafeng-zhishang-llkhbxo-63pjw-gk9pihnbfr94v|Hanji 815]] says Pang's current wife kills the son of his former wife, Zhu Liang impeaches Pang, and Pang is dismissed after previously receiving imperial concern.
- Reputation limit: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-815-kebei-gudai-zhongchen-de-jiafeng-zhishang-llkhbxo-63pjw-gk9pihnbfr94v|Hanji 815]] has Duan Gongcheng defend Pang's loyalty and popular reputation, but that defense does not survive the later household scandal.
- Court context: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-815-kebei-gudai-zhongchen-de-jiafeng-zhishang-llkhbxo-63pjw-gk9pihnbfr94v|Hanji 815]] places Pang under repeated slander and impeachment pressure before the household event supplies a sharper attack route.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The current evidence base is one episode, so the concept should not yet be generalized beyond similar court-household cases. Hanji 815 also cites disaster and epidemic conditions around Pang's dismissal, so household misconduct is best read as an enabling liability inside a wider accountability frame, not as the sole causal explanation.

## What Changed

- Created the concept from Pang Can's dismissal sequence in Hanji 815.
- Separated household-liability analysis from generic slander because the decisive leverage comes from domestic misconduct, not fabricated accusation.
- Linked the concept to moral reputation as a partial but insufficient defense.
