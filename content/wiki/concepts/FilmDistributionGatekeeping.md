## Definition

Film distribution gatekeeping is the concentrated control exercised by distributors, streaming platforms, acquisition executives, internal approvers, and infrastructure owners over which films receive financing for release and access to broad audiences.

## Current Synthesis

The evidence now identifies two related gates. In independent-film acquisition, a completed and festival-selected film can still depend on a small buyer pool constrained by budgets, competing acquisitions, release strategy, and corporate approval. Sales agents can improve access and evidence, but they do not remove that gate.

A second, more qualified gate appears when technology companies participate in production or control important distribution routes: films scrutinizing technology leaders may depend on infrastructure connected to their subject. Reported distribution questions around proposed films about [[SamAltman]] and [[ElonMusk]] do not demonstrate that a platform suppressed either project. The broader synthesis is therefore about structural exposure and potential conflict, not proven censorship.

## Key Claims

- Production completion and festival selection do not guarantee public availability.
- A small viable buyer pool concentrates attention and bargaining power.
- Internal corporate approval can remain decisive after an acquisition executive shows interest.
- Competing films consume limited budgets and organizational attention.
- Streaming competition can widen seller leverage, while mergers and spending cuts can narrow it.
- Gatekeeping shapes cultural visibility without proving that selected films are artistically superior.
- Platform control can create a subject-distribution conflict when critical work depends on routes influenced by the industry it examines.

- Approval chain: [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-20c3269bc63dde599e9e1898d7da833b-33908266-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-20c3269bc63dde599e9e1898d7da833b-33908266|Anatomy of a movie market]] describes a private screening for the executive whose approval was needed before Apple's acquisition.
- Budget competition: [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-20c3269bc63dde599e9e1898d7da833b-33908266-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-20c3269bc63dde599e9e1898d7da833b-33908266|Anatomy of a movie market]] says bidding on one film can absorb buyers' budgets and attention.
- Structural change: [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-20c3269bc63dde599e9e1898d7da833b-33908266-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-20c3269bc63dde599e9e1898d7da833b-33908266|Anatomy of a movie market]] connects the softer market to post-COVID theatrical weakness, mergers, and reduced spending.
- Subject-distribution conflict: [[tech-20261123-1123-mp-tech-pod-128-tech-20261123-1123-mp-tech-pod-128|Hollywood brings Big Tech's drama to the big screen]] reports [[JoeStreet]]'s concern that technology companies' production and distribution influence can shape whether films critical of technology leaders reach audiences.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The approximate buyer count applies to the documentary segment and commercial level described by Cinetic, not to all films or distribution routes. Direct digital release, niche platforms, self-distribution, community exhibition, and later catalogue acquisition can provide alternatives, though the sources do not evaluate their scale or economics.

The technology-film episode identifies reported distribution difficulty and a troubling structural possibility, not a verified act of suppression, a complete ownership map, or an industry-wide causal estimate. Commercial uncertainty, legal risk, project readiness, audience expectations, and ordinary acquisition strategy remain alternative explanations for any particular film's path.

## What Changed

- Extended the model from buyer concentration and internal approval to potential conflicts between critical subject matter and technology-controlled distribution.
- Preserved the distinction between structural exposure and evidence of actual suppression.
