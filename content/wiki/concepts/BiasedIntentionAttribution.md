# Biased Intention Attribution

## Definition
Biased intention attribution is the asymmetric inference of motives from ambiguous behavior: observers give themselves or an in-group situational and protective explanations while interpreting comparable out-group action as hatred, hostility, or attack.

## Current Synthesis
Intentions are invisible, so people infer them from behavior, context, bodily cues, and prior stories. That makes social coordination possible but also leaves gaps that identity, emotion, political messaging, and group loyalty can fill. The episode's Democratic-Republican and Israeli-Palestinian examples describe a recurring asymmetry in which one's own group's aggression is framed as protective love while an opponent's is framed as hostile hatred.

A practical correction is to treat motive as a hypothesis. Pausing to consider fatigue, stress, misunderstanding, or other situational pressure can prevent an ambiguous slight from becoming proof of malice. This is not a universal demand to assume innocence: direct evidence, repeated patterns, severe stakes, and credible danger still constrain charitable interpretation.

## Key Claims
- Observable conduct underdetermines motive, especially when signals are indirect or ambiguous.
- People have better access to situational explanations for their own behavior than for another person's behavior.
- Group loyalty encourages protective explanations for allies and hostile explanations for opponents.
- Political narratives can exploit invisible motives by supplying a malevolent interpretation without proving it.
- Charitable alternatives can reduce avoidable conflict when retained as revisable hypotheses.

- Correction - [[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-e107f1ec59fe4e6311a12befafe07923-88839568-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-e107f1ec59fe4e6311a12befafe07923-88839568]] recommends pausing to ask what a person is and is not trying to do and to consider situational pressure.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
Not every hostile inference is biased, and not every unpleasant act is innocent. The source supplies no methods or effect sizes for the group studies and does not establish equal symmetry across conflicts. In policing, estrangement, abuse, or other high-stakes settings, uncertainty about intent can coexist with a justified safety response based on observable risk. Charitable interpretation should widen hypotheses, not erase evidence.

## What Changed
- Established motive attribution as a group-sensitive and correctable inference problem.
