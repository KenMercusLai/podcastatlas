# Victory Overreach Failure / 数胜必亡式胜利反噬

Victory overreach failure / 数胜必亡式胜利反噬 is the ruler-failure pattern [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-69-bikan-gudai-diwang-qipa-sifa-da-pandian-lukvjsktg-pgfjhizvypwmdmw-13]] draws from [[QiMinWang|齐湣王]] after the destruction of [[SongState|宋国]]. The episode quotes the older warning associated with [[LiKe|李克]] and [[WeiWenhou|魏文侯]]: repeated war exhausts the people, repeated victory makes the ruler arrogant, and the combination can destroy the state.

The source makes Qi Min Wang the active case. His victory over Song gives Qi extraordinary prestige and territory, but he treats that moment as permission to attack [[ChuState|楚国]], pressure the Three Jin, swallow East and West Zhou, and imagine himself as Son of Heaven. The strategic result is isolation: Qin attacks Qi, and Yan finds other states ready to join revenge because Qi has become intolerable.

[[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-70-xihuan-huayang-zuosi-de-qiguo-junzhu-luuyasp5-nhnikdcozzaueosb-md]] shows the terminal phase of the same pattern. [[LeYi|乐毅]] defeats Qi in coalition, takes [[Linzi|临淄]], and then pursues because he believes Qi Min Wang's internal unpopularity has made the state brittle. Qi Min Wang's failed refuge in Wey, Zou, and Lu, followed by [[ZhuoChi|卓齿]]'s seizure and killing of him, makes overreach end as personal exposure as well as state defeat.

This is related to [[PostVictoryOppressionRisk|post-victory oppression risk]], but it is more statecraft-specific. The problem is not only that a victorious side mistreats others; it is that victory changes the ruler's risk perception and destroys the feedback, popular support, and alliance environment that made survival possible.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-142-zhechang-juhui-xiangyu-shudiao-le-yiqie-ln7dv2jxrk6lt1c4szylsrb41k3r|Hanji 142]] adds a Chu-Han case through [[XiangYu|项羽]] after [[HongmenYan|鸿门宴]]. Xiang Yu has the force to enter [[Xianyang|咸阳]] and dominate the post-Qin coalition, but victory turns into plunder, capital burning, the killing of [[ZiyingQin|子婴]], rejection of [[HanSheng|韩生]]'s [[GuanzhongRegion|关中]] base advice, and lethal anger at criticism. The overreach is not a long series of later wars here; it is the immediate conversion of victory into lost statecraft.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-151-huanyuan-pengcheng-zhizhan-xiangyu-zai-fengshen-1-lspx-bqe5kczq6bmn6thlerfbrmu|Hanji 151 part 1]] adds a Liu Bang-side version after the capture of [[Xuzhou|彭城]]. [[LiuBang|刘邦]] has just used [[DongGongXincheng|Dong Gong]]'s mourning logic, coalition bargaining, and [[XiangYu|项羽]]'s Qi distraction to seize the Chu capital, but the army immediately relaxes into treasure, women, wine, and feasts. The source marks the danger by saying even [[ZhangLiang|张良]] and [[ChenPing|陈平]] are silent, so the overreach belongs to command atmosphere and discipline, not only one ruler's appetite.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-151-huanyuan-pengcheng-zhizhan-xiangyu-zai-fengshen-2-lqy1uu29xbfipo9e0kq6obkl3oay|Hanji 151 part 2]] gives the tactical punishment for that relaxation. Xiang Yu secretly returns, seizes [[XiaoCounty|萧县]], cuts Liu Bang's retreat, and attacks the coalition command center from the unexpected west. Here overreach does not mature into years of bad policy; it becomes an immediate readiness failure that lets [[CavalryShockCommandStrike|骑兵奇袭指挥中枢]] turn a captured capital into a catastrophic rout.

[[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-162-2-liubang-ruhe-shoushi-wei-gao-quan-zhong-de-xiashu-lqvye2zmqhu3otk0uwu7lakf2prz|Hanji 162-2]] adds a different Chu-Han angle: repeated victory can still create strategic exhaustion when the commander must personally answer every front. [[XiangYu|项羽]] defeats [[PengYue|彭越]] and retakes Xingyang/[[Chenggao|成皋]], but the episode reads his movement between Chenggao, [[Xiapi|下邳]], and Xingyang as the cost Liu Bang's multi-front pressure imposes. The failure is not post-victory indulgence here; it is victory achieved at a tempo that leaves the winning side tired and reactive.

## Key Claims
- Victory can be destabilizing when it inflates ruler ambition faster than state capacity.
- Repeated military success can hide exhaustion among subjects and officials.
- Overreach after victory can turn former victims, rivals, and anxious neighbors into a common-enemy coalition.
- Killing remonstrants converts strategic overconfidence into [[CourtFeedbackCollapse|court feedback collapse]].
- The strongest moment in a ruler's reputation can become the opening of decline if it removes restraint.
- Once overreach destroys social and diplomatic protection, military defeat can become a refuge failure and bodily vulnerability for the ruler.
- Hanji 142 adds that even one decisive victory can create overreach if the commander treats the conquered center as spoil rather than as a base for governable order.
- Hanji 151 part 1 adds that victory overreach can follow successful legitimacy work: a coalition may win the capital and still lose vigilance before the enemy's operational response.
- Hanji 151 part 2 adds the payoff: post-capture overreach can be punished within a single battle if an opponent preserves surprise, cuts retreat, and strikes the command center before the victorious side regains discipline.
- Hanji 162-2 adds that victories can be strategically expensive even when they are real: if every local win requires the same commander to rush across fronts, visible success may mask exhaustion and loss of initiative.
