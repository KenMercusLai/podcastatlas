## Definition

Outer-relative command appointment risk is the court-governance problem created when imperial in-laws, consort-family members, or maternal relatives receive heavy offices or military commands. The risk is not only that relatives may perform badly; it is that selection quality, punishment, favoritism, subordinate reporting, public law, and reward legitimacy become entangled with kinship or intimate favor.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 372-2 supplies an earlier Western Han selection-stage case. Han Wudi wants to ennoble [[LadyLiWesternHan|李夫人]]'s brother [[LiGuangliWesternHan|李广利]], but the episode says a military-merit norm makes direct reward publicly difficult. Giving Li the Dayuan command creates a route to merit, yet [[SimaGuang|司马光]]'s criticism is that preserving the appearance of the rule through an untested favorite commander puts soldiers and the state at risk. Here the structural danger begins before accountability: private favor can distort who receives command and can use war itself as a reward-legitimation device.

Hanji 749 makes the later Eastern Han branch through Fifth Lun's warning about Ma Fang. Fifth Lun does not say relatives can receive no honor or wealth. His narrower argument is that heavy office creates an accountability dilemma: if the relative fails, punishment damages kinship; if the ruler protects the relative, law and public standards lose credibility.

The campaign result complicates but does not erase the warning. Ma Fang defeats Buqiao and helps end the revolt, so the source does not present the appointment as operationally incompetent. Yet Geng Gong's later removal after offending Ma Fang shows a second-order problem: once a relative commander has power, subordinates and monitors may treat guessed preference as a political fact.

The concept therefore separates eventual merit from structural risk. A relative or favored consort-family member can succeed in the field and still enter through distorted selection or make the accountability environment less clean, especially when command is expected to generate the merit that legitimizes a private reward or when officials must evaluate someone whose punishment may damage the ruler's family ties.

## Key Claims

- The danger of heavy in-law appointment is a law-versus-kinship dilemma, not merely presumed incompetence.
- Granting wealth or title to relatives is structurally different from giving them military or administrative command.
- Command can be used prospectively to manufacture the merit needed to legitimize a favored relative's later title.
- Formal compliance with a merit rule does not protect the state if private favor determines who gets the chance to command.
- Field success can coexist with distorted accountability around subordinates and monitors.
- Geng Gong's removal after offending Ma Fang illustrates anticipated-preference risk without proving direct retaliation.
- The concept is a narrow appointment-risk branch within the broader field of outer-relative power.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Hanji 372-2 does not independently prove that Li Guangli lacked all military ability, that favorite-family advancement was the war's only cause, or that military merit was an exceptionless legal requirement. Its durable contribution is the selection-and-legitimation risk identified by the episode and attributed to Sima Guang. Hanji 749 does not show Ma Fang failing in battle; on the contrary, it reports major victories and surrender. It also does not prove that Ma Fang ordered Geng Gong's impeachment. The concept should therefore be kept structural: kinship or consort-family command can distort entry and accountability even when the appointee later succeeds.

## What Changed

- Added the earlier Li Guangli case, extending the concept from punishment and subordinate-accountability risk to selection-stage distortion.
- Added command-as-merit-manufacture: war can become the mechanism that makes a private reward appear publicly earned.
- Preserved both qualifications: Li Guangli's total unfitness is not independently established, and Ma Fang's actual victory blocks a simplistic anti-relative competence claim.
