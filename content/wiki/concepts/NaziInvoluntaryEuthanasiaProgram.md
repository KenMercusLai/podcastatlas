# Nazi Involuntary-Euthanasia Program

## Definition

The Nazi involuntary-euthanasia program was the state-directed killing of people labeled genetically diseased or unfit, extending eugenic reproductive control into lethal disability persecution under medical and administrative authority.

## Current Synthesis

The episode places the program on an ideological and institutional continuum. The July 1933 hereditary-health law authorized compulsory sterilization through courts staffed by a judge and doctors, presenting bodily coercion as scientific health policy rather than punishment. In 1939, [[AdolfHitler]] authorized killing people classified as genetically diseased and backdated the decision to the start of war.

Physicians, lawyers, civil servants, and local officials helped turn the racial-health premise into procedure. Christian protest, including Clemens von Galen's 1941 sermon, demonstrates that moral opposition persisted, but the professional participation described by the episode shows how false biological categories acquired lethal administrative force.

## Key Claims

- The program extended [[EugenicGovernance|eugenic governance]] from reproductive exclusion into killing.
- Medical, judicial, and bureaucratic form helped represent violence as health protection rather than crime.
- It targeted Germans whom the regime claimed belonged to the national community but classified as biologically burdensome.
- Professional expertise widened state capacity and did not guarantee moral or scientific restraint.
- Public and religious resistance remained possible, though it did not undo the wider persecution system.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The page follows one narrative podcast episode rather than a comprehensive institutional history. “Euthanasia” was the regime's euphemistic framing; the victims did not provide voluntary, informed consent. Numerical estimates, the exact effects of protest, specific trial outcomes, and individual professional motives remain source-scoped.
