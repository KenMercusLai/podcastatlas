# Quantum Computing Commercialization Gap

## Definition
The quantum computing commercialization gap is the distance between a quantum computer demonstrating specialized theoretical or experimental capability and a business delivering reliable, economical value on practical workloads.

## Current Synthesis
[[MartinShkreli|Martin Shkreli]]'s source rejects the idea that quantum computing is simply faster general-purpose computing. It distinguishes algorithms with known quantum advantage, such as factoring or discrete logarithms, from ordinary sorting, arithmetic, storage, and business workflows. Present systems may remain scientifically important while low effective speed, error management, limited memory architecture, narrow workload fit, and weak customer economics delay commercial value.

The concept therefore separates four questions: whether a quantum effect is real, whether a useful algorithm exists, whether hardware can run it at relevant scale and reliability, and whether a company can capture returns before capital or valuation outruns deployment. A negative answer today does not establish permanent impossibility, just as long-run possibility does not make today's equity a sound investment.

## Key Claims
- Quantum advantage is workload-specific rather than a general multiplier on every computation.
- Useful commercialization requires scale, reliability, memory and I/O, software, validation, cost, and a customer problem valuable enough to beat classical alternatives.
- Scientific funding can be rational before a mass-market business model exists.
- Company valuation and short-selling outcomes can diverge from technical fundamentals for long periods.
- Future cryptographic risk can justify migration planning even while present general-purpose commercial utility remains limited.

## Evidence
- Workload boundary - [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-martin-shkreli-big-pharmas-blind-spot-betting-against-quantum-what]] contrasts factoring and elliptic-curve problems with sorting and ordinary arithmetic.
- Hardware and business boundary - [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-martin-shkreli-big-pharmas-blind-spot-betting-against-quantum-what]] cites low operating rates, missing RAM-like capability, public-system limitations, and unclear applications.
- Investment-timing boundary - [[all-in-with-chamath-jason-sacks-friedberg-martin-shkreli-big-pharmas-blind-spot-betting-against-quantum-what]] reports that Shkreli lost millions shorting quantum companies despite maintaining technical skepticism.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode supplies no hardware benchmark, error-correction analysis, peer-reviewed survey, company comparison, or independent market data. Early systems can look commercially weak before enabling technologies and applications mature, and research value need not depend on near-term revenue. Narrow quantum uses can also matter greatly even without replacing classical computers. The source's failed short demonstrates timing risk, not that public valuations were correct or that the technical thesis was false.

## What Changed
- Created a commercialization framework separating specialized quantum capability from general usefulness and current investment merit.
