# Journalism-to-Policy Latency

## Definition

Journalism-to-policy latency is the delay between publication of a public argument and its uptake by an official, coalition, or institution capable of turning attention into a proposal, negotiation, or decision.

## Current Synthesis

The episode's 2017 argument appears politically inactive for years before a staff-mediated introduction brings it to [[AdrinNazarian]]. Its later influence does not show that journalism directly caused policy. It shows a pathway: memorable framing preserves an issue, an intermediary routes it to an officeholder, political authority converts it into a proposal, and public support plus organized resistance move it into bargaining.

Latency changes how impact should be judged. Immediate adoption is not the only meaningful outcome, but later resemblance is also insufficient evidence of influence. Credible attribution requires a traceable chain, while durable change still depends on legal authority, coalition building, evidence, and implementation.

## Key Claims

- A public argument can remain available for later use even when it produces no immediate institutional response.
- Intermediaries such as staff members can be decisive in routing media to decision-makers.
- Agenda influence is distinct from enactment, implementation, and durable outcome.
- A documented uptake chain supports influence more strongly than thematic similarity alone.

## Evidence

### Delayed uptake

- [[revenge-of-a-good-walk-spoiled-revenge-of-a-good-walk-spoiled]] reports that a staff member introduced Nazarian to Gladwell's earlier episode nearly a decade after its original release.

### Conversion into bargaining

- [[revenge-of-a-good-walk-spoiled-revenge-of-a-good-walk-spoiled]] follows the argument into a parcel-tax proposal, polling, club resistance, and possible compromise.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

One case cannot establish how often journalism changes policy or whether the proposal will pass, survive legal review, or deliver public benefit. The source is also the journalist's own follow-up and may emphasize his influence. Policy conditions, prior advocacy, staff research, public opinion, and institutional incentives may be equally or more important.

## What Changed

- Established a staged model separating publication, routing, agenda uptake, bargaining, enactment, and outcome.
- Added traceable intermediary evidence as a qualification for causal claims about media influence.
