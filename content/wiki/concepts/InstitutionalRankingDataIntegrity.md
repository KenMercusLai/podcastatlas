# Institutional Ranking Data Integrity

## Definition

Institutional ranking data integrity is the requirement that self-reported inputs to consequential rankings remain traceable to underlying records, stable definitions, explicit inclusion rules, and accountable correction processes.

## Current Synthesis

The Columbia case shows why a plausible field-level answer is not enough. Class size, faculty status, terminal degrees, student–faculty ratio, and instructional spending each depend on category boundaries, but those boundaries must reconcile with course catalogs, federal reports, faculty records, and audited financial statements. A ranking submission loses integrity when the institution cannot explain those reconciliations clearly.

The source also separates input integrity from estimated benefit and from intent. [[LaurenLavelle]]'s model suggests disputed inputs may have affected the score by different amounts, yet a small benefit does not make an unsupported figure acceptable. Conversely, a discrepancy and modeled benefit do not by themselves identify who prepared the number or prove coordinated deception. Accountability therefore requires both technical correction and a transparent account of scope, duration, cause, and control changes.

## Key Claims

- High-stakes self-reported metrics should reconcile with independent operational, regulatory, personnel, and financial records.
- Category definitions and inclusion rules are part of the evidence, not administrative details that can remain implicit.
- Multiple discrepancies across independently checkable inputs are stronger warning signals than one contested classification.
- The size of a discrepancy, its modeled ranking payoff, and the intent behind it are separate questions.
- A credible correction identifies which figures were wrong, for how long, why, and what controls changed.
- Ranking withdrawal can reduce future participation without resolving the historical record or responsibility for past submissions.

## Evidence

### Cross-record verification

- [[the-pushkin-prize-for-egregiously-deceptive-self-promotion-the-pushkin-prize-for-egregiously-deceptive-self-promotion]] reports that [[MichaelThaddeus]] compared Columbia's ranking inputs with roughly 30,000 class records, federal faculty reporting, faculty qualifications, ratios, and audited spending.

### Benefit is not integrity

- [[the-pushkin-prize-for-egregiously-deceptive-self-promotion-the-pushkin-prize-for-egregiously-deceptive-self-promotion]] says [[LaurenLavelle]] modeled an approximately eight-point combined benefit from three faculty-resource inputs but only a two- or three-point effect from changing instructional spending by roughly $1 billion.

### Correction quality

- [[the-pushkin-prize-for-egregiously-deceptive-self-promotion-the-pushkin-prize-for-egregiously-deceptive-self-promotion]] says Columbia announced corrections and changed collection methods, while Thaddeus disputed whether it had identified all false figures or their duration.
- [[the-pushkin-prize-for-egregiously-deceptive-self-promotion-the-pushkin-prize-for-egregiously-deceptive-self-promotion]] uses [[TempleUniversity]]'s external investigation, student compensation, and referral to authorities as stronger forms of remediation after an adjudicated case.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source is a structured podcast summary, not the underlying Columbia submissions, definitions, datasets, audited statements, ranking algorithm, Temple case record, or institutional reviews. Category differences can sometimes be legitimate, and a reconstructed ranking model may not reproduce the official system exactly. The case supports verification and disclosure standards without proving who acted, whether every discrepancy was intentional, or which remediation model is appropriate in every institution.

## What Changed

- Created a higher-education data-integrity standard that separates traceability, score effect, intent, and post-discovery accountability.
