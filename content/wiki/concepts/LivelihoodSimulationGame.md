# Livelihood Simulation Game

## Definition

A livelihood simulation game is a rule-based model that turns the income, costs, assets, uncertainty, and failure conditions of an occupation into repeatable player decisions and consequences.

## Current Synthesis

[[TheFarmingGame]] makes agricultural livelihood legible through acreage, crops, debt, credit, harvests, operating expenses, misfortune, bankruptcy, and the goal of leaving an off-farm job. [[board-game-season-board-game-season]] says its designers drew adverse events from lived experience and repeatedly adjusted the economy so the model remained difficult but not futile.

The simulation has two simultaneous functions. It teaches selected economic pressures by making players absorb consequences, and it gives practitioners a comic distance from risks they already know. Its explanatory power remains designed: the rules determine which farms, labor, policies, environmental effects, household roles, and definitions of success become visible.

## Key Claims

- Repeated decisions and consequences can make livelihood economics more tangible than description alone.
- Starting assets, credit limits, random events, and victory conditions encode a theory of how the occupation works.
- Difficulty must remain credible to practitioners while leaving enough agency for play to feel meaningful.
- Lived experience can improve relevance without making the model complete or representative.
- A simulation can serve education, entertainment, stress discussion, and product storytelling at the same time.

## Evidence

### Economic translation

- [[board-game-season-board-game-season]] describes inherited acreage, starting debt, a credit line, harvest uncertainty, expenses, additional borrowing, bankruptcy, and off-farm employment.

### Calibration

- [[board-game-season-board-game-season]] says George and Anne repeatedly tested the game's harsh economics until success remained possible.

### Multiple uses

- [[board-game-season-board-game-season]] reports classroom, family, mental-health, advocacy, and commercial uses for the same rule system.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode does not compare learning outcomes with other teaching methods or inspect how different players interpret the rules. A simulation can overstate randomness, individual choice, finance, or competition while omitting labor relations, public support, ecological costs, discrimination, household care, regional variation, and collective action. Authenticity to one family's experience is not universal representativeness.

## What Changed

- Established a general category for games that model the economics and uncertainty of earning a living.
