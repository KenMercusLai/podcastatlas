# Antihypertensive Medication Adherence / 降压药依从性

## Definition
Antihypertensive medication adherence is the consistent, clinician-guided use and review of blood-pressure medicine, including stable timing, response monitoring, side-effect reporting, and avoidance of unilateral stopping or substitution.

## Current Synthesis
The sources frame adherence as stability rather than rigid perfection or drug dependence. Regular dosing supports steadier exposure and pressure control, whereas planned omission, abrupt stopping, or interpreting normal treated readings as cure can allow pressure to rise again. Adherence also includes verifying response, reporting suspected adverse effects, completing appropriate laboratory review, and accepting clinician-guided combination treatment when one drug is insufficient. A single-pill combination may reduce pill burden, but convenience does not make the drug choice universal.

## Key Claims
- Antihypertensive medicines are generally intended for continuous daily use unless the prescriber gives different instructions.
- Five weekdays on and two weekend days off can undermine stable exposure and control.
- Large blood-pressure fluctuations are treated as a risk concern rather than evidence that intermittent dosing is harmless.
- A consistent approximate time is useful, but adherence should not become anxiety about one exact minute.
- Suspected adverse effects should prompt review and possible clinician-guided adjustment rather than unilateral cessation.
- Drug selection, including use of multiple drugs or a single-pill combination, depends on patient context, control, comorbidity, possible organ damage, contraindications, interactions, and monitoring rather than on convenience or another person's regimen.
- Normal readings during treatment usually demonstrate effective control, not proof that medication is unnecessary.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources offer general public-education principles, not drug-specific missed-dose or combination instructions. Individual medicines differ in duration, rebound risk, contraindications, interactions, pregnancy safety, kidney or cardiac implications, laboratory needs, and what to do after a late or missed dose. Patients should follow their prescriber's instructions and seek qualified advice for side effects, pregnancy planning, or uncontrolled readings.

## What Changed
- Added the distinction between long-term disease control and drug dependence.
- Added multi-drug and single-pill combination therapy as adherence-supporting but individualized options.
- Added laboratory monitoring and pregnancy safety to the medication-review boundary.
