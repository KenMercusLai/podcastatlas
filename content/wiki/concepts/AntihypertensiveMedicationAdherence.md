# Antihypertensive Medication Adherence / 降压药依从性

## Definition
Antihypertensive medication adherence is the consistent, clinician-guided use and review of blood-pressure medicine, including stable timing, response monitoring, side-effect reporting, and avoidance of unilateral stopping or substitution.

## Current Synthesis
The current source frames adherence as stability rather than rigid perfection. Regular dosing supports steadier drug exposure and blood pressure, whereas planned weekend omission or abrupt stopping can produce renewed elevation or rebound. If a dose is late, a side effect is suspected, or control remains poor, the general lesson is to preserve continuity where appropriate and obtain drug-specific medical advice rather than invent a new schedule or copy another patient's medicine.

## Key Claims
- Antihypertensive medicines are generally intended for continuous daily use unless the prescriber gives different instructions.
- Five weekdays on and two weekend days off can undermine stable exposure and control.
- Large blood-pressure fluctuations are treated as a risk concern rather than evidence that intermittent dosing is harmless.
- A consistent approximate time is useful, but adherence should not become anxiety about one exact minute.
- Suspected adverse effects should prompt review and possible clinician-guided adjustment rather than unilateral cessation.
- Drug selection depends on patient context, control, comorbidity, and possible organ damage rather than on friends' or online recommendations.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source offers a general public-education principle, not drug-specific missed-dose instructions. Individual medicines differ in duration, rebound risk, contraindications, interactions, kidney or cardiac implications, and what to do after a late or missed dose. Patients should follow their prescriber's instructions and seek qualified advice for side effects or uncontrolled readings.

- Created an adherence model centered on continuity, response verification, and clinician-guided adjustment.
- Separated approximate routine consistency from perfectionistic timing.
