# Antihypertensive Medication Adherence / 降压药依从性

## Definition
Antihypertensive medication adherence is the consistent, clinician-guided use and review of blood-pressure medicine, including stable timing, response monitoring, side-effect reporting, and avoidance of unilateral stopping or substitution.

## Current Synthesis
The sources frame adherence as safe continuity rather than rigid perfection or drug dependence. Regular dosing supports steadier exposure and pressure control, whereas planned omission, abrupt stopping, wrong-frequency dosing, copying another person's prescription, or interpreting normal treated readings as cure can create avoidable risk. Adherence also includes allowing time for the chosen medicine to reach its expected effect, verifying response, retaining enough packaging information to identify medicines, reporting suspected adverse effects, completing appropriate laboratory review, and accepting clinician-guided adjustment or combination treatment when needed. A single-pill combination may reduce pill burden, but convenience does not make the drug choice universal.

## Key Claims
- Antihypertensive medicines are generally intended for continuous daily use unless the prescriber gives different instructions; planned weekend omission can undermine stable exposure and control.
- Large blood-pressure fluctuations are treated as a risk concern, while a consistent approximate dose time is useful without becoming anxiety about one exact minute.
- Suspected adverse effects or delayed improvement should prompt monitoring and clinician-guided review rather than rapid self-switching or unilateral cessation.
- Drug selection, including use of multiple drugs or a single-pill combination, depends on patient context, control, comorbidity, possible organ damage, contraindications, interactions, and monitoring rather than on convenience or another person's regimen.
- Dose frequency, pill-organizer use, and preservation of drug names and packaging are safety issues, especially when family members help older adults manage several medicines.
- Diuretic use does not justify automatic potassium supplementation because potassium loss or retention depends on the medicine, dose, duration, diet, kidney function, and clinical context.
- Normal readings during treatment usually demonstrate effective control, not proof that medication is unnecessary.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources offer general public-education principles, not drug-specific missed-dose, overdose, onset, potassium, or combination instructions. Individual medicines differ in duration, rebound risk, contraindications, interactions, pregnancy safety, kidney or cardiac implications, laboratory needs, and what to do after a late, missed, or extra dose. Suspected overdose, symptomatic low pressure, weakness, confusion, fainting, or severe uncontrolled readings can require urgent assessment. Patients should follow their prescriber's instructions and seek qualified advice for side effects, pregnancy planning, or uncontrolled readings.
