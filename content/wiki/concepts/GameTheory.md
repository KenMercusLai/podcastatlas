# Game Theory

Game theory is the study of strategic decisions where each actor's best move depends on what others do. [[how-to-win-a-penalty-shootout-with-game-theory]] adds a compact sports example: a penalty taker and goalkeeper are not choosing in isolation, because each side is trying to anticipate the other's direction, timing, and habits.

[[79-gewei-lingdao-danfan-zan-xuedian-boyilun-690004262]] adds a workplace and contract-theory version through [[YiQieJieQiyue|《一切皆契约》]]. The source uses [[PrisonersDilemma]], [[TitForTatCooperation]], [[InformationAsymmetryInContracts]], and [[SignalDesign]] to show how employees, managers, publishers, and firms can end up in bad collective outcomes even when each actor is responding to incentives.

The episode's contribution is practical rather than mathematical. It shows that [[GameTheory]] can matter in a familiar event where execution skill is real but not sufficient; the best technical shot can become a poor strategic choice if it is too predictable.

[[22-zuqiu-jingjixue-duzhe-bubi-shi-qiumi-570874427]] reinforces the penalty branch with a second football example. It uses Jens Lehmann's visible crib sheet in the 2006 Germany-Argentina shootout and Franck Ribery-style randomization to show that the game includes psychology, signaling, and mixed strategy, not only historical shot charts.

[[106-bie-zai-haipa-chongtu-kuai-lai-xuexi-tanpan-shu-742539645]] adds a negotiation version of strategic interdependence. In that source, one side's offer, concession, and silence matter because the other side is inferring [[BATNA]], [[ReservationValue]], [[ZoneOfPossibleAgreement]], and intent from every signal.

[[would-you-trust-an-economist-with-your-economy]] adds a trust-repair version through [[BenHo]]. The episode uses game-theory language around costly signals: after experts lose trust, cheap statements are less persuasive than actions that impose time, money, risk, or accountability on the speaker.

## Key Claims
- Strategic settings require thinking about the opponent's beliefs, not only one's own strongest action.
- High-stakes expert environments can produce behavior closer to theory than low-stakes lab games.
- Data changes the game because it can reveal opponent tendencies but can also make the data user's own behavior legible.
- In the Soccernomics discussion, even a visible note or ritual can become a strategic signal if it changes what the opponent believes the goalkeeper knows.
- Negotiation adds information asymmetry: each party is trying to learn the other's alternatives, bottom line, priorities, and biases while protecting its own.
- Trust repair is also strategic because audiences infer sincerity from whether the expert's promise is costly or easy to fake.
- Episode 79 adds that workplace and publishing games are often contract problems: cooperation, discounting, effort, credentials, and flattery all depend on what others are expected to do.
