## Definition

朝贡体系经济激励 / Tribute System Economic Incentive is the pattern where tribute participation is encouraged by material returns: travel money, rewards,回赐, above-market purchase prices, hosted reception, or controlled trade access.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 422-2 argues that tribute should not be read only as fear, admiration, or cultural submission. The episode treats the system as a political and cultural order with a real economic engine. A court seeking "万国来朝" prestige may overpay for tribute goods, host envoys, and return gifts in order to make recognition visible.

The concept's current value is corrective. Tribute can still signal hierarchy, cultural order, and diplomacy, but the source insists that outsiders may enter the system because the exchange is profitable. That means tribute missions can also generate fraud, competition, fiscal strain, and even violence when access or prices are disputed.

Hanji 517 adds a compact Western Han field case. Du Qin says Jibin's delegation contains neither royal relatives nor senior ministers but merchants presenting commerce as tribute. The incentive is not only the exchange itself: imperial rewards and a protected escort can shift travel and security costs onto Han. The court's Pishan-only escort therefore limits the subsidy without rejecting all contact.

## Key Claims

- Tribute participation can be materially rational for smaller polities, merchants, or frontier powers when prestige-seeking courts subsidize recognition beyond the practical value of tribute goods.
-回赐, purchase prices, envoy reception, and trade access turn ritual hierarchy into an economic opportunity.
- Once tribute becomes profitable, fake envoys and rival claimants can exploit the system.
- Broken or blocked tribute-trade expectations can transform diplomatic ritual into security conflict.
- The concept does not reduce all tribute to profit, but political labels and participant status should be checked separately because a mission called tribute may function mainly as merchant access.
- Protection and transport can be part of the subsidy when the receiving court bears route risk for visiting traders.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources are short explanatory episodes and do not provide a full comparative history of tribute institutions. Hanji 422-2 compresses Han, Ming, Mongol, Japanese, Siamese, and Qing examples into one interpretive arc; Hanji 517 attributes the merchant diagnosis to Du Qin without independently identifying each envoy. The concept should therefore remain a bounded incentive lens: it qualifies prestige narratives without reducing all tribute to cash arbitrage or denying that merchants could also carry political messages.
