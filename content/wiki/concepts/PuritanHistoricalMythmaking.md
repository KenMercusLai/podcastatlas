# Puritan Historical Mythmaking

## Definition

Puritan historical mythmaking is the conversion of the mixed record of the [[Puritans]] into reusable cultural images of black-clad joylessness, sexual repression, theocracy, intolerant conformity, foundational liberty, or godly national purpose.

## Current Synthesis

The stereotype persists because it serves opposing stories. A progress narrative needs a repressive starting point from which later America supposedly advances toward liberty, while religious or nationalist narratives can make Puritan self-government an origin story stripped of exclusion and colonial violence. Hawthorne's scarlet letter and Miller's Salem allegory helped make Puritan New England available as a general symbol of hypocrisy, blacklisting, and groupthink.

Correcting false details does not require rehabilitating the colony. Colorful clothing, dancing, taverns, marital pleasure, divorce, procedural safeguards, and mediation qualify the caricature, while compulsory conformity, Quaker executions, patriarchy, Indigenous dispossession, enslavement, cultural suppression, and war sustain a serious critical judgment on different grounds.

## Key Claims

- Later memory often treats the Puritans as a single stereotype rather than a changing historical community.
- Claims about black clothing, universal prudishness, divorce prohibition, and routine scarlet-letter punishment compress or invent social practice.
- Literature made Puritan New England a portable symbol for repression and conformity.
- Political narratives alternately cast the colony as theocratic failure or a cradle of representative liberty.
- Salem's exceptional evidentiary collapse can obscure ordinary law without making ordinary law benign.
- Myth correction should replace false accusations while retaining evidence of religious coercion and colonial violence.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode's corrective framing is itself selective and relies on one historian's edited conversation. Some popular claims may contain partial truths even when overgeneralized, and neither the benign-founding nor the total-repression narrative should replace town-level, chronological, Indigenous, legal, and theological evidence.
