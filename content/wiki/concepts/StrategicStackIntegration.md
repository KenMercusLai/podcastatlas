# Strategic Stack Integration

## Definition
Strategic stack integration is selective vertical integration: a company controls the layers that create differentiation, resilience, or bargaining power while buying components that have become interchangeable commodities.

## Current Synthesis
The episode uses IBM's mainframe longevity and Nvidia's ecosystem breadth to argue that durable advantage can come from coordinating hardware, engineering, software, and applications as a system. Control across layers lets a company improve end-to-end performance and move value when one profit pool is commoditized. The qualifier is selectivity: owning every layer can waste capital and create rigidity, so integration should follow differentiation and dependency rather than completeness for its own sake.

## Key Claims
- End-to-end coordination can improve reliability, optimization, and customer switching costs.
- Control of several layers can let a company commoditize one layer and preserve economics in another.
- Commodity inputs should usually be purchased unless ownership protects a differentiated capability or critical dependency.
- Integrated ecosystems compete through coordination and distribution, not only through the quality of one model or component.
- Long-lived integrated platforms can still become vulnerable to internal inertia, capital intensity, or a disruptive external layer.

## Evidence
### Long-duration integrated system
- [[88-audio-nikesharvink-88-audio-nikesharvink]] presents IBM's mainframe business as a 65-year example, noting that IBM retained design, engineering, resilience, and software while no longer operating fabrication.

### Profit-pool flexibility
- [[88-audio-nikesharvink-88-audio-nikesharvink]] describes Nvidia as spanning infrastructure and application-related layers and argues that stack control lets firms shift profits when one layer is attacked.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The episode supplies strategic examples rather than comparative performance evidence. Vertical integration can increase capital requirements, management scope, antitrust exposure, internal transfer-pricing problems, and correlated technical failure. IBM's database history also shows that an integrated incumbent can suppress a new layer when it threatens an established profit pool. The correct boundary between differentiated and commodity layers changes over time.

## What Changed
- Created the concept as a selective-control strategy rather than a blanket endorsement of full vertical ownership.
