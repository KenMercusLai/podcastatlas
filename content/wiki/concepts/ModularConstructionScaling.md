# Modular Construction Scaling

## Definition
Modular construction scaling is the transition from isolated off-site building projects to a durable production system in which factories, standardized components or volumetric units, local installation, codes, procurement, finance, logistics, design, and demand support repeated output.

## Current Synthesis
[[default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-76efcd195d1b26a4faab0a64b31c1972-52966951-default-mp3-ywr3ahjkcgo-76efcd195d1b26a4faab0a64b31c1972-52966951]] treats modular construction as technically credible but institutionally incomplete in the United States. Parallel factory and foundation work can compress schedules; controlled conditions can support automation, waste reduction, safer work, and broader labor access; and completed high-rise projects rebut the idea that modular necessarily means small or visually crude.

The binding constraint is ecosystem continuity. Factories need predictable demand, projects need early design and regulatory alignment, and workers and local firms need a transition path. [[OperationBreakthrough]] and [[Katerra]] show different ways learning can be interrupted, while [[VolumetricBuildingCompanies]] and Singapore illustrate the role of coordinated delivery and demand-backed capacity.

## Key Claims
- Factory and site work can proceed in parallel, creating a real schedule advantage over strictly sequential field construction.
- Controlled production can enable automation, reduce waste or emissions, and change workplace accessibility and safety.
- Technical feasibility does not create scale without steady demand, compatible codes, logistics, finance, factories, skills, and early design coordination.
- Large capital infusions can accelerate experimentation but cannot compress every institutional transition into a short timetable.
- Public procurement deadlines or off-site requirements can provide demand certainty, but their results remain context-dependent.
- A hybrid model of centralized standardized production and small-firm local installation may preserve distributed delivery while gaining manufacturing repetition.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source gives limited evidence on lifecycle maintenance, defect rates, financing, transport constraints, factory utilization, consumer demand, and comparative total cost. Company schedule and labor claims may reflect advocacy, and national market-share comparisons may use different definitions of modular or prefabricated work. [[Katerra]] shows execution risk but does not establish technical infeasibility; successful towers show feasibility but not universal economics.

## What Changed
- Created the concept to separate modular technical feasibility from the institutions required for repeated scale.
