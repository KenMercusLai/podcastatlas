# Circular Migration

## Definition

Circular migration is repeated movement between a home community and a work destination rather than a single one-way transition to permanent settlement.

## Current Synthesis

[[general-chapman-s-last-stand-general-chapman-s-last-stand|General Chapman’s Last Stand]] presents twentieth-century Mexican migration to the [[UnitedStates]] as a recurring labor circuit. Young men could work seasonally, return to families and identities centered in [[Mexico]], and travel north again because crossing was comparatively easy and inexpensive.

The concept changes both measurement and policy analysis. Entry totals overstate durable population growth when many entrants later leave, so net migration and return probability matter alongside apprehensions or admissions. Circulation is also behaviorally contingent: when work incentives persist but crossing becomes more costly and dangerous, migrants already inside may stay longer and reunite with family rather than risk another round trip.

## Key Claims

- Repeated entries do not necessarily represent repeated additions to the resident population.
- Departures and return probabilities are necessary for distinguishing circulation from settlement.
- Family, identity, and community location can keep the home country central even when income is earned abroad.
- Low crossing cost can support return movement rather than simply increasing permanent immigration.
- Enforcement can change trip duration and household location even when it does not eliminate the incentive to migrate.
- Legal permission to travel may reduce settlement pressure when migrants can credibly expect to return for later work.

## Evidence

- Household pattern: [[general-chapman-s-last-stand-general-chapman-s-last-stand]] describes young male workers repeatedly traveling north and returning to families in Mexico.
- Measurement: [[general-chapman-s-last-stand-general-chapman-s-last-stand]] contrasts gross entries with net migration and says border systems count arrivals more readily than departures.
- Historical offset: [[general-chapman-s-last-stand-general-chapman-s-last-stand]] reports project estimates that returns offset most undocumented entries between 1965 and 1985.
- Behavioral response: [[general-chapman-s-last-stand-general-chapman-s-last-stand]] says dangerous, expensive routes reduced return probability and encouraged longer stays and family reunification.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

Circulation is a population pattern, not a claim that every Mexican migrant was male, temporary, undocumented, economically motivated, or likely to return. The source summary does not independently verify its historical percentages or separate enforcement effects from labor demand, exchange rates, demography, legalization, social networks, family change, or conditions in either country. Easier mobility could increase trips even if it reduces the duration of each stay, so entry volume and resident population must remain analytically distinct.

## What Changed

- Created the concept to distinguish repeated movement, gross entries, net migration, and permanent settlement.
- Added crossing cost as a factor that can alter return behavior rather than only initial entry.
