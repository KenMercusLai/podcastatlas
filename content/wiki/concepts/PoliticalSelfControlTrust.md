## Definition

Political self-control and delegation trust is the principle that high office requires conduct to remain predictable under emotion, pleasure, status, and provocation; talent alone does not make power safely delegable.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 1149 contrasts [[CaoZhi|曹植]] and [[CaoPi|曹丕]] through succession reliability. Cao Zhi's literary brilliance and emotional force are politically weakened by drinking and the reported Sima Gate violation, while Cao Pi is presented as more controlled and capable of managing officials and appearances.

Hanji 1160 widens the comparison through [[LadyBianCaoCao|卞夫人]]. Her reported response to a death rumor preserves Cao Cao's followers without pretending certainty, while her middle-earring choice and non-triumphal response to Cao Pi's designation show restraint under material and status gain. The same source adds the gate officer's execution to Cao Zhi's breach, making political self-command a question of consequences imposed on others, not only personal reputation.

The point is narrower than “poets cannot govern” or “quiet people are trustworthy.” Delegation depends on whether a person can accept correction, respect power boundaries, recover from disappointment, and behave consistently enough that others can plan around the office. [[XingYongLateHan|刑颙]] and [[LiuZhenLateHan|刘桢]] matter because corrective counsel is part of building that reliability.

Hanji 1159 adds duration, coaching, and victory as separate tests. [[JiaXu|贾诩]] reportedly advises Cao Pi to keep doing ordinary work and wait rather than force the succession question, while [[WuZhi|吴质]] helps him respond to Cao Zhi's literary advantage through filial emotion. This shows that visible control can be learned, advised, and strategically presented rather than treated as a fixed essence. [[XinXianying|辛宪英]] then observes Cao Pi's elation after designation and warns that inherited responsibility calls for gravity, making post-success conduct a countercheck on the earlier reliability judgment.

## Key Claims

- Talent and delegability are distinct judgments.
- Boundary violations carry more weight when authority would magnify their consequences.
- Receptivity to unwelcome counsel is part of self-command.
- The strongest test of restraint may come after fear, disappointment, or victory rather than during ordinary conditions.
- A powerful person's loss of control can transfer punishment and risk to subordinates.
- Emotional expression does not itself disqualify a leader; unpredictable conduct under power is the relevant risk.
- Visible restraint can reflect coaching and strategy as well as stable character, so succession comparisons should not be reduced to one anecdote.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episodes offer moral-political contrasts, not a comprehensive succession study or validated personality model. Cao Pi's restraint may include coaching, performance, and political technique as well as character, while Cao Zhi's loss cannot be assigned to one event without broader evidence. Xin Xianying's warning is a critique of demeanor, not proof of later governing failure. Lady Bian's reported silence and moderation do not prove a general rule that accommodation is safer or more virtuous than dissent. Literary talent and political competence are not inherently opposed, and exact dialogue, motives, legal consequences, and chronology remain source-scoped.

## What Changed

- Added patience through ordinary duty as a long-competition form of self-command.
- Distinguished visible restraint from fixed character by adding adviser coaching and emotional presentation.
- Added Xin Xianying's post-victory warning as counterevidence to a simple Cao Pi reliability story.
