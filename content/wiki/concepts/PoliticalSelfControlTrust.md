## Definition

Political self-control and delegation trust is the principle that high office requires conduct to remain predictable under emotion, pleasure, status, and provocation; talent alone does not make power safely delegable.

## Current Synthesis

Hanji 1149 contrasts [[CaoZhi|曹植]] and [[CaoPi|曹丕]] through succession reliability. Cao Zhi's literary brilliance and emotional force are politically weakened by drinking and the reported Sima Gate violation, while Cao Pi is presented as more controlled and capable of managing officials and appearances.

The point is narrower than “poets cannot govern.” Delegation depends on whether a person can accept correction, respect power boundaries, and behave consistently enough that others can plan around the office. [[XingYongLateHan|刑颙]] and [[LiuZhenLateHan|刘桢]] matter because corrective counsel is part of building that reliability.

## Key Claims

- Talent and delegability are distinct judgments.
- Boundary violations carry more weight when authority would magnify their consequences.
- Receptivity to unwelcome counsel is part of self-command.
- Emotional expression does not itself disqualify a leader; unpredictable conduct under power is the relevant risk.
- Succession comparisons should not be reduced to one anecdote.

## Evidence

- [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1149-jiemi-sanguo-dingji-moushi-xunyou-de-guanchang-shengcunshu-ljv1fwephe86-qczfy0o1n2yh7um|Hanji 1149]] uses Cao Zhi's drinking, Sima Gate conduct, and resistance to Xing Yong alongside the more controlled Cao Pi portrait.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode offers a moral-political contrast, not a comprehensive succession study. Cao Pi's restraint may include performance and political technique as well as character, while Cao Zhi's loss cannot be assigned to one event without broader evidence. Literary talent and political competence are not inherently opposed.

## What Changed

- Established a qualified distinction between brilliance and reliable delegation.
