# Nonviolent Resistance Strategy

## Definition

Nonviolent resistance strategy uses organized protest, strikes, boycotts, stay-at-home actions, parallel institutions, and other forms of noncooperation to remove the participation and institutional support on which political power depends.

## Current Synthesis

The episode separates strategic effectiveness from moral purity. Its reported comparative finding is that maximalist nonviolent campaigns succeeded about twice as often as violent campaigns, not because opponents were persuaded by virtue alone but because nonviolent methods usually permit broader and more diverse participation.

Breadth matters through networks. Participants connect a movement to businesses, civil servants, media, political elites, police, and security forces; tactical innovation lets pressure continue when demonstrations become easy to repress; and discipline helps potential allies see the movement as a credible coalition rather than a threat. [[Otpor]] and the [[SudaneseProfessionalsAssociation]] illustrate these mechanisms, while Spain and the [[NonviolentMovementCredibilityCrisis]] show how violence can shrink support and strengthen repression.

## Key Claims

- Nonviolent methods generally lower physical, skill, and role barriers to participation relative to armed struggle.
- Large, diverse coalitions create relationships into an opponent's [[PillarsOfSupport]] and make defection more likely.
- Strikes, boycotts, stay-at-home actions, and parallel institutions can remove cooperation without requiring direct confrontation.
- Tactical innovation and organizational resilience help campaigns adapt when authorities repress familiar methods.
- Discipline protects coalition growth because violence can alienate third parties and unify security forces.
- Near-term campaign success does not guarantee durable democracy or make every nonviolent campaign effective.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The source does not claim universal success, harmlessness, or equal feasibility under all regimes. Dataset construction, campaign coding, case comparability, repression, foreign intervention, movement goals, and the durability of outcomes can all change interpretation. Sudan shows that a coded near-term success can precede renewed military domination.
