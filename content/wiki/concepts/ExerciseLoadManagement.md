# Exercise Load Management / 运动负荷管理

## Definition
Exercise load management / 运动负荷管理 is the practice of matching training frequency, volume, intensity, progression, recovery, technique, and medical risk to a person's current capacity so exercise builds health without creating preventable injury or systemic harm.

## Current Synthesis
[[vol-212-pingshi-jiuzuo-zhoumo-wanming-yundong-yundong-yixueke-yisheng-gei-dagongrende-fang-lian-fei-zhinan-lqtwr4m-9npye9giib1-om-tylmy]] develops the concept for sedentary workers and weekend athletes. The source's core correction is that exercise risk is not simply "movement wears joints down"; risk rises when long sitting, weak muscles, sudden long-distance running, fatigue, poor technique, or external supports exceed the body's prepared capacity.

[[vol-211-yue-yundong-yue-kualian-zhengxing-yisheng-yu-xinxueguan-yisheng-de-jianshen-qumei-zhinan-lsp1xlzq1iapc-qale-mzutigv0u]] widens the same principle from musculoskeletal injury into whole-body risk. Excessive volume, sleep-deprived late training, caffeine-supported persistence, uncertain supplements, and poor adaptation can turn a health or appearance goal into rhabdomyolysis-like muscle breakdown, kidney burden, electrolyte disturbance, heart and blood-pressure strain, or emergency response.

Together the sources treat gradual progression as a general rule across gym training, running, cycling, tennis, [[HYROX]], outdoor endurance events, and body-composition goals. The practical rule is to choose training around purpose and capacity: ordinary, repeatable structures such as two or three gym sessions a week, lower weekend intensity when weekdays are sedentary, and weekday strength or core work are safer than forcing intensity through fatigue, social comparison, or appearance anxiety.

## Key Claims
- Safe exercise depends on matching load to current capacity, goals, recovery, and medical context, not on choosing a universally safe or unsafe sport.
- Gradual progression matters across endurance and strength activities: jumping from no base to long runs, marathons, intense functional-fitness sequences, or daily hard training raises risk.
- Weekend athletes should reduce single-session intensity and use weekday strength or function work to close the gap between daily inactivity and weekend demand.
- Fatigue is a risk multiplier because it makes movement form deteriorate and can also hide systemic recovery debt when sleep, caffeine, and willpower are used to keep training.
- Cardiovascular and metabolic context matters: baseline hypertension, organic heart disease, cardiopulmonary reserve, creatine kinase elevation, kidney burden, and electrolyte problems can change what "same intensity" means.
- Professional athletes, social-media high-intensity routines, and appearance-driven overtraining are poor default templates for ordinary workers without the same preparation, recovery, and supervision.
- Equipment, supports, supplements, and wearable heart-rate cues may help in specific contexts, but they do not replace progression, strength, technique, recovery, and professional assessment when risk is high.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The sources do not argue against intense training, competition, endurance goals, or exercise for appearance in general. They argue that intensity should be earned by preparation, technique, recovery, and medical context. These episodes are public education, not individualized training, cardiology, dermatology, medical-aesthetic, supplement, emergency-care, or rehabilitation prescriptions, and pain, swelling, locking, neurological symptoms, suspected acute injury, collapse, known heart disease, or abnormal exercise response still require qualified assessment.

## What Changed
- VOL.212 adds exercise load management as the reusable safety frame behind running, gym, cycling, tennis, HYROX, weekend-exercise, and support-device advice.
- VOL.211 extends the frame into appearance-driven overtraining, cardiovascular risk, sleep/caffeine recovery debt, supplement caution, rhabdomyolysis, equipment safety, and public emergency response.
