# Sensory Substitution

## Definition
Sensory substitution is the translation of information normally carried by one sensory channel into structured patterns available through another channel so that the brain can learn to use the information.

## Current Synthesis
The source joins cortical flexibility to device design. Sound can be encoded as vibration on the wrist, visual structure as tactile patterns, and directional information as a vibrating belt. Learning depends on stable correlations between the substituted signal and events in the world; the concept therefore concerns usable information mapping, not a claim that skin, tongue, or vibration literally becomes an eye or ear.

## Key Claims
- The brain can learn regularities in structured input delivered through atypical peripheral channels.
- Cross-modal correlation and practice are central to interpreting substituted signals.
- Substitution can support accessibility after sensory loss or absence.
- The same principle may extend toward sensory enhancement or addition, but evidence maturity differs by use case.
- Subjective reports of hearing- or seeing-like experience do not alone establish identical phenomenology.

## Evidence
- Wrist vibration - [[science-tools-of-learning-memory-dr-david-eagleman-scim5564931565]] describes [[Neosensory]] translating sound into tactile patterns.
- Earlier systems - [[science-tools-of-learning-memory-dr-david-eagleman-scim5564931565]] reviews tactile visual substitution and tongue-based visual input for blind users.
- Added direction channel - [[science-tools-of-learning-memory-dr-david-eagleman-scim5564931565]] describes a vibrating belt through which users can learn a persistent sense of north.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The source does not supply detailed accuracy, training-duration, retention, usability, or controlled-comparison results. Repairing access to existing environmental information and adding a genuinely new human sense are related but not equivalent claims.

## What Changed
- Established substitution, accessibility, and augmentation branches from Eagleman's examples.
