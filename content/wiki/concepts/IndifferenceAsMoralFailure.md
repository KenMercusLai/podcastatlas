# Indifference as Moral Failure

## Definition

Indifference as moral failure is the view that wrongdoing includes failing to notice, care, or act when one has a meaningful capacity to help, even if one did not cause the original harm.

## Current Synthesis

The episode presents James Keenan's reading of the Good Samaritan: the people who pass an injured traveler are not accused of causing the injury, but their capacity to help makes nonresponse morally significant. [[MalcolmGladwell]] applies that frame to routine American gun violence that can disappear behind legal abstractions, policy categories, medical success, or geographic distance.

Care here is not only emotion. [[AbdullahPratt]] makes it operational through sustained clinical service, trauma-center advocacy, and [[CommunityGunshotFirstAid|training]] that equips young people to help before professionals arrive. The frame should still be bounded by actual capacity, safety, role, and institutional duty; it must not transfer systemic responsibility onto people already bearing the harm.

The refugee episode lowers the action threshold further by calling kindness “entry-level caring” or a temporary suspension of indifference. [[ChioReichman]]'s survival and the Ontario sponsorship case show how limited acts can become consequential through [[DistributedKindness]], while preserving the distinction between useful contribution and complete rescue.

## Key Claims

- Moral responsibility can arise from a realistic capacity to help, not only from causing harm.
- Attention is ethically consequential because ignored routine suffering can remain outside public and institutional priorities.
- Care becomes more credible when expressed through preparation, presence, and sustained action.
- Individual action and institutional obligation are complementary rather than interchangeable.
- Capacity, safety, knowledge, role, and likely consequences limit what can reasonably be demanded.
- A care ethic must not make affected communities responsible for repairing failures imposed on them.
- Limited capacity can support a bounded duty without requiring heroism, generosity, or total responsibility.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The sources offer theological and narrative arguments, not a complete theory for allocating duties among individuals, institutions, and governments. People may lack safety, expertise, authority, or resources, and intervention can sometimes worsen harm. Bystander action after a shooting cannot discharge upstream public obligations, and small acts can leave dangerous gaps when institutions treat them as substitutes for durable support.

## What Changed

- Created a capacity-sensitive account of indifference that joins moral attention to practical action without shifting systemic burdens onto harmed communities.
- Added entry-level caring and distributed accumulation while preserving the boundary between bounded help and complete rescue.
