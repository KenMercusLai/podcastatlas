# Indigenous American Mythology / 美洲原住民神话

Indigenous American mythology / 美洲原住民神话 is the broad field introduced in [[32-yindianren-jiang-le-yi-bai-wan-ci-de-gushi-shi-maerkesi-de-laoshi-596386714]]. The episode treats "印第安人" as a convenience label for many peoples and regions rather than a unified religion: North American animal-person stories, South American flood and resource-origin tales, Maya and Aztec cosmologies, Inca and other regional memories, and colonial-era recordings all sit beside one another.

The concept matters because the source keeps the field plural. North American material often foregrounds [[CoyoteTrickster|Coyote / 凯奥蒂]], animals, local origins, and diffuse sacred power; Mesoamerican material is described through more organized gods, world layers, corn-made humanity, ball games, and sacrifice; South American tales often emphasize floods, jaguars, resource origins, and deaths that explain plants or objects. The source's useful move is not to systematize all of this, but to preserve the oral, regional, and colonial layers.

## Key Claims
- The field should be read as plural and regional, not as one "Indian mythology" system.
- [[NativeAmericanOralLiterature]] is the necessary transmission frame because many stories arrive through oral variants, collectors, translation, and later retelling.
- [[HumanAnimalContinuityInMyth]] is central: animals can be ancestors, gods, spouses, teachers, enemies, and human kin.
- [[StoryMotifTransmission]] helps compare fire theft, flood survival, moon and star origins, monster bellies, animal markings, and food-origin stories without proving one route.
- [[MythAsHistoricalEvidence]] remains a guardrail because ecological, colonial, sacrificial, and migration explanations in the episode are often plausible readings rather than settled evidence.
