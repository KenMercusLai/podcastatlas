## Definition

The Toxcatl massacre was the 1520 killing of unarmed Mexica festival participants by Spaniards under [[PedroDeAlvarado|Pedro de Alvarado]] in Tenochtitlan's temple courtyard.

## Current Synthesis

The episode treats the massacre as a catastrophic preventive strike built on uncertainty. The garrison faced diminishing supplies, absent servants, rumors of an attack or hostage rescue, and the loss of [[Malinche]] as its principal interpreter. Alvarado read the feast of Toxcatl, with warrior dancing and an effigy of Huitzilopochtli, as the moment to strike first. An Indigenous account in the Florentine Codex instead describes Spaniards closing exits and cutting down unarmed dancers and other celebrants.

Whatever Alvarado believed, the political effect is clear: the attack converted an unstable occupation into open urban war, drove the Spaniards back into the palace, and made [[Moctezuma]]'s remaining authority insufficient to restore accommodation. The episode also uses the scene to expose the moral contradiction between Spanish condemnation of human sacrifice and their own much larger act of killing.

## Key Claims

- The alleged Mexica attack plan is unproven, while the Spanish decision to attack festival participants is central to the surviving narrative.
- Isolation, rumor, supply anxiety, missing translation, and possible allied incentives amplified threat perception.
- Closing the courtyard exits made the violence an organized attack on people with little chance to escape.
- The massacre was the proximate trigger for armed resistance and the siege of the Spanish palace.
- Religious difference shaped Spanish fear but cannot explain away command responsibility or civilian harm.
- Conflicting and mediated sources require separating secure political consequences from uncertain motives and details.

## Evidence

- Threat environment: [[388-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-festival-of-blood-part-5-glt8784071073]] links missing supplies, rumors, interpreter absence, and possible Tlaxcalan reports to Spanish fear.
- Courtyard attack: [[388-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-festival-of-blood-part-5-glt8784071073]] summarizes the Florentine Codex account of sealed exits, metal weapons, mutilation, and flight.
- Political rupture: [[388-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-festival-of-blood-part-5-glt8784071073]] follows calls to arms, the breach of the gates, Spanish retreat, and palace siege.
- Moral contrast: [[388-the-fall-of-the-aztecs-the-festival-of-blood-part-5-glt8784071073]] contrasts the festival's intended sacrifice with the scale of Spanish killing.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The episode cannot recover the exact sequence, casualty count, Alvarado's private motive, Tlaxcalan informants' role, or whether any rescue or attack plan existed. The Florentine Codex is an Indigenous account mediated through a later Franciscan project, not an unfiltered transcript. Calling celebrants unarmed follows the cited account and should not be expanded into claims about every person present or every later phase of fighting.

## What Changed

- Established the massacre as the proximate rupture between hostage occupation and open war.
- Distinguished an uncertain threat claim from well-attested organized killing and its strategic consequences.
