# Dignity-Based Persuasion / 尊严激将式说服

## Definition
Dignity-based persuasion is a status-sensitive method that makes accommodation or surrender feel incompatible with the target's identity, rank, past experience, or claimed strength, then directs that emotional pressure toward a concrete strategic choice.

## Current Synthesis
Across the current cases, dignity is not a substitute for interest analysis. [[SuQin|苏秦]] praises a ruler's resources before portraying service to Qin as endless concession; [[MaoSui|毛遂]] turns Chu's past humiliation and present strength into a reason to join resistance; [[LuZhonglian|鲁仲连]] makes Qin's proposed imperial order morally unlivable before adding personal-risk calculation. Hanji 1100 adds [[ZhugeLiang|诸葛亮]]'s late-Han variant: he narrows [[SunQuan|孙权]]'s choice to resistance or surrender, contrasts Liu Bei's claimed refusal boundary with capitulation, then answers the resulting capacity challenge with troops, enemy weaknesses, and postwar benefit.

The durable pattern is therefore layered: activate a status boundary, make inaction or submission carry identity cost, and support the desired action with a plausible material case. The method varies by audience and can be coercive, manipulative, or historically weak even when it succeeds rhetorically.

## Key Claims
- Dignity pressure is strongest when attached to the target's own rank, resources, past humiliation, or political identity.
- Status provocation works with material argument: it creates motivation, while force, risk, and benefit analysis make action credible.
- The same method can protect an existing self-image through praise or threaten it through shame and unfavorable comparison.
- Narrowing the apparent options can intensify dignity pressure by making delay look like disguised submission.
- Persuasive success does not validate every premise; historical precedent, force claims, dialogue, and inferred emotion still require source criticism.

## Evidence
- Strength and submission cost: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-32-shishang-shouwei-lianheguo-mishuzhang-suqin-lgfkk45yq1c0yblequ-d1t7mol3|Zhouji 32]] has Su Qin adapt praise, resource claims, and humiliation-of-service language to multiple rulers during the hezong tour.
- Humiliation and commitment: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-96-maosui-zijian-2-ltlobdujy-hdipimfdupvwod2ech|Zhouji 96 part 2]] has Mao Sui combine sword-backed proximity, memories of Qin's attacks, Chu's claimed strength, and a blood-oath commitment.
- Existential dignity with source critique: [[zizhi-tongjian-zhouji-96-maosui-zijian-3-lj-cr4vg4e1qdfjs6yez85-3zuiv|Zhouji 96 part 3]] makes Qin submission a civilizational refusal boundary, then questions the speech's precedent and moral binary.
- Dignity plus operational closure: [[zizhi-tongjian-hanji-1100-chibi-zhizhan-qian-de-jingxian-tanpan-zhugeliang-ruhe-ba-siju-panhuo-lm0fojshbaktskmyd3hyu-tq4mnn|Hanji 1100]] has Zhuge Liang provoke Sun Quan against surrender, then answer whether the proposed coalition can fight and what victory could yield.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications
The current sources are popular-history narrations, and the speeches themselves come through layered historical and literary traditions. Zhouji 96 part 3 explicitly shows that effective dignity rhetoric can rest on unstable precedent and oversimplified moral contrast. Hanji 1100's exact dialogue, emotional effects, force totals, and neatly staged four-part sequence are likewise episode-bounded. Dignity pressure can also crowd out deliberation or become coercive, as Mao Sui's armed proximity makes especially clear.
