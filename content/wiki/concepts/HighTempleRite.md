## Definition

High-Temple Rite / 高庙礼 is the ancestral-temple legitimation frame in which a ruler makes conquest, restoration, or dynastic continuity visible by reporting achievement to founding ancestors and installing ritual order around the ruling house.

## Current Synthesis

Qinji 121 uses the concept to explain Qin Shi Huang's first post-unification journey away from [[Xianyang]]. The episode reads the westward route through [[LongxiCommandery|Longxi]] and [[BeidiCommandery|Beidi]] as movement toward Qin old lands and [[YongchengQin|Yongcheng]], where a ruler could report major achievement to ancestral temples. [[ZhouWuwang|King Wu of Zhou]] functions as a precedent for victory reported before the ancestral line.

Hanji 684 adds an Eastern Han restoration version through Liu Xiu's Luoyang ritual construction. Liu Xiu establishes a high temple in Luoyang, sets seasonal offerings to Han ancestors, builds the sheji altar beside the ancestral temple, and creates a southern altar for heaven-earth and spirit sacrifices. The source pairs those actions with Chang'an's destruction under [[ChimeiRebellion|Chimei]] and with the movement of Western Han imperial tablets to Luoyang.

The synthesis now separates two uses of the same ritual grammar. Qinji 121 emphasizes achievement reporting from a newly unified empire back to Qin old lands. Hanji 684 emphasizes restoration and transfer: the old western capital is ruined, while the new eastern capital becomes a place where Han ancestral and state-sacrifice institutions can stand again. In both cases, ritual makes political order legible through geography, ancestry, and visible procedure.

## Key Claims

- High-temple practice turns conquest or restoration into a reportable achievement before founding ancestors.
- The rite makes travel and capital-building politically meaningful rather than ordinary inspection or construction.
- Qinji 121 links westward travel to Qin old homeland and ancestral achievement reporting after unification.
- Hanji 684 links Luoyang ritual construction to Eastern Han restoration and reception of Western Han symbolic inheritance.
- The concept is an interpretive frame for terse chronicle notices, not a complete reconstruction of ritual manuals or exact ceremony.

## Counterevidence & Qualifications

The concept remains source-bounded and interpretive. Qinji 121 and Hanji 684 support ritual-legitimacy readings, but neither source note provides a complete ritual manual, exact liturgical sequence, or full custody chain for every tablet. Hanji 684's tablet-transfer detail is treated as a restoration signal rather than proof that all Western Han ritual custody questions are resolved.
