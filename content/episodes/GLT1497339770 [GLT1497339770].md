# Custer, Sitting Bull, and the Black Hills

## 概览

This episode follows George Armstrong Custer after the Washita River episode, tracing his uneasy mixture of ambition, literary self-fashioning, political weakness, and military flair as he returns to prominence on the northern plains.

The hosts connect Custer’s views of Native Americans to wider nineteenth-century ideas about “civilization,” Darwinian language, railroads, bison extermination, assimilation, and the pressure of American expansion. They stress that U.S. policy was framed by its advocates as assimilation rather than simple slaughter, while also showing how coercive and destructive that policy was.

The narrative builds from the Yellowstone expedition, where Custer performs effectively under pressure, to the Black Hills expedition of 1874, where rumors of gold transform a military survey into a political crisis. By the end, Grant faces the question of peace or war after failed attempts to buy the Black Hills from the Lakota.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Custer’s Paradoxical View of Native Americans**

[事实] The episode opens with Custer’s writing from *My Life on the Plains*, where he says he would prefer life on the open plains to confinement on a reservation if he were Native American.
[事实] The hosts point out that Custer immediately qualifies this sympathy by arguing that Native Americans cannot be allowed to stand in the path of “civilization.”
[推测] Custer’s language suggests a worldview in which admiration for Plains life coexists with acceptance of its destruction.

[03:03] **Custer After Washita**

[事实] The hosts recap that previous episodes introduced Crazy Horse, Sitting Bull, and Custer’s role after the Washita River massacre.
[事实] Custer has been drifting since the late 1860s, spending time in Chicago and Michigan and having a serious but unspecified quarrel with his wife Libby.
[推测] The hosts infer that the quarrel may have involved womanizing, given references to his gambling, drinking, and known taste for women.

[04:26] **Custer’s Religion, Guilt, and Marriage**

[事实] Custer’s father wrote letters about guilt and moral improvement, but the hosts say Custer himself does not appear especially religious in surviving letters or biographies.
[事实] Custer loved Libby and was very close to her, even though his conduct repeatedly failed his own standards.
[推测] Custer’s letters of remorse indicate genuine shame, but not necessarily a deep religious framework.

[05:43] **Restlessness, New York, and Bad Investments**

[事实] In 1870 Custer and Libby were posted to Hays City, Kansas, where Wild Bill Hickok was sheriff.
[事实] Custer dreamed less of frontier life than of returning to New York, networking with wealthy figures, and investing in boom industries such as mining and railroads.
[事实] His silver mine investment appears to have gone badly, with promised investment failing to materialize.
[推测] Custer’s frontier image partly concealed a strong desire for eastern celebrity and financial success.

[07:24] **Kentucky, Reconstruction, and the Klan**

[事实] Custer was posted to Kentucky during Reconstruction, where the Ku Klux Klan had a large paramilitary presence.
[事实] The hosts say Custer was weak in repressing the Klan and spent time with people sympathetic to or involved in it.
[事实] His Democratic politics and sympathy for the South put him at odds with Sheridan and President Grant.
[推测] Custer’s stance reflected genuine belief rather than career calculation, since it damaged his standing with powerful patrons.

[09:24] **European Fascination with the Plains**

[事实] Custer met Grand Duke Alexis of Russia along with Buffalo Bill.
[事实] The hosts discuss European aristocrats and adventurers visiting the Plains during the Indian Wars for hunting, danger, and exoticized frontier experience.
[推测] The Plains appealed to Europeans as a setting of romance and violence in an increasingly standardized modern world.

[10:53] **Custer as Writer, Naturalist, and Darwinian Thinker**

[事实] Libby wanted Custer to become a literary star, and he began writing the pieces later associated with *My Life on the Plains*.
[事实] Custer was interested in fossils, science, natural history, and theories about Native American origins.
[事实] He argued that Native Americans were human but destined by nature to remain “savage” and fade away.
[推测] His views drew on wider Darwinian and scientific language circulating in the 1870s.

[12:40] **Deep Time, Fossils, and the Language of Extinction**

[事实] The hosts discuss the West as a landscape where fossils and geological layers made cycles of extinction visible.
[事实] They connect this scientific imagination to Custer’s belief that Native peoples were being superseded by white Americans.
[推测] The fossil-rich landscape may have reinforced the era’s tendency to treat human cultures as subject to natural extinction.

[14:49] **Railroads, Bison, and Extermination**

[事实] In 1873 Custer was stationed at Fort Abraham Lincoln near Bismarck as the Northern Pacific Railway planned to pass through Lakota territory.
[事实] The hosts say railroads brought settlers and bison slaughter; more than three million bison were killed by non-Native hunters on the southern plains from 1872 to 1874.
[事实] Sherman and Sheridan used the language of extermination about bison and sometimes about Native peoples.
[推测] Destroying the bison was understood by U.S. commanders as a way to break Plains independence.

[17:51] **Assimilation and Cultural Suppression**

[事实] The hosts distinguish U.S. military policy from Nazi racial extermination, saying Sherman and Sheridan would have described their policy as assimilation.
[事实] They explain that Grant’s peace policy aimed to move Native peoples onto reservations, provide supplies and schools, and make them American in the style of immigrant assimilation.
[推测] Even when framed as humanitarian, this policy meant the suppression of Native lifeways and political autonomy.

[20:31] **Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, and the Survey Parties**

[事实] A survey mission in 1871 met little difficulty, but a 1872 mission brought Sitting Bull and Crazy Horse together in resistance.
[事实] Crazy Horse intimidated the surveyors, while Sitting Bull sat smoking a pipe under American fire without being hit.
[事实] The Americans were unsettled and retreated, angering Sheridan.
[推测] Sitting Bull’s calm under fire strengthened his reputation for courage and spiritual power.

[21:45] **Custer Returns on the 1873 Yellowstone Expedition**

[事实] Sheridan sent another expedition in 1873, giving Custer a new chance for action.
[事实] Custer dressed in buckskin, wore long hair, hunted fossils, and brought enthusiasm to the expedition.
[事实] He developed a friendship with the scout Bloody Knife, who had personal reasons to hate the Lakota.
[推测] Custer’s rapport with scouts shows a more socially adaptable side than his caricature as merely reckless.

[25:31] **The August 1873 Yellowstone Clashes**

[事实] Bloody Knife warned Custer that the expedition was being followed and that an ambush was likely.
[事实] On August 4, Lakota and Cheyenne fighters trapped Custer and about 90 men near the Yellowstone River.
[事实] Custer kept his men together, deployed them defensively, and avoided being lured into a rash charge.
[推测] This episode complicates the image of Custer as always reckless, though his broader confidence in cavalry superiority remained dangerous.

[27:52] **Weapons and Military Confidence**

[事实] The hosts note that the popular image of U.S. rifles versus Native bows and arrows is misleading, because the Lakota also had firearms.
[事实] Custer complained that government agents gave guns to people the army then had to fight.
[事实] American weapons were often better, but cavalry carbines could malfunction and the soldiers were poorly trained.
[推测] Custer’s confidence helped his men believe they could win despite practical weaknesses.

[30:00] **Custer’s Dramatic Charge and Renewed Fame**

[事实] On August 11, Custer’s men were again cornered near the Yellowstone while Sitting Bull watched from across the river.
[事实] Custer held his position until Stanley’s relief force arrived, then led a dramatic charge to the tune “Garryowen.”
[事实] The Lakota withdrew, Sheridan praised Custer, and newspapers celebrated him as a returning hero.
[推测] Custer’s theatrical style made him especially valuable to journalists and to the army’s public image.

[33:20] **The Panic of 1873 and Pressure on the Frontier**

[事实] After the break, the hosts describe the Panic of 1873 as a severe railroad-driven crash, including the failure of Jay Cooke’s empire.
[事实] They say a quarter of New York City’s population lost their jobs in the months after the crash.
[事实] Economic distress increased pressure on the frontier as people sought new lives as miners, farmers, or homesteaders.
[推测] The depression made expansion into Native-held lands more politically urgent for Grant’s government.

[35:27] **The Black Hills and the Question of Ownership**

[事实] Sheridan wanted a fort in or near the Black Hills, which lay outside the Great Sioux Reservation in “unceded Indian territory.”
[事实] The hosts challenge the idea that the Black Hills were timelessly sacred to the Lakota, saying the Lakota had conquered them only decades earlier.
[事实] They say the Black Hills mattered to the Lakota because they had won them, they were rich in bison, and they provided useful resources such as pine.
[推测] Giving up the Black Hills would have been politically humiliating for Lakota leaders.

[38:21] **Custer’s Black Hills Expedition**

[事实] Custer led ten companies of the Seventh Cavalry into the Black Hills with Gatling guns, scouts, scientists, geologists, fossil hunters, journalists, and President Grant’s son Fred.
[事实] Sheridan warned Custer not to take too many journalists, but Custer cultivated the press.
[事实] Custer’s Civil War record, charisma, and media appeal helped him remain useful to superiors despite repeated misconduct.
[推测] Custer’s legend was already being created before Little Bighorn, making his later death a ready-made dramatic climax.

[42:17] **Mythmaking and the Armed Picnic**

[事实] The hosts discuss the difficulty of separating fact from fiction in frontier stories, using Calamity Jane’s possible presence as an example.
[事实] During the Black Hills expedition, the Lakota were largely absent, Sitting Bull was hunting buffalo, and Crazy Horse was mourning the death of his daughter.
[事实] One historian described the expedition as taking on the aspect of an armed picnic.
[推测] The lack of immediate conflict allowed the expedition to appear adventurous rather than provocative.

[44:23] **Gold Fever in the Black Hills**

[事实] At the end of July 1874, miners with Custer’s expedition found a trace of gold.
[事实] The amount was small, and some observers, including Fred Grant, doubted there was meaningful gold.
[事实] Newspapers seized on the discovery with sensational headlines, and people began flooding toward the Black Hills.
[推测] Custer amplified the gold story partly because he understood what journalists wanted from him.

[46:47] **The Rush and Its Consequences**

[事实] Sheridan initially tried to keep miners out because their arrival threatened to destabilize relations with the Lakota.
[事实] General Crook was tasked with helping manage the situation, but the influx could not be stopped.
[事实] The Cheyenne were especially disturbed by American activity in the Black Hills because they took the area seriously.
[推测] The gold rush made a confrontation far more likely by turning a disputed region into a magnet for settlers and speculators.

[47:59] **Sitting Bull and the Sun Dance Gathering**

[事实] In the summer of 1875, Sitting Bull encouraged the Cheyenne and Lakota to gather at Rosebud Creek.
[事实] He danced, smoked pipes with chiefs, mimed an ambush, and declared that the Great Spirit had given their enemies into their power.
[事实] The hosts say Sitting Bull, Crazy Horse, and their supporters were clearly preparing to fight.
[推测] The gathering signaled a move from local resistance toward wider military coordination.

[49:57] **Grant’s Failed Attempt to Buy the Black Hills**

[事实] In autumn 1875, President Grant sent a commission led by Iowa Senator William B. Allison to buy the Black Hills from the Lakota.
[事实] Crazy Horse refused to attend, and Sitting Bull reportedly said he would not sell even a pinch of soil.
[事实] Spotted Tail said the Lakota would sell only for enough money to sustain them for seven generations, about 60 million dollars, while the commission was authorized to offer about five million.
[推测] The Lakota price was effectively a refusal, because selling the hills cheaply would have undermined their authority and reputation.

[52:02] **The Road to War**

[事实] After the talks failed, the commissioners recommended that Congress set a price and cut off reservation supplies if the Lakota refused it.
[事实] Grant faced a choice between maintaining peace, coercing the Lakota, or moving toward war.
[事实] On November 3, 1875, Grant called Crook, Sheridan, and other officials to a secret White House meeting about peace or war.
[推测] The episode frames this meeting as a turning point for both the Lakota and Custer.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s value lies in how it keeps the military narrative tied to larger forces: railroad finance, depression-era migration, press mythology, bison destruction, and racial thinking. Custer is treated neither as a simple villain nor as a heroic caricature, but as a volatile figure shaped by ambition, politics, science, celebrity, and genuine battlefield ability.

A major strength is the way the hosts connect individual moments, such as Sitting Bull smoking under fire or Custer’s theatrical charges, to broader structures of power and mythmaking. The discussion also repeatedly marks uncertainty, especially around Custer’s private conduct, Calamity Jane’s presence, and the sacred status of the Black Hills.

[推测] The main limitation is that the episode depends heavily on the hosts’ interpretive framing rather than Native voices quoted at length from primary sources. Still, it is useful for listeners who want a vivid narrative of how the Black Hills crisis emerged and how Custer’s legend was building before Little Bighorn.
