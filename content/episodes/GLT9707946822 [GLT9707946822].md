# Alexander the Great, Part Two: Persia, India, and the Making of a Legend

## Overview

This episode picks up after Alexander’s time in Egypt and follows his push east against Darius III, through Gaugamela, Babylon, Susa, Persepolis, Afghanistan, and India. The central question is not only what Alexander conquered, but why he kept going when stopping would have been safer and politically rational.

The hosts repeatedly return to the tension between Alexander as strategic ruler and Alexander as mythic overreacher. One reading sees his pursuit of Darius, his adoption of Persian customs, and his eastern campaigns as necessary steps in taking over the Persian imperial machine; another sees growing restlessness, violence, and an inability to stop.

The discussion closes by asking whether Alexander’s empire could have survived, why his legend mattered to Romans and later religious traditions, and whether “greatness” is still a useful category for understanding someone whose charisma, violence, and historical afterlife are inseparable.

## Segment Summary

[00:10] **The Moral Problem of Alexander**

[事实] The episode opens with Robert Lowell’s poem on Alexander, using it to frame him as both uniquely great and guilty of terrible crimes.
[事实] The hosts compare Alexander to an apex predator and debate whether it is fair to blame a fourth-century BC king for conquest.
[事实] Demosthenes is cited as someone who did blame Alexander, reportedly saying the stench of his corpse would fill the world.
[推测] The opening sets up the episode’s main tension: Alexander is treated as both morally troubling and historically irresistible.

[01:54] **From Egypt Back to Persia**

[事实] The previous episode had brought Alexander to Egypt, where he founded Alexandria and visited the oracle of Amun at Siwa.
[事实] Alexander now prepares to attack Darius III, despite having had the option to accept a deal giving him the western part of the Persian Empire.
[事实] Parmenion is described as having advised Alexander to accept Darius’s offer.
[推测] Refusing the deal is presented as a defining Alexander moment: ordinary prudence is rejected in favor of limitless ambition.

[03:00] **Darius Prepares for Gaugamela**

[事实] Darius gathers a new army in Babylon, including elephants, scythed chariots, members of his family, and cavalry from Bactria and Sogdiana.
[事实] Bessus, a distant cousin of Darius from Bactria, is introduced as a villainous figure with cavalry from the eastern fringes of the empire.
[事实] Darius uses a scorched-earth policy and chooses Gaugamela as the place to face Alexander.
[推测] The hosts treat Darius’s plan as militarily plausible, even though it ultimately fails.

[04:46] **The Battle of Gaugamela**

[事实] Alexander reaches the battlefield in October 331 BC and sees a Persian army that Greek sources claim numbered one million, though the hosts consider that unrealistic.
[事实] The hosts suggest Darius may have had around 100,000 men, while Alexander had about half that number.
[事实] Alexander delays before attacking, then a story is told of him oversleeping because he is no longer worried and expects victory.
[事实] In the battle, a gap opens in the Persian line, Alexander’s cavalry drives through it, and the Persians flee.

[07:07] **Babylon, Susa, and Persepolis**

[事实] After Gaugamela, Alexander takes Babylon, Susa, and then Persepolis.
[事实] In Babylon and Susa, he behaves with restraint and works with local elites, including appointing or retaining Persian officials.
[事实] The hosts argue that Alexander understands he cannot rule the Persian Empire without bringing Persians into his new order.
[推测] This marks a shift from conquest as plunder to conquest as imperial succession.

[10:38] **The Burning of Persepolis**

[事实] Persepolis is described as the dynastic capital most closely associated with the Achaemenid kings.
[事实] The hosts discuss competing explanations for Alexander burning Persepolis: deliberate revenge for Xerxes’ burning of Athens, or a drunken episode involving Ptolemy’s courtesan Thaïs.
[事实] They agree that Alexander did burn Persepolis, while disagreeing about the reliability of the story behind it.
[推测] Burning Persepolis could serve multiple political messages: revenge for Greeks, rejection of the Achaemenid dynasty, and preservation of Persian imperial structures elsewhere.

[13:30] **Darius’s Death and Alexander’s New Role**

[事实] After Persepolis, Alexander pursues Darius, who has fled with Bessus.
[事实] Bessus decides to make himself king, while Darius is later found abandoned and dying in a litter or cart.
[事实] Darius is said to have thanked Alexander for looking after his mother and wife before dying.
[事实] Alexander can then present himself as the avenger of Darius and heir of the Achaemenids.

[15:02] **Afghanistan and Macedonian Discontent**

[事实] The hosts debate whether Alexander had to pursue Bessus into Afghanistan to secure his claim as ruler of Persia.
[事实] Discontent among the Macedonian high command appears around this period, including the alleged plot involving Philotas, son of Parmenion.
[事实] Alexander has Philotas and Parmenion killed.
[推测] The campaign exposes a widening gap between Alexander’s imperial vision and what many Macedonian soldiers thought they had signed up for.

[17:11] **Persian Customs and Callisthenes**

[事实] Alexander begins wearing Persian clothing, including a purple cloak and diadem.
[事实] He asks his men to greet him in the Persian manner known as proskynesis.
[事实] Callisthenes, Aristotle’s associate and the expedition historian, refuses the ritual and is later killed.
[推测] The dispute over Persian court ritual shows how cultural integration could look like betrayal to Macedonian companions.

[18:36] **Rocks, Roxana, and the Eastern Campaigns**

[事实] The Afghan campaign lasts about three years and is described as harsh, confusing, hot, cold, and filled with difficult mountain operations.
[事实] Alexander kills or loses several close associates during this period, including Black Cleitus, who had saved him at the Granicus.
[事实] Alexander founds cities named Alexandria across Afghanistan and marries Roxana, daughter of Oxyartes.
[推测] The marriage to Roxana is interpreted as part of an effort to pacify a difficult region.

[21:08] **Beyond Afghanistan**

[事实] Alexander goes beyond Afghanistan into areas identified with modern Uzbekistan and Tajikistan.
[事实] The hosts disagree over whether this was mad overextension or strategically connected to securing former Persian lands.
[事实] They note that Greek foundations in Afghanistan endured longer than Greek rule in Persia itself.
[推测] Alexander’s eastern push can be read as both rational imperial consolidation and personal addiction to conquest.

[21:52] **Into India**

[事实] Alexander invades India in 326 BC after roughly eight years on campaign.
[事实] One explanation given is that parts of the Indus region had belonged to the Persian Empire, making them legitimate targets in Alexander’s imperial logic.
[事实] A ruler from Taxila comes to Alexander and invites his involvement.
[推测] The hosts’ disagreement here turns on whether Alexander is still claiming Persia or chasing the edge of the world.

[23:03] **Porus and the Hydaspes**

[事实] Alexander reaches Taxila, then confronts King Porus on the river Hydaspes, identified with the Jhelum.
[事实] Porus brings elephants and a large army during the monsoon.
[事实] Alexander crosses the river, defeats Porus, and then keeps him as an allied ruler rather than killing him.
[推测] Alexander’s treatment of Porus fits the recurring pattern of defeating local power and then incorporating it.

[24:06] **The Mutiny and the Limits of Conquest**

[事实] Alexander’s men are exhausted by monsoon conditions, snakes, long campaigning, and news that more of India lies ahead.
[事实] The traditional story says the army refuses to go farther at the Hyphasis and Alexander turns back.
[事实] The hosts debate the reliability of the sources, which are later writers drawing on lost earlier accounts.
[推测] One interpretation is that the mutiny preserves Alexander’s heroic image by making his return look forced rather than chosen.

[30:27] **The Desert March Home**

[事实] After turning back in India, Alexander does not return by the route he came.
[事实] He marches through a terrible desert associated with Cyrus the Great’s failed crossing.
[事实] The march goes badly: many people die, the baggage train suffers, locals are hostile, and Alexander loses contact with his fleet.
[推测] The desert crossing is presented as one of the least strategically convincing decisions of Alexander’s career.

[32:04] **A Persian-Style Monarchy**

[事实] Back from the east, Alexander marries daughters of Darius and holds a mass wedding in which many Macedonians marry Persians.
[事实] He reforms the army by sending some Macedonians home and bringing in Persians and Afghans.
[事实] At Opis, Macedonian soldiers mutiny because they feel they are being replaced and excluded from the new empire.
[推测] Alexander’s integration policy is less universal brotherhood than a focused attempt to fuse Macedonian rule with Persian imperial structures.

[34:22] **Hephaestion, Arabia, and Death**

[事实] In the last year of Alexander’s life, Hephaestion dies of fever, and Alexander builds a monument for him.
[事实] Alexander is said to be planning a new expedition to Arabia while in Babylon.
[事实] Alexander falls ill on 28 May 323 BC and dies on 11 June 323 BC.
[事实] The hosts debate murder versus disease, with one arguing that malaria or another illness better fits the length of his final sickness.

[36:36] **History, Romance, and the Sources**

[事实] The hosts discuss the difficulty of writing Alexander’s life as history because many surviving accounts are late and shaped by earlier lost sources.
[事实] They mention Arrian, Quintus Curtius Rufus, Diodorus, Plutarch, and lost authors such as Cleitarchus, Callisthenes, Nearchus, Aristobulus, and Ptolemy.
[事实] Tom stresses the blurred boundary between history and romance in Alexander traditions.
[推测] Source uncertainty is not a side issue in the episode; it becomes one of the main themes.

[38:43] **Alexander’s Religious and Legendary Afterlives**

[事实] Later traditions turn Alexander into a more fantastical figure, including stories of him traveling to the ends of the earth, using a submarine, or being lifted by birds.
[事实] Jewish tradition develops a story of Alexander visiting Jerusalem and acknowledging the God worshipped there.
[事实] Christian traditions can transform him into a Christian-like figure, despite his pre-Christian life.
[事实] In the Qur’an, he is connected with the “two-horned one,” which the hosts link to the horns of Amun.

[40:37] **Could the Empire Have Lasted?**

[事实] The hosts discuss the question of whether Alexander’s empire was too large to survive.
[事实] After his death, his companions fight one another, with Seleucus, Ptolemy, Antipater, Cassander, and Antigonus among the successor figures mentioned.
[事实] One view is that the empire might have lasted if Alexander had lived longer, secured succession, and made Persia the center of gravity.
[推测] The empire’s collapse is linked less to size alone than to the lack of succession and the reluctance of Macedonian successors to rule from the east.

[44:23] **Where Is Alexander’s Body?**

[事实] Alexander’s body is said to have ended up in Alexandria, where Augustus later visited it.
[事实] A story is mentioned that Augustus knocked off the tip of Alexander’s nose, though one host doubts it.
[事实] Another theory says Venetian visitors looking for Saint Mark’s body may have taken Alexander’s remains from Alexandria to Venice.
[推测] The Venice theory is treated more as a compelling story than as something firmly established.

[45:29] **Why Alexander Matters**

[事实] The hosts argue that Alexander inaugurates the Hellenistic Age and a synthesis of Greek, Persian, Mesopotamian, and Jewish influences.
[事实] They cite the idea that without Alexander there would be no Islamic civilization, and arguably no Christianity in its known form.
[事实] Koine Greek is described as one major cultural result of Alexander’s conquests.
[推测] The episode presents Alexander’s historical significance as cultural and religious, not only military.

[46:52] **Was Alexander Actually “Great”?**

[事实] A listener asks whether Alexander was actually great, and in comparison to whom.
[事实] The hosts say the earliest known reference to Alexander as “great” appears in Plautus in the early second century BC.
[事实] Roman fascination with Alexander is discussed through Pompey, Augustus, Caracalla, Crassus, Julius Caesar, Mark Antony, Trajan, and Julian.
[推测] Roman failures in the east helped strengthen Alexander’s legend as the conqueror who succeeded where others could not.

[49:26] **Alexander, Augustus, and Leadership**

[事实] The hosts compare Alexander with figures such as Julius Caesar, Genghis Khan, Augustus, and Napoleon.
[事实] One host says Alexander would win a fight, but Augustus would win the peace.
[事实] Alexander’s empire did not last, while Augustus’s did.
[推测] Alexander and Augustus are presented as opposite poles of ancient leadership: conquest and charisma versus settlement and durability.

[50:23] **Modern Fascination with Greatness**

[事实] The hosts return to the opening question of Alexander’s crimes and greatness.
[事实] They debate whether his acts should be judged as crimes by the standards of his age, with Tyre mentioned as a possible exception.
[事实] They say modern people are suspicious of the ideal of greatness Alexander embodies, yet remain fascinated by his charisma.
[推测] Alexander’s appeal survives because he embodies danger, ambition, and scale in a way ordinary rulers do not.

[51:48] **The Inner Alexander**

[事实] The episode ends with a listener asking whether, at age 31, he can conquer a similar-sized empire by his next birthday.
[事实] Dominic reads the closing lines of his children’s history of Alexander.
[事实] The passage says historians still argue whether Alexander was a noble champion or a selfish monster, and suggests remembering him as a boy who loved stories of gods and heroes.
[推测] The ending deliberately softens the violence of Alexander’s story by turning it toward imagination, glory, and childhood dreams of adventure.

## Podcast Commentary / Conclusion

[推测] The episode’s strongest value is the way it balances narrative energy with historiographical caution. It gives listeners the dramatic sweep of Gaugamela, Persepolis, Afghanistan, India, and Babylon while repeatedly asking how much of the story can actually be trusted.

[推测] Its highlight is the central disagreement between the hosts: one emphasizes Alexander’s strategic logic as heir to Persia, while the other pushes harder on mythmaking, source problems, and the possibility that Alexander’s story has been shaped by later Roman concerns.

[推测] The main limitation is that the fast pace compresses highly complex campaigns and source debates into a conversational format, so listeners looking for a precise military chronology or full scholarly apparatus would need more detail elsewhere.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in ancient history, empire, historical memory, and the question of how violent conquerors become enduring cultural myths.
