# Carthage Must Be Destroyed

## 概览

This episode traces how Rome moved from defeating Hannibal to annihilating Carthage itself. The hosts frame the story through Virgil’s *Aeneid*, especially Dido’s curse against Aeneas’s descendants, which later Romans would read as pointing toward Hannibal.

The central argument is that Carthage was militarily crippled after the Second Punic War, but Roman fear, trauma, opportunism, and greed kept treating it as an existential threat. Cato’s campaign for destruction, Massinissa’s pressure on Carthage, and Carthage’s treaty breach gave Rome the pretext it wanted.

The episode then follows the Third Punic War: Rome’s ultimatum, Carthage’s desperate resistance, Scipio Aemilianus’s final siege, and the city’s destruction in 146 BC. It ends by placing Carthage’s fall alongside Corinth’s destruction as a sign that Rome had become the overwhelming power of the Mediterranean.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **Virgil, Dido, and the Mythic Origin of Roman-Carthaginian Hatred**

[事实] The episode opens with lines from Virgil describing the sack of Troy and links them to the legendary foundations of Carthage in the *Aeneid*.
[事实] Dido falls in love with Aeneas, but Aeneas leaves Carthage because the gods have destined him to go to Italy and lay the foundations for Rome.
[事实] Before killing herself, Dido curses Aeneas’s descendants and calls for endless war between their peoples.
[事实] The hosts explain that Romans reading Virgil would understand Dido’s future avenger as Hannibal.

[05:04] **Hannibal’s Long Shadow Over Rome**

[事实] Hannibal is described as still haunting Roman memory more than a century and a half after his death.
[事实] The hosts stress that Rome remembered Hannibal’s campaign as the “destruction of Italy,” with massive casualties and repeated devastation of fields, vineyards, and orchards.
[事实] They note that historians debate the actual scale of the damage, but Roman perception of devastation mattered politically and culturally.
[推测] Rome’s later treatment of Carthage was driven less by present danger than by the traumatic memory of Hannibal.

[09:21] **Carthage After Defeat**

[事实] After Hannibal’s defeat, Rome imposed a punitive settlement on Carthage: a 50-year indemnity, loss of overseas territories, a fleet limited to ten warships, and Roman control over foreign policy.
[事实] Carthage could not legally go to war without Roman permission.
[事实] Massinissa of Numidia, a Roman ally, repeatedly encroached on Carthaginian territory.
[事实] Carthage sent embassies to Rome to complain, but Roman sympathy lay with Massinissa and against Carthage.

[12:56] **Rome’s Expanding Mediterranean Power**

[事实] The hosts widen the map to show Rome consolidating power in Spain, North Africa, Greece, and Macedonia.
[事实] In 168 BC, Aemilius Paulus defeated the Macedonian phalanx at Pydna and abolished the Macedonian monarchy.
[事实] Rome divided Macedon into weaker political units and took Greek elites hostage in Italy.
[事实] Polybius, later the major historian of Rome’s rise, was among the Greek hostages taken to Rome.

[18:11] **Polybius and Scipio Aemilianus**

[事实] Polybius became familiar with Roman politics and leading Roman families during his years in Rome.
[事实] He formed an important relationship with Scipio Aemilianus, the son of Aemilius Paulus and adoptive grandson of Scipio Africanus.
[事实] Scipio Aemilianus had already shown military promise at Pydna and in Spain.
[推测] The hosts present Scipio Aemilianus as both heir to Rome’s great anti-Hannibal lineage and a more complex figure than a simple Carthage-hater.

[21:00] **Cato’s Campaign Against Carthage**

[事实] Polybius regarded Carthage’s complaints against Massinissa as just, reflecting the Scipionic circle’s more sympathetic view.
[事实] Cato represented the opposite position and saw leniency toward Carthage as dangerous.
[事实] After visiting Carthage in 152 BC, Cato was alarmed by its renewed prosperity, grain stores, upgraded harbors, and dockyard capacity.
[事实] Cato used a fresh Carthaginian fig in the Senate to show how close Carthage was to Rome, and began ending speeches with the demand that Carthage be destroyed.

[26:23] **Debating Annihilation and Roman Motives**

[事实] The hosts discuss whether Cato’s demand can be called genocidal, while acknowledging the term is anachronistic.
[事实] Scipio Nasica opposed Cato with the counter-argument that Carthage should be preserved.
[事实] According to the episode, some Romans believed Rome needed a worthy enemy to avoid decadence and internal conflict.
[事实] The Senate also needed a legal and religious justification, because Romans wanted to believe the gods supported their wars.

[30:10] **Massinissa Provides the Pretext**

[事实] Massinissa continued attacking Carthaginian lands, and Carthage lost hope that Rome would restrain him.
[事实] In 151 BC, Carthage went to war against Massinissa without Roman permission, breaching the treaty of 201 BC.
[事实] Massinissa defeated the Carthaginian army near Oroscopa and forced humiliating terms on it.
[事实] The hosts note that Carthage had also just finished paying its indemnity, giving Rome a financial incentive to seize Carthaginian wealth directly.

[33:39] **Rome’s Ultimatum**

[事实] In 150 BC, Rome began mobilizing for an invasion of Africa, and Carthage tried to appease Rome by condemning its defeated general Hasdrubal and sending envoys.
[事实] The envoys learned that a Roman task force had already gone to Sicily.
[事实] After Roman forces landed in North Africa, Carthaginian envoys were ordered to surrender all weapons, armor, and war machines.
[事实] Only after Carthage handed these over did Rome reveal its true demand: the city had to be evacuated, resettled at least ten miles from the sea, and levelled.

[36:59] **Carthage Chooses Resistance**

[事实] Carthage refused the Roman terms, even though the people understood this could mean destruction.
[事实] People who advocated accepting Rome’s terms were attacked or killed.
[事实] The city freed slaves, turned public spaces and temples into workshops, melted metal for weapons, and used women’s hair for catapult ropes.
[事实] Hasdrubal was reprieved and returned to command as Carthage prepared for a desperate war.

[39:25] **The Siege Stalls**

[事实] Carthage held out longer than expected because of its triple walls, food stores, and rainwater cisterns.
[事实] Roman soldiers suffered disease after camping beside stagnant water in summer.
[事实] Hasdrubal proved effective at guerrilla warfare, attacking supply lines and launching sallies.
[事实] In 148 BC, Massinissa died, and a Macedonian pretender briefly offered Carthage the possibility of an ally.

[41:50] **Scipio Aemilianus Takes Command**

[事实] Rome turned to Scipio Aemilianus as the commander most likely to finish the war.
[事实] Although he was younger than the usual minimum age for the consulship, public pressure and senatorial acceptance allowed him to be elected.
[事实] In 146 BC, Scipio arrived in Africa and Hasdrubal withdrew his forces inside Carthage.
[事实] Scipio built a mole across the harbor entrance, cutting off Carthage’s food supply from the sea.

[45:36] **Starvation and No Return**

[事实] Hasdrubal imposed a military dictatorship inside Carthage and reserved food for his soldiers while civilians starved.
[事实] Corpses began to fill the streets of the city.
[事实] Hasdrubal tortured captured Roman soldiers on the walls and threw their bodies toward Roman positions.
[推测] This act made negotiation politically impossible and locked both sides into a fight to the end.

[46:49] **The Final Assault and Sack**

[事实] Scipio attacked through the harbors rather than over the walls, using the mole as a launch point.
[事实] Roman soldiers entered the marketplace, stripped a temple of gold, and began advancing through the city street by street.
[事实] They cleared multi-story houses, bridged narrow streets with planks, burned buildings behind them, and left civilians and fighters buried in the rubble.
[事实] After six days of fighting, most surviving Carthaginians surrendered.

[50:00] **Hasdrubal, His Wife, and the Survivors**

[事实] Hasdrubal surrendered and grovelled before Scipio Aemilianus.
[事实] Hasdrubal’s wife condemned his surrender, killed their two sons, threw their bodies into a fire, and then threw herself into the flames.
[事实] Hasdrubal was spared and taken to Rome for Scipio’s triumph, while Roman deserters were executed.
[事实] About 50,000 surviving Carthaginians were enslaved.

[50:50] **The Destruction of Carthage**

[事实] The city was stripped of treasure, its temples were demolished, and its libraries were emptied.
[事实] The Romans preserved one 28-volume agricultural treatise by translating it into Latin.
[事实] The hosts emphasize that the story about Rome sowing Carthage with salt is not found in ancient sources.
[事实] The Senate ordered what remained of the city to be razed and cursed anyone who tried to settle there again.

[53:02] **Rome’s New Mediterranean Order**

[事实] The destruction of Carthage in 146 BC was followed within months by the Roman destruction of Corinth.
[事实] The hosts argue that these twin destructions showed the Mediterranean world that Rome would tolerate no rival or serious disobedience.
[事实] They state that within a century the Mediterranean would become effectively a Roman lake.
[事实] The episode closes with Polybius’s account of Scipio weeping over Carthage and fearing that Rome too would one day fall.

[事实] The hosts close the Carthage series and announce that the next series will cover the fall of the Incas.
[事实] They say club members will receive all six episodes of the Inca series on Monday.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main strength is its ability to connect myth, memory, diplomacy, military history, and imperial psychology into one coherent explanation for Carthage’s destruction. It makes clear that Rome’s decision was not only strategic, but also shaped by fear, resentment, legality, and greed.

[推测] The most powerful sections are the descriptions of the final siege and the repeated return to literary memory, from Virgil at the opening to Polybius and Homer at the end. This gives the episode a strong tragic frame rather than presenting the Third Punic War as a simple military narrative.

[推测] A limitation is that the surviving evidence is heavily Roman, a problem the hosts themselves acknowledge when they discuss the loss of Carthaginian literature. Listeners looking for Carthage’s own perspective will necessarily get a fragmentary account.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in Roman expansion, the Punic Wars, imperial violence, and the way historical trauma can shape later policy.
