# Occult Politics in Britain, from Merlin to the Second World War

## 概览

This episode explores how magic, prophecy, witchcraft accusations, astrology, and occult reputations intersected with British political power. Dr. Francis Young argues that these beliefs were not just marginal curiosities: they helped shape royal self-presentation, propaganda, rebellion, and anxieties about national security.

The discussion moves from Merlin and medieval kingship to Renaissance occult science, Reformation polemic, civil war propaganda, and wartime spiritualism. A recurring conclusion is that magic could empower rulers symbolically, but it could also mislead them or become a weapon used by enemies.

The episode also stresses that “occult politics” changed over time. Medieval and early modern elites could treat astrology, alchemy, prophecy, and angelic communication as serious knowledge, while later governments were more likely to treat occult claims as propaganda, fraud, or security risks.

## 分段落总结

[00:40] **Opening Hex Against Putin**

[事实] The episode opens with a ritual curse directed at Vladimir Putin, invoking spirits and calling for his soldiers to refuse to fight and his people to rise against him.

[事实] The hosts explain that this hex is reportedly performed every Saturday at 7:33 p.m. Eastern Daylight Time under the hashtag “hex Putin.”

[事实] Sunflower seeds are discussed because of a widely shared story of a Ukrainian woman telling Russian soldiers to carry seeds so flowers would grow where they died.

[推测] The opening frames modern political magic as a living phenomenon, not just a historical curiosity.

[02:23] **Introducing Francis Young and Occult Politics**

[事实] Dr. Francis Young joins the episode to discuss his book *Magic in Merlin’s Realm: A History of Occult Politics in Britain*.

[事实] The book is described as a history of the occult uses made by rulers and political actors in Britain.

[推测] The guest is positioned as someone who can connect folklore, medieval religion, monarchy, and political culture into one long historical story.

[04:23] **Merlin’s Origins**

[事实] Francis Young says the Merlin tradition is largely shaped by Geoffrey of Monmouth in the 12th century.

[事实] Geoffrey presents Merlin less as a standard wizard and more as a cunning artificer, adviser, craftsman, and master of secret wisdom.

[事实] Geoffrey combines earlier figures, including Myrddin Wyllt, a wild man of the woods, and Merlin Ambrosius, linked to the story of Vortigern’s castle and the red and white dragons.

[事实] Merlin’s famous achievements include bringing Stonehenge from Ireland to Britain.

[07:37] **Solomon as the Model for Magical Kingship**

[事实] Young argues that medieval Christian and Muslim monarchs were expected to model themselves on Solomon.

[事实] Solomon was associated not only with splendour and building projects but also with occult wisdom and command over spirits.

[事实] This made magic hard to separate from ideals of royal grandeur and sacred kingship.

[推测] The Merlin figure made sense because rulers wanted advisers who could embody wisdom beyond ordinary political counsel.

[08:30] **Merlin as Prophet Rather Than Wizard**

[事实] Merlin is described in some traditions as the son of an incubus, the result of a failed attempt to create the Antichrist.

[事实] Because of this origin story, Merlin does not need to invoke spirits like ordinary magicians; he already possesses extraordinary powers.

[事实] Young says medieval Merlin was more famous as a prophet than as a Harry Potter-style wizard.

[事实] Merlinic prophecy influenced both popular rebellions and royal decision-making.

[10:26] **Medieval Belief in Merlin’s Reality**

[事实] Young says Merlin’s historical existence was not seriously questioned by historians until the 17th century.

[事实] In Plantagenet England, people would have taken Merlin’s role as Arthur’s adviser and prophet as historically true.

[推测] Merlin’s power came partly from existing across both literature and assumed history.

[11:20] **New Knowledge from the Islamic World**

[事实] Young argues that Merlin emerges in the 12th century amid an influx of knowledge from the Islamic world into Western Europe and Britain.

[事实] This knowledge included astrology, alchemy, and forms of divination believed to expand the ability to predict the future.

[事实] Young calls these forms of knowledge “disruptive spiritual technologies.”

[事实] Merlin helped make sense of new and potentially dangerous knowledge by giving it a British frame.

[14:13] **Britain as a Magical Island**

[事实] Merlin’s British identity helped link Britain with magic, even as his fame spread into French courtly culture.

[事实] Young says this association goes back to classical writers such as Pliny the Elder, who connected Britain with magical reputation.

[事实] Megalithic monuments such as Stonehenge contributed to the image of Britain as a misty, mysterious island of druids and wizards.

[推测] Merlin strengthened a pre-existing idea that Britain was especially suited to occult knowledge.

[17:48] **Roger Bacon, Henry III, and Royal Occult Advice**

[事实] Young identifies Roger Bacon as an early real-world figure willing to take on a Merlin-like role for a king.

[事实] Bacon advised Henry III and wrote treatises on occult topics dedicated to him.

[事实] Michael Scot is also mentioned as a figure with a major magical reputation, though Young warns that later stories exaggerated such reputations.

[事实] Henry III’s Westminster Abbey Cosmati pavement is described as a microcosm of the world with Solomonic and occult significance.

[18:58] **The Coronation Pavement as Magical Machinery**

[事实] Young says the Westminster Abbey pavement represents the relationship between microcosm and macrocosm.

[事实] He explains that coronations were often timed astrologically for a propitious moment.

[事实] The king sitting on the pavement during coronation is described as a process by which cosmic spiritual powers enter and empower the ruler.

[推测] In this interpretation, royal ritual could function as both political ceremony and magical technology.

[20:45] **Richard II and Magical Quietism**

[事实] Richard II is described as a king who became too invested in occult belief and prophecy.

[事实] Young argues that Richard came to believe his own propaganda as a mystical world emperor who would bring peace.

[事实] Young calls this danger “magical quietism,” where a ruler stops acting pragmatically because prophecy or divination becomes too important.

[事实] Richard’s fear of toads is discussed as an example of misinterpreting a sign before Henry Bolingbroke’s return.

[24:31] **Sorcery Accusations as Political Weapons**

[事实] The hosts connect the 15th century with accusations of sorcery and necromancy used against political enemies.

[事实] Young discusses Joan of Arc’s trial, where some wanted to accuse her of witchcraft as an explanation for her military success.

[事实] The witchcraft charge was not ultimately the main basis of conviction; heresy and cross-dressing became central.

[事实] Young says claiming Joan defeated the English through witchcraft would have embarrassed the English army.

[26:50] **The Reformation and Catholicism as Sorcery**

[事实] Young says the Reformation transformed the political use of witchcraft accusations.

[事实] Protestant propagandists, including John Bale under Thomas Cromwell’s influence, argued that the Mass and Catholicism were forms of sorcery.

[事实] Transubstantiation was especially vulnerable to being presented as magical or necromantic.

[事实] Earlier Lollards had already criticized transubstantiation as a doctrine of witchcraft.

[30:02] **John Dee as Elizabethan Merlin**

[事实] John Dee is presented as the most self-conscious Merlin-like figure in English history.

[事实] Young links Dee’s role to the Tudor dynasty’s Arthurian self-image and Welsh claims of descent from Arthur.

[事实] Dee was already connected with Elizabeth before she became queen, and she sometimes relied on his advice.

[事实] Young says Dee exaggerated the extent of his influence on Elizabeth in his own self-presentation.

[32:35] **John Dee’s Learning and Conjuring**

[事实] Dee was associated with mathematics, astrology, calendar reform, a major library, and the phrase “British Empire.”

[事实] Young says astrology was not automatically regarded as forbidden magic in Dee’s world.

[事实] Dee spent his life denying that he was a sorcerer, though after his death evidence emerged that he had practiced angelic conjuring through crystals and mediums.

[推测] Dee’s career shows how Renaissance learning could blur boundaries between science, religion, and magic.

[35:05] **Edward Kelley and the Crisis of Angelic Messages**

[事实] Edward Kelley served as Dee’s long-term medium, claiming to see angels in a crystal and reporting their messages.

[事实] Kelley claimed the angels commanded him and Dee to swap wives.

[事实] Dee agonized over this command and eventually persuaded himself it came from God.

[事实] Young says the episode damaged Dee deeply and left him diminished.

[36:34] **The Occult Meaning of the British Empire**

[事实] Young says Dee’s phrase “British Empire” looked backward to an imagined Arthurian empire rather than simply forward to later imperial expansion.

[事实] Dee was influenced by reports of fair-skinned or Welsh-speaking Native Americans and by legends such as the voyage of St. Brendan.

[事实] In Dee’s view, Merlin’s genius helped Arthur reach the New World and exploit its resources.

[事实] Young connects this background to Shakespeare’s *The Tempest*, where Prospero can reflect Merlin, Dee, and James I.

[39:31] **Civil War Propaganda and Magical Enemies**

[事实] During the civil wars, prophecy and accusations of occult assistance became politically charged.

[事实] Prince Rupert’s poodle Boy was portrayed by Puritan pamphleteers as a witch’s familiar, even a transformed witch from Lapland.

[事实] Royalists accused Oliver Cromwell of selling his soul to the devil, especially using the repeated date of 3 September in his victories and death.

[事实] Young distinguishes between Puritans who may have sincerely believed in witchcraft and royalists who may have used the charge more rhetorically.

[43:00] **Puritan Anti-Catholicism and Witchcraft**

[事实] Puritans already associated Catholicism and ritualism with witchcraft or satanic practice.

[事实] William Laud’s ritual changes, such as bowing at the name of Jesus or using incense, were interpreted by Puritans as signs of devilish worship.

[推测] This made it easier for Puritan writers to describe royalist or high-church opponents as occult threats.

[44:25] **James II, Monmouth, and the Protestant Wind**

[事实] Young identifies the reign of James II, from 1685 to 1688, as the last period when the occult had a measurable impact on British rulers or national politics.

[事实] During Monmouth’s rebellion, stories circulated that James used Catholic priests or “papist conjurers” to raise devils against Monmouth’s army.

[事实] In 1688, pamphlets suggested James tried to raise a wind to stop William of Orange from landing.

[事实] The eventual favourable wind that helped William reach Britain became known as the “Protestant wind.”

[45:23] **James II’s Magical Hat**

[事实] Pamphlet stories claimed James II owned a magical hat that let him hear what his enemies were saying.

[事实] Young says there is an image of James wearing a large military hat, which is presumed in the discussion to be the same hat from the story.

[推测] The magical hat story reflects how anti-Catholic political ridicule could turn royal objects into occult symbols.

[46:28] **Second World War Folklore and Helen Duncan**

[事实] The episode notes that the Tower of London raven legend, in the form that Britain stands while ravens remain there, is presented as a Second World War product.

[事实] Young discusses Helen Duncan, a Portsmouth medium who claimed during a seance to materialize a drowned sailor from HMS Barham.

[事实] HMS Barham had recently sunk, and the information was sensitive.

[事实] An MI5 officer attending the seance was alarmed because he wondered how Duncan had obtained classified information.

[48:01] **The Witchcraft Act and D-Day Security**

[事实] Helen Duncan was prosecuted in 1944 under the 1735 Witchcraft Act.

[事实] Young says the 1824 Vagrancy Act was more commonly used by then for fortune telling or seances, but the older Witchcraft Act allowed a custodial sentence.

[事实] Several historians believe Duncan was imprisoned during D-Day to prevent any possible disclosure of invasion secrets.

[推测] The authorities may not have needed to believe in her powers fully; they only needed to see her as a possible source of panic, rumour, or leaked information.

[49:06] **Did Officials Believe in the Occult Threat?**

[事实] Young says it is difficult to know exactly what officials believed about Duncan’s powers.

[事实] His own view is that they likely thought they could not be too careful during wartime.

[事实] He says seances could be treated as a form of careless talk that might endanger the country.

[事实] Duncan would be the last known public case where the establishment concerned itself with the interface of occult claims and government policy.

[50:07] **Later Echoes in Modern Politics**

[事实] The discussion briefly mentions mediums in 10 Downing Street during the Blair years.

[事实] Princess Diana and Prince Charles are mentioned as royal figures connected in different ways with occult or mystical practitioners.

[事实] Prince Charles is linked in the discussion to Jungian mysticism and eco-mysticism.

[事实] Dominic Sandbrook recalls that Margaret Thatcher received an astrologer’s warning before the Falklands landings and corrected its grammar.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s strength is its range. It connects medieval legend, Tudor imperial imagination, Reformation propaganda, civil war pamphleteering, and wartime intelligence anxiety without treating occult belief as a simple joke or as timeless superstition.

The most valuable thread is the distinction between belief, symbolism, and political use. Merlin, Roger Bacon, John Dee, and Helen Duncan occupy very different worlds, but each shows how claims about hidden knowledge could matter when power was uncertain.

[推测] The episode is especially suitable for listeners interested in British political culture, monarchy, religion, folklore, and the history of ideas. Its limitation is that it moves quickly across many centuries, so some examples are necessarily sketched rather than deeply developed.
