# Geoffrey Chaucer and The Canterbury Tales: A Window onto Medieval England

## 概览

This episode uses Geoffrey Chaucer and *The Canterbury Tales* to revisit the wider world of 14th-century England: the Black Death, the Hundred Years’ War, the Peasants’ Revolt, London trade, social mobility, and the rise of English as a literary language.

The hosts argue that Chaucer should not only be seen as an old, intimidating school-text author, but as a radical and “newfangled” writer whose work captures a society in motion. His life, from a London wine-trade family to royal service and diplomatic work, mirrors the social turbulence of the age.

The discussion moves from plague and pilgrimage to war, commerce, literary form, class hierarchy, and gender. The central conclusion is that *The Canterbury Tales* matters not just as literature, but as one of the richest historical windows onto medieval English society.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Opening With Middle English**

[事实] The episode begins with a reading of the opening of *The Canterbury Tales* in Middle English.

[事实] The hosts identify Chaucer as a major figure from the age of Edward III and Richard II, born around 1342 and dying in 1400.

[事实] They describe him as the founding father of English literature, while also noting that many readers find him intimidating because of the language.

[推测] The opening is used to make Chaucer feel both distant and alive: difficult at first, but central to understanding medieval England.

[03:30] **Chaucer As A Modern Medieval Figure**

[事实] The hosts stress that Chaucer was not merely venerable or old-fashioned in his own time, but radical, modern, and associated with transformation in the 14th century.

[事实] They note that Chaucer coined or used the idea of “newfangledness.”

[事实] His tomb in Westminster Abbey became the nucleus around which Poets’ Corner grew.

[推测] The episode frames Chaucer as a writer whose historical importance comes from being at the edge of social, linguistic, and cultural change.

[04:18] **Pandemic, Pilgrimage, And Thomas Becket**

[事实] Tom says he read *The Canterbury Tales* during the Covid pandemic after a planned walk along the pilgrims’ route from London to Canterbury was cancelled.

[事实] The pilgrims in *The Canterbury Tales* travel to Canterbury to seek the shrine of Thomas Becket, which was celebrated for miracles and healing.

[事实] Chaucer grew up in a London parish with an altar to St Thomas and often passed through Canterbury on business between London and Calais.

[推测] Reading the poem during Covid made its themes of illness, cure, travel, and communal movement more immediate for Tom.

[06:16] **The Black Death In Chaucer’s Life**

[事实] Chaucer was about six when the Black Death arrived in England in 1348.

[事实] The hosts say London was hit very hard, while Chaucer’s family may have survived partly because they were sent to Southampton shortly before the plague arrived.

[事实] Many of Chaucer’s London relatives died, and his parents inherited property, giving the family greater wealth.

[事实] Plague remained a recurring presence in England and became endemic in London by the time Chaucer wrote *The Canterbury Tales*.

[08:08] **Plague Inside The Canterbury Tales**

[事实] The hosts point to the figure of Saturn in the Knight’s Tale and the Pardoner’s Tale as examples of plague and death appearing in Chaucer’s work.

[事实] The Pardoner’s Tale involves three riotous men searching for Death, finding gold, betraying one another, and all dying.

[事实] The Pardoner is described as a preacher who tells moral stories while selling bogus relics.

[推测] The story links plague, fear of death, greed, and moral corruption in a way that reflects lived experience after epidemic disaster.

[10:34] **Pilgrimage As Social Reconnection**

[事实] Chaucer describes himself going to the Tabard Inn in Southwark, where he meets 29 other pilgrims before they travel to Canterbury together.

[事实] The hosts say April was the month when the plague season ended in London, with January to March especially lethal.

[事实] They compare Chaucer’s framing with Boccaccio’s *Decameron*, where people isolate from plague outside Florence.

[推测] The Canterbury pilgrimage can be read as the reverse of plague isolation: a celebration of meeting strangers, travelling, and re-entering society.

[12:48] **War, Court, And Diplomacy**

[事实] Chaucer’s family wealth helped him become a page in the household of Lionel, the third son of Edward III.

[事实] He witnessed the courtly world of Edward III and joined the king’s 1359 campaign in France.

[事实] In 1360, Chaucer was captured and ransomed for 15 pounds by Edward III.

[事实] The hosts argue that Chaucer saw both the glory and horror of the Hundred Years’ War, and that his career suggests he was more naturally a diplomat than a fighter.

[15:08] **Italy, Vernacular Literature, And Cosmopolitan London**

[事实] Chaucer travelled to places including Navarre and Italy, and Italian literary culture influenced his development as a poet.

[事实] The hosts reject the idea that writing in English made Chaucer anti-foreign; they argue he was influenced by continental writers using their own languages.

[事实] Chaucer grew up in a cosmopolitan London ward with Italian and Flemish merchants and could speak languages including French, Latin, and Italian.

[推测] Chaucer’s English literary innovation came from international exposure rather than cultural isolation.

[16:48] **John Of Gaunt, Wool, And London Politics**

[事实] Chaucer’s sister-in-law married John of Gaunt, creating a distant family connection to one of England’s most powerful magnates.

[事实] Chaucer travelled to Italy as John of Gaunt’s agent.

[事实] In 1374, Chaucer became controller of the wool custom and held the role for over a decade.

[事实] The hosts describe him as an effective civil servant who lived above Aldgate and walked to work near the Tower.

[18:49] **The Peasants’ Revolt And Survival**

[事实] Chaucer’s role in trade and his connection to John of Gaunt placed him near dangerous political rivalries in London.

[事实] During the Peasants’ Revolt, Sir Richard Lyons was dragged out and beheaded by a mob, and rebels entered London through Aldgate.

[事实] The hosts say there is only one reference to the Great Revolt in Chaucer’s poetry, in the Nun’s Priest’s Tale, mentioning Jack Straw and murdered Flemings.

[推测] Chaucer’s survival through plague, war, revolt, and court politics suggests diplomatic skill and an ability to navigate unstable power structures.

[21:31] **A Society In Flux**

[事实] The hosts describe Chaucer as the grandson of a pub landlord who became connected to the Duke of Lancaster.

[事实] They argue that *The Canterbury Tales* brings together people from many social backgrounds and multiple perspectives.

[事实] Marion Turner is cited for the idea that Chaucer gives detailed voices to characters from ordinary life.

[推测] The poem’s social range makes it valuable to historians because it presents medieval England as a complex social mosaic rather than a fixed hierarchy.

[26:06] **The Storytelling Game**

[事实] The host of the pilgrimage proposes that each pilgrim tell two tales on the way to Canterbury and two on the way back.

[事实] The best storyteller is to receive a supper paid for by the others.

[事实] The pilgrims draw lots, and the Knight, the most socially prestigious figure, tells the first tale.

[推测] The setup initially appears to follow medieval hierarchy, but Chaucer quickly turns it into a structure that disrupts hierarchy.

[28:40] **Reality, Fiction, And Self-Parody**

[事实] Harry Bailey, the host in *The Canterbury Tales*, was a real Southwark innkeeper and MP known from contemporary records.

[事实] Chaucer also includes a version of himself in the poem.

[事实] The fictional Chaucer tells the Tale of Sir Thopas so badly that the host stops him, then tells another story that is described as boring.

[推测] This blurring of real people, fiction, and comic self-mockery makes the poem feel unusually modern.

[30:16] **Hierarchy Under Pressure**

[事实] The hosts explain the medieval tripartite model of society: those who fight, those who pray, and those who labor.

[事实] *The Canterbury Tales* includes figures corresponding to these groups, including the Knight, the Parson, and the Ploughman.

[事实] Chaucer appears to admire the Knight, Parson, and Ploughman, while portraying several clerical figures as corrupt or flawed.

[推测] Chaucer respects traditional ideals but shows that they are insufficient to describe the society he actually inhabits.

[31:46] **Cecily Champagne And The Statute Of Labourers**

[事实] Documents found in 1873 led to long-standing suspicion that Chaucer had been accused in a case involving “raptus.”

[事实] The hosts say more recent documents showed the case related to the Statute of Labourers, not rape.

[事实] According to the episode, Chaucer had offered Cecily Champagne higher wages to work for him, and the legal issue concerned whether she had left her previous employer voluntarily.

[推测] The case presents Chaucer as someone participating in, and benefiting from, post-plague labor mobility.

[33:57] **The Miller Interrupts The Order**

[事实] After the Knight’s Tale, the host turns toward the Monk, but the drunken Miller interrupts.

[事实] The Miller is described as vulgar, low-class, drunk, and socially inappropriate as the next speaker after the Knight.

[事实] The Miller’s Tale is bawdy and involves sexual comedy, buttocks, farting, and humiliation.

[推测] The Miller’s interruption is a literary version of social upheaval, breaking the expected order of medieval hierarchy.

[35:51] **The Wife Of Bath**

[事实] The Wife of Bath has been married five times and says she is looking for a sixth husband.

[事实] She is described as handsome, rosy-cheeked, gap-toothed, broad-hipped, and wearing bright red stockings.

[事实] She is a wealthy clothmaker and has made pilgrimages to Rome, Santiago de Compostela, and Jerusalem three times.

[推测] Her wealth, mobility, and confidence make her one of the clearest symbols of the new social energies Chaucer is interested in.

[36:48] **The Wife Of Bath’s Tale**

[事实] The Wife of Bath tells a story about a knight who commits rape and faces execution at King Arthur’s court.

[事实] Guinevere allows him to live if he discovers what women most desire.

[事实] An ugly old woman gives him the answer: women most desire sovereignty over their husbands.

[事实] The knight must marry the old woman, then finally gives her the power to choose her own form, after which she becomes both beautiful and faithful.

[推测] The tale turns a story of male violence into a lesson about female authority and consent within marriage.

[38:28] **The Wife Of Bath’s Prologue**

[事实] The Wife of Bath’s prologue is twice as long as her tale and lets her speak in her own voice.

[事实] She discusses her five husbands, the tricks she used against them, and the domestic abuse some inflicted on her.

[事实] She is described as rebellious, witty, larger than life, sexually confident, and contemptuous of arguments for women’s subordination.

[事实] Marion Turner is cited as calling her the first ordinary woman in English literature.

[40:48] **Women, Work, And Wealth After The Black Death**

[事实] The hosts connect the Wife of Bath to cloth manufacturing around Bath and the Cotswolds wool economy.

[事实] They say post-Black Death conditions in Northern Europe allowed many women more choices, more autonomy, repeated marriage, and greater participation in the economy.

[事实] Widows could accumulate property through inheritance, especially in trade contexts such as London.

[推测] Chaucer’s Wife of Bath reflects real social and economic possibilities for women rather than simply inventing a fantasy of female independence.

[44:09] **Alice Chaucer And Social Mobility**

[事实] Chaucer died in 1400, shortly after Henry IV came to the throne.

[事实] Chaucer’s granddaughter Alice was twice widowed by age 24 and inherited substantial wealth.

[事实] Alice married William de la Pole, later Duke of Suffolk, and retained his lands and property after he was executed.

[事实] Alice’s descendants became close enough to royal power that Chaucer’s great-great-grandson John de la Pole was named Richard III’s heir in 1484.

[推测] Alice’s career extends the episode’s theme that late medieval England allowed extraordinary social ascent.

[47:09] **Chaucer’s Literary Legacy**

[事实] Chaucer’s works were printed by Caxton and he became the first English writer to have a complete works edition under Henry VIII.

[事实] His Westminster Abbey tomb was later improved to reflect his status as the father of English literature.

[事实] The hosts credit Chaucer with helping legitimize English as a literary language alongside Latin and French.

[事实] They argue that his London dialect helped shape southeastern English as a model for later English.

[推测] The episode presents Chaucer as both a literary founder and a historical witness to the making of a recognizably modern English society.

## 播客点评/总结

[事实] The episode’s main value is that it treats Chaucer not only as a literary monument, but as a guide to plague, war, trade, class mobility, gender, and language in 14th-century England.

[事实] Its strongest sections connect specific tales and characters, especially the Pardoner, the Miller, and the Wife of Bath, to wider historical forces.

[推测] The main limitation is that listeners unfamiliar with *The Canterbury Tales* or medieval English may find some references dense, especially when the discussion moves quickly between literature, biography, and dynastic history.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in medieval England, literary history, the Black Death, social change, and the origins of English as a major literary language.
