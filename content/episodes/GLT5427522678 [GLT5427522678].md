# Beards: From Medieval Barbers to Victorian Virility and Modern Counterculture

## 概览

This episode traces the modern history of beards from the high medieval period through the twentieth century, treating facial hair as a social code rather than a trivial fashion detail. The hosts connect beards to religion, masculinity, medicine, military culture, technology, celebrity, politics, and generational rebellion.

A recurring conclusion is that the meaning of a beard is unstable. In one period it can signal godly masculinity, martial toughness, or patriarchal authority; in another it can imply dirtiness, eccentricity, radical politics, or comic oddness. Shaving itself is also shaped by material conditions, from dangerous razors and barber-surgeons to Sheffield steel and Gillette disposable blades.

The discussion moves chronologically: medieval shaving risks and humoral medicine, Reformation and Puritan disputes, Shakespearean beard symbolism, Enlightenment clean-shavenness, Peter the Great’s war on Russian beards, the Crimean and Victorian beard revival, germ theory and Edwardian uniformity, the First World War, interwar suspicion of beards, and finally the Beatles, hippies, hipsters, Jeremy Corbyn, and Prince Harry.

## 分段落总结

[01:00] **Opening With Lord Raglan and the Modern Beard**

[事实] The episode opens with a hostile quotation about beards attributed to Lord Raglan, commander of British forces in the Crimean War, though the hosts note it may have been invented by George MacDonald Fraser for the Flashman novels.

[事实] The hosts frame the episode as the continuation of their earlier discussion of ancient and early medieval beards.

[事实] They describe the episode’s focus as the story of “modern beards,” after previously covering classical and early medieval examples.

[推测] The Raglan quotation sets up the episode’s central theme: beards are never just hair, but carry political, cultural, and moral meanings.

[02:54] **Medieval Shaving, Barbers, and Medical Ideas**

[事实] The hosts revisit the idea that clean-shavenness could suggest priestliness, godliness, or closeness to the divine, while a full beard could suggest a more worldly masculinity.

[事实] They explain that shaving in earlier periods was technically difficult and risky, involving small knives or axe-like razors, often handled by servants, slaves, or barbers.

[事实] In medieval England, barbers were also surgeons or dentists, and the Worshipful Company of Barbers was founded in London in 1308 before receiving a royal charter in 1462.

[事实] The episode emphasizes that shaving accidents could be dangerous before antibiotics, because cuts could become infected.

[事实] Early modern medical thinking connected beards to male fertility, semen, puberty, heat, and bodily vitality, with Eleanor Rycroft and Hildegard of Bingen discussed as examples of this tradition.

[07:04] **Power, Kingship, and Renaissance Beard Fashion**

[事实] The hosts argue that late medieval and early modern powerful men often wore beards, citing Edward III, Henry IV, Charles V, and Philip II of Spain.

[事实] Henry V is presented as an outlier because he was compared to a priest and associated with a more clean-shaven religious image.

[事实] Christopher Oldstone-Moore is cited as identifying four great beard moments: the second century AD, the high Middle Ages, the Renaissance, and Queen Victoria’s reign.

[事实] William Fisher’s study of Tudor and Stuart portraits is cited for the claim that bearded men outnumbered clean-shaven men by about ten to one.

[推测] The discussion suggests that elite portraiture is one of the main kinds of evidence available for tracking beard fashion in these periods.

[09:38] **Reformation, Protestant Beards, and Puritan Shaving**

[事实] The hosts debate whether beards became especially important with the Reformation, noting that German Protestant ambassadors with long beards made an impression during Edward VI’s reign.

[事实] Edward VI is described as initially laughing at and disapproving of very long German beards, while Protestant England soon saw beards become more mainstream.

[事实] Thomas Cranmer is mentioned as someone who grew a beard in Edward VI’s reign.

[事实] The hosts argue that later radical Protestant or Puritan identity could move in the opposite direction, making clean-shavenness a marker of sober piety.

[事实] Puritans are described as associating beards with bodily waste, soot, vanity, and dandified excess.

[12:28] **Elizabethan Beard Styles and Social Meaning**

[事实] By the Elizabethan period, different beard styles are presented as carrying different meanings.

[事实] A spade beard is associated with soldiers, a Van Dyke beard with courtiers, and a swallow-tail beard with eccentricity or “character.”

[事实] The hosts note that maintaining a Van Dyke beard would require effort and expense.

[推测] The episode treats beard styles in this period as a kind of visual language through which men displayed profession, status, temperament, and self-presentation.

[13:28] **Shakespeare, Masculinity, and Bearded Women**

[事实] Shakespeare’s plays are used as evidence that adult male characters were generally assumed to have beards.

[事实] The phrase “beardless boy” is described as a recurring object of mockery in Shakespearean and Jacobean drama.

[事实] The hosts discuss beard-pulling as a serious insult, citing Hamlet and Gloucester in King Lear.

[事实] Macbeth’s witches are mentioned as bearded women whose facial hair signals supernatural disorder and a world turned upside down.

[事实] Pliny is discussed as having written about women who grew beards, including accounts of sudden transformations.

[16:33] **The Eighteenth-Century Clean-Shaven Gentleman**

[事实] By the eighteenth century, men were described as being almost entirely clean-shaven, with the smooth face becoming the ideal of the enlightened gentleman.

[事实] Eleanor Rycroft is cited for the idea that a smooth, youthful, clear face suggested an open mind.

[事实] Alan Withey is cited for describing Georgian shaving culture as tied to urbanity, civility, politeness, commerce, and barber shops as public spaces like coffee houses.

[事实] The hosts connect clean-shavenness to an age of wigs, artifice, sober dress, and restrained male display.

[事实] Sheffield steel razors, tallow soaps, and badger or boar hair shaving brushes helped make private self-shaving more common.

[20:02] **Shaving Manuals and the Dangers of Razors**

[事实] Jean-Jacques Perret’s eighteenth-century shaving manual, Pogonotomy, is discussed as an early guide to shaving oneself.

[事实] The hosts say early shaving manuals focused less on getting the closest shave and more on avoiding injury.

[事实] Henry David Thoreau’s brother John is cited as dying from lockjaw after cutting himself while shaving.

[事实] Lord Carnarvon is mentioned as dying after nicking a mosquito bite while shaving and later suffering sepsis and pneumonia.

[推测] These examples reinforce the episode’s point that shaving fashion depended partly on whether shaving could be made safe and routine.

[22:23] **Peter the Great and Russia’s Beard Tax**

[事实] In seventeenth-century Russia, beards are described as religiously significant in Orthodox culture and associated with prophets, apostles, Jesus, and the image of man created by God.

[事实] Patriarchal authorities are described as condemning beardlessness and treating shaving as a serious religious offense.

[事实] Peter the Great returned from his European tour in 1698 and personally shaved some of his boyars soon after arriving back in Moscow.

[事实] Peter later imposed a tax on beards, with taxpayers receiving a medallion showing that the tax had been paid.

[事实] The hosts tell a story of a Russian man carrying his shaved-off beard with him because he believed he might need it after death.

[28:12] **Crimea and the Victorian Beard Revival**

[事实] The second half resumes with the Crimean War, described as a major event in the history of beards.

[事实] British troops in Crimea grew beards because they spent months in harsh conditions without adequate soap or unfrozen water.

[事实] Photographs of bearded soldiers returned home and helped turn beards into signs of military status, manliness, and prestige.

[事实] The hosts argue that the Crimean War accelerated or amplified the Victorian beard revival.

[事实] Darwin, Tennyson, and W. G. Grace are discussed as figures associated with the great Victorian beard.

[30:38] **Celebrity, Advertising, and Victorian Masculinity**

[事实] W. G. Grace is described as the first mass-appeal sportsman and as someone whose distinctive beard became part of his public image.

[事实] Grace’s beard helped make him recognizable in drawings, photographs, and advertising.

[事实] Ambrose Burnside is discussed as an American Civil War general whose facial hair gave its name to sideburns.

[事实] The hosts connect the spread of beard fashions to photography, advertising, consumerism, and celebrity image-making.

[推测] The Victorian beard is presented as modern in its own time because it rejected the clean-shaven Georgian look and aligned with new martial and public ideals.

[33:01] **Victorian Health Claims, Gender Anxiety, and Germ Theory**

[事实] Victorian medical writers claimed beards were healthy, protecting the throat, preserving teeth, and strengthening gums.

[事实] Darwin’s view is summarized as treating beards as advertisements of virility that could appeal to women and be shaped by sexual selection.

[事实] Gender historians are described as arguing that men may emphasize facial hair when women’s roles are expanding or when masculinity feels threatened.

[事实] By the late nineteenth and early twentieth centuries, germ theory changed the medical consensus, and beards became suspect as possible breeding grounds for disease.

[事实] A 1907 French experiment is cited in which mustachioed men were said to leave bacteria, food particles, and even spider-leg hair on women’s lips after kissing.

[36:08] **Edwardian Uniformity and the Return of the Smooth Face**

[事实] The Edwardian period is described as moving toward corporate uniformity, office work, clerks, and white-collar sameness.

[事实] The hosts argue that long, individualistic beards did not fit the Edwardian preference for a uniform workforce.

[事实] Youth, vigor, dynamism, and rejection of the previous generation are also given as reasons for clean-shavenness returning.

[事实] Oscar Wilde, Herbert Asquith, young Winston Churchill, Kaiser Wilhelm II, and Franz Ferdinand are discussed as examples of prominent non-bearded figures, though Wilhelm had a mustache.

[推测] Clean-shavenness in this period seems to combine hygiene, modernity, workplace discipline, and generational distancing from Victorian fathers.

[37:20] **Gillette, the First World War, and Interwar Suspicion**

[事实] King Camp Gillette developed the idea of the disposable razor blade in the 1890s and began producing it in the early twentieth century.

[事实] The First World War boosted shaving because officers wanted uniformity, and soldiers needed to reduce lice and germs.

[事实] American troops entering the war in 1917 were told to bring their own shaving kits, and Gillette made packs for trench conditions.

[事实] After the war, the hosts describe the interwar years as a bad period for beards, with Lenin, Trotsky, George V, and George Bernard Shaw mentioned as exceptions or leftovers.

[事实] In interwar and mid-century British culture, beards are linked to eccentricity, pacifism, socialism, vegetarianism, artistic pretension, and social oddness through examples from Just William, George Orwell, and Lucky Jim.

[41:27] **The Beatles, Youth Culture, and the 1960s Beard**

[事实] The hosts argue that youth culture from the mid-1950s onward was strongly focused on hair, but most youth styles still assumed men would be clean-shaven.

[事实] The Beatles began experimenting with mustaches and beards around late 1966 and early 1967, with full facial hair visible around the launch of Sgt. Pepper.

[事实] Photographs of the 1966 World Cup final are used as contrast, with England’s players and most of the crowd described as clean-shaven.

[事实] The Beatles’ later Rishikesh period is discussed, especially George Harrison’s longer commitment to a beard and interest in Transcendental Meditation.

[推测] The hosts suggest that 1960s beards may have helped masculinize otherwise flamboyant clothing, while also playing ironically with Victorian or imperial imagery.

[44:22] **False Beards, Hipsters, and Late-Twentieth-Century Eccentricity**

[事实] Pop music magazines in the 1960s advertised mail-order beards, mustaches, and sideburns that buyers could attach to their faces.

[事实] Buyers could send a cutting of their own hair so the seller could match the color.

[事实] The hosts say reliable statistics on modern beard prevalence are hard to find.

[事实] Even in the 1960s and 1970s, public figures with beards are described as relatively rare, with beards still suggesting eccentricity or bohemianism.

[事实] George Best’s beard is discussed as part of an image of dissipation and distance from team discipline.

[45:36] **Modern Political Beards and Closing Reflections**

[事实] In 1970s British political cartoons, beards are described as associated with Liberals, the IRA, and schoolteachers.

[事实] Prince Harry and Jeremy Corbyn are discussed as possible holders of the most famous modern British beards.

[事实] The Economist is cited as calling Corbyn’s beard a counter-cultural badge of defiance against smooth-faced capitalists.

[事实] A 2017 survey is described as finding that 69% of newspaper comment pieces about Jeremy Corbyn mentioned his beard.

[事实] The hosts close by joking about famous bearded and non-bearded men, their producer Theo’s changing facial hair, and what kinds of beards they themselves might wear.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it turns a seemingly comic subject into a serious history of symbols, technology, gender, and power. Its strongest moments are the links between beard fashion and broader historical shifts: Reformation identity, Georgian civility, Crimean photography, Victorian masculinity, germ theory, corporate uniformity, and countercultural politics.

[推测] The tone is deliberately light and digressive, with jokes about historical figures, cricket, politics, Shakespeare, and pop culture. That makes the episode accessible, though listeners wanting a tightly sourced academic account may find the banter and side remarks occasionally distracting.

[推测] The episode is well suited to listeners who enjoy cultural history, everyday objects as historical evidence, and the way fashion becomes political. It is less suited to anyone looking for a complete global history of facial hair, because the discussion is mainly focused on Britain and Western Europe, with Russia and the United States appearing as major supporting examples.
