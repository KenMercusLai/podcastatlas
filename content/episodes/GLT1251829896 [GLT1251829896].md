# Are We Living Through a 17th-Century Moment?

## 概览

The episode asks whether plague, political division, restrictions on public life, statue disputes, and anxieties about national breakdown make the present resemble the 17th century. Tom Holland and Dominic Sandbrook use the English Civil War, the Great Plague, the Restoration, religious conflict, print culture, and Scottish politics as points of comparison.

Their central conclusion is mixed: there are striking parallels in media disruption, political polarization, public-health fear, and moralized conflict, but the scale of 17th-century violence and mortality was far greater. The hosts repeatedly stress that today’s crises are serious, yet not comparable in severity to plague deaths, civil war casualties, and the broader devastation of the 1600s.

The discussion also argues that the 17th century continues to shape later British political identities. The hosts connect Cavaliers, Roundheads, Whigs, Tories, Cromwell, Brexit geography, Scottish nationalism, and modern culture wars to older arguments about religion, liberty, authority, and dissent.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Opening Question**

[事实] The hosts introduce the episode by asking whether plague or pestilence, statue-smashing, restrictions around Christmas, and political instability mean the present is entering a “17th century moment.”
[事实] They frame the topic as a comparison between current events and the crises of 17th-century Britain.

[00:58] **Listener Responses on Greatness**

[事实] The hosts discuss feedback from earlier episodes about “greatness,” including suggestions involving Xi Jinping, Erdogan, Queen Elizabeth, Jimmy Anderson, and Boris Johnson.
[事实] They note arguments that conquest, unification, national renewal, or moral authority can all be used to claim greatness.
[推测] This opening correspondence sets up one of the episode’s broader themes: historical labels are unstable and depend heavily on later interpretation.

[03:45] **Modern Civil War Scenarios**

[事实] A listener suggests that a future U.S. division might run between central states and peripheral states rather than along a simple red-state/blue-state line.
[事实] Dominic says American secession or state governments refusing federal direction is very unrealistic but not impossible to imagine in a nightmare scenario.
[推测] The hosts use the U.S. example to move from modern polarization into the older British experience of civil conflict.

[06:16] **Seventeenth-Century Timeline**

[事实] Tom summarizes the Stuart period: Elizabeth I is succeeded by James VI of Scotland, who becomes James I of England; Charles I follows; civil war breaks out; Charles I is executed; Cromwell’s Protectorate is established; Charles II is restored; London suffers plague in 1665 and fire in 1666; James II is later driven out and replaced by William.
[事实] Dominic describes this as the “greatest hits” needed to understand the episode’s comparisons.

[07:59] **Plague and Lockdown Parallels**

[事实] The hosts compare Covid-era restrictions with London’s experience of plague in 1665.
[事实] Tom says the royal court retreated to Oxford during the plague.
[事实] Dominic cites Samuel Pepys worrying that wigs might carry plague infection, comparing that anxiety to modern fears about contaminated post or surfaces.
[推测] The comparison suggests that fear-driven behavior during epidemics can look familiar across centuries, even when the diseases and medical contexts differ.

[09:17] **Climate Crisis and Global Crisis**

[事实] Tom connects 17th-century catastrophe to the Little Ice Age and cites Geoffrey Parker’s book *Global Crisis*.
[事实] He says Parker places Britain’s civil war and pandemic experience in a global context of climate change, revolution, war, and collapse.
[事实] Dominic says Parker describes large population losses across Europe, including a fall of about a third in much of Europe and about half in Germany.
[推测] The hosts treat climate stress as one possible parallel between the 17th century and the early 21st century, while Tom says he does not know how future historians will judge the present.

[10:55] **The Limits of the Comparison**

[事实] Tom says one reason to compare the present with the 17th century is to show contrasts that may reassure listeners.
[事实] Dominic says about 100,000 people died in London in 1665, roughly a quarter of the population.
[事实] Tom and Dominic state that Britain’s current divisions are not like the violent divisions of the 1640s and 1650s.
[事实] Dominic says more people died per head in the civil wars of the 1640s than in either world war.
[推测] The episode’s main corrective is that modern crisis language can obscure how extreme 17th-century death and disorder actually were.

[12:17] **Print Culture and Social Media**

[事实] Dominic argues that 17th-century divisions were driven partly by technology and new media: printing presses, pamphlets, and the early newspaper world.
[事实] He compares inflammatory reports about Protestant suffering in Europe or Catholic atrocities in Ireland to misinformation spreading on Twitter and Facebook.
[事实] Tom describes a breakdown of Crown-licensed information control and an explosion of pamphleteering as royal authority weakened.
[推测] The hosts imply that new information systems can destabilize societies when institutions cannot manage the speed and emotional force of what circulates.

[13:30] **Milton and Free Speech**

[事实] Tom identifies John Milton as a poet, author of *Paradise Lost*, secretary to Cromwell during the Commonwealth, and a major defender of Charles I’s execution.
[事实] Tom says Milton celebrated freedom of speech and the flowering of printed argument.
[事实] The hosts note that, as now, people also worried whether some forms of expression should be regulated.
[推测] Milton becomes a bridge between the 17th-century pamphlet world and contemporary arguments about platform governance and free speech.

[14:24] **Religion and Freedom of Conscience**

[事实] Tom says the English Civil War was partly about religion and that religion mattered enormously in a devoutly Christian country.
[事实] Dominic notes that modern readers can find 17th-century religious disputes baffling, including arguments over altars, saints’ images, priestly vestments, and prayer books.
[事实] Tom argues that many people believed national religious unity was necessary to avoid God’s wrath.
[事实] He says that in the 1650s some religious groups increasingly emphasized freedom of conscience rather than requiring the whole country to share one belief.
[推测] The discussion presents religious fragmentation as one origin point of later ideas about pluralism and individual conscience.

[17:35] **Boris Johnson and Stuart Analogies**

[事实] The hosts discuss Jonathan Healey’s comparison between Boris Johnson and James II, including conflict with bishops, problems with Scotland, and banning Guy Fawkes Night.
[事实] Dominic asks whether Boris Johnson is more like Charles II: boisterous, unreliable, and associated with “merry monarch” energy.
[事实] Tom says Johnson’s recent restrictions made him look more like Oliver Cromwell than Charles II.
[推测] The analogy is playful, but it shows how quickly modern leaders can be mapped onto 17th-century archetypes of pleasure, authority, and prohibition.

[18:39] **Christmas, Puritans, and Cromwell**

[事实] Dominic says the Puritans of the 1640s and 1650s did restrict Christmas because they thought it should not be centered on jollity, presents, and indulgence.
[事实] Tom says they opposed giving liberty to “carnal and sensual delights.”
[事实] The hosts also complicate Cromwell’s dour reputation, saying he could be jolly, enjoyed music, and danced at his daughter’s wedding.
[推测] The segment challenges a simple caricature of Cromwell while using Christmas restrictions as an accessible modern parallel.

[19:55] **Cromwell, Levellers, and Radical Memory**

[事实] A listener asks whether Cromwell was a hero or villain.
[事实] The hosts discuss portrayals of Charles I by Alec Guinness and Peter Capaldi, then turn to the Levellers as radical figures associated with ideas like broader suffrage.
[事实] Dominic connects Tony Benn’s interest in the Levellers to later left-wing traditions.
[事实] Tom says comparisons between 17th-century and modern politics are not only about direct parallels but also about lines of dissent, even when those lines are partly bogus.
[推测] The hosts suggest that later political movements often borrow 17th-century symbols to create ancestry and legitimacy.

[24:08] **Civil War Geography, Brexit, Whigs, and Tories**

[事实] Dominic cites Robert Tombs’s *The English and Their History* as arguing that British politics is deeply shaped by arguments over the meaning of the 17th century.
[事实] Tom says studies after the Brexit vote mapped Civil War royalist and parliamentary areas onto Leave and Remain areas with a fairly strong match.
[事实] He says London and areas tied to continental European commerce tended to align with Parliament in the 17th century and Remain in the 21st.
[事实] Tom also says there is no clear political line of descent because Tories, Whigs, royalists, liberals, and Cromwell’s legacy are repeatedly reinterpreted.
[推测] The episode treats the Civil War less as a settled inheritance than as a reusable political language.

[27:11] **Scotland and Nationalism**

[事实] Tom says the 17th century offers a model for thinking about an independent Scotland separated from England and Wales.
[事实] He identifies Charles I’s attempt to impose a particular model of religion on Scotland as one cause that helped precipitate civil war across Britain and Ireland.
[事实] Tom argues that the religious print of Scottish identity endured even after Christian faith declined.
[推测] He links modern Scottish nationalism to an older biblically shaped sense of Scots as an elect people.

[28:43] **Witch Hunts and Modern Shaming**

[事实] Dominic raises the 17th century as a major age of witch hunting and asks whether online attacks on J.K. Rowling are comparable.
[事实] Tom says J.K. Rowling is not like the typical accused witch because she has a powerful public voice and is more like a religious leader or pamphlet-war participant.
[事实] Tom says the impulse to find scapegoats, shame people who fall from the true faith, and attribute social ills to individual moral failings resembles witch-hunting dynamics.
[事实] He also says witch persecution was one of the few things Catholics and Protestants could share in across Europe.
[推测] The hosts use witch hunting to compare older religious persecution with modern moralized public denunciation.

[30:45] **Book Recommendations**

[事实] Tom recommends Geoffrey Parker’s *Global Crisis* and Paul Lay’s *Providence Lost*.
[事实] Dominic recommends Christopher Hill’s *God’s Englishman* and Samuel Pepys’s diary.
[事实] Tom also recommends Iain Pears’s novel *An Instance of the Fingerpost*, which he describes as a mystery set around the end of the Protectorate and the Restoration.
[事实] Dominic agrees that *An Instance of the Fingerpost* is one of the great historical novels.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s value lies in using historical comparison without flattening the past. The hosts enjoy playful analogies between modern politics and the Stuarts, Cromwell, Puritans, pamphleteers, and witch hunters, but they also repeatedly emphasize that the 17th century was far more violent and deadly than the present.

A major strength is the way the episode connects familiar current anxieties, including pandemic fear, online misinformation, culture-war denunciation, Scottish nationalism, and Brexit geography, to older disputes over religion, authority, conscience, and media. The discussion is conversational and often comic, but it gives listeners a useful map of why the 17th century still matters.

The main limitation is that many comparisons are exploratory rather than fully argued. Some points, especially the links to Brexit, Scottish nationalism, and J.K. Rowling, are suggestive rather than developed in depth.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners who enjoy British political history, historical analogies, and fast-moving conversation that mixes scholarship with jokes and contemporary references.
