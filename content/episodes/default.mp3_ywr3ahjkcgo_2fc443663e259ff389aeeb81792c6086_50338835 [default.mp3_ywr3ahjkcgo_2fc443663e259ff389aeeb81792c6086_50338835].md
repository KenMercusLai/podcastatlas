# How to Change: Engineering Better Habits Around Our Human Flaws

## Overview

Behavioral scientist Katy Milkman explains why knowing what is good for us rarely guarantees that we will do it. Distraction, forgetfulness, impulsiveness, laziness, and “present bias”—the tendency to favor immediate gratification over future rewards—repeatedly derail our long-term goals.

The episode argues that these tendencies should be treated as design constraints rather than moral failings. Instead of relying on willpower alone, we can make worthwhile activities more enjoyable, combine temptations with chores, divide large goals into smaller steps, provide immediate feedback, and use fresh starts to renew motivation.

The discussion then turns to “future-proofing” our lives. Deadlines, carefully chosen defaults, automated savings, and commitment devices can protect our future selves from decisions made in moments of temptation. The central conclusion is that behavior change works best when the intervention is matched to the specific obstacle holding us back.

## Segment-by-segment summary

[00:00] **Why knowing the right answer is not enough**

[Fact] Many serious problems are not caused by a lack of solutions: people already know that exercise, sleep, healthy eating, and responsible spending are beneficial.

[Fact] The difficulty lies in consistently acting on that knowledge while different short- and long-term interests compete within us.

[Fact] The episode examines how people can engineer their environments and minds to make beneficial behavior easier.

[02:09] **The recurring obstacles to behavior change**

[Fact] Katy Milkman identifies distraction, impulsiveness, forgetfulness, and laziness as common barriers to achieving goals.

[Fact] Her own examples include leaving a suitcase on a bus, sending an unnecessarily blunt email, forgetting a breakfast appointment, and struggling to exercise after long days.

[Fact] “Present bias” causes people to overweight immediate rewards, such as eating another snack or buying a new gadget, while undervaluing future health and financial security.

[Fact] Recognizing these weaknesses is a useful first step, but insight alone does not prevent them from influencing later decisions.

[13:28] **Make worthwhile behavior more enjoyable**

[Fact] Engineers in Stockholm turned subway stairs into a playable piano keyboard, after which stair use rose by 66 percent during the short-term experiment.

[Fact] Research discussed in the episode found that people usually focus on the most effective or demanding way to pursue a goal rather than the most enjoyable one.

[Fact] Choosing an enjoyable workout, a pleasant healthy diet, or a fun learning activity can improve persistence because people are less likely to abandon the process.

[16:02] **Temptation bundling**

[Fact] Milkman developed “temptation bundling” by allowing herself to enjoy page-turning audiobooks only while exercising.

[Fact] Pairing entertainment with exercise made her eager to visit the gym, reduced her awareness of the workout’s discomfort, and limited time spent consuming entertainment elsewhere.

[Fact] The technique can also pair podcasts with household chores, treats with studying, or a favorite restaurant with a difficult meeting.

[Fact] Temptation bundling simultaneously increases engagement with a beneficial task and places limits around an indulgence.

[18:50] **Small goals and immediate feedback**

[Fact] People were more willing to commit to saving five dollars per day than 35 dollars per week or 150 dollars per month, even though the amounts are equivalent.

[Fact] Breaking a large objective into small steps can make it feel more attainable.

[Fact] In an experiment involving shower faucets, people who saw their water consumption in real time reduced their usage more than people whose consumption was merely recorded.

[Fact] Immediate feedback makes progress visible and allows people to experience a present reward—such as a check mark or sense of achievement—while pursuing a distant outcome.

[22:18] **Using fresh starts to renew motivation**

[Fact] Gym attendance, diet-related searches, and goal setting rise around temporal landmarks such as a new week, month, year, birthday, or holiday associated with beginnings.

[Fact] These landmarks create a psychological separation between the “old me” and the “new me,” allowing people to distance themselves from earlier failures.

[Fact] Fresh starts also interrupt everyday routines and encourage reflection on larger goals.

[Fact] A fresh start can be harmful when someone is already performing well: baseball players having strong seasons declined more after cross-league trades that erased their season-to-date statistics, while struggling players benefited from the reset.

[27:54] **Designing effective vaccination reminders**

[Fact] A team of 150 scientists tested vaccination messages through Walmart pharmacies and two health systems, using flu vaccination in 2020 to inform later COVID-19 outreach.

[Fact] The tests involved more than 700,000 Americans and compared messages based on social norms, protecting loved ones, humor, and other approaches.

[Fact] The most effective message across all three sites simply reminded recipients that a vaccine had been reserved for them or was waiting for them.

[Speculation] Milkman suggests that this wording may work by creating a sense of ownership, implying a medical recommendation, and making vaccination seem easier, but the study did not establish which explanation was decisive.

[30:38] **When ignorance supports persistence**

[Fact] Graduate student George Dantzig reportedly mistook two famous unsolved mathematical problems on a classroom board for homework and eventually solved them.

[Fact] Because he believed the problems were ordinary assignments, he expected solutions to exist and persisted rather than being intimidated by their reputation.

[Speculation] Not knowing how often others have failed may sometimes help people approach a difficult task with less fear and more attention to finding a solution.

[33:22] **The conflict between present and future selves**

[Fact] The present self receives immediate pleasures, while the future self bears many of their consequences in health, savings, workload, and relationships.

[Fact] People frequently make present decisions—staying awake late or accepting another project—that impose costs on their future selves.

[Fact] Recognizing this internal conflict makes it possible to design rules that protect future interests.

[36:14] **Deadlines as protection against procrastination**

[Fact] In Dan Ariely’s experiment, students worked under evenly spaced deadlines set by the professor, self-selected deadlines, or one final deadline with maximum flexibility.

[Fact] Students subject to evenly spaced external deadlines performed best, those able to create earlier deadlines performed second best, and those facing only the final deadline performed worst.

[Fact] The results show that restrictions can improve performance when unconstrained flexibility would otherwise encourage procrastination.

[38:01] **Turning default behavior into an advantage**

[Fact] Changing a workplace retirement plan from opt-in to automatic enrollment with an opt-out option increased participation by about 35 percentage points.

[Fact] Defaults are powerful because people follow the path of least resistance and may also interpret the preselected option as a recommendation.

[Fact] Default settings have influenced retirement saving, organ donation, and prescription choices without removing people’s freedom to choose.

[Fact] Individuals can design their own defaults by stocking healthier food, changing a browser’s opening page, or automatically transferring part of each paycheck into savings.

[41:56] **Planning now for future savings**

[Fact] The “Save More Tomorrow” program asks people to commit future raises to increased retirement saving.

[Fact] People are more willing to accept a future reduction in take-home pay than an immediate one.

[Fact] Linking higher contributions to a raise makes saving feel less painful because the additional contribution comes from income the employee has not yet become accustomed to spending.

[42:59] **Commitment devices and costly failure**

[Fact] Cash commitment devices impose a predetermined penalty when a person fails to keep a promise to their future self.

[Fact] In smoking-cessation research, participants able to risk money on passing a later nicotine test achieved a 30 percent higher success rate than a control group receiving standard support without the financial commitment.

[Fact] Directing forfeited money to an organization the participant dislikes can make failure especially costly.

[Fact] Victor Hugo reportedly locked away his clothes so that he could not go out socially and would instead finish *The Hunchback of Notre Dame*.

[45:52] **The Ulysses contract**

[Fact] In the story of Ulysses and the sirens, the crew blocked their ears with wax while Ulysses was tied to the mast, allowing the ship to pass the temptation safely.

[Fact] The story illustrates a commitment device: a person restricts future choices in advance, before temptation can overpower judgment.

[Fact] Advance planning is presented as the strongest defense against predictable episodes of present bias.

[48:03] **Treat behavior change as an engineering problem**

[Fact] Milkman argues that people should examine their flaws analytically rather than judge themselves for being impulsive, distracted, or lazy.

[Fact] Effective problem solving begins by identifying the particular force opposing the desired behavior and then designing a strategy around it.

[Fact] One-size-fits-all advice is less useful than matching the intervention—fun, feedback, defaults, deadlines, or commitment devices—to the actual barrier.

[Fact] Understanding personal limitations can increase success because it allows people to work around those limitations instead of depending on willpower alone.

## Podcast review/summary

[Speculation] The episode’s greatest strength is its practical reframing of self-control. It replaces vague advice to “try harder” with concrete behavioral designs that listeners can apply to exercise, saving, work, and other recurring goals.

[Speculation] Its many studies and personal stories make abstract concepts such as present bias, defaults, and commitment devices easy to understand. The distinction between present and future selves provides a particularly clear framework for recognizing why advance planning matters.

[Speculation] A limitation is that several examples are memorable demonstrations or short-term studies, and the conversation does not examine in depth how durable their effects are or how well they generalize across different people and circumstances. Some proposed psychological mechanisms, including why the “reserved for you” vaccine message worked, remain explicitly uncertain.

[Speculation] The episode is especially useful for listeners who repeatedly set reasonable goals but struggle to sustain them, as well as for educators, employers, and policymakers interested in designing environments that make beneficial choices easier.
