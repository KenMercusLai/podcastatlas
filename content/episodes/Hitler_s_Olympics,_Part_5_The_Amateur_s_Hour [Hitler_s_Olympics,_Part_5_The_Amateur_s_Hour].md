# Hitler’s Olympics, Part 5: The Amateur’s Hour

## Overview

This episode examines how Avery Brundage, president of the American Olympic Committee, helped secure US participation in the 1936 Berlin Olympics despite extensive evidence of Nazi persecution. It contrasts his professed devotion to equality and amateur competition with the treatment of Jewish high jumper Margaret Lambert.

Brundage traveled to Germany in 1934 to investigate discrimination against Jewish athletes. Accompanied by Nazi officials and interpreters, he dismissed or failed to pursue testimony about exclusion, violence, and inadequate training facilities, then returned home declaring that Olympic principles would be respected.

The episode considers whether Brundage was merely deceived by Nazi assurances or knowingly accepted their propaganda. His correspondence, antisemitic language, ambition to join the International Olympic Committee, and conduct in Germany lead the hosts to conclude that he understood what he was enabling.

Lambert’s experience exposes the consequences. The Nazis compelled her to train as evidence of supposed inclusion, then excluded her after she equaled the German high-jump record. As Brundage entered the IOC and watched Hitler open the Games, Lambert was seeking a way to leave Germany.

## Segment Summary

[00:43] **Margaret Lambert’s rise and exclusion**

[Fact] Margaret Lambert, born Gretel Bergmann in 1914, grew up in southern Germany and became one of the country’s leading high jumpers.

[Fact] After Hitler became chancellor in 1933, her sports club expelled her because she was Jewish.

[Fact] The episode introduces Avery Brundage as the official whose decisions would affect whether Lambert could compete at the Berlin Olympics.

[03:35] **Brundage’s Olympic creed meets Nazi Germany**

[Fact] Brundage presented amateurism, equal opportunity, and meritocracy as the foundations of the Olympic movement.

[Fact] By 1934, he was seeking a seat on the International Olympic Committee while confronting the question of whether the Games could be held under the Nazi regime.

[Inference] The episode frames his response as an attempt to preserve both his idealized vision of the Olympics and his route into its governing elite.

[04:41] **Sport as an instrument of Nazi ideology**

[Fact] Nazi policies excluded Jewish people from public life and denied Jewish athletes access to the facilities and training available to non-Jewish Germans.

[Fact] Physical fitness served the regime’s military and racial objectives, while the Olympics offered economic and propaganda value.

[Fact] Nazi admiration for an imagined ancient Greek past also made hosting the Games symbolically important.

[Fact] Contemporary broadcasts, press reports, and American diplomatic correspondence documented antisemitic brutality and discrimination as early as 1933.

[08:45] **The boycott movement and Brundage’s ambitions**

[Fact] Calls for the United States and other countries to boycott the Berlin Games gained momentum.

[Fact] Brundage publicly argued that discrimination by class, creed, or race would undermine the modern Olympics.

[Fact] Privately, however, he described the boycott campaign as a Jewish effort to sabotage the Games.

[Fact] US participation mattered because the country would bring a major team, create a dramatic medal contest, and lend the Berlin Olympics moral legitimacy.

[Inference] The hosts suggest that delivering the American team would strengthen Brundage’s prospects of joining the IOC, although they state that they found no explicit agreement making his appointment conditional on it.

[15:07] **The ideal of a “pure” Olympic world**

[Fact] The hosts describe Brundage as a self-made businessman who longed to enter the IOC’s aristocratic social world.

[Fact] They connect his devotion to preserving the Olympics with his disappointing performance as an athlete at the 1912 Games.

[Fact] Even as Hitler consolidated dictatorial power and Germany rearmed, the American Olympic Committee selected Brundage to investigate conditions in Germany.

[Inference] His belief that the Olympics could remain separate from politics allowed him to treat saving the Games as more important than confronting the regime hosting them.

[16:16] **Brundage’s managed tour of Germany**

[Fact] Brundage arrived in Germany in September 1934 without speaking German and relied on Nazi interpreters, including Reich Sports Leader Hans von Tschammer und Osten.

[Fact] Nazi sports literature openly portrayed limited Jewish participation as useful international propaganda that could conceal the regime’s actual persecution.

[Fact] Brundage nevertheless developed a friendly relationship with Nazi sports officials during his six-day visit.

[Inference] The structure of the trip gave Nazi authorities substantial control over what Brundage heard and how it was presented.

[19:04] **The Kaiserhof meeting and the whitewash**

[Fact] At Berlin’s Hotel Kaiserhof, Brundage questioned Jewish sports representatives in the presence of Nazi officials and his IOC associate Siegfried Edström.

[Fact] Jewish representatives explained that their facilities were being closed, their events disrupted, and participants assaulted.

[Fact] According to an account by a Jewish sports official present, Brundage dismissed some complaints, mentioned that his own Chicago club excluded Jews, and showed little interest in reports of violence.

[Fact] Nazi officials promised that qualified Jewish athletes would be allowed to compete, despite their lack of meaningful opportunities to train.

[Fact] Brundage accepted these assurances, returned to the United States, and the American Olympic Committee unanimously voted to attend the Berlin Games.

[24:06] **Deceived idealist or willing collaborator**

[Fact] The episode presents a charitable interpretation in which Brundage was an idealist lulled into false confidence by the Nazis.

[Fact] It then compares him to “Mr. C” from Dorothy Thompson’s essay “Who Goes Nazi?”—an ambitious, socially insecure, antisemitic outsider seeking status and influence.

[Fact] Brundage kept a file labeled “Jewish problem” and continued to portray opposition to the Games as manipulation by organized Jewish interests.

[Fact] Edström had written to Brundage defending Nazi action against Jewish influence, and Brundage later repeated similar antisemitic claims in his own correspondence.

[Inference] The hosts reject the idea that Brundage was simply fooled and conclude that he knowingly aligned himself with the Nazi position.

[29:41] **Margaret Lambert as a propaganda device**

[Fact] Nazi authorities threatened Lambert’s family and pressured her to try out so that her candidacy could be cited as evidence that Jewish athletes received fair treatment.

[Fact] Lambert feared both exclusion and the possibility that, if she won, she would be expected to salute Hitler from the podium.

[Fact] Despite inferior training conditions and no meaningful competition, her anger drove her to equal the German high-jump record shortly before the Games.

[Fact] The authorities then rejected her on the false claim that she had recently performed poorly.

[Fact] Lambert concluded that her candidacy had always been a sham intended to deceive the world.

[33:32] **Brundage enters the IOC as Lambert leaves Germany**

[Fact] The episode argues that Lambert’s treatment should have disproved Brundage’s claims about pure competition and equal opportunity.

[Fact] Brundage obtained an IOC seat one month before the Berlin Games, replacing the only member who had resisted holding them in Nazi Germany.

[Fact] On August 1, 1936, Brundage watched Hitler open the Olympics in Berlin while Lambert applied for a visa to leave Germany.

[Fact] Lambert never again lived in her home country.

[Inference] Their contrasting outcomes encapsulate the episode’s argument: Brundage’s indifference advanced his institutional ambitions while helping the Nazi regime legitimize its Games.

## Podcast Commentary/Summary

The episode’s chief strength is its use of Lambert’s testimony to test Brundage’s abstract claims about meritocracy. Her athletic excellence, deliberate deprivation of training opportunities, and fraudulent exclusion demonstrate how meaningless “equal competition” becomes when political power controls access to sport.

Its second strength is the combination of archival correspondence, contemporary reporting, and the translated account of the Kaiserhof meeting. Together, these sources support the progression from possible self-deception to conscious complicity, while the hosts still acknowledge where motives or institutional bargains cannot be proven directly.

[Inference] The psychological comparison between Brundage and Dorothy Thompson’s “Mr. C” is interpretive rather than definitive and may feel more speculative than the documentary portions. Even so, it provides a coherent explanation for the tension between his outsider identity, craving for elite acceptance, antisemitism, and Olympic idealism.

This episode is especially valuable for listeners interested in Olympic history, institutional complicity, propaganda, and the political misuse of supposedly neutral ideals. It shows how claims that sport is “above politics” can themselves protect political power from scrutiny.
