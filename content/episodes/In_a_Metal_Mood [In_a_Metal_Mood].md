# In a Metal Mood: Pat Boone and the “Right Kind” of Cultural Appropriation

## Overview

Malcolm Gladwell reconsiders Pat Boone, the famously wholesome 1950s crooner whose career included sanitized covers of Black R&B hits and, decades later, a lounge-style heavy-metal album. The episode asks whether Boone merely exploited other cultures or transformed their work in a way that helped new audiences discover the originals.

Comparisons with Georgia Gibbs and Elvis Presley establish a distinction between copying that displaces an originator and reinterpretation that remains visibly different from its source. Little Richard’s changing opinion of Boone supplies the central evidence: Boone’s covers initially harmed him but also helped white listeners discover and seek out his music.

Taco Bell becomes the episode’s culinary analogy. Though inspired by Mexican food, its test-kitchen inventions depart so radically from authentic Mexican cuisine that Gladwell treats them as creative adaptations rather than replicas.

The episode concludes that cultural appropriation can produce something valuable when it transforms its source, acknowledges its own derivative character, and leaves audiences with a reason to return to the original. On that basis, Gladwell launches a campaign to place Pat Boone on the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame ballot.

## 分段落总结

[02:22] **Pat Boone’s unlikely heavy-metal transformation**

[事实] In 1997, Pat Boone released *In a Metal Mood: No More Mr. Nice Guy*, covering artists including Judas Priest, Metallica, Ozzy Osbourne, Led Zeppelin, Deep Purple, and Dio.

[事实] Boone’s public identity as an evangelical Christian and middle-aged crooner made him appear to possess one of the least metal backgrounds imaginable.

[事实] Guitarist Dave Hill argues that Boone’s unwavering commitment to being a crooner can itself be considered “pretty metal.”

[推测] The album provides a deliberately absurd test case for deciding whether radical stylistic borrowing constitutes theft, parody, or genuine creative interpretation.

[06:01] **A campaign for the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame**

[事实] Gladwell announces that the episode officially launches his campaign to secure Pat Boone’s induction into the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame.

[事实] Boone reportedly spent about 220 consecutive weeks on the charts, a record that stood for decades and was later exceeded by Lil Wayne and Drake.

[事实] Despite his commercial success, Boone has never reached the Hall of Fame ballot.

[推测] Gladwell presents Boone’s exclusion as an example of how moral judgments about an artist’s methods can eclipse that artist’s historical influence.

[08:24] **Finding artistic commitment in “Holy Diver”**

[事实] Dave Hill regards Dio’s “Holy Diver” as a quintessential metal song and praises its lengthy, ominous introduction.

[事实] Boone retains that extended opening rather than shortening it, demonstrating a willingness to commit to the original song’s structure.

[事实] Boone then transforms the song into a jazz-lounge or cocktail-party performance whose emotional effect differs sharply from Dio’s threatening original.

[推测] The contrast suggests that Boone is not trying to impersonate Dio; the distance between the two versions is the source of Boone’s creative contribution.

[15:41] **Why Pat Boone remains unacceptable to music insiders**

[事实] Boone built much of his 1950s career by recording versions of Black R&B songs associated with performers such as Little Richard and Fats Domino.

[事实] Former Hall of Fame executive Terry Stewart says most people involved considered Boone inappropriate for induction because they believed he had stolen the music.

[事实] The program describes cultural borrowing as a recurring feature of Boone’s career rather than an isolated experiment on *In a Metal Mood*.

[推测] Boone’s reputation embodies a broader dispute over whether commercial success gained through cross-cultural adaptation should be treated as historical achievement or exploitation.

[17:47] **Taco Bell as the culinary Pat Boone**

[事实] Taco Bell founder Glen Bell was a white entrepreneur who began selling tacos in Southern California during the 1950s and later built a chain around Mexican-themed food and architecture.

[事实] Gladwell initially compares Bell with Boone because both men built businesses by borrowing from cultures outside their own.

[事实] The comparison creates the episode’s central moral dilemma: condemning Boone on appropriation grounds might require condemning Taco Bell on the same grounds.

[推测] Taco Bell allows the episode to move beyond music and test whether the ethical character of appropriation depends on how extensively borrowed material is transformed.

[19:00] **Copying versus transforming**

[事实] Georgia Gibbs’s recording of LaVern Baker’s “Tweedle Dee” closely reproduced Baker’s arrangement, making it the panel’s clearest example of cultural appropriation.

[事实] Otis Blackwell wrote songs for Elvis Presley and made demos whose sound Elvis followed so closely that the panel struggles to distinguish the two singers.

[事实] Gladwell argues that the harm occurs when a celebrated performer takes a work and its creator’s culturally rooted style without materially changing either.

[事实] Public Enemy’s criticism of Elvis is presented as evidence of the resentment his cultural position generated among some Black listeners.

[推测] The episode’s emerging standard judges appropriation less by the mere act of borrowing than by whether the borrower erases, replaces, or redirects attention away from the source.

[25:42] **Why Taco Bell is not authentic Mexican food**

[事实] Glen Bell gradually abandoned hamburgers and developed a menu of inexpensive tacos, tostadas, beans, and burritos before opening Taco Bell.

[事实] Taco Bell does not offer several staples associated with Mexican cuisine, including carnitas, mole, and soft corn tortillas.

[事实] A trial of soft-corn-tortilla street tacos prompted complaints from customers who believed the tortillas were raw.

[事实] Taco Bell personnel describe the brand as Mexican-inspired rather than authentic Mexican food.

[推测] Its products are designed to give unfamiliar customers a recognizable entry point, placing Taco Bell in the business of interpretation rather than faithful reproduction.

[29:44] **The test kitchen as evidence of invention**

[事实] The Naked Chicken Chalupa uses fried chicken as the shell and fills it with lettuce, cheese, tomatoes, and avocado ranch sauce.

[事实] Its creators struggled both to engineer the product and to name it in a way consumers could understand.

[事实] Taco Bell also developed an Indian-market item built around a spiced potato shell and collaborated with Frito-Lay on the technically complex Doritos Locos Taco.

[事实] Gladwell argues that copied products would not require this degree of testing because testing is necessary for invention.

[推测] These creations demonstrate how appropriation can become generative when a borrowed form serves as a framework for something recognizably new.

[35:24] **“Tutti Frutti” and the value of creative distance**

[事实] Boone simplified the rhythm of Little Richard’s “Tutti Frutti,” remained squarely on the beat, and turned it into a smoother pop performance.

[事实] The panel contrasts this approach with white cover versions that closely duplicated Black originals.

[事实] Little Richard initially said Boone’s recording prevented his version from receiving white radio play and being stocked in record stores.

[事实] Little Richard later called Boone’s cover a blessing because it introduced white listeners to him and encouraged them to seek out his original recording.

[推测] Boone’s limitations as an R&B performer may have preserved space for Little Richard by making the source’s distinctive qualities impossible to mistake for Boone’s own.

[38:02] **The “right kind” of cultural appropriation**

[事实] Gladwell concludes that successful cultural appropriation can create something new while widening the audience for the authentic source.

[事实] He contrasts Boone with Elvis, arguing that Elvis’s highly effective versions could eliminate listeners’ perceived need to return to performers such as Big Mama Thornton.

[事实] Boone is compared to a messenger who prepares audiences for an original artist rather than presenting himself as the definitive source.

[事实] Gladwell asks listeners to campaign for Boone’s appearance on the Rock & Roll Hall of Fame ballot.

[推测] The episode’s final defense is not that appropriation is harmless, but that its consequences vary according to transformation, displacement, and the visibility left to the originator.

[41:30] **The difficulty of sounding like Pat Boone**

[事实] In a closing exchange, Dave Hill attempts Boone’s lounge-style delivery of “Holy Diver” but says he cannot reproduce Boone’s swagger.

[事实] Gladwell and Hill agree that Boone’s transformation of the song involves a genuine degree of difficulty.

[事实] Hill says it would be foolish to assume that either Dio’s or Boone’s performance could be reproduced casually.

[推测] The coda reinforces the episode’s claim that an unfashionable style can still involve craft, commitment, and a distinctive artistic identity.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s greatest strength is its memorable comparison between Pat Boone and Taco Bell. Moving between music history and fast-food development turns an abstract ethical debate into a practical distinction among imitation, transformation, and invention.

[推测] Its use of side-by-side recordings is especially effective because listeners can hear how Georgia Gibbs, Elvis, and Boone relate differently to their sources. Little Richard’s ambivalent assessment also prevents the argument from becoming a simple defense or condemnation of Boone.

[推测] The framework remains limited because originality does not by itself resolve questions of unequal access, credit, ownership, or financial reward. The analogy with Taco Bell illuminates creative transformation but cannot fully represent the racial and institutional conditions of the 1950s music industry.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners interested in popular-music history, cultural exchange, authorship, and arguments that challenge settled reputations through provocative comparisons.
