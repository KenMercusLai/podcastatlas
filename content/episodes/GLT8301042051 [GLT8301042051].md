# The September Massacres: Panic, Justice, and Terror in 1792 Paris

## 概览

The episode returns to the French Revolution in September 1792, opening with Danton’s famous call for daring as Paris faces Prussian invasion and internal panic. The hosts frame the episode around the September Massacres, which they describe as among the most horrific events of the Revolution.

The discussion traces how military crisis, rumors of internal conspiracy, factional rivalry, and fear of prison breakouts created the conditions for killings in Parisian prisons. The hosts emphasize that the massacres were not simply a chaotic mob frenzy: many killings followed improvised tribunals, some prisoners were spared, and the violence had a chillingly methodical character.

A major thread is interpretation. Revolutionary sympathizers at the time often presented the killings as harsh necessity or popular justice, while counter-revolutionary accounts turned them into stories of demonic, sexualized brutality. The hosts explore both the factual uncertainty and the moral problem of violence committed by people who believed they were defending virtue and the nation.

[00:41] **Danton’s Speech and the Episode’s Focus**

[事实] The hosts open with Danton’s September 1792 speech calling for defense of the homeland and repeated daring.

[事实] They identify the episode’s subject as the September Massacres, warning that the discussion will be graphic.

[事实] They describe the massacres as mobs or “death squads” breaking into prisons and killing around a thousand people.

[03:37] **France After the Storming of the Tuileries**

[事实] The hosts recap that France is at war, the Tuileries has been stormed, the king and queen are imprisoned in the Temple, and politics in Paris is in chaos.

[事实] They describe a struggle between the Girondins, associated with Brissot, and the Montagnards around Robespierre.

[事实] The Legislative Assembly has called for a National Convention elected by near-universal male suffrage, with the monarchy effectively finished.

[推测] The political order is shown as collapsing into a contest between revolutionary factions rather than between monarchy and revolution.

[08:16] **War Panic and Rumors of Treason**

[事实] The Prussians have crossed the border and the Duke of Brunswick has threatened exemplary vengeance against Paris.

[事实] Paris is full of rumors about noblemen in sewers, hidden weapons, prison breakouts, and criminals conspiring with foreign enemies.

[事实] The Commune, Assembly, and Paris sections are meeting constantly while surveillance committees and troops fill the streets.

[推测] The hosts present fear of “enemies within” as central to the psychology that made prison violence imaginable.

[09:32] **Longwy, Verdun, and Danton’s Mobilization**

[事实] On 26 August 1792, news reaches Paris that the Prussians have taken Longwy, leaving Verdun as the remaining major fortress.

[事实] Some Girondins suggest evacuating the government to Tours, while Robespierre argues that Paris must stand and fight.

[事实] Danton orders city gates closed, barricades raised, and volunteers recruited across Paris.

[事实] The provisional government also warns citizens about traitors within, including former royal advisers, priests, journalists, criminals, speculators, and others.

[12:08] **Raids, Prison Panic, and Radical Incitement**

[事实] By 28 August, Danton has ordered raids across Paris to search for guns, enemy agents, documents, and letters.

[事实] The diarist Rosalie Jullien is cited as an example of educated revolutionaries who believed the city was full of traitors.

[事实] Radical papers and writers openly discuss killing prisoners, including Fréron, Fabre d’Églantine, and Marat.

[推测] The rhetoric of purging traitors helps turn rumor into a practical political demand.

[15:47] **Verdun Falls and the Crisis Peaks**

[事实] The Prussians reach Verdun on 29 August, and news of its surrender arrives in Paris on Sunday, 2 September.

[事实] Church bells ring, cannons sound the alert, posters call citizens to arms, and Danton gives his famous address.

[事实] The hosts say some Paris sections appear to have discussed eliminating prisoners, though later destruction of documents makes the details uncertain.

[事实] When the chief prison inspector warns Danton about prisoner safety, Danton replies that he does not care about the prisoners.

[推测] Danton’s response is presented as morally troubling, even if the hosts debate whether military emergency partly explains it.

[18:36] **The First Killings at the Abbaye and Carmes**

[事实] Around 2:30 p.m. on 2 September, prisoners being moved to the Abbaye prison are stopped, dragged out, and attacked.

[事实] An improvised tribunal is set up, and prisoners are led out one by one and killed with knives, axes, hatchets, and other tools.

[事实] The hosts stress that the first killings are quiet, considered, and methodical rather than a spontaneous frenzy.

[事实] A separate group breaks into the Carmes prison, where many priests are killed after another improvised tribunal.

[21:56] **Death Squads, Sections, and Responsibility**

[事实] The hosts argue that “death squads” is a more accurate term than “mobs” because the killers operated in organized teams.

[事实] Stanislas Maillard is named as one known organizer, though few names of perpetrators are known.

[事实] The hosts say the killings were probably organized at a low level through sections and local revolutionary networks rather than directly by Robespierre or Danton.

[事实] Major political figures knew what was happening and did not stop it; Danton described the deaths as an indispensable sacrifice to appease Paris.

[推测] The episode treats non-intervention and ideological encouragement as forms of responsibility, even without direct command.

[25:00] **The Massacres Spread to Other Prisons**

[事实] On 3 September, killings continue at the Abbaye and spread to prisons including Saint-Firmin, Saint-Bernard, Bicêtre, and the Salpêtrière.

[事实] Victims include priests, petty criminals, forgers, young prisoners, and prostitutes.

[事实] The hosts note that some victims were very young, including prisoners under 18 at Bicêtre.

[事实] Forgers are targeted because sans-culottes associate forged paper money with counter-revolutionary plots and rising grain prices.

[推测] The category of “traitor” expands far beyond political enemies to include socially marginal or criminalized people.

[27:51] **Survival, Tribunals, and François Jourgniac Saint-Méard**

[事实] The hosts discuss François Jourgniac Saint-Méard, a royalist journalist and army officer who survived the Abbaye prison.

[事实] Saint-Méard later wrote an account titled *My Agony of 38 Hours*, describing how he heard prisoners being killed in silence.

[事实] He says a guard helped him prepare for the tribunal, and he survived by giving answers that persuaded the killers he was not a conspirator.

[事实] The hosts emphasize that more than half of detainees in the targeted prisons survived.

[推测] The survival of many prisoners reinforces the hosts’ argument that the violence had a grim judicial logic rather than being indiscriminate slaughter.

[31:05] **The Princesse de Lamballe**

[事实] The hosts introduce the Princesse de Lamballe as a close friend and loyal attendant of Marie Antoinette.

[事实] She is described as a Savoyard princess, widowed young, nervous, socially awkward, and deeply loyal to the queen.

[事实] Revolutionary pamphlets had made her part of a pornographic demonology of the court, including claims about a lesbian relationship with Marie Antoinette.

[事实] On 3 September, she is brought before a tribunal at La Force and refuses to swear hatred of the king and queen.

[36:47] **Lamballe’s Death and the Stories Around It**

[事实] The hosts say what is firmly known is that Lamballe’s body, without her head, was delivered to a section, and that her remains were later privately buried by family servants.

[事实] Many later accounts describe grotesque mutilations, cannibalism, and sexual violence, but the hosts treat many of these claims as exaggerated or fictional.

[推测] The story that her head was placed on a pike and taken toward the Temple is treated as plausible by the hosts, but not certain.

[事实] The hosts connect these stories to counter-revolutionary propaganda and to the pre-existing pornographic language used against royal women.

[40:54] **Ritualized Violence and Competing Interpretations**

[事实] The hosts argue that many atrocity stories were probably invented or exaggerated, while also saying some mutilation or abuse is not inherently implausible during mass killings.

[事实] They stress that the killings often involved tribunals, crowds listening to evidence, and executions carried out in silence.

[事实] They describe the atmosphere as solemn, ritualistic, and linked to ideas of purging sin or disease from the body politic.

[推测] The episode interprets the massacres as violence committed in the name of virtue, which makes them more chilling than random brutality.

[44:16] **Aftermath in Paris and Copycat Killings**

[事实] The massacres last about four days and die down on the morning of 6 September.

[事实] Paris is left with bodies in streets, courtyards, and prison corridors; the Commune sends people to clean the prisons with vinegar.

[事实] Some bloodstains, especially at La Force, cannot be removed.

[事实] Copycat killings occur elsewhere in France, including Versailles, where 44 people are killed and heads are displayed on palace gates.

[45:21] **Parisian Approval and Revolutionary Justice**

[事实] The hosts say many Parisians accepted or supported the massacres as necessary.

[事实] Contemporary newspapers describe the killings as a duty, an extreme but appropriate measure, or a way to prevent prison breakouts.

[事实] Rosalie Jullien calls the violence an “atrocious necessity” and frames it as vengeance for treachery.

[事实] The hosts connect the episode to public execution traditions and the growing use of the guillotine.

[推测] The guillotine appears in the discussion as a way revolutionary authorities could make killing seem more orderly and less embarrassing.

[48:06] **Historians Debate the September Massacres**

[事实] Pierre Caron explains the massacres through the Tuileries fighting, vengeance, panic, and the Prussian advance.

[事实] Simon Schama attacks that contextual approach as morally evasive and compares the massacres to later forms of ideological cleansing.

[事实] The hosts discuss Antoine de Baecque’s research showing that some infamous claims about Lamballe’s mutilation were not supported by section records.

[事实] David Andress is presented as emphasizing popular self-defense, while Jeremy Popkin is described as seeing the massacres as a major problem for positive interpretations of the Revolution.

[推测] The historiographical debate turns on whether context explains the violence without excusing it, and whether revolutionary virtue made atrocity easier to justify.

[55:08] **Toward the National Convention and Valmy**

[事实] Elections to the National Convention are underway while the massacres occur.

[事实] Major figures including Brissot, Robespierre, Danton, and Marat are standing for the Convention, which will meet on 20 September.

[事实] The Prussians continue advancing toward Paris with 80,000 men, while a smaller French army under Dumouriez follows them.

[事实] The hosts close by setting up the coming confrontation at Valmy, which they say will change European history.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] A strong point is the attention to historical interpretation: the hosts distinguish between what is known, what is reported, what is propaganda, and what later historians have projected onto the event. This makes the episode useful for listeners interested not only in the French Revolution but also in how historical violence is remembered.

[事实] The hosts repeatedly warn that the episode is graphic, and the transcript includes detailed descriptions of killings and mutilation claims.
