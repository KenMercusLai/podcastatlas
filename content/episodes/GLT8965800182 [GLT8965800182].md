# Darius and the Road to Marathon

## 概览

This episode traces how a local revolt by Ionian Greeks against Persian rule escalated into a direct confrontation between Athens and the Persian King of Kings, Darius. The hosts frame the burning of Sardis in 498 BC as the decisive provocation that made Persian retaliation against Athens inevitable.

The discussion first builds Darius as a ruler: heir to Cyrus’s vast empire, possible usurper, ruthless suppressor of rebellion, religious-ideological innovator, and highly effective administrator. His empire is presented not only as a military system, but as a cosmic and moral order justified through Persian ideas of truth, order, and the lie.

The second half follows the Ionian Revolt: Aristagoras’s failed attack on Naxos, his sudden embrace of democracy, Athens and Eretria’s intervention, the burning of Sardis, and the eventual Persian destruction of Miletus. The episode ends with the Persian expedition of 490 BC reaching Marathon, setting up the next episode’s battle.

## 分段落总结

[00:00] **Sponsor and Historical Framing**

[事实] The episode opens with a Lloyds sponsorship segment that links dynasties, ambition, leadership, and the House of Wessex.

[推测] The sponsor framing uses dynastic history to prepare listeners for a story about power, legitimacy, and long-term political consequences.

[01:33] **Darius Hears of Sardis**

[事实] The hosts quote Herodotus’s story that Darius, after hearing that Athenians and Ionians had captured and burned Sardis, asked to be reminded at every meal: “Master, remember the Athenians.”

[事实] The event is dated to 498 BC and presented as the moment when Persian invasions of Greece became inevitable.

[推测] The hosts use a comparison with George W. Bush hearing about 9/11 to stress how shocking and politically unavoidable Darius’s response seemed within the Persian worldview.

[03:25] **Why Sardis Mattered**

[事实] The Athenians had crossed the Aegean to join Ionian Greeks, who were Persian subjects on the Aegean coast of Anatolia and had risen in revolt.

[事实] The rebels attacked Sardis, the capital of the Persian governor, captured and burned the lower city, and destroyed a temple of Cybele.

[事实] The hosts say Darius could not ignore such an insult and that the episode will explain the Persian suppression of the revolt and the road to Marathon.

[推测] The burning of a prominent religious site made the raid more than a military incident; it became a symbolic affront to imperial authority.

[06:04] **Darius and the Persian Empire**

[事实] Darius is introduced as the King of Kings and, at this point, the most powerful man on earth.

[事实] The Persian Empire under Darius stretched from the Aegean to the Hindu Kush and the Indus.

[事实] The hosts connect Darius’s power to Cyrus the Great, who had transformed the Persians from provincial obscurity into rulers of the greatest empire yet seen.

[推测] By beginning with Darius rather than Athens, the episode asks listeners to see the coming conflict from the Persian imperial perspective.

[07:12] **Cyrus’s Conquests**

[事实] Cyrus the Great defeated the Medes, incorporated them as junior partners, conquered Lydia and Sardis, and thereby brought the Ionian Greeks under Persian rule.

[事实] The Lydians are described as wealthy and associated with the invention of money; their last king, Croesus, remained a byword for wealth.

[事实] Cyrus conquered Babylon in 539 BC, making his rise to greatness definitive.

[推测] The hosts use Cyrus’s career to show that Darius inherited not just territory, but an imperial tradition of conquest and universal ambition.

[11:13] **Cambyses, Bardia, and Darius’s Rise**

[事实] After Cyrus died in 529 BC, his son Cambyses became king, while the younger son Bardia was entrusted with eastern provinces.

[事实] Darius, a distant royal cousin, served as Cambyses’s lance-bearer, a prestigious position that gave him access to royal secrets.

[事实] Darius’s own account says Cambyses had Bardia killed, a false Bardia then rebelled, Cambyses died from an accidental thigh wound, and Darius killed the impostor.

[推测] The hosts strongly doubt this official story and suggest Darius may have orchestrated a coup against the real Bardia.

[16:00] **Rebellion and Terror**

[事实] After Darius announced his accession, rebellions broke out in Persia, Media, and Elam, the heartlands of the empire.

[事实] Darius took two years to crush these revolts and used extreme punishments, including mass impalement and mutilation.

[事实] A Median pretender was mutilated, displayed, and then impaled after his supporters were killed around him.

[推测] The brutality is presented as central to Darius’s consolidation of rule, not as an incidental feature of his reign.

[20:46] **Truth, the Lie, and Holy War**

[事实] The hosts explain Persian thought as a cosmic struggle between truth and order, called Arta, and the lie, called Draugr.

[事实] Darius cast himself as the agent of Arta and his enemies as agents of the lie, demons, or false gods.

[事实] In 520 BC, during another Elamite rebellion, Darius promised soldiers divine blessings in life and after death if they fought the “faithless” Elamites.

[推测] The hosts present this as a major ideological innovation because it linked conquest, moral duty, cosmic order, and posthumous reward.

[24:08] **Darius as Administrator**

[事实] Darius stabilized the Persian Empire for roughly two centuries until Alexander’s conquest.

[事实] He founded Persepolis as a palace, administrative center, and showcase of power.

[事实] He built a precisely organized tribute system across the empire and was mocked by Persian nobles as a “shopkeeper.”

[推测] The episode argues that Darius’s administrative skill was as important as his military ruthlessness.

[25:35] **Universal Empire**

[事实] The hosts say Darius combined fiscal extraction with an ideological claim that Persian rule reflected divine order.

[事实] Darius portrayed submission to him as absorption into cosmic truth and order, not merely political defeat.

[事实] Peoples outside the empire could be understood as belonging to the realm of the lie.

[推测] This worldview made expansion appear not only permissible but necessary.

[27:36] **The Scythian Campaign**

[事实] Darius targeted the Scythians, whom the Persians called Saka, horsemen of the northern steppe.

[事实] He marched through Sardis, crossed the Hellespont into Europe, bridged the Danube, and entered the steppe.

[事实] The campaign failed because the Scythians used mobility and scorched-earth tactics, making them impossible to corner.

[推测] The hosts treat this as an early example of the difficulty imperial armies face in the steppe regions later associated with failed invasions.

[30:49] **Ionian Tyrants and Miltiades**

[事实] Greek rulers subject to Darius guarded the Danube bridge, including Ionian tyrants who owed their positions to Persian backing.

[事实] Miltiades, an Athenian aristocrat ruling the Thracian Chersonese, proposed cutting the pontoon bridge and trapping Darius.

[事实] The Ionian ruler Histias of Miletus opposed the plan because the tyrants depended on Darius.

[推测] Miltiades’s proposal reveals early Greek resistance to Persia, but also the limits of solidarity among Greek elites tied to Persian power.

[33:40] **Histias Rewarded and Removed**

[事实] Histias ruled Miletus, described as the richest, most sophisticated, and most intellectually important Ionian city.

[事实] Darius rewarded Histias’s loyalty with gifts, land, and favor, including Myrcinus in Thrace.

[事实] Histias was later promoted to royal table companion, which forced him to leave Miletus for the Persian court.

[推测] The hosts suggest this promotion was a gilded cage designed to limit Histias’s regional ambitions.

[36:27] **Aristagoras Takes Over Miletus**

[事实] Histias’s nephew and son-in-law Aristagoras replaced him as ruler of Miletus.

[事实] Aristagoras was ambitious and hoped to build a family power base in the Aegean.

[事实] Miletus was experiencing social tensions, conspiracies, and pressure for democracy.

[推测] Aristagoras’s later rebellion grew from both personal ambition and fear that his own rule was becoming unstable.

[37:29] **The Failed Naxos Expedition**

[事实] Aristagoras proposed a Persian-backed attack on Naxos, an island in the central Aegean.

[事实] Darius approved the expedition, and the Persians supplied 200 ships under the supervision of Megabates.

[事实] The siege of Naxos lasted four months, failed, and left Aristagoras financially and politically exposed.

[推测] The failure forced Aristagoras into a desperate political pivot.

[39:40] **Aristagoras Embraces Democracy**

[事实] Facing likely dismissal by the Persians, Aristagoras abdicated his tyranny and declared support for democracy.

[事实] He urged other Ionian cities to overthrow their tyrants, and many did so.

[事实] Aristagoras then proclaimed a general revolt against Persian rule.

[推测] The hosts present his democratic conversion as tactical rather than principled.

[41:13] **The Ionian Revolt Begins**

[事实] Aristagoras arranged for Ionian squadrons in the Persian fleet to defect, capturing the fleet for the rebels.

[事实] In winter 499 BC, he sailed to mainland Greece to seek support.

[事实] His mission would draw the young Athenian democracy into conflict with Darius.

[推测] The revolt became dangerous to Persia because it moved from local unrest to a wider Aegean crisis.

[42:37] **Athens as a Revolutionary Democracy**

[事实] Aristagoras arrived in Athens in winter 499 BC and could expect a sympathetic hearing.

[事实] Athens had expelled the tyrant Hippias in 510 BC and established democracy in 507 BC.

[事实] The hosts compare Athens’s revolutionary energy to France in the 1790s.

[推测] Athens’s recent democratic transformation made it receptive to an Ionian appeal framed as resistance to tyranny.

[46:16] **Athens’s Persian Problem**

[事实] In 507 BC, Athens had sent ambassadors to the Persian governor in Sardis and offered earth and water as submission while seeking help against neighbors.

[事实] After Athens defeated its local enemies, many Athenians regretted that submission.

[事实] When the exiled tyrant Hippias appeared in Sardis, the Persian governor ordered Athens to take him back, but Athens refused.

[推测] Aristagoras’s appeal offered Athens a way to act first against a Persian threat it already feared.

[48:10] **Athens and Eretria Join the Revolt**

[事实] Aristagoras told the Athenians that Persians were weak, and the Athenians underestimated Persian power.

[事实] In spring 498 BC, Athens sent 20 ships across the Aegean.

[事实] Eretria, a merchant city on Euboea, also joined the expedition.

[推测] The hosts imply that ignorance of Persian scale and capability made the Greek intervention easier to approve.

[49:44] **The Burning of Sardis and Athenian Withdrawal**

[事实] The Athenians and Eretrians joined the Ionians, marched to Sardis, trapped the governor in the Acropolis, and burned the lower city.

[事实] The Greeks became alarmed after the burning of Cybele’s temple.

[事实] Persian cavalry attacked the retreating Greeks near the coast, routed them, and badly damaged the Athenian force.

[事实] The surviving Athenians returned home and ignored later appeals from Aristagoras.

[推测] The raid showed Athens that Persian warfare was far more formidable than Aristagoras had claimed.

[51:52] **Persia Crushes the Ionians**

[事实] The Persians focused first on suppressing the Ionians, which took four years.

[事实] In 494 BC, the Ionian fleet was destroyed near Lade, and Miletus was stormed.

[事实] The hosts describe Miletus’s men slaughtered, women raped, sons castrated, daughters enslaved, and survivors deported into the Persian Empire.

[事实] A tragedy about Miletus’s destruction upset the Athenian audience so much that the playwright was fined.

[推测] The destruction of Miletus became a traumatic warning to Athens about what Persian vengeance could mean.

[54:01] **Histias and the End of the Revolt**

[事实] Aristagoras had already died in 497 BC in Myrcinus.

[事实] Histias returned from Darius’s court, but the Persian governor Artaphanes suspected him of having helped cause the rebellion.

[事实] Herodotus tells a story that Histias sent a secret revolt message tattooed on a slave’s shaved scalp.

[事实] Histias was captured, impaled, beheaded, and his head was sent to Darius, who reportedly gave it a dignified burial.

[推测] Histias is portrayed as clever but fatally untrustworthy.

[56:03] **Miltiades Returns to Athens**

[事实] Miltiades escaped Persian pursuit from the Chersonese and reached Phalerum, the harbor of Athens.

[事实] He was prosecuted in Athens for his tyranny in the Chersonese but was acquitted.

[事实] He was then elected as one of Athens’s ten generals.

[推测] The hosts suggest Athenians distrusted Miltiades but valued him because he was their most experienced fighter against Persians.

[58:02] **Why Darius Turned West**

[事实] The hosts ask why Persia would bother punishing Athens, a small and distant state.

[事实] They argue that delayed punishment risked encouraging other hostile Greek states.

[事实] They also say Darius’s cosmic worldview treated Athens as a stronghold of the lie that had to be purged.

[推测] Persian motives are presented as both geopolitical and ideological.

[60:00] **Mardonius and Persian Pressure on Greece**

[事实] Darius pursued a two-part policy: conquer the land approaches to Greece and intimidate Greek cities into submission.

[事实] His son-in-law Mardonius conquered Macedon, extending Persian rule to the foothills of Mount Olympus.

[事实] Mardonius’s fleet was wrecked off Mount Athos, and he himself was badly wounded.

[推测] Persia was tightening pressure on Greece before launching the more direct attack on Athens and Eretria.

[61:23] **Athens and Sparta Reject Submission**

[事实] In 491 BC, Persian ambassadors demanded earth and water from mainland Greek cities, and many submitted.

[事实] Athens rejected the demand, tried and executed the Persian ambassadors, with Miltiades instigating the legal process.

[事实] Sparta also refused submission and threw the Persian ambassadors down a well.

[推测] These actions made compromise impossible and left Athens especially committed to fighting.

[63:33] **The 490 BC Persian Expedition**

[事实] In early 490 BC, a large Persian army, perhaps 25,000 men according to Herodotus, set out for the west.

[事实] The expedition was commanded by Artaphanes, Darius’s nephew, and Datis, a Mede experienced in Greek warfare.

[事实] Datis was ordered to conquer Naxos and other Aegean islands, then reduce Athens and Eretria to slavery.

[推测] Datis’s command combined punishment, conquest, and practical knowledge of Greek cities.

[65:24] **Naxos, Delos, and Persian Policy**

[事实] Datis captured Naxos within days, looted it, and enslaved its inhabitants.

[事实] On nearby Delos, he made large offerings of incense to Apollo.

[事实] The hosts say the burning of Naxos and the incense at Apollo’s altar together summed up Persian policy: intimidate rebels and reward submission.

[推测] Datis’s actions show Persian power operating through both terror and diplomacy.

[66:17] **The Fall of Eretria**

[事实] The Persian fleet reached Euboea and attacked Eretria before Athens.

[事实] The Eretrians chose to withdraw inside the city rather than oppose the landing.

[事实] After a five-day siege, two Eretrian aristocrats opened the gates, the Persians sacked the city, and the inhabitants were taken onto ships.

[推测] Eretria’s fall illustrates how internal division could be as dangerous as Persian military strength.

[67:31] **The Fleet Turns to Marathon**

[事实] After destroying Eretria, the Persian fleet sailed toward Attica.

[事实] The Persians chose Marathon because it had a sheltered bay, a long beach for ships, and a flat plain suitable for cavalry.

[事实] The episode ends by setting up the Battle of Marathon as one of history’s most famous confrontations between Greeks and Persians.

[推测] The hosts signal that the next episode will question whether Marathon should simply be understood as a battle between despotism and democracy.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it makes Marathon feel less like an isolated heroic battle and more like the outcome of decades of Persian imperial expansion, internal Greek politics, opportunism, revolt, and retaliation.

[事实] The hosts repeatedly use modern analogies, including 9/11, revolutionary France, Afghanistan, Napoleon, Hitler, and the film 300, to explain ancient events to a general audience.

[推测] Those analogies make the story vivid and accessible, though they may also lead listeners to carry modern assumptions into ancient Persian and Greek politics.

[推测] This episode is especially useful for listeners who want narrative history with political interpretation, imperial context, and strong character portraits; it is less suited to anyone looking for a narrow military breakdown of Marathon itself, which is reserved for the next episode.
