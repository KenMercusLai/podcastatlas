# The Formula

## Overview

This episode investigates the supposedly secret formula behind Thomas’ English muffins, especially the process that creates their signature “nooks and crannies.” Producer Ben Naddaff-Hafrey teams up with baker and Culinary Institute of America instructor Rachel Wyman to reverse-engineer the product.

Their experiment reproduces the muffins’ interior structure but not an indistinguishable taste: 61% of culinary students identify the odd sample in a blind test. Yet nearly 80% prefer Rachel’s sourdough version, suggesting that duplicating a famous product may matter less than understanding—and improving—its underlying principles.

The episode broadens the investigation by contrasting Thomas’ corporate trade secret with the Carthusian monks’ closely guarded Chartreuse formula. It argues that a secret can express trust and community, or become a legal and managerial instrument used to control workers.

## Segmented Summary

[00:41] **The Quest to Crack a Famous Muffin Recipe**

[Fact] Ben Naddaff-Hafrey begins investigating the secret recipe behind Thomas’ English muffins and becomes deeply absorbed in reverse-engineering it.

[Fact] The recipe and manufacturing process are said to be known by only seven employees, and owner Bimbo Bakeries sued executive Chris Botticella when he planned to take another job.

[Fact] The episode presents that lawsuit as part of a broader corporate movement to classify information as trade secrets.

[Inference] Reproducing the muffin would test whether the formula deserves its formidable legal and cultural reputation.

[04:04] **A Search for Baking Expertise**

[Fact] Ben visits the former Thomas bakery in Chelsea but cannot gain access to the remains of Samuel Bath Thomas’ oven.

[Fact] Several bakers explain that high-hydration dough creates holes and that industrial ingredients contribute to the commercial muffin’s texture, but they show little enthusiasm for making an exact replica.

[Fact] Ben wants to compare a reverse-engineered muffin with Thomas’ product because he believes the trade-secret claim gave Bimbo excessive power over Botticella.

[Fact] He needs expert help but cannot afford the several thousand dollars requested by one baker.

[07:21] **Recruiting Rachel Wyman**

[Fact] Ben discovers Rachel Wyman through a 2014 episode of *Doughnut Showdown*, where she demonstrates detailed knowledge of flour protein and wins the competition.

[Fact] Rachel teaches baking and pastry arts at the Culinary Institute of America and previously developed products for major grocery retailers.

[Fact] She says reverse-engineering commercial foods was central to her former work, including creating bread served by the Cheesecake Factory.

[Fact] Rachel obtains approval from the institute and recruits her students to help reproduce Thomas’ nooks and crannies.

[10:54] **Iterating Toward the Right Texture**

[Fact] The first experimental recipes fail to produce the desired holes, leading the team to increase hydration and deliberately overproof the dough.

[Fact] Rachel replaces high-quality flour and butter-based griddle preparation with ingredients and methods closer to those listed on the Thomas’ package.

[Fact] Her students observe that their early muffins taste better than Thomas’ and tell her she needs to make them taste worse.

[Fact] After about a week of testing, the team produces muffins with small, deep nooks and crannies that visually resemble the commercial product.

[15:20] **Preparing the CIA Taste Test**

[Fact] Ben visits the Culinary Institute of America in Hyde Park, New York, intending to use its students as blind-test participants.

[Fact] The students’ technical food knowledge makes him worry that they may be unusually capable of detecting differences.

[Fact] Rachel prepares one recipe based on the Thomas’ ingredient list, including vinegar, and another using sourdough as her own variation.

[Fact] The ingredient list alone is insufficient because the outcome also depends on ratios, fermentation, handling, and other process choices.

[19:43] **A Convincing Visual Match**

[Fact] When opened, Rachel’s muffins and Thomas’ muffins appear nearly identical to Ben and the students.

[Fact] A preliminary tasting reveals that several students strongly dislike the commercial muffin, describing its smell and taste as resembling cardboard.

[Fact] The team decides Rachel’s muffins must become stale to better match the packaged product.

[Fact] An uncovered batch becomes too dry, but Rachel finds week-old vinegar-based muffins in a bag that look and taste much closer to Thomas’.

[22:19] **The Triangle Test Fails**

[Fact] Participants receive three coded samples: either two Thomas’ pieces and one Rachel piece, or two Rachel pieces and one Thomas’ piece.

[Fact] The codes are randomized-looking numbers to reduce bias from familiar sequences such as A-B-C or 1-2-3.

[Fact] About 61% of participants correctly identify the different sample, compared with an ideal indistinguishability result of 33%.

[Fact] The result shows that the team has not produced a perceptually identical copy.

[24:11] **Chartreuse and a Different Kind of Secret**

[Fact] Ben compares the muffin formula with Chartreuse, a liqueur made by the Carthusian order from a secret recipe reportedly containing 130 herbs.

[Fact] Former Carthusian Father Michael Halloran explains that Chartreuse supports the monks’ contemplative life rather than a pursuit of wealth or fame.

[Fact] After leaving the order with knowledge of the recipe, Father Michael was neither threatened nor legally restrained; the monks trusted him not to disclose it.

[Fact] He distinguishes a secret, which can be known, from a mystery, which cannot be fully captured in rational terms and must instead be lived.

[Inference] The comparison frames the Chartreuse formula as a communal bond, while the muffin formula is portrayed as corporate mystification used for control.

[30:40] **A Better Muffin Instead of an Exact Copy**

[Fact] A second experiment asks participants which of two muffins they prefer rather than whether they can distinguish them.

[Fact] Because the older vinegar-based batch has been used up, Rachel’s sourdough muffin is compared with Thomas’.

[Fact] Nearly 80% of participants prefer Rachel’s recipe.

[Fact] The team reproduces the characteristic nooks and crannies but creates a muffin that tastes different—and, according to the test, substantially better.

[32:22] **The Executive at the Center of the Lawsuit**

[Fact] Ben eventually speaks with Chris Botticella, the former Bimbo executive accused of attempting to take the secret recipe to a competitor.

[Fact] Botticella says he still loves baking and considers Bimbo a good company, although he dislikes what happened in his case.

[Fact] He dismisses the recipe’s mystique, saying a muffin is not exceptionally difficult to produce.

[Fact] Botticella claims that employees mixing the product can see the formula and that it is not secured in the manner its reputation suggests.

[Fact] Bimbo Bakeries had not responded to repeated requests for comment by the time the episode was recorded.

[34:37] **Secrets, Myths, and Corporate Control**

[Fact] The episode concludes that the best secrets bind people together rather than trapping them.

[Fact] Ben characterizes the Thomas’ nooks-and-crannies story as marketing mythology that acquired the force of a legal standard.

[Inference] The enduring value of a secret may depend less on exclusive technical knowledge than on the community, history, and meaning attached to its source.

[35:29] **The Practical Formula**

[Fact] Rachel’s improved recipe and the vinegar-based version are made available in the episode’s show notes.

[Fact] Overproofing and refrigerating the dough are identified as key steps.

[Fact] Rachel explains that refrigeration slows fermentation and gives the dough enough structure.

[Fact] The episode recommends opening English muffins with a fork because cutting them with a knife damages the nooks and crannies.

## Podcast Commentary/Summary

[Inference] The episode’s chief strength is its ability to turn a narrow food investigation into an accessible examination of trade secrets, labor mobility, trust, and corporate power. The baking experiments give its larger argument a concrete and entertaining foundation.

[Inference] The contrast between Thomas’ muffins and Chartreuse is memorable because it separates secrecy used to sustain a community from secrecy enforced through legal authority. Father Michael’s distinction between a secret and a mystery provides the episode with its clearest conceptual insight.

[Inference] Its limitation is that the experimental results do not prove the original trade secret is technically trivial, since the team did not reproduce the full industrial process and failed the indistinguishability test. Botticella’s claims are also presented without a recorded response from Bimbo Bakeries.

[Inference] This episode is particularly suited to listeners interested in food science, intellectual property, workplace power, investigative storytelling, and humorous deep dives into seemingly ordinary products.
