# The Second Ku Klux Klan: Spectacle, Politics, Scandal, and Collapse

## 概览

This episode traces the second Ku Klux Klan at its 1920s peak, focusing on how it became not only a violent racist and sectarian organization but also a mass social movement wrapped in picnics, parades, family entertainment, Protestant respectability, and political organization.

The discussion moves from the 1923 Kokomo picnic in Indiana to women’s participation, Klan violence in places such as Texas and Illinois, and the internal power struggles that brought Hiram Evans to the top of the national organization. Indiana then becomes the central case study through David C. Stevenson, whose political machine helped make the Klan dominant there.

The episode’s major conclusion is that the second Klan rose quickly because it fused anxiety, bigotry, spectacle, social belonging, and local political machinery. It collapsed just as dramatically because scandals, violence, corruption, political overreach, and the murder of Madge Oberholtzer destroyed its claim to respectable moral guardianship.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **Kokomo and the Scale of the Second Klan**

[事实] The episode opens with a 1923 article from *The Fiery Cross* describing a vast Independence Day Klan picnic in Kokomo, Indiana, as a patriotic and religious awakening.

[事实] The hosts recap that the second Klan had been revived through Thomas Dixon’s *The Klansman*, *The Birth of a Nation*, and William J. Simmons’s attempt to build a profitable fraternal order.

[事实] The Klan’s message of “100% Americanism” targeted African Americans, Jews, and especially Catholics, and at its peak it may have attracted four to five million members.

[推测] Kokomo is used as the episode’s gateway because it captures the second Klan’s unusual mix of spectacle, mass politics, moral language, and extremist exclusion.

[05:15] **The Klan as Family Entertainment**

[事实] The Kokomo picnic was reported as drawing 200,000 people, though the hosts say lower estimates still put attendance at at least 50,000.

[事实] The event included special trains, huge picnic tables, a children’s area, boxing, choirs, circus performers, films, an airplane with a flashing white cross, and an acrobat performing on the plane.

[事实] Similar Klan events across America included barbecues, parades, baseball tournaments, picnics, burning crosses, searchlights, flares, and fireworks.

[推测] The Klan’s public events worked as recruitment infrastructure because they made membership feel ordinary, communal, and entertaining for white Protestant families.

[08:07] **Respectability and Social Normalization**

[事实] The hosts stress that many people remember the 1920s Klan as “good fun,” with costumes, social events, food, and public spectacle.

[事实] Historian Kathleen Blee is cited to show how weddings, christenings, picnics, contests, parades, spelling bees, rodeos, and circuses made the Klan a normal part of white Protestant majority life.

[事实] The episode also warns that this social normality existed alongside vigilante violence.

[推测] The discussion argues that the second Klan’s danger lay partly in its ability to disguise hatred as wholesome civic participation.

[09:24] **Women in the Klan**

[事实] Historian Linda Gordon is cited as estimating that about half a million women joined the Klan.

[事实] Women initially formed unofficial groups before organizations such as the Queens of the Golden Mask, the Ladies of the Invisible Empire, and eventually the Women of the Ku Klux Klan emerged.

[事实] Women organized much of the practical work, including picnics, transport, catering, and party planning, while the Klan also supported women’s voting and work.

[推测] The example of Daisy Barr shows why simple labels such as “far right” or “progressive” can fail to capture the contradictory politics of some 1920s Klan members.

[12:23] **Texas State Fair and the Proximity of Violence**

[事实] At the Texas State Fair in October 1923, an entire day was designated as Klan Day, organized by Dallas Klan number 66.

[事实] The event attracted 150,000 people and advertised fireworks, a masked band concert, Klan drills, rodeos, and social entertainment.

[事实] The Dallas Klan was also known for kidnapping and flogging, and Hiram Evans, later imperial wizard, had been associated with violent beatings.

[事实] Evans gave a speech describing African Americans, Jews, and Catholics in openly bigoted terms.

[14:24] **How Violent Was the Second Klan?**

[事实] The hosts say historians disagree about how central violence was to the second Klan, partly because the answer varies by state.

[事实] The episode distinguishes Klan violence from lynching, noting that lynching peaked before the Klan’s major rise and was often public and daylight, while Klan violence was typically nocturnal and vigilante.

[事实] The hosts still emphasize that Klan violence was serious, especially in the North and Midwest where Catholics and groups such as the Knights of Columbus resisted.

[推测] The segment suggests that focusing only on lynching can obscure the specific forms of intimidation and vigilante control practiced by the second Klan.

[16:54] **Williamson County as a Klan War Zone**

[事实] Williamson County, Illinois, became a major example of Klan violence in a coal-mining region with labor unrest, European immigrants, Catholics, saloons, and anxiety about vice.

[事实] The Klan arrived through church respectability, handing money to a minister who praised them as standing for something good.

[事实] S. Glenn Young led armed Klan squads that raided across the county, took prisoners, attacked immigrants, and provoked protests from French and Italian consuls.

[事实] The Illinois authorities sent in the National Guard, and Young was later killed in a gunfight with bootlegger Charlie Burger.

[21:59] **Internal Corruption and the Fall of the Founders**

[事实] The episode returns to William J. Simmons, Edward Young Clark, and Elizabeth Tyler, who had made large sums from the Klan.

[事实] Regional Klan leaders accused Clark and Tyler of corruption, objected to scandals involving alcohol and sex, and lost confidence in Simmons.

[事实] Tyler resigned, Simmons took a “sabbatical,” and Clark briefly became acting imperial wizard before Hiram Evans maneuvered against them.

[推测] The Klan’s internal politics are presented as a scramble for money, prestige, and control rather than a disciplined ideological hierarchy.

[24:52] **Hiram Evans Takes Power**

[事实] Hiram Wesley Evans was a Texas dentist who called himself “the most average man in America” and used that persona as part of his populist appeal.

[事实] Evans had led the Dallas lodge and was linked to violent Klan activity before becoming central to national membership organization.

[事实] In November 1922, Evans and allied leaders pressured Simmons into accepting the empty title of emperor, while Evans became imperial wizard.

[事实] Evans later bought Simmons off for $146,000, leaving himself in charge of the Klan.

[30:12] **Evans’s Reforms and Political Ambitions**

[事实] Evans tried to clean up Klan corruption by putting recruiters on salary, clamping down on drinking, and calling for vigilante violence to be restrained.

[事实] He wanted the Klan to enter mainstream politics and hired Washington speechwriters and consultants.

[事实] Evans moved toward national political influence and even sold the Klan’s Atlanta “imperial palace” to the Catholic Church to fund the move.

[推测] Evans’s reforms were less a moral transformation than an effort to make the Klan respectable enough to gain political power.

[34:24] **Indiana and David C. Stevenson**

[事实] Indiana is presented as the state where the Klan was most embedded, with a white Protestant population and a strong anti-Catholic atmosphere.

[事实] The hosts tell an anti-Catholic anecdote from North Manchester in which people supposedly mistook a corset salesman for the Pope after a preacher warned that the Pope might arrive by train.

[事实] David C. Stevenson claimed to be a wealthy, decorated war hero but was actually the son of a poor Texas sharecropper, a school dropout, army recruiter, and traveling coal salesman.

[事实] Stevenson had a history of violence toward women before becoming a major Klan organizer.

[39:09] **Stevenson’s Indiana Machine**

[事实] Stevenson became a spectacularly successful Klan recruiter, and in southern Indiana he reportedly persuaded one third of the adult white male population to join.

[事实] He turned *The Fiery Cross* into a weekly newspaper and recruited thousands of paper boys to sell it across the Midwest.

[事实] At the Kokomo picnic, Stevenson arrived by plane in a silk and purple robe and was inaugurated as grand dragon of Indiana.

[推测] Stevenson’s theatrical self-presentation helped make the Klan feel powerful, glamorous, and politically inevitable to supporters.

[43:09] **Power, Corruption, and Predation**

[事实] Stevenson made large sums from the Klan, bought a summer house and a yacht, drank heavily, and cultivated powerful political contacts.

[事实] The transcript describes multiple allegations or incidents of Stevenson sexually assaulting women.

[事实] Stevenson built a political “Military Machine” in Indiana that gathered data on candidates, coordinated endorsements, mobilized ministers, distributed voting guidance, and transported Protestant voters to polls.

[事实] In 1924, almost every Klan-backed candidate in Indiana was elected, from local offices to school boards.

[45:22] **The Klan Enters National Politics**

[事实] By 1924, Linda Gordon’s figures suggest the Klan may have had about 16 senators, 75 congressmen, and 11 governors loyal to it.

[事实] Calvin Coolidge was a Protestant Republican who might have seemed attractive to the Klan, but the hosts say he disliked the Klan, supported Black civil rights, praised immigrants, and did not knowingly appoint Klansmen.

[事实] At the Democratic convention, the Klan favored William Gibbs McAdoo over Catholic New York governor Al Smith, but neither won the nomination.

[事实] A resolution condemning the Klan at the Democratic convention failed by only four votes out of more than a thousand.

[50:24] **Backlash and Decline in Texas**

[事实] After the 1924 election cycle, the Klan suffered a backlash in Texas, where its preferred candidate for governor lost.

[事实] Anti-Klan candidates won across the board, the Texas legislature passed a law against mask wearing, and Klansmen were stripped of public offices and pushed out of the Democratic Party.

[事实] Dallas Klan membership fell from 13,000 to barely 1,000 within a few years.

[推测] The Texas reversal showed that the Klan’s attempt to become a national kingmaker exposed it to public resistance and electoral defeat.

[51:13] **Madge Oberholtzer and Stevenson’s Crime**

[事实] Madge Oberholtzer met Stevenson on January 12, 1925, at a party for the inauguration of Indiana governor Ed Jackson, who was widely seen as the Klan’s puppet.

[事实] On March 15, 1925, Stevenson’s secretary summoned her, after which Stevenson and his men forced her to drink whiskey and took her by train toward Chicago.

[事实] Stevenson raped and assaulted her, and she later bought mercury bichloride tablets in an attempt to kill herself.

[事实] Oberholtzer died on April 14 from infected bite wounds and kidney failure from mercury poisoning.

[55:30] **Conviction and Indiana Political Earthquake**

[事实] Stevenson was charged with rape, kidnapping, assault and battery, and second-degree murder.

[事实] In November 1925 he was found guilty, sentenced to life in prison, and sent to Michigan City penitentiary.

[事实] When Governor Jackson did not pardon him, Stevenson handed confidential papers to the *Indianapolis Times* revealing bribes paid to local officials.

[事实] The scandal damaged Indiana Republicans, took down major officials, and helped reduce Indiana Klan membership from hundreds of thousands to about 4,000 within two years.

[57:13] **Why the Second Klan Collapsed**

[事实] The hosts argue that the Klan’s message was too negative, focused heavily on hatred of Catholics and other enemies, and lacked a positive vision beyond vague patriotism and family values.

[事实] They say the Klan’s secrecy and exclusivity were poorly suited to democratic politics, which required compromise and coalition building.

[事实] Linda Gordon’s interpretation is noted: the Klan may have declined partly because it had won some battles, including eugenics laws and the restrictive 1924 Johnson-Reed immigration regime.

[事实] Public awareness of Klan violence, scandals, the Depression, and Evans’s failed anti-communist and anti-union rebrand all contributed to the movement’s loss of momentum.

[61:29] **The Third Klan and Later Legacy**

[事实] A third Klan was founded in 1946 at Stone Mountain, Georgia, the same place associated with the second Klan’s founding.

[事实] The third Klan was anti-communist and intensely racist, and it became infamous for atrocities including Birmingham church bombings and killings of civil rights workers in 1963 and 1964.

[事实] The hosts say the third Klan never became a mass movement and today has fewer than 3,000 members according to researchers.

[推测] The episode frames the second Klan as more historically distinctive than the later Klan because it briefly achieved mass respectability and political penetration.

[63:07] **Was the 1920s Klan American Fascism?**

[事实] The hosts compare the Klan with European fascism but argue it was not a European fascist party because it lacked a dynamic leader cult, irredentist foreign policy, and an interest in war.

[事实] They identify similarities including victimhood, family rhetoric, hatred of outsiders, paramilitary violence, costumes, parades, rituals, torchlit processions, and airplanes.

[事实] Dominic says there has never been a mass fascist movement in the United States, but that if one had existed, it might have looked like the 1920s Klan.

[推测] The Klan is presented as an American form of reactionary mass politics shaped by local Protestant culture, vigilantism, and social anxiety rather than by European fascist doctrine.

[65:15] **Vigilantism and American Mythology**

[事实] The hosts discuss whether the Klan’s imagery of riding out to impose order drew on the mythologized Wild West and the American cult of the vigilante.

[事实] Indiana’s frontier past and vigilante traditions are linked to the Klan’s local success.

[事实] The hosts compare masked vigilantes in popular culture, including Batman and *Watchmen*, with racist paramilitaries enforcing order outside ordinary law.

[推测] The episode closes by suggesting that the Klan was not an alien intrusion into American history but drew on recognizable American myths of self-appointed justice.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s strongest value is that it refuses to treat the second Klan as only a hooded terrorist group. It shows how the movement operated through entertainment, churches, women’s organizations, newspapers, elections, and family rituals, which makes its mainstream appeal easier to understand.

[推测] Its main limitation is that the discussion is necessarily selective: it moves quickly across states, historians, scandals, and personalities, so listeners wanting a fuller account of Black, Catholic, Jewish, immigrant, or women’s perspectives would need more focused material.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in American political culture, extremist movements, the 1920s, and the relationship between social respectability and violence. It is also useful for anyone thinking about how fringe ideology can become normalized through community life and local political organization.
