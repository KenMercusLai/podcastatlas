# Ancient Roman Holidays and the Origins of Tourism

## 概览

This episode compares modern holidays with Roman elite leisure, asking how far ancient Romans shared today’s desire for sea views, escape, comfort, cultural prestige, and self-improvement. Pliny the Younger’s villa near Rome becomes the starting point: a luxurious retreat whose appeal sounds strikingly modern.

The discussion then moves to the Bay of Naples, presented as the closest Roman equivalent to a high-end resort zone. Villas, oyster beds, heated pools, spas, yachts, and reputations for decadence all appear alongside Roman anxieties about luxury, softness, privacy, and political responsibility.

In the second half, the focus shifts to ancient tourism abroad: Greece, Asia Minor, Egypt, and eventually Christian pilgrimage. Roman elites sought famous sites, mythological resonance, ruins, art, and historical atmosphere, while also looting, staging, restoring, and sometimes inventing the past for visitors.

## 分段落总结

[01:15] **Pliny’s Villa as a Roman Holiday Home**

[事实] The episode opens with Pliny the Younger’s description of his Laurentian villa west of Rome in the early second century AD.

[事实] Pliny’s villa is described as having sea views, a terrace, quiet rooms, a sunny chamber, and spaces designed for rest and contemplation.

[事实] The hosts compare Pliny’s villa to a very high-end modern holiday home, while noting that Pliny owned it outright.

[推测] Pliny’s description is used to suggest that some elite desires around holidays, including sun, sea, silence, and beautiful views, have deep historical continuity.

[04:30] **Roman Holy Days and Public Leisure**

[事实] The hosts explain that Roman “holidays” existed in the sense of state-defined religious and commemorative days.

[事实] Roman games were not always official holidays, but people in Rome could receive time off to attend spectacles.

[事实] These public spectacles are connected to the “circuses” part of the phrase “bread and circuses.”

[推测] The episode distinguishes mass public leisure in Rome from the private villa-based holidays available only to the rich.

[06:13] **Leisure, Luxury, and Moral Anxiety**

[事实] The hosts discuss the Roman phrase *otium cum dignitate*, meaning leisure that did not disgrace the person enjoying it.

[事实] Roman elites were expected to use leisure for elevated activities such as philosophy, reading, or contemplation.

[事实] The Romans are described as nervous about laziness, luxury, softness, and self-indulgence.

[推测] The anxiety around leisure shows that Romans wanted pleasure but felt the need to morally justify it.

[07:42] **The Bay of Naples as a Roman Resort**

[事实] The Bay of Naples is presented as the epicentre of Roman elite desire for sea, fine dining, novelty, and beauty.

[事实] The area appealed partly because it offered a taste of Greek culture without requiring Romans to travel all the way to Greece.

[事实] Educated elite Romans had been taught Greek literature and philosophy, making Campania’s Hellenic flavour especially attractive.

[推测] Campania functioned for Roman elites like a familiar but exotic destination, offering controlled escapism rather than true adventure.

[10:02] **Sergius Orata and Luxury Development**

[事实] Sergius Orata is described as a Roman figure from the 90s BC who developed Campania’s oyster beds.

[事实] He transformed the Lucrine Lake into a major oyster-farming area and attracted gourmands and food snobs.

[事实] He is also associated with heated swimming pools and with developing high-end coastal properties for wealthy Roman clients.

[推测] The hosts present him as a loose Roman equivalent of a tourism entrepreneur or property developer.

[13:10] **Baiae, Spas, and Resort Architecture**

[事实] Baiae is described as the most notorious pleasure resort on the Bay of Naples.

[事实] It was famous for sulphur baths and for spectacular architecture connected to Roman concrete technology.

[事实] The hosts explain that Roman engineers used concrete that could set underwater and developed large domes over hot pools and baths.

[推测] Baiae’s architecture helped make it a landmark of elite pleasure, comparable in social function to later fashionable resort towns.

[15:15] **Puteoli and Broader Urban Entertainment**

[事实] Puteoli, near Baiae, is described as a major port where grain ships arrived to supply Rome.

[事实] It also had hotels, brothels, and entertainment for people who were not necessarily senators.

[事实] Petronius’s *Satyricon* is discussed as likely set in Puteoli and as offering insight into non-senatorial pleasures.

[推测] The Roman leisure economy was not identical to modern tourism, but it included recognisable spaces for travel, entertainment, and paid pleasure.

[16:39] **Yachts, Decadence, and Reputation**

[事实] The hosts say wealthy Romans had yachts in the Bay of Naples and used them for parties and pleasure.

[事实] Baiae had a reputation as a place where elites could let their hair down.

[事实] Vespasian is described as initially refusing to go to Baiae because he saw it as decadent, though he eventually visited late in life.

[推测] Baiae’s appeal depended partly on the same thing that made it morally suspect: it offered privacy, pleasure, and release from public discipline.

[18:01] **Tiberius on Capri**

[事实] Tiberius is described as a highly impressive general, administrator, and successor to Augustus.

[事实] The hosts say he became exhausted by Rome and retired to Capri, where the Villa Jovis was one of several villas associated with him.

[事实] Suetonius records shocking stories about Tiberius’s behaviour on Capri, while other accounts portray him more innocently as interested in gardening, literature, and restrained dinners.

[推测] The discussion frames the darker stories as partly shaped by Roman suspicion of secluded elite privacy.

[23:23] **Absent Rulers and Political Resentment**

[事实] The hosts discuss Sejanus, Tiberius’s powerful deputy, and the resentment caused by Tiberius’s withdrawal from Rome.

[事实] They compare Roman criticism of absent rulers with modern complaints about political leaders taking holidays during crises.

[事实] They then turn to Nero’s Golden House, which senators saw as monstrous because it brought luxury-villa pleasures into central Rome.

[推测] Nero’s project may also be read as a populist pleasure park, giving ordinary Romans access to experiences normally reserved for elites.

[28:38] **Greece as the Roman Grand Tour**

[事实] In the second half, the hosts describe Greece as a destination for Roman elites seeking old-world culture.

[事实] Athens, Sparta, Delphi, and Olympia are named as sites that had deep resonance for Romans educated in Greek literature and philosophy.

[事实] The hosts compare Roman elite interest in Greece with later American tourists visiting Europe.

[推测] Greece functioned as both cultural classroom and prestige destination for Roman elites.

[30:04] **Aemilius Paulus and Touring Greek Sites**

[事实] Lucius Aemilius Paulus is presented as the first recorded Roman figure to take something like a tour of Greek sites.

[事实] After defeating Perseus of Macedon at Pydna in 168 BC, he visited sites including Delphi, Athens, Corinth, Argos, Sparta, and Olympia.

[事实] Livy says some famous places proved less impressive in person than they were by reputation.

[推测] The episode treats this as an early example of a familiar tourist disappointment.

[33:01] **Loot as Souvenir**

[事实] The hosts compare modern souvenirs with Roman generals bringing back ships full of loot.

[事实] Lucius Mummius is discussed as sacking Corinth in 146 BC and taking bronzes, statues, works of art, and people back to Rome.

[事实] Sulla is described as sacking Athens and taking major cultural objects, including the works of Aristotle and columns from a temple of Zeus.

[推测] Roman elite collecting blurred tourism, conquest, cultural admiration, and plunder.

[35:55] **Greek Responses and Roman Display**

[事实] Dio of Prusa is cited as criticising Rhodians for replacing the names on old statues with the names of visiting Romans.

[事实] Dio also says Roman removal of statues could be acceptable because the objects were displayed in Rome rather than left in obscure places.

[事实] The hosts compare this argument with modern debates over the Elgin Marbles.

[推测] The episode suggests that arguments over cultural property have ancient parallels, even if Roman moral assumptions were very different.

[37:00] **Asia Minor, Myth, and Art**

[事实] The hosts say Romans also travelled further east, including to places such as Ephesus and Troy.

[事实] Roman tourists were especially interested in places with mythological, historical, artistic, or numinous significance.

[事实] The discussion links this to Grand Tour travellers seeking uplift, aesthetics, and fun.

[推测] Roman tourism valued places where art, myth, and elite education reinforced each other.

[38:00] **Ruins and Historical Melancholy**

[事实] Byron is discussed in connection with Cicero and Servius Sulpicius’s letter reflecting on ruined Greek cities.

[事实] The passage describes Aegina, Megara, Piraeus, and Corinth as once-famous cities lying in ruins.

[事实] The hosts connect this to the tourist feeling of awe before ruins and the passing of time.

[推测] Roman and later travellers shared a taste for melancholy reflection at ancient sites.

[40:15] **Athens as History and Finishing School**

[事实] Cicero describes Athens as full of historical associations, including the tomb of Pericles and places linked to Demosthenes.

[事实] The hosts compare Athens for Romans to a mixture of Grand Tour Rome and Oxford for Rhodes scholars.

[事实] Hadrian is described as especially devoted to Greece and repeatedly travelling there.

[推测] Athens offered Roman elites both intellectual legitimacy and an immersive sense of cultural inheritance.

[40:43] **Hadrian Rebuilds the Past**

[事实] Hadrian developed Athens on a large scale and completed the temple of Zeus that Sulla had taken columns from.

[事实] He placed Athens at the head of a confederation of Greek cities, trying to restore some sense of its fifth-century BC glory.

[事实] In Sparta, Hadrian supported the revival of staged old customs such as girls wrestling and boys being whipped.

[推测] Hadrian’s tourism was not just passive sightseeing; it involved rebuilding and staging the past he wanted to experience.

[45:23] **Jerusalem and Egypt as Problematic Destinations**

[事实] Augustus praised Gaius for passing through Judea without stopping to visit Jerusalem.

[事实] The hosts say this implies Jerusalem was a possible Roman tourist attraction, especially Herod’s temple.

[事实] Egypt was full of attractions but was politically sensitive because senators were not allowed to travel there without permission.

[推测] Roman tourist curiosity could conflict with expectations of discipline, rank, and imperial security.

[47:10] **The Colossi of Memnon and Tourist Graffiti**

[事实] Roman visitors to Egypt were fascinated by two colossal statues of Amenhotep III, which they believed represented Memnon.

[事实] One statue was said to make a sound at dawn, attracting visitors.

[事实] Tourists paid priests to inscribe their names on the statues, leaving a record of Roman visitors.

[事实] Julia Balbilla, travelling with Hadrian and Sabina, had four poems inscribed on the statue.

[推测] The inscriptions resemble later tourist graffiti, but they also preserve valuable evidence of ancient travel.

[51:00] **Christian Pilgrimage and Invented Itineraries**

[事实] The hosts discuss Christian pilgrimage as a successor to classical tourism, with Jerusalem and Rome becoming major destinations.

[事实] They note that medieval pilgrimage involved excitement and travel but was also dangerous and difficult.

[事实] Helena, Constantine’s mother, is described as travelling to Jerusalem and finding the True Cross.

[推测] Fourth-century Christian pilgrims and local communities helped construct a sacred tourist map that still shapes biblical tourism.

[54:12] **Continuities in Holiday Culture**

[事实] The episode ends by stressing continuities between Pliny, Grand Tourists, Thomas Cook pioneers, early package tourists, and modern travellers.

[事实] The hosts say many holiday behaviours are recognisable across long periods of history.

[事实] They close with references to upcoming episodes on French history through film, Lady Jane Grey, and China in the Second World War.

[推测] The final argument is that tourism changes its infrastructure and ideology, but many emotional expectations remain familiar.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its ability to connect ancient Roman elite practices with recognisable modern holiday habits without claiming they are identical. The strongest thread is the tension between pleasure and moral judgement: Romans wanted leisure, but they worried about what leisure revealed.

[推测] The discussion is especially effective when it moves between material culture and emotion: villas, oysters, pools, yachts, ruins, inscriptions, and pilgrimage sites all become evidence for how people imagined escape and status.

[推测] Its main limitation is that the surviving evidence mostly comes from elites, which the hosts explicitly acknowledge. Ordinary Roman experiences appear only indirectly, through public games, Puteoli, and literary glimpses.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in Roman social history, the history of travel, the Grand Tour, cultural memory, and the long continuity between tourism, status, and the search for meaningful places.
