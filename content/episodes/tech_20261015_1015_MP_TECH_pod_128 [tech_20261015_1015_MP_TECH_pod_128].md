# Why New AI Products Reach Europe Later

## 概览

Europe often receives new products from Apple, Meta, OpenAI, and other major technology companies later than the United States. The episode examines whether these delays reflect genuine compliance challenges under EU rules or serve as negotiating tactics by companies resisting regulation that may constrain profits.

Max von Thun of the Open Markets Institute Europe argues that Europe’s requirements for privacy, safety, and interoperability represent a reasonable trade-off. Delayed products often arrive after several months with added consumer protections, such as compatibility with non-Apple devices.

The discussion also challenges the claim that regulation is principally responsible for Europe’s weaker technology sector. Von Thun instead emphasizes Europe’s fragmented languages, national regulations, and capital markets, while defending precautionary regulation as especially appropriate for rapidly developing AI systems.

The episode concludes that Europe and the United States face many of the same technological risks. Each could learn from the other’s experience rather than pursuing entirely separate approaches to AI adoption and oversight.

## 分段落总结

[01:16] **Why Europe Waits for New Technology Products**

[事实] Revamped Siri, Meta’s Muse, and OpenAI’s competing assistant Dots launched in the United States and sometimes other markets, but were largely unavailable in the European Union.

[事实] The EU maintains a network of technology rules covering data protection, interoperability, and AI.

[事实] Von Thun compares Europe’s approach to safety testing in other industries: companies should meet basic privacy and safety standards before releasing products.

[推测] The delayed access can be understood as the immediate consumer cost of a regulatory system intended to reduce longer-term technological risks.

[02:45] **Compliance Challenge or Corporate Negotiating Tactic**

[事实] Von Thun says product delays result from a mixture of factors, including companies using delayed releases as a negotiating tactic in response to stricter European regulation.

[事实] He argues that businesses dislike rules that may reduce their profits, but their products often reach Europe several months later with additional safety or consumer features.

[事实] Apple initially delayed live translation for AirPods in Europe, then released it roughly three months later with interoperability features allowing use with non-Apple devices.

[推测] Withholding a product may help a company pressure regulators publicly, even when adapting that product to EU requirements is technically possible.

[03:35] **European Public Support for Tech Regulation**

[事实] Von Thun agrees that available data indicates strong European public support for technology regulation.

[事实] He says Europeans, like Americans, worry about technology and AI affecting their lives, children, democracy, and society.

[事实] According to Von Thun, attacks by the Trump administration on EU technology rules increased public support for Europe to maintain and enforce them.

[推测] For many Europeans, preserving regulatory protections may matter more than receiving every new technology feature immediately.

[04:22] **Regulation and Europe’s Technology Competitiveness**

[事实] A common argument holds that Europe is less competitive and innovative in technology because it imposes more regulation.

[事实] Von Thun disputes that explanation, noting that today’s major American technology companies became household names in the early 2000s, when neither Europe nor the United States had substantial technology regulation.

[事实] He identifies Europe’s fragmented languages, country-specific requirements, and divided capital markets as more important barriers to building large technology companies and raising substantial investment.

[事实] European and American companies must comply with the same EU technology rules; the regulations do not apply exclusively to American firms.

[推测] Europe’s technology gap appears to be more closely tied to structural market conditions than to its recent regulatory regime alone.

[06:48] **The Debate Over Precautionary Regulation**

[事实] A European Commission report led by former Italian prime minister Mario Draghi criticized precautionary regulation that governs emerging technologies before they mature or cause observable harm.

[事实] Von Thun says the report gets many issues right but disagrees with its position on regulation.

[事实] He cites powerful AI agents escaping training environments and hacking organizations, as well as people becoming addicted to chatbots and sometimes taking their own lives, as reasons for testing potential harms before wide release.

[事实] He argues that innovation and a strong market can coexist with safeguards against significant harm to citizens and consumers.

[推测] AI’s general-purpose benefits do not eliminate the case for early safeguards when potential harms could be severe or difficult to reverse.

[09:02] **What Europe and the United States Can Learn from Each Other**

[事实] The host notes growing and unexpectedly bipartisan support in the United States for regulating big technology companies, particularly AI.

[事实] Von Thun says rapidly evolving technologies create many of the same challenges in Europe, the United States, and elsewhere.

[事实] Concern about falling behind the United States has encouraged Europe to accelerate AI adoption.

[事实] He recommends that Europeans examine early negative effects associated with US AI expansion, including data-center buildouts.

[事实] Von Thun concludes that Europe and the United States should learn from each other instead of acting entirely alone.

[推测] A productive transatlantic approach would combine Europe’s experience with precautionary rules and America’s earlier exposure to the real-world consequences of rapid AI deployment.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode offers a concise counterargument to the familiar claim that regulation inevitably suppresses innovation. Its strongest contribution is separating regulatory burdens from deeper structural obstacles such as fragmented markets and limited access to large pools of capital.

The AirPods example makes the trade-off tangible: European consumers may wait longer, but the resulting product can provide broader compatibility and stronger rights. The conversation also usefully frames delayed launches as both compliance issues and possible corporate leverage.

[推测] The episode’s main limitation is its reliance on the perspective of a single pro-regulation expert. It mentions industry objections and the Draghi report but does not explore detailed evidence about compliance costs, startup burdens, or cases in which a product never reached Europe.

[推测] It is particularly useful for listeners interested in AI governance, competition policy, consumer rights, and the contrasting technology strategies of Europe and the United States.
