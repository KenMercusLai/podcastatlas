# The Peasants' Revolt: Why 1381 Exploded

## 概览

This episode explains the origins of the 1381 Peasants' Revolt, arguing that the label “peasants’ revolt” is misleading. The hosts frame it less as a spontaneous uprising of the poorest and more as a coordinated rebellion by ambitious, upwardly mobile rural communities in Kent, Essex, Suffolk, and the Home Counties.

The discussion links the revolt to the failing Hundred Years’ War, unpopular taxation, resentment toward royal government, and post-Black Death labor tensions. The poll tax is presented as the immediate trigger, but the deeper grievances involve serfdom, wage controls, legal records, landlords, monasteries, lawyers, and officials.

The episode also explores the radical religious and political ideas associated with John Ball and the organizational role of Wat Tyler. It ends with London effectively under threat from rebels gathering at Blackheath and Mile End, setting up the next episode’s confrontation with Richard II and the royal government.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **John Ball And The Radical Opening**

[事实] The episode begins with Thomas Walsingham’s hostile account of John Ball’s sermon, including the famous line, “When Adam delved, and Eve span, who then was a gentleman?”

[事实] Walsingham presents Ball as arguing that all people were created equal by nature and that servitude came from unjust oppression.

[推测] The hosts use this opening to show how later memories of the revolt were shaped by elite fear of radical equality.

[01:55] **Why “Peasants’ Revolt” Is Misleading**

[事实] Tom argues that the revolt was not simply led by peasants at the bottom of society, but by people closer to “Middle England.”

[事实] The revolt is described as the first major moment when England’s common people appear on the public political stage.

[推测] The episode suggests that the revolt’s historical importance comes from its mixture of social aspiration, political radicalism, and organized pressure.

[03:33] **A Foundational Moment For English Radicalism**

[事实] The hosts discuss how the Peasants’ Revolt became iconic for the British left and for narratives of English egalitarianism.

[事实] Juliet Barker calls it the “Great Revolt” rather than the “Peasants’ Revolt” and argues that the rebels had a radical political agenda.

[事实] Nigel Saul describes the events of 1381 as unique.

[推测] The hosts accept the revolt’s radical reputation while warning that it should not be reduced to simple proto-socialism.

[06:03] **The Hundred Years’ War Background**

[事实] The revolt is placed within the wider context of the Hundred Years’ War between England and France.

[事实] By Edward III’s death in 1377, England had lost much of its earlier success in France and retained mainly Calais and Gascony.

[事实] Richard II became king on June 21, 1377, at the age of 10, creating political instability.

[事实] French attacks, failed English campaigns, and a shipwrecked fleet contributed to a sense that the war effort was going badly.

[08:19] **Taxation And Parliament**

[事实] Under Edward III, taxation required consent from the king’s subjects as represented in Parliament.

[事实] The “commons” in Parliament were local gentry, prosperous landowners, justices of the peace, and similar property-owning figures.

[事实] These landowners believed wage earners should also help carry the financial burden of war.

[推测] The episode presents this parliamentary consent as important for later English history, but also as a source of resentment when taxes became broadly imposed.

[09:49] **The First Poll Taxes**

[事实] A poll tax was introduced in January 1377, requiring most people to pay four pence, except children under 14 and beggars or vagrants.

[事实] A second poll tax followed two years later, with 15 graduated categories from four pence up to nearly seven pounds.

[事实] Money from the second tax was invested in a fleet that sank off Cornwall.

[推测] The failed military spending helped create the impression that ordinary taxpayers were funding incompetence.

[11:06] **Unpopular Princes And John Of Gaunt**

[事实] Thomas of Woodstock led an unsuccessful campaign in France in 1380 and returned in January 1381.

[事实] John of Gaunt was the richest person in England, wanted a throne in Spain, and was suspected of ambitions closer to home.

[事实] His Savoy Palace on the Strand became a highly visible symbol of wealth and inequality.

[推测] John of Gaunt’s prominence made him an easy target for anger about elite privilege and failed war policy.

[13:55] **The Third Poll Tax And Enforcement**

[事实] In November 1380, Parliament reluctantly granted a third poll tax.

[事实] The new tax rose to 12 pence per person for the poorest, a much heavier burden than earlier versions.

[事实] Officials were sent out to find people who had avoided payment, including John Legge, who became notorious for invasive checks on young women.

[推测] The enforcement methods turned tax resentment into moral outrage against royal government.

[16:44] **Officials, Sheriffs, And Robin Hood Resentment**

[事实] Simon Sudbury, Archbishop of Canterbury and Lord Chancellor, and Sir Robert Hales, Lord High Treasurer, were blamed for the crisis.

[事实] Justices of the peace, MPs, sheriffs, and tax administrators also became targets of popular anger.

[事实] The hosts link this atmosphere to the rise of Robin Hood ballads, which expressed hostility toward oppressive elites.

[推测] The revolt drew strength from a broader popular culture of resistance to corrupt or predatory authority.

[18:18] **The Black Death And Labor Conflict**

[事实] The Black Death reduced England’s population from about six or seven million in the 1340s to around three million by the 1380s.

[事实] Labor shortages pushed wages upward, alarming landowners.

[事实] The 1351 Statute of Labourers attempted to freeze wages at pre-Black Death levels.

[事实] Serfs faced restrictions on movement, land sales, labor obligations, court attendance, and inheritance through dues such as the heriot.

[推测] Demographic collapse gave laborers more leverage while making landlords more determined to defend old controls.

[20:44] **Legal Documents As Targets**

[事实] Lords, abbeys, and officials used written records to enforce labor obligations, dues, and wage controls.

[事实] Legal documents, lawyers, scholars, abbeys, and monks became objects of intense hatred among those resisting these restrictions.

[推测] Destroying documents was not random vandalism; it was a way to attack the legal machinery that preserved serfdom and landlord power.

[21:23] **Aspirational Rebels In Wealthier Regions**

[事实] The hosts emphasize that many rebels were upwardly mobile people seeking land, wages, and better social status.

[事实] The revolt was concentrated in Kent, East Anglia, and the Home Counties around London.

[事实] These regions benefited from trade links with Flanders and the export of English wool.

[事实] Nigel Saul is quoted as saying the revolt was a movement of the ambitious and assertive rather than simply the poor and downtrodden.

[推测] The episode presents the rebellion as driven by frustrated aspiration as much as deprivation.

[23:13] **Local Unrest Before The Revolt**

[事实] Before 1381, parts of East Anglia and Kent saw strikes, refusals to work at harvest, mass trespassing, poaching, and attacks on document repositories.

[事实] Juliet Barker’s example of Lakenheath shows villagers resisting officials from the Abbey of Bury St Edmunds.

[事实] In 1379, the entire village was fined for breaking the Statute of Labourers.

[推测] These local conflicts show that the poll tax did not create rebellion from nothing; it intensified tensions already building for years.

[27:09] **The First Violence And Brentwood**

[事实] The first recorded violence against the poll tax in 1381 occurred near Bicester, where tax collector William Payable was beaten and his horse was mutilated.

[事实] The major escalation began in Brentwood, Essex, on May 30, when commissioners tried to uncover people missing from poll tax records.

[事实] Thomas Baker of Fobbing said no one would pay more because they had already paid and had receipts.

[事实] John Bampton tried to arrest Baker, violence followed, and Bampton’s men fled.

[推测] Once villagers had resisted royal commissioners, they had strong incentives to broaden the revolt rather than wait for punishment.

[31:14] **Coordination And The Bocking Oath**

[事实] The revolt was coordinated by leaders with horses who traveled across Essex to connect villages and organize support.

[事实] On June 2, a mass assembly at Bocking swore an oath to destroy certain royal allegiances and accept only laws they themselves chose.

[事实] The hosts describe this as a very radical agenda.

[推测] The sophistication of the organizing suggests that the uprising was planned and networked, not merely spontaneous disorder.

[32:51] **Targeted Attacks In Essex**

[事实] Rebels attacked properties linked to hated officials, including Hospitaller property associated with Sir Robert Hales.

[事实] They burned houses belonging to Sir John Gilsborough, the Speaker of the House of Commons and a figure connected to the poll tax.

[事实] The official responsible for poll tax assessments in Essex was captured, beheaded, and paraded.

[事实] Repositories of documents were systematically destroyed across Essex.

[推测] The violence was both symbolic and practical: it punished officials while weakening the records needed to enforce obligations.

[35:19] **Kent, Rochester, And Canterbury**

[事实] In Kent, anger was intensified by John Legge’s activity and by the imprisonment of Robert Belling in Rochester Castle.

[事实] Rebels marched on Rochester Castle, captured the constable Sir John Newton’s children, and forced Newton to act as their spokesman to the king.

[事实] Kentish rebels sent horsemen across the county, burned legal documents, hunted MPs and JPs, and captured Canterbury.

[事实] In Canterbury, they declared Simon Sudbury deposed as archbishop and burned county records taken from the sheriff’s house.

[推测] The rebels increasingly believed that only royal approval could secure the abolition of their obligations.

[37:40] **Wat Tyler And The March On London**

[事实] Wat Tyler emerges as the best-known figurehead of the Peasants’ Revolt.

[事实] The hosts say almost nothing is known for certain about Tyler before 1381, though some have suggested he may have had military experience.

[事实] Tyler became captain and leader of the men of Kent and led them from Canterbury toward London on June 10.

[事实] The rebels’ demands included abolition of serfdom, freedom to work for chosen employers at negotiated wages, and seizure of church wealth.

[推测] Tyler’s role appears to have been organizational as well as symbolic.

[41:13] **John Ball’s Radical Religion**

[事实] John Ball was probably from Colchester, trained as a priest in York, and repeatedly clashed with Simon Sudbury.

[事实] Sudbury condemned Ball as a vagabond and preacher of doctrines contrary to church teaching.

[事实] Ball attacked wealthy landowners, the church hierarchy, and even the pope.

[事实] The hosts connect Ball’s message with Christian egalitarian themes from Acts of the Apostles.

[推测] Ball’s preaching gave religious authority to social and political rebellion.

[43:26] **The Blackheath Sermon Problem**

[事实] Walsingham claimed Ball preached at Blackheath on June 12, where the Kentish rebels assembled near London.

[事实] The hosts argue Ball could not have given that sermon there because he had only been released from prison on June 11 and no contemporary record places him in London at that time.

[事实] They still describe Ball’s ideas as deeply influential on the rebels.

[推测] Walsingham may have exaggerated Ball’s presence to make the revolt seem more dangerous and heretical to elite readers.

[46:49] **London Under Pressure**

[事实] The hosts describe the revolt as a fusion of material grievances, frustrated aspirations, and apocalyptic religious excitement.

[事实] Rebels from Kent gathered at Blackheath, while rebels from Essex and Suffolk gathered at Mile End outside London’s eastern walls.

[事实] Richard II, Simon Sudbury, Robert Hales, and other elites were inside London.

[事实] The episode ends on a cliffhanger, describing the royal government as facing a genuine popular revolt unlike anything it had previously confronted in England.

[推测] The setup positions the next episode around whether the monarchy can absorb, negotiate with, or crush this unprecedented challenge.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s main value is its layered explanation of why the 1381 revolt happened. It avoids treating the poll tax as the whole story and instead connects war finance, elite unpopularity, labor scarcity, legal coercion, religious radicalism, and local organization.

A major strength is the challenge to the familiar “peasants with pitchforks” image. The hosts repeatedly emphasize that many rebels were ambitious, mobile, and economically active people whose expectations were blocked by old structures of lordship and legal control.

[推测] The episode is especially useful for listeners interested in how revolutions begin: it shows how a specific tax dispute can become a larger attack on law, property, church wealth, and political authority. Its limitation is that it stops before the central confrontation in London, so the narrative is deliberately incomplete.

[推测] It is best suited to listeners who want political and social context rather than a simple blow-by-blow battle narrative.
