# The Titchborne Claimant, Zadie Smith, and Victorian Fraud

## 概览

This episode discusses the Titchborne case, presented as one of the most dramatic legal and social scandals of Victorian Britain. Tom Holland and Dominic Sandbrook speak with Zadie Smith about how the case became the centre of her novel *The Fraud*.

The core story is that Sir Roger Titchborne was presumed drowned after a shipwreck, but years later a large working-class butcher, identified in the discussion as Arthur Orton, appeared claiming to be him. Sir Roger’s mother accepted him, while the wider Titchborne family rejected the claim, triggering long civil and criminal proceedings.

The conversation treats the case not only as a strange inheritance dispute, but as a window into Victorian class politics, empire, race, popular media, courtroom theatre, and populism. Andrew Bogle, a formerly enslaved Jamaican man and key witness, becomes central to the discussion because his testimony and life story connect the case to slavery, Jamaica, Australia, and Britain.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **A Victorian Moral Tornado**

[事实] The episode opens with Arabella Keneally’s description of the Titchborne case as a “moral tornado” that stirred prejudice, justice, anger, ambition, devotion, cowardice, and courage.
[事实] Dominic introduces the case as the centrepiece of Zadie Smith’s novel *The Fraud* and calls it one of the greatest trials of Victorian England.
[事实] Tom says the story contains curses, disputed inheritances, false identities, and a case that obsessed Victorian Britain for years.
[推测] The hosts frame the case as unusually suitable for historical storytelling because it combines legal drama with social conflict and melodrama.

[03:17] **Why the Case Captured Public Imagination**

[事实] Tom reads a passage from *The Fraud* describing the saga as involving aristocrats, Catholics, money, sex, mistaken identity, inheritance, judges, snobbery, exotic locations, working-class struggle, and maternal love.
[事实] Zadie Smith joins the episode and says the case is difficult to summarize because explaining it can consume the whole event.
[事实] She describes Sir Roger Titchborne as a Catholic aristocrat whose ship went down while he was travelling, after which his mother placed increasingly wide advertisements seeking his return.
[事实] About 20 years later, a man appeared with Andrew Bogle, a Black Jamaican former servant of the Titchborne family, and claimed to be her son.

[05:13] **The Claimant’s Implausible Identity**

[事实] Smith says the claimant, Arthur Orton, differed sharply from Sir Roger: Roger had been tall, thin, educated, French-speaking, and aristocratic, while Orton was a large butcher from Wapping with a Cockney accent and no French.
[事实] Despite these differences, Roger’s mother accepted the claimant as her son, gave him £1,000 a year, and then died.
[事实] Her death left the claimant to pursue the Titchborne estate through the courts.
[推测] The mother’s acceptance is presented as possibly driven by longing, perversity, or emotional need rather than reliable identification.

[06:19] **Class, Empire, and Race in Court**

[事实] The hosts note that the case ran through the 1860s and 1870s and was reported in gazettes and scandal sheets.
[事实] Smith says the case placed a Jamaican witness, an eccentric Irish lawyer from Cork, and a working-class Englishman at the centre of English power.
[事实] She says these figures dramatized major 19th-century anxieties about Jamaicans, Irish people, and the working classes.
[推测] The court became a symbolic stage for imperial and class tensions that extended far beyond the inheritance claim itself.

[08:04] **The Titchborne Dole and the Family Curse**

[事实] The conversation turns to the Titchborne Dole, a family legend involving Lady Mabella crawling around land while dying so that its produce would be given annually to the poor.
[事实] According to the story, she warned that if the charity stopped, seven brothers and then seven sisters would be born, after which the family would fall into ruin.
[事实] Smith says the family later ended the dole after too many poor people came for charity, and the curse then appeared to take effect.
[推测] The curse gives the legal case the atmosphere of a Victorian novel, where inheritance, family decline, and moral punishment are intertwined.

[10:29] **Sir Roger Before the Disappearance**

[事实] Sir Henry Titchborne was captured by the French in 1803 during the Napoleonic Wars, and the family line became complicated through marriages and inheritances.
[事实] Roger Titchborne, son of James and Henriette, became heir to the estate after other possible heirs were unavailable or died.
[事实] Smith describes Roger as sporty, violent, not academically impressive, adored by his mother, and unexpectedly important to the inheritance.
[事实] In 1853, Roger travelled to South America in his early twenties after an unimpressive school and military career.

[12:21] **Sealed Letters and the Shipwreck**

[事实] Before leaving, Roger buried sealed letters in the grounds of Titchborne House; they were later found and destroyed.
[事实] During the trial, the claimant was asked what was in the letters and invented a story that Roger had impregnated his cousin.
[事实] Roger later sailed toward Jamaica and his ship was wrecked.
[事实] His mother refused to believe he was dead, consulted clairvoyants, and sent agents to search for him, including in Australia.
[推测] The missing letters functioned as a narrative gap that allowed suspicion, scandal, and invention to flourish.

[15:27] **Arthur Orton, Andrew Bogle, and the Australian Connection**

[事实] The claimant emerged in Australia after a man from Wagga Wagga suggested he might be Roger Titchborne.
[事实] Smith says Andrew Bogle had been enslaved in Jamaica, served Sir Henry as a page, married in England, moved to Australia, and later encountered the claimant in Sydney.
[事实] Bogle said he recognized the claimant, though Smith says that recognition seems impossible to her.
[事实] After Bogle supported the claim, the Titchborne family stopped his £50 annual payment.
[推测] Bogle’s support could be read either as sincere belief or as a calculated risk for a larger future reward.

[18:03] **Bogle’s Ambiguous Role**

[事实] Smith says admiration for Bogle appeared widespread, regardless of which side people took.
[事实] She describes him in the trial transcripts as calm, sane, sometimes witty, sometimes confessional, and more dignified than many others in the courtroom.
[事实] She refers to a Borges story that imagines Bogle as a kind of fiction-maker seeking personal reparations.
[推测] The episode leaves Bogle morally ambiguous, suggesting that sincerity and strategy may both have shaped his actions.

[19:42] **The Claimant Reaches Europe**

[事实] In Sydney, the claimant made major mistakes, including getting his supposed mother’s name wrong and being unable to speak French.
[事实] He sailed to Europe with Bogle, taking the better accommodation while Bogle and his family travelled in steerage.
[事实] In Paris, the claimant avoided meeting Roger’s mother directly, hid in a darkened hotel room, covered his face, and claimed illness.
[事实] Roger’s mother still recognized him as her son.

[22:29] **After the Mother’s Recognition**

[事实] After Roger’s mother accepted him, she gave the claimant £1,000 a year but soon died.
[事实] The rest of the Titchborne family refused to recognize him.
[事实] The claimant then sued the family to pursue the estate.
[事实] Smith says a telling detail was that he visited Wapping and asked after the Orton family when he arrived in London.

[24:00] **Subscription Funds and Working-Class Support**

[事实] When the Titchborne family cut off money, working people in England raised funds to support the claimant’s case.
[事实] Smith says this popular support interested her because working-class people were often treated harshly by the courts.
[事实] She argues that many supporters saw the case as a chance for “one of our own” to win, even though that logic conflicted with the claim that he was an aristocrat.
[推测] The case’s appeal depended less on factual consistency than on emotional and political resentment toward elite institutions.

[25:38] **Courtroom as Theatre**

[事实] Smith and the hosts describe the trial audience as treating the courtroom like entertainment, with food, laughter, and applause during cross-examinations.
[事实] Smith says the line between court and theatre was thin at that moment in British legal history.
[事实] She says courtroom behaviour and legal procedure were very different from modern expectations, with summings-up lasting months.
[推测] The trial became a public performance as much as a legal process.

[26:56] **Edward Keneally and Populist Politics**

[事实] Edward Keneally, an eccentric barrister from Cork, became the claimant’s defence lawyer in the criminal case.
[事实] Smith says Keneally had previously been a poet and behaved in court by telling extremely long stories.
[事实] The hosts discuss Keneally’s unusual religious and historical claims, including his belief that he was a messenger of God.
[事实] Keneally built a political movement around the case through the Magna Charter Association and the *Titchborne Gazette*.
[事实] Smith says the movement included support for working men’s rights, anti-Catholicism, anti-vaccination views, and fringe elements.

[30:08] **Victorian Populism and Modern Parallels**

[事实] Dominic asks whether the case resembles modern conspiracy cultures driven by new media.
[事实] Smith says the analogies are hard to avoid because both involve populism, though she frames this case as left-wing, class-based populism.
[事实] She says public pressure affected the second trial, whose jury was deliberately filled with working people.
[推测] The case suggests that irrational mass politics can still pressure institutions into limited reform.

[32:03] **Andrew Bogle’s Historical Backstory**

[事实] Smith says the plantation scenes in *The Fraud* are based on facts from Jamaican plantation records digitized by UCL.
[事实] She says these records gave names, jobs, children, and plantation details, including Bogle’s birth on Hope plantation.
[事实] She was interested in Victorian lives that passed through many worlds, from plantation slavery to London courts.
[事实] She says the claimant and Bogle became popular figures, with rallies, banners, and merchandise such as models, plates, and cups.

[34:51] **Witnesses, Legal Excess, and Collapse of the Case**

[事实] The hosts note that false or dubious witnesses appeared, including a Dane named Jean-Louis who claimed to have helped rescue Sir Roger.
[事实] Smith says the legal system allowed enormous numbers of witnesses, with discussions of 210 or 250 witnesses.
[事实] The case ultimately fell apart over tattoos: a school acquaintance said Roger had distinctive tattoos that the claimant lacked.
[事实] After the jury decided it had heard enough, the claimant was imprisoned for 14 years.

[36:25] **Aftermath of the Claimant and Bogle**

[事实] After release, the claimant confessed to being Arthur Orton for newspaper money, then retracted the confession.
[事实] He died broke and was buried near Smith’s house in Paddington Cemetery.
[事实] Around 5,000 people reportedly came to see him buried, and a cardboard sign on the coffin identified him as Sir Roger Titchborne.
[事实] Andrew Bogle died poor in King’s Cross and was also buried in a pauper’s grave.
[推测] The burial detail shows that public fascination and ambiguity survived the legal verdict.

[38:24] **The Titchbornes and the Word “Titchy”**

[事实] Smith says the Titchborne house no longer belongs to the family and that their wealth did not seem to continue.
[事实] Tom notes that the baronetcy expired in 1968.
[事实] Smith says one legacy of the case is the word “titchy,” through the music hall performer Little Titch.
[事实] Little Titch was short, round, associated with Arthur Orton in appearance, performed in blackface, and became famous for a “Big Boots” routine.
[推测] The case’s themes continued indirectly through popular entertainment and language.

[40:30] **Writing Historical Fiction**

[事实] Dominic asks Smith whether she feels obliged to keep to historical facts when writing fiction.
[事实] Smith says the facts are very important to her and that she sees little point in manipulating them beyond reason.
[事实] She says the Titchborne case barely needed fictionalizing because it was already like fiction.
[事实] Smith says her main fictional change was the character Mrs. Touchet, whom she extended beyond her usual lifespan or realm.
[事实] She describes the novel as engaging with Victorian novels and the things they hide, especially the colonial spaces where troublesome sons disappear.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s value lies in showing how one inheritance fraud became a lens on Victorian Britain’s deeper structures: class resentment, imperial movement, slavery, newspaper culture, legal theatre, and populist politics. The discussion is especially strong when it uses the claimant and Andrew Bogle to connect a sensational legal case to broader historical forces.

The standout element is Zadie Smith’s treatment of historical fiction. She emphasizes that the facts themselves are strange and powerful enough, and the episode makes clear why the Titchborne case appealed to her as material for *The Fraud*.

[推测] A limitation is that the conversation favours narrative energy over legal precision; listeners looking for a strict procedural account of the trials may find it selective. It is best suited to listeners interested in Victorian culture, literary history, courtroom drama, and the relationship between fact, fiction, and public belief.
