# Heart of Darkness, Joseph Conrad, and the Belgian Congo

## 概览

This episode examines Joseph Conrad’s *Heart of Darkness* as both a major work of literary modernism and a historical response to the Belgian Congo. The hosts connect the novella to their preceding episodes on Leopold II’s Congo Free State, arguing that its atmosphere of horror, corruption, and moral disintegration grows directly out of Conrad’s own Congo experience.

The discussion moves from Conrad’s biography and 1890 journey up the Congo River to the structure and plot of the novella: Marlow’s voyage, the mysterious figure of Kurtz, the violence surrounding ivory extraction, and the famous final words, “the horror, the horror.”

In the second half, the hosts debate what the “darkness” means: Belgian colonial violence, European imperialism, human evil, or all of these at once. They also address major critiques by Edward Said and Chinua Achebe, especially the charge that the book itself reproduces racist assumptions even while exposing imperial brutality.

[事实] Dominic Sandbrook and Anthony Scaramucci announce a live show at Theatre Royal Drury Lane in London on March 30, 2025.
[事实] The show is described as covering U.S. political history and assassinations from Lincoln to JFK, including attempted assassinations such as Ronald Reagan’s.

[04:20] **Opening With Conrad’s “The Horror”**

[事实] The main episode opens with a reading from Joseph Conrad’s *Heart of Darkness*, including Kurtz’s famous final words, “the horror, the horror.”
[事实] Tom Holland frames the novella as historically relevant because it gathers many of the themes discussed in the previous episodes on the Belgian Congo.
[推测] The choice to begin with Kurtz’s death scene signals that the episode will treat the novella as both literature and historical testimony.

[05:35] **Why Heart of Darkness Matters**

[事实] The hosts describe *Heart of Darkness*, first published in *Blackwood’s Magazine* in 1899, as one of the most celebrated novellas ever written.
[事实] Dominic Sandbrook calls it one of the most influential works of literature of the last 120 years and a major example of literary modernism.
[事实] They emphasize its unsettling style: dreamlike prose, shifting time, and narrators within narrators.
[事实] They note its influence on T.S. Eliot, including “Mr. Kurtz, he dead” and “the horror, the horror.”

[07:34] **A Prophecy of European Darkness**

[事实] Tom suggests that *Heart of Darkness* seems prophetic because the darkness located in the Belgian Congo later appears to engulf Europe in 1914.
[事实] Dominic agrees that the novella prefigures twentieth-century cultural commentary on humanity’s capacity for evil.
[推测] The hosts are reading the Congo not only as a colonial setting but as an early warning about violence within European civilization itself.

[08:39] **Imperialism and Cultural Afterlife**

[事实] Dominic argues that *Heart of Darkness* is now the preeminent cultural representation of Western imperialism in Africa.
[事实] The hosts contrast it with *King Solomon’s Mines*, presenting Conrad’s book as the dark inverse of a cheerful imperial adventure story.
[事实] They cite later cultural echoes including the *Far Cry* video games and Francis Ford Coppola’s *Apocalypse Now*.
[事实] They also note that the making of *Apocalypse Now* became its own “Heart of Darkness” story.

[10:26] **From Anti-Imperial Classic to Contested Canon**

[事实] The hosts say the novella was initially often read as a critique of imperialism and the “civilising mission.”
[事实] They explain that many twenty-first-century critics instead see the book as imperialistic and racist.
[事实] Dominic says *Heart of Darkness* has come to symbolize debates over the Western canon, imperialism, Europe’s role in the world, and literary history.

[11:42] **Basic Plot and Conrad’s Claim**

[事实] Dominic summarizes the novella as the story of Marlow, a sea captain who takes a steamboat up the Congo to find Mr. Kurtz, an ivory trader who seems to have lost his mind.
[事实] Conrad described the book as experience pushed only slightly beyond the actual facts.
[事实] The hosts present the novella as both autobiographical and a portrait of a real political situation.

[13:00] **Conrad’s Origins**

[事实] Joseph Conrad was born Józef Teodor Konrad Korzeniowski in 1857 to an impoverished Polish noble family in what is now Ukraine.
[事实] His mother died when he was seven, and his father, Apollo Korzeniowski, a poet and Polish revolutionary, died when Conrad was eleven.
[事实] Conrad was brought up by relatives and wanted to go to sea.
[事实] The hosts explain that his family background also exposed him to possible long conscription in the Russian army.

[14:59] **A Sailor Writing in His Third Language**

[事实] Conrad joined the British Merchant Marine and sailed to places including Australia, Bangkok, India, and Singapore.
[事实] His early fiction drew on maritime and adventure themes but was stranger, darker, and more hallucinatory than conventional adventure writing.
[事实] English was Conrad’s third language, and he apparently did not speak it before the age of twenty-one.
[事实] Dominic describes Conrad as reserved, sensitive, ironic, skeptical about human nature, conservative, and in some ways anti-democratic.

[17:40] **Contract With a Congo Company**

[事实] In 1890 Conrad had already signed a contract with a Belgian shipping firm involved in commerce from the upper Congo.
[事实] The company exported ivory and rubber from the interior and ran steamers and trading stations.
[事实] Dominic says Conrad needed money but also had a childhood dream of going to the blank space on the African map.
[事实] Conrad still appeared to believe in the civilising mission when he wrote to his uncle at the time.

[19:36] **Journey to the Congo**

[事实] Conrad boarded the SS *Ville de Maceio* on May 10, 1890, for what his biographer called the most traumatic journey of his life.
[事实] Before reaching the Congo, another passenger warned him that people who went there either died or came home early.
[事实] About a month later, Conrad reached Boma, the capital of the Congo Free State, then travelled upriver toward Matadi.

[20:32] **Meeting Roger Casement and Seeing Atrocity**

[事实] At Matadi, Conrad met Roger Casement, the British consul and Irish patriot.
[事实] Conrad wrote warmly of Casement, calling him intelligent and sympathetic.
[事实] The hosts connect this stage of Conrad’s journey to scenes of chained laborers, starvation, disease, and forced work that appear in the novella.
[事实] Tom notes that Leopold’s justification for forced labor was framed as progress and technology, but Conrad’s fiction undercuts those claims.

[23:06] **Overland Hardship and Violence**

[事实] Conrad’s diary records mosquitoes, bad nights, lack of water, muddy creeks, heavy sun, cold nights, and misery.
[事实] He passed a skeleton tied to a post.
[事实] He saw a government official beating porters and intervened to stop it.
[推测] The hosts suggest that Conrad’s movement inland made the violence of the colonial system harder for him to ignore.

[24:29] **Leopoldville and the River Voyage**

[事实] Conrad reached Leopoldville, now Kinshasa, on August 2.
[事实] His intended steamer was damaged, so he transferred to the *Roi des Belges*, where he served as second-in-command rather than captain.
[事实] He had fallen out with Camille Delcommune, a Belgian company manager who became a model for the company manager in *Heart of Darkness*.
[事实] The boat travelled around a thousand miles in a month through a lonely, desolate landscape of burned villages and people hiding from the Force Publique.

[27:15] **Stanley Falls and the Seed of Kurtz**

[事实] On September 1, Conrad reached Stanley Falls, now Kisangani, the last navigable point on the river.
[事实] Conrad later described this as the place of his childhood dream and as a crushing end to that ideal.
[事实] The crew picked up Georges-Antoine Klein, a sick French agent with dysentery, who died on the return journey.
[事实] Dominic says Klein was one model for Kurtz, and that Conrad initially named Kurtz “Klein” while drafting the novella.

[29:03] **Illness, Silence, and Disillusionment**

[事实] After returning to Kinshasa, Conrad wrote that he had dysentery and fever and bitterly regretted coming.
[事实] He said the Europeans there were repugnant to him and that he was repugnant to them.
[事实] Dominic notes that Conrad did not explicitly name Congo violence as the cause of his unhappiness in that letter.
[推测] The hosts suggest Conrad may have been shocked by what he saw and alienated the other Europeans by visibly judging them.

[31:36] **Conrad’s Later Verdict on the Congo**

[事实] The paper trail for Conrad’s final Congo weeks disappears, and he is next clearly seen back in Brussels in January 1891 and London in February.
[事实] Dominic says Conrad later reached a severe verdict on the Congo Free State, calling it a vile scramble for loot.
[事实] In 1903 Conrad described Leopold and his agents as a gigantic and obscene beast.
[事实] Conrad also told Cunningham Graham that Roger Casement could tell him things Conrad had tried to forget or never fully knew.

[32:45] **From Sailor to Writer**

[事实] Conrad returned to the Merchant Marine for several years and retired from the sea in 1894.
[事实] He then devoted himself to writing novels and stories for magazines such as *Blackwood’s* and *The Strand*.
[事实] In December 1898 he began the work that became *Heart of Darkness*.
[事实] He described its theme to the publisher as the criminality of inefficiency and selfishness in civilising work in Africa.

[33:45] **Publication and Payment**

[事实] *Heart of Darkness* was published in February, March, and April 1899.
[事实] Blackwood’s paid Conrad £60, which Dominic says was roughly equivalent to almost £50,000 in relative income terms.
[事实] The hosts say this shows how well short fiction and journalism could be paid at the time.

[34:53] **The Framing Narrative on the Thames**

[事实] The hosts explain that Marlow is not technically the narrator; the story is framed by another narrator aboard the Nellie near Gravesend on the Thames.
[事实] The people on the boat resemble people Conrad knew, including a company director, accountant, and solicitor.
[事实] Marlow begins by saying Britain too had once been one of the dark places of the earth.
[事实] He imagines Roman soldiers arriving in ancient Britain and compares empire to robbery with violence.

[37:42] **Ivory, the Unnamed Congo, and Kurtz**

[事实] Marlow’s story is set in the age of ivory rather than rubber.
[事实] The Congo, Belgium, Leopold, and Brussels are never directly named, though the setting is clear.
[事实] The hosts suggest that this lack of naming helps universalize the story.
[事实] Marlow is sent upriver to retrieve Kurtz, a brilliant agent praised for writing, painting, progress, and gathering extraordinary amounts of ivory.

[39:31] **Kurtz as Enlightenment and Violence**

[事实] Kurtz is described as an emissary of pity, science, and progress, and as a figure produced by all Europe.
[事实] He also writes a report for the International Society for the Suppression of Savage Customs.
[事实] That report ends with the phrase “exterminate all the brutes.”
[推测] The hosts read Kurtz as both the ideal of European civilization and the revelation of its violence.

[40:15] **Marlow Reaches Kurtz**

[事实] Marlow’s journey upriver contains distorted versions of adventure-story episodes, including drums, fear, and an attack near the station.
[事实] At Kurtz’s station, the worst suspicions are confirmed: posts are topped with severed heads.
[事实] Kurtz is worshiped by locals as a god, behaves violently, and is sick and emaciated.
[事实] The company manager says Kurtz’s methods are “unsound.”

[42:15] **Kurtz’s Death and the Famous Words**

[事实] Kurtz dies on the return journey downstream.
[事实] His final words are “the horror, the horror.”
[事实] Dominic cites critic David Denby calling this the most famous literary death scene since Shakespeare.
[推测] Tom draws a parallel with Faustus, who sells his soul for earthly riches and is dragged toward damnation.

[43:08] **Marlow Returns to Europe**

[事实] Unlike *Apocalypse Now*, *Heart of Darkness* does not end with Kurtz’s death.
[事实] Marlow returns to Europe feeling changed and alienated from Brussels.
[事实] He feels loyalty to Kurtz and keeps a packet of Kurtz’s papers from the company.
[事实] In the final scene, he lies to Kurtz’s fiancée, telling her Kurtz’s last word was her name.

[45:27] **Break and Podcast Promotion**

[事实] Before the break, the hosts promote another Goalhanger podcast, *Legacy*, hosted by Peter Frankopan and Afua Hirsch.
[事实] The promoted season focuses on Genghis Khan, his empire, violence, postal system, and inconsistent treatment of women.
[事实] Additional advertising follows before the main discussion resumes.

[47:55] **What Does “The Horror” Mean?**

[事实] The second half opens with another reading from *Heart of Darkness*, in which Marlow says Kurtz had judged and had glimpsed a truth.
[事实] Tom asks whether Kurtz’s horror is about himself, the Congo, imperialism, or the human condition.
[事实] Dominic says scholars have tried for over a century to answer those questions.
[推测] The episode treats the ambiguity of Kurtz’s final vision as central to the novella’s power.

[50:00] **Possible Real-Life Models for Kurtz**

[事实] Dominic lists several possible models for Kurtz: Major Edmund Barttelot, Arthur Hodister, Karl Peters, and Léon Rom.
[事实] Barttelot was a British officer associated with Stanley’s expedition and behaved violently before being shot.
[事实] Hodister was a ruthless ivory trader rumored to be worshiped as a god.
[事实] Peters was a violent German explorer nicknamed Hangman Peters, and Rom was a Belgian Force Publique captain linked to killings and human heads around a flower bed.

[52:30] **Kurtz as Literary and Philosophical Figure**

[事实] Dominic says Kurtz is probably all and none of the real-life models.
[事实] The hosts connect Kurtz to literary and philosophical traditions, including the Byronic hero and Faust.
[事实] Kurtz is associated with devils and the powers of darkness in the novella.
[事实] He is also presented as the embodiment of progress and civilization.

[53:39] **Knowledge, Paper, and Colonial Control**

[事实] The hosts discuss Kurtz’s long report as a symbol of the European project in Africa.
[事实] They describe that project as categorizing, mapping, recording, and turning Africa into accounts and ledgers.
[事实] Tom connects this to European colonialism and invokes Edward Said’s critique of knowledge as control.
[推测] The report’s final violent phrase suggests that bureaucratic knowledge and exterminatory violence are linked in the novella.

[54:37] **The Book as an Attack on Colonial Profit**

[事实] Dominic says the novella clearly attacks colonialism at one level.
[事实] Marlow rejects his aunt’s idealistic view that Europeans are improving Africa and says the company exists for profit.
[事实] The skulls, skeletons, violence, and forced labor in the book resemble a charge sheet against empire.
[事实] Tom compares Conrad’s inversion of colonial violence to H.G. Wells transposing imperial horrors back onto Britain in *The War of the Worlds*.

[56:30] **Conrad and the British Empire**

[事实] The hosts complicate the anti-imperial reading by noting that both the framing narrator and Marlow speak favorably of the British Empire.
[事实] Conrad himself supported Britain in the Boer War and wrote that liberty could be found under the English flag.
[事实] Ford Madox Ford later said the British Empire was, for Conrad, the perfection of all human perfections.
[推测] The hosts suggest that Conrad’s critique of Leopold’s Congo does not automatically make him anti-imperialist in a modern sense.

[58:05] **Edward Said’s Critique**

[事实] Dominic discusses Edward Said, the Palestinian American critic and author of *Orientalism*.
[事实] Said argued that Conrad remained imperialist because the novella never finally rejects imperialism or imagines liberation for Africans.
[事实] Tom and Dominic think a more programmatic ending would weaken the literary complexity of the book.
[推测] Their response suggests they value the novella’s ambiguity over a clearly ideological resolution.

[60:00] **Chinua Achebe’s Critique**

[事实] Dominic describes Chinua Achebe’s 1975 University of Massachusetts lecture as a foundational moment in postcolonial literary studies.
[事实] Achebe argued that Conrad was a thoroughgoing racist and that *Heart of Darkness* dehumanizes Africans.
[事实] Achebe objected to the way Africa functions as a place where Europeans discover darkness within themselves.
[事实] Dominic says parts of Achebe’s critique are justified.

[61:31] **Africa as Primeval and Empty**

[事实] The hosts acknowledge that the novella presents Africa as primeval, prehistoric, and unfathomable.
[事实] Tom counters that the silence and emptiness are partly produced by European violence, because people have fled.
[事实] Dominic notes that readers might not always connect these passages unless they read closely.
[推测] The debate turns on whether Conrad’s imagery exposes colonial devastation or repeats older stereotypes of Africa.

[63:03] **The Absence of African Characters**

[事实] Dominic says a stronger critique is that the book has no serious African characters.
[事实] He notes that African speech is often represented as chanting, grunting, or strange incantation rather than as ordinary human language.
[事实] He also says some physical descriptions of African suffering are dehumanizing.
[事实] The hosts agree that Kurtz’s African woman is the point where Conrad’s literary mastery falls into Victorian melodrama and stereotype.

[64:18] **Marlow Is Not Simply Conrad**

[事实] Tom argues that the racist language cannot automatically be treated as Conrad’s own because Marlow is not Conrad.
[事实] Dominic warns that separating author and narrator can be used too conveniently to excuse anything troubling in the text.
[事实] Tom points out that Conrad, if present in the story, is closer to the listener hearing Marlow than to Marlow himself.
[推测] The hosts treat narrative framing as important, but not as a complete answer to the charge of racism.

[65:22] **Limits of Historical Knowledge**

[事实] The hosts say Marlow’s struggle to understand Kurtz mirrors the difficulty of grasping the truth of what happened in the Belgian Congo.
[事实] Tom notes that the novella was written before E.D. Morel began his forensic investigation of Congo abuses.
[事实] He also acknowledges that African accounts of what happened are largely absent from this story.
[事实] Dominic agrees that the Congo series has often been about what Europeans did rather than African perspectives.

[66:27] **Racism, Moral Power, and Reading Literature**

[事实] Dominic says the question of whether the book is racist is less interesting than the moral issues the book raises.
[事实] He says Conrad reflected assumptions of his age but that the overall effect of the book forces readers to confront imperialism, colonialism, and the civilising mission.
[事实] The hosts quote E.D. Morel as calling it the most powerful thing ever written on the subject.
[推测] They reject reducing the book to a verdict based only on whether it meets contemporary ideological standards.

[67:40] **The Darkness in Humanity**

[事实] Dominic argues that the key line is Marlow’s statement that England too had been one of the dark places of the earth.
[事实] He says the darkness is not simply in the Congo or England but in humanity and the human soul.
[事实] Tom adds that Conrad is playing on the phrase “darkest Africa” while turning it back toward London and the Thames.
[事实] The hosts connect imperial racism toward Africans with earlier imperial attitudes such as Romans toward Britons.

[69:17] **Conrad’s Bleak View of Human Nature**

[事实] Dominic says Conrad had a deeply bleak, skeptical, ironic, and conservative view of mankind.
[事实] He quotes Conrad’s letters describing humanity as silly, cowardly, brutal, vain, and motivated by gain and personal advantage.
[事实] Tom links this skepticism to Conrad’s doubt about progress and earthly utopias.
[推测] The hosts present *Heart of Darkness* as an expression of Conrad’s broader pessimism, not only a commentary on one colony.

[70:18] **Late Victorian Darkness**

[事实] Dominic places Conrad alongside late Victorian works concerned with hidden evil and repressed urges.
[事实] He mentions Émile Zola’s *La Bête Humaine*, Bram Stoker’s *Dracula*, and Freud’s *The Interpretation of Dreams*.
[事实] These works share anxieties about darkness, violence, desire, and impulses buried beneath civilized life.
[推测] The episode suggests that *Heart of Darkness* belongs to a wider fin-de-siècle mood of fear about what lies beneath civilization.

[71:41] **Continuing Influence and Final Judgment**

[事实] Tom notes that anti-racist and anti-imperialist works have also drawn language from *Heart of Darkness*, including *Exterminate All the Brutes*.
[事实] The hosts say the echoes and reverberations of the novella are complex.
[事实] Dominic calls condemning the book simply as racist a waste of time because the book is so rich.
[事实] They end by reading the novella’s closing image of the Thames seeming to lead into the heart of an immense darkness.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s main value is that it treats *Heart of Darkness* neither as a simple anti-imperial pamphlet nor as a text to be dismissed only as racist. The strongest parts are the movement between biography, Congo history, literary form, and later critical debate.

The discussion is especially useful for listeners who know the novella but want to understand how closely it draws on Conrad’s 1890 Congo journey. The links to Roger Casement, Georges-Antoine Klein, Camille Delcommune, Léon Rom, and the Force Publique make the historical background concrete.

Its limitation is also acknowledged within the episode: the discussion, like much of the surviving record, is heavily mediated through European voices. African perspectives are discussed as absent rather than recovered in detail.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners interested in imperial history, literary history, the Congo Free State, and debates over the Western canon; it may be less satisfying for those looking for a close reading centered primarily on African testimony or Congolese historical memory.
