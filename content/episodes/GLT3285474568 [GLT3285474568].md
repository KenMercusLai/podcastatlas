# The Rest Is History: The Fall of Magdala and the Abyssinian Hostage Crisis

## 概览

This episode covers the climax of the British expedition to Abyssinia in 1868, when Sir Robert Napier led an imperial force deep into the Ethiopian highlands to free European hostages held by Emperor Theodros II at Magdala. The hosts use George MacDonald Fraser’s novel *Flashman on the March* as an opening hook, but repeatedly stress that Flashman’s role is invented while the emperor, his mountain fortress, the British expedition and the hostage crisis are historical.

The central argument is that the rescue was driven as much by imperial prestige as by humanitarian concern. Britain could not afford to be humiliated by an African king in front of its Indian and Middle Eastern subjects, and the campaign became the most technologically ambitious military operation of its day, with railways, telegraph wire, photographers, elephants and Snider breech-loading rifles. The result was a brutally one-sided victory, the suicide of Theodros and a rush to loot his citadel.

The discussion moves from the cabinet decision in London, through Napier’s logistical planning and the long march inland, to Theodros’s erratic behaviour, the Battle of Magdala, the failed peace negotiations and the emperor’s death with the silver duelling pistol given to him by Queen Victoria. It ends with the campaign’s aftermath: a leaderless Abyssinia, roughly twenty years of chaos, and Theodros’s later status in Ethiopia as an anti-imperial hero.

## 分段落总结

[00:00] **Dramatised opening, Flashman framing and the crisis in outline**

[事实] The episode opens with a dramatised reading from *Flashman on the March*, in which Flashman, in chains, meets a staggeringly drunk Theodros II, King of Abyssinia.

[事实] The hosts remind listeners that the book is a novel, not a work of history, but is deeply rooted in historical records; Flashman’s secret mission is invented, while Theodros, his mountain lair and the British expedition are real.

[事实] They describe the episode as the melodramatic climax of the story and compare it to a British imperial version of the Iranian hostage crisis, joking about Jimmy Carter, railways and elephants.

[事实] By summer 1867 the crisis had been rumbling for around four years: Theodros was furious that Queen Victoria had not answered his letters, and he was holding missionaries, British Consul Captain Charles Duncan Cameron and Foreign Office agent Hormuz Rassam at Magdala.

[推测] The Flashman material gives the episode an entertaining entry point, but the hosts’ main purpose is to separate the fictional narrator from the documented imperial history around him.

[04:24] **The cabinet decides on intervention**

[事实] On 19 August 1867, Lord Derby chaired a cabinet at 10 Downing Street; Derby was a wealthy, classically educated Conservative grandee who worked closely with Benjamin Disraeli and relied on him in the Commons.

[事实] The cabinet decided the situation could not stand, blamed the Liberals for failing to resolve it and feared humiliation in the eyes of Britain’s subjects in India and South Arabia, especially Aden.

[事实] Ministers had already contacted officials in Bombay, put the Indian army on alert and agreed that the authorities in India would meet the costs; a penny was eventually added to income tax to help pay for the expedition.

[事实] Lieutenant General Sir Robert Napier of the Royal Engineers, then commander-in-chief in Bombay, was appointed to lead the expedition.

[推测] The cabinet’s language about face and humiliation suggests that imperial credibility, not only the hostages’ safety, was the decisive political motive.

[06:17] **Robert Napier: the engineer chosen to command**

[事实] Napier was born in Ceylon, now Sri Lanka, into an army family; he had fought in two wars against the Sikhs, commanded the defence of Lucknow during the Indian Mutiny and led troops into Beijing during the Second Opium War.

[事实] He was popular with his men and was described as charming, polite, brave, level-headed and methodical, with no appetite for wasting soldiers’ lives; the hosts compare him to Ulysses S. Grant, though not as corrupt or as boring.

[事实] He was 57, had recently lost his beloved wife and had just married an 18-year-old woman from Montgomeryshire in Wales; she lived until 1930 and died aged 89.

[推测] His engineering background and reputation for methodical preparation made him especially suited to a campaign whose main problems were distance, terrain and supply.

[08:57] **Why Abyssinia looked like a graveyard of imperial confidence**

[事实] The hosts note that recent European interventions had a checkered record: the First Anglo-Afghan War and the disastrous 1842 retreat from Kabul, the Crimean War and the French intervention in Mexico under Maximilian.

[事实] The Abyssinian interior was not properly mapped, consisted of mountains and gorges, offered useless intelligence, had a punishingly hot climate, and carried risks of cholera, typhoid, lions, snakes and baboons.

[事实] Napier’s officers had no relations with the local warlords rebelling against Theodros, did not know how much support he had, where to land, which route to take to Magdala or how to withdraw safely.

[事实] After scouring libraries in Bombay for references to Abyssinia, they concluded in a report that there were “few countries about which we know less.”

[推测] The hosts present the expedition as a much riskier gamble than later imperial triumphalism made it appear.

[11:17] **Napier’s plan and the imperial supply machine**

[事实] Napier worked backwards from the rescue: 5,000 men would be needed to extract the hostages, another 10,000 to guard supply lines stretching hundreds of miles back to the coast, and a coastal base with connections to Suez and Bombay.

[事实] The expedition drew on resources across the British Empire and calculated supplies in extraordinary detail, including salt beef, salt pork, vinegar, mustard, rum, champagne, beer, Bibles, prayer books, pickaxes and gunpowder.

[事实] One especially revealing item was copper telegraph wire; the campaign was described at the time as rivalling the adventures of Alexander the Great, Cortés or Pizarro and as the most technologically advanced military campaign in history.

[事实] Napier took seven photographers, a portable telegraph system, water pumps and flares, along with geographers, meteorologists, a geologist from Calcutta, a British Museum archaeologist and pressmen including Henry Morton Stanley and G. A. Henty.

[推测] The level of detail shows that the expedition was as much an exercise in imperial logistical spectacle and state capacity as a narrow hostage-rescue operation.

[14:01] **Cash, currency and the cost of imperial prestige**

[事实] The expedition needed cash to buy supplies and pay local warlords; the preferred currency in the region was the Austrian silver Maria Theresa tala, first minted by Maria Theresa in 1741, with the 1780 edition especially prized.

[事实] Napier’s agents bought up old coins in Central Europe, and the official mint in Vienna produced 20,000 talas a week for the expedition; the coins were still being used as currency in East Africa in the 1950s.

[事实] Derby’s government initially budgeted £2 million, which ballooned to £4 million before departure and ended up costing the British taxpayer £10 million, equivalent to tens of billions of pounds today.

[事实] The hosts describe the campaign as a surgical strike with no plans to occupy Abyssinia or change its regime, but argue that prestige in the eyes of the Ottomans and French was inseparable from the humanitarian case.

[事实] A rhyme in *Punch* declared, “dash the expense, however immense, we can nothing cheaper than fight,” which the hosts interpret as meaning that losing face would cost more than the money.

[推测] In practice, British policymakers appear to have treated the hostages’ plight and imperial credibility as a single political problem.

[17:58] **Landing at Anersley Bay and the advance inland**

[事实] By the final week of October 1867, an advance party of Bombay Marines, Bombay cavalry, engineers and sappers had landed at Anersley Bay, now the Gulf of Zula in Eritrea, and found an anchorage for 100 ships.

[事实] They sent back specifications for three large piers, and by the new year of 1868 Britain had landed 13,000 soldiers, 20 miles of railway track and 44 elephants shipped across the Indian Ocean in specially reinforced transports, seven more elephants than Hannibal took to Italy.

[事实] The force also brought hundreds of tons of coal, tramway sets and pile-driving machinery; sappers blasted cliffs to find a route up to the highland plateau, which lay 8,000 feet above sea level, followed by another 350 miles through gorges and ravines to Magdala.

[事实] On 7 January 1868 Napier came ashore from the SS *Octavia* in full uniform and medals, with ship’s guns firing a salute and a Marine band playing; he immediately demanded more mule drivers and boots for his men.

[事实] Within a couple of weeks the British had begun building a railway and had established a field telegraph to their advance guard, unspooling copper wire as they moved.

[推测] The ceremony of Napier’s landing and the scale of the engineering were intended to overawe local powers as much as to move supplies.

[20:13] **The march to Magdala and the diplomacy of Ras Kassa**

[事实] On 25 January 1868 Napier set off with 5,000 men as the spearhead and 7,000 behind to guard the supply lines; the column was British and Indian, including Sikhs and recruits from across the subcontinent, many in turbans but all wearing British army red jackets.

[事实] Behind them stretched a supply chain seven miles long, with 44 elephants dragging heavy field guns and military bands playing as they marched.

[事实] At the end of February they entered Tigray, the kingdom of Theodros’s rival Ras Kassa, through which the road to Magdala ran; Napier stopped, greeted him with full military honours, offered gifts including an Arabian horse, a rifle and glassware, and promised that his troops would not loot or steal and would pay for every sack of grain.

[事实] Napier also promised Ras Kassa the friendship of Queen Victoria; Ras Kassa gave him a lion-skin cloak and agreed to send weekly supplies, which he did.

[推测] There was an unwritten quid pro quo: Ras Kassa expected Napier to deal with Theodros once and for all, and the British needed safe passage and local supply.

[23:28] **The hostages at Magdala and the race with Theodros**

[事实] The hostages had been stuck in huts at Magdala since the summer of 1867, chained, playing cards and reading the Bible, but eating unusually well from old stores, including four-course meals; some were ill and depressed.

[事实] In December 1867 a traveller brought Dr Blanc news that the British had landed on the coast; Blanc later described the joy of hearing it after months of mental agony.

[事实] The hostages were then in unbearable tension over whether Theodros would return to Magdala before the British and whether he would kill them or agree to see sense.

[事实] Theodros had been away on campaign for nearly two years, fighting rebels, and in autumn 1867 he began heading back towards Magdala with about 50,000 people, including camp followers and prisoners.

[推测] The hostages’ relatively comfortable diet contrasted sharply with their legal captivity and the psychological strain of not knowing whether they would survive.

[25:02] **Theodros returns with his guns and his delusions**

[事实] Theodros brought his favourite guns with him, including a 70-ton mortar with a two-foot-wide mouth, cast by Swiss and German missionaries and named Sebastopol because of his love of the Crimean War; Napier had assumed he would leave such heavy guns behind, but Theodros dragged them back to Magdala.

[事实] By early 1868 Theodros knew the British had landed and had intercepted messages from Napier to local chiefs; instead of a coherent defensive plan, he said he had always wanted to see a disciplined European army and planned to test his guns, impress the British and sign a treaty.

[事实] He told his courtiers about a prophecy that a European king would meet an Ethiopian king, after which a great king would come, and said he did not know whether he would be that great king or someone else.

[事实] He also resumed writing affectionate letters to Rassam, his hostage, saying he was looking forward to showing him Sebastopol.

[事实] On 27 March 1868 Theodros arrived at Magdala in a storm; Rassam was shocked at how gaunt and haggard he looked; Theodros greeted him with “today we must all be English,” said the two happiest days of his life were the casting of Sebastopol and seeing Rassam again, then feared being despised because he was black and spoke of Rassam cursing his corpse, before turning bitterly and calling Rassam a fool who had plotted to ravish their wives and reduce their people to bondage.

[推测] His behaviour suggests grandiosity, isolation and a paranoid attachment to Rassam rather than a workable military or political strategy.

[32:15] **The British close in and Theodros prepares for battle**

[事实] By early April Napier’s men had reached the Jidda Gorge, about 50 miles from Magdala, after travelling more than 300 miles; on 6 April they caught their first glimpse of the summit of Magdala.

[事实] Napier sent ahead a final ultimatum demanding the release of Rassam, Cameron, Dr Blanc, Lieutenant Pridot and the other Europeans in Theodros’s power; there was no reply.

[事实] Theodros scanned the mountains with his telescope, and on 7 April, the Tuesday before Easter, he saw the first red British uniforms across the Bechelor gorge; he shouted excitedly, “hurrah, there go the donkeys,” and told the Swiss missionary Theophilus Valbair, “there you will see your brethren who have come from England to kill me.”

[事实] The next day he dressed in a gold-threaded silk robe and white pantaloons; Rassam said he looked more like a harlequin than a sovereign; Theodros released 70 Abyssinian captives and gave his army a speech acknowledging that the British were better equipped and stronger, with gold-bedecked dress and treasures borne by elephants, before urging them to enrich themselves with the spoils of “these white slaves.”

[事实] A grizzled veteran said they would tear the “asses” to pieces once they appeared; Theodros called him an old fool and warned that their bellies would be filled with bullets before they knew where they were.

[推测] Theodros’s theatrical dress and contradictory rhetoric show him trying to control a situation that he no longer understood.

[36:47] **The massacre of prisoners before Good Friday**

[事实] On the Thursday before Easter, after drinking heavily and sleeping it off, Theodros was woken by prisoners complaining that the guards were too slow to remove their shackles; he called for a sword, stabbed two prisoners to death and ordered about 300 remaining prisoners to be thrown over the cliff, with guards sent down to finish off survivors.

[事实] The killing took two hours, and that evening a servant heard Theodros praying aloud, confessing to God that he had been drunk when he ordered the massacre and asking forgiveness because drink had been taken.

[推测] The massacre marks Theodros’s moral and mental collapse on the eve of battle and explains why the hostages feared he might kill them all.

[40:13] **Good Friday and the Battle of Magdala**

[事实] On Good Friday, 11 April 1868, Theodros ordered the European hostages back up to Magdala; Rassam urged him to ask Napier for terms, but Theodros refused, saying Napier had been sent by a woman, meaning Queen Victoria.

[事实] Theodros kept about 7,000 loyalists on the heights with his guns and sent them to charge the British as soon as they reached the plateau, telling them, “take leave of your families, for today you will have the honour to die with a king.”

[事实] Napier’s men climbed through punishing heat with heavy packs and little water; Henry Morton Stanley remembered a terrible, suspicious silence, and the hosts say many feared this would be “Afghanistan Mark II”; for the first time, at least a proportion of a British army went into combat wearing khaki rather than red.

[事实] Around four o’clock Theodros shouted that the British were like the Philistines who made war against David, called them slaves and women and urged his men to help themselves to their riches; the storm broke, he ordered his gunners to open fire, and the Abyssinians charged with spears and war songs.

[事实] The guns were so inaccurate that shells sailed over the British; one gun exploded; the Abyssinians expected the British to have to reload, but the British had Snider breech-loading rifles and could fire about 30 to 40 rounds a second across the line, so the expected pause never came.

[事实] The first to fall were Abyssinian chiefs and captains wearing bright scarlet shirts; hundreds were shot down as they kept charging, and Theodros watched from the heights in disbelief.

[推测] The battle demonstrated the decisive advantage of industrial firepower over massed courage, making the outcome brutally one-sided.

[47:56] **Casualties, peace overtures and the failed suicide**

[事实] After about two hours the fighting died down; Theodros had lost just under 1,000 men dead and thousands wounded, while the British had two men killed and about twenty wounded.

[事实] Theodros sent Rassam a letter saying he had attacked first because he could not allow people to come and fight him, that he had thought the British were women but found they were men, and that he now needed Rassam to reconcile him with them.

[事实] Rassam suggested sending the Reverend Flad and Lieutenant Pridot to Napier at dawn; the plain was covered with bodies, many being mangled by hyenas; under a white flag they reached the British camp, where soldiers cheered and took them to Napier.

[事实] Napier dictated a gracious reply promising honourable treatment for Theodros and his family if he submitted to Queen Victoria and delivered the Europeans safely that day, with 24 hours to decide.

[事实] Theodros did not understand whether “honourable treatment” meant captivity or help against the rebels; he had expected the British to be impressed by him and become friends, and his rambling reply did not mention the hostages.

[事实] He then sat alone, prayed, bowed three times and put a pistol in his mouth; his officers rushed forward and pulled his arm away, so the bullet only grazed his ear; he sobbed and again ordered the Europeans to be brought down.

[推测] The failed suicide and his inability to answer Napier clearly show that he was no longer pursuing a political strategy, only trying to avoid surrender.

[52:55] **The hostages are released, but peace collapses**

[事实] Rassam was summoned alone to the cliff edge, where Theodros told him they had always been on good terms, blamed bad men for his treatment of Rassam, and asked Rassam to speak up for him when he was taken prisoner; he said that unless Rassam befriended him, he would either kill himself or become a monk.

[事实] Rassam rejoined the other hostages and they trudged down to the British camp, passing through cheering British lines; they were shown to Napier’s tent, where Colonel Merriweather, the British agent in Aden, had brought champagne, and they celebrated.

[事实] On Easter Sunday Theodros sent Napier another erratic letter saying Satan had put the thought of dying by his own gun into him, and sent 1,000 cows and 500 sheep as a peace offering, asking, “surely it is peace now.”

[事实] Napier refused the animals, apparently because local custom meant that accepting them would amount to accepting peace; Theodros concluded that the British had got what they wanted and now wanted to kill him.

[事实] Theodros considered fleeing to Lake Tana, but his men refused to run away with him, saying they would stay with him until death but that he must make peace with the foreigners.

[推测] Napier’s refusal of the livestock, whatever its legal or diplomatic logic, closed off the easiest route to a negotiated ending.

[56:03] **The final assault, the death of Theodros and the looting of Magdala**

[事实] Napier and his officers did not want another battle and already had the hostages, but they feared that the return march would be attacked by rival warlords unless Theodros was finished off; taking him prisoner and bringing him back to London was considered.

[事实] On Easter Monday, Theodros tried to lead a last attack, but his men refused halfway down the mountain, saying they would rather seek death in Magdala; many slipped away, and he returned to the citadel with a dwindling band of loyalists, stacking boulders against the outer gate.

[事实] Napier ordered his guns to shell the citadel; by about four o’clock on the afternoon of Easter Monday, British troops were advancing up the path, shells were landing between the inner and outer gates, and troops with scaling ladders were forcing the outer gate with crowbars.

[事实] Theodros shouted, “flee, I release you from your allegiance, but I shall never fall into the hands of an enemy,” put a gun in his mouth and killed himself; the pistol beside his body was the silver duelling pistol given to him by Queen Victoria.

[事实] Dr Blanc performed the post-mortem and later wrote that death gave an air of calm grandeur to a man whose career had been remarkable and whose cruelties were almost unparalleled; Rassam organised the funeral in the Coptic church on the Magdala hilltop the next day.

[事实] After the defenders surrendered, British troops looted manuscripts, relics, vestments, crowns, goblets, musical instruments, Staffordshire pottery, umbrellas, wine, carpets, lion skins, toy soldiers and cameras; Napier arrived with bands playing “God Save the Queen” and “See the Conquering Hero Comes,” ordered a proper distribution of the booty and held a gigantic auction a few days later.

[推测] The auction turned the sack of Magdala into an orderly imperial souvenir market, giving the plunder a veneer of discipline.

[63:41] **Limited aims, a leaderless Abyssinia and the memory of Theodros**

[事实] Napier had no interest in installing a client regime or making Abyssinia safe for democracy; his orders were to get in, get the hostages and get out again, and he told his army that the Queen and people of England had entrusted them to release their countrymen and vindicate the honour of their country: “We’ve done it. We’re going home.”

[事实] Abyssinia was left leaderless and suffered about twenty years of chaos and civil war, followed by the war in Sudan, the Mahdi and General Gordon; it ended only at the end of the century under Menelik II, who beat the Italians at the Battle of Adwa in 1896, a landmark humiliation of a European army by an African one.

[事实] Napier marched back to the coast by June, loaded the railway tracks, telegraph wires and elephants back onto ships, and eight days later embarked for Suez; in England he was treated as a hero, thanked by Parliament, received by the Queen, promoted to Field Marshal and made Baron Napier of Magdala; he died in 1890 and a statue was put up in Kensington at Queen’s Gate.

[事实] In Ethiopia, Theodros is now a cult figure: his image appears on t-shirts, badges and the walls of bars in Addis Ababa, and he is seen as a patriotic hero who died fighting European imperialism and as a symbol of Ethiopian independence; Emperor Theodros Square in central Addis Ababa contains a replica of Sebastopol, while the real gun lies half-buried at Magdala, never fired and showing no sign of ever having worked.

[事实] The hosts close by saying the whole business could have been avoided if Queen Victoria had answered her letters, and they trail next week’s episodes on the French Revolution and the Terror, along with jokes about a Theodros-style letter-writing contest and a trip to see Sebastopol.

[推测] The episode’s larger point is that a rescue operation framed as both humanitarian and prestige-saving achieved its narrow aim but left behind a destructive political vacuum.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode is strong narrative history: it uses *Flashman* as a hook but keeps returning to the documented record, and it balances high politics, logistics, technology, personality and violence. Its best material includes the sheer scale of Napier’s preparations, the Maria Theresa currency and champagne details, the contrast between British khaki and Abyssinian scarlet, and the recurring motif of Queen Victoria’s gift pistol.

Its limitations are those of a vivid, anecdote-led episode rather than a scholarly survey. The story is told mainly through British and European sources, with Ethiopian perspectives appearing largely at the end through the memory of Theodros and the brief reference to Menelik II; listeners wanting a fuller account of Abyssinian society, local politics or the experience of ordinary Ethiopians would need to look elsewhere. [推测] The hosts’ comic asides also sometimes sit awkwardly beside the massacre of prisoners and the lopsided slaughter at Magdala.

It will appeal most to listeners of *The Rest Is History*, fans of British imperial and military history, and anyone interested in Victorian adventure, hostage-rescue operations or the logistics of nineteenth-century warfare. [推测] It is best treated as an entertaining introduction to a dramatic episode rather than a standalone account, and would pair well with Philip Marston’s *The Barefoot Emperor* and Ethiopian historiography.
