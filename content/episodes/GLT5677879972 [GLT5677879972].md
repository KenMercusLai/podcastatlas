# Comedy, History, and the Licensed Fool

## 概览

This episode of *The Rest Is History* explores how comedy has treated history, and how historical settings have given comedians ways to talk about power, trauma, class, taboo, and national memory.

Tom Holland and Dominic Sandbrook speak with Al Murray about *1066 and All That*, Spike Milligan, court jesters, satire, Monty Python, *Blackadder*, and the shifting limits of taste. A recurring conclusion is that comedy is never detached from its moment: what is funny, acceptable, shocking, or useful depends heavily on context, status, audience, and technology.

The discussion also argues that comic works can shape popular historical imagination more powerfully than formal history books, especially when they create memorable shorthand for complex events.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **Comedy As A Way Into History**

[事实] The episode opens by framing the subject as both the history of comedy and the use of history as comedy.
[事实] Tom Holland and Dominic Sandbrook introduce Al Murray, known for the Pub Landlord and for the Second World War podcast *We Have Ways of Making You Talk*.
[推测] The hosts set up comedy not as a light side topic, but as a serious route into how societies remember the past.

[02:35] **1066 And All That**

[事实] Dominic names *1066 and All That* as the first funny history book he read and calls it an “Ur text” for comic history.
[事实] Al Murray says the book was also formative for him and that some of his early understanding of English history came through Sellers and Yeatman’s comic categories.
[事实] They discuss the book’s “good king, bad king, good thing, bad thing” simplification of history.
[推测] The appeal of the book comes from how closely its parody resembled the simplified way history was often taught.

[05:22] **History Ends, Then Comedy Continues**

[事实] The speakers recall that *1066 and All That* ends before the First World War, with America becoming “top nation” and history coming to a stop.
[事实] Al Murray discusses writing *The Last 100 Years (Give or Take) and All That* during lockdown.
[事实] He says writing a book is unlike stand-up because a stand-up show is continually reshaped with audiences, while a book is handed over and fixed.
[推测] Murray’s comments suggest that comic history is harder to calibrate without live audience feedback.

[08:34] **How Recent Is Too Recent?**

[事实] Al says he wound his own book down around 1999 because the most recent decades felt too close.
[事实] The hosts discuss the idea that historians often need 20 or 30 years before the “dust” settles.
[事实] Al says it would be folly to contain Trump historically because he may turn out to be only an early sign of a larger process.
[推测] The episode treats comic history and academic history as sharing a problem: both struggle to judge events that are still unfolding.

[09:43] **The Second World War And Comic Memory**

[事实] Tom lists Spike Milligan, *Dad’s Army*, and *’Allo ’Allo!* as examples of comedy connected to the Second World War.
[事实] Al argues there is actually a surprisingly small amount of Second World War comedy given the war’s central historical importance.
[事实] He describes *’Allo ’Allo!* as a parody of *Secret Army* rather than a direct parody of the war itself.
[推测] The discussion distinguishes comedy about war from comedy about later cultural representations of war.

[11:07] **Spike Milligan And Processing War**

[事实] Al describes Spike Milligan’s war memoirs as an attempt to digest and deal with his wartime experience, including battle fatigue and a nervous breakdown.
[事实] He calls Milligan the most important comic voice in post-war British comedy.
[事实] Al argues that *The Goon Show* is about war, class, and incompetent authority, but hides this through surrealism.
[推测] Milligan’s comedy is presented as a way of processing trauma indirectly before addressing it directly in memoir.

[12:44] **Dark Humour In Extreme Conditions**

[事实] Dominic links wartime comedy to the *Wipers Times* and trench humour in the First World War.
[事实] Al agrees that war generates humour and says surviving examples often depend on whether the humour was written down.
[事实] He says humour can be momentary and hard to pin down because it often exists only in the air.
[推测] The episode suggests that historical comedy archives are shaped by accidents of preservation as much as by what people found funny.

[13:41] **Taste, Taboo, And Changing Boundaries**

[事实] Dominic raises slavery as an example of a subject that seems instinctively hard to treat comically.
[事实] Tom points to *Up Pompeii!* and Frankie Howerd’s slave character, connecting it to Roman comic traditions.
[事实] Al says questions of comedy boundaries are often questions of taste, and tastes change.
[事实] He argues that debates about “pushing boundaries” shift depending on which boundaries are being pushed.

[17:04] **What Changes, And What Still Makes People Laugh**

[事实] Al gives the example of Roland le Petour, a fool in Henry II’s time who was rewarded for a farting act.
[事实] The speakers laugh at the story, while Al also notes that many Shakespearean and Greek comedies no longer seem obviously funny to modern audiences.
[推测] The discussion implies that some comic mechanisms, such as bodily humour, travel better across time than many social or literary conventions.

[18:39] **Status And Licensed Mockery**

[事实] Tom connects jesters, Roman comic slaves, and soldiers mocking officers as examples of humour shaped by status.
[事实] Al says comedy in these settings helps accommodate systems of authority.
[事实] He explains the idea that a fool could speak truth to a king because the fool had a special licensed position.
[推测] Comedy is treated here as a controlled safety valve inside unequal social systems.

[20:01] **Natural And Artificial Fools**

[事实] Al distinguishes between “natural” fools, understood in pre-modern theology as innocent, and “artificial” fools who performed foolishness.
[事实] He says artificial fools were more likely to get into trouble because their innocence was understood as an act.
[事实] He discusses Will Somers, Henry VIII’s fool, as someone who survived across Tudor courts.
[事实] He also describes Richard Tarleton as a figure in the transition from court fool to professional comedian.

[24:19] **The Fool As Safety Valve**

[事实] Al tells a story about a Chinese court fool, “Twisty Pole,” using a joke to persuade an emperor not to lacquer the Great Wall of China.
[事实] After the break, Dominic asks whether courtiers knowingly used fools to deliver messages nobody else could safely say.
[事实] Al gives examples from Tudor England, including Wolsey’s fool Sexton being handed over to Henry VIII.
[事实] He quotes Erasmus on fools being able to speak truth and even insults with pleasure rather than offence.

[27:59] **Satire, Levity, And Political Comedy**

[事实] Dominic raises Jonathan Coe’s criticism that Britain may have become too complacent through satire and programmes such as *Have I Got News for You*.
[事实] Al says the problem begins with defining “satire.”
[事实] He argues that jokes about politicians and news are not automatically satire.
[事实] He suggests Coe may really be objecting to too much levity rather than too much satire.

[29:38] **Class, The Satire Boom, And Spitting Image**

[事实] Al says *The Goon Show* can be read as surrealist satire, but was not always recognized that way.
[事实] He says the 1960s “Satire Boom” was treated as a major break partly because its performers came from elite backgrounds.
[事实] Al discusses writing for the revived *Spitting Image* and says the original show is remembered through rosy tinted spectacles.
[推测] The episode suggests that who performs comedy affects whether audiences label it as satire, nonsense, or mere entertainment.

[31:15] **Juvenal, Filth, And Historical Sensibilities**

[事实] Tom and Al discuss Juvenal as a satirist whose work is filthy, bitter, misogynistic, and hostile to immigrants.
[事实] Tom says Juvenal would definitely get cancelled now.
[事实] Al uses this to return to the difficulty of defining satire across time.
[推测] Ancient satire is presented as historically important but not morally or aesthetically easy for modern audiences.

[32:18] **Context And Outrage**

[事实] The speakers discuss *Beyond the Fringe* and its “futile gesture” sketch about the First World War.
[事实] Al argues that such jokes may have resembled black humour already used by servicemen, but became shocking because of who said them and where.
[事实] He gives Jimmy Carr’s Headley Court joke as an example of a joke changing meaning when moved from one setting to another.
[事实] Al says outrage can be performative, just as laughter can be performative.

[36:39] **Old Comedy And Modern Broadcast Standards**

[事实] Dominic asks how broadcasters should handle older comedy that now seems dated or offensive, including Spike Milligan blacking up in the 1970s.
[事实] Al answers that broadcasters could commission new comedy instead of filling schedules with old material.
[事实] He says comedy lives in the present moment and old comedy often loses its reference points.
[推测] The episode does not offer a fixed rule for warnings or removals, but leans toward treating old comedy as historically situated rather than timelessly reusable.

[37:50] **Taboo, Commerce, And The Comic Marketplace**

[事实] A listener asks whether taboo subjects generate comedy because people are not supposed to laugh at them.
[事实] Al says claiming to say the unsayable can sell tickets and make a comedian’s name.
[事实] He says modern audiences now pay for their own preferred fools, rather than kings maintaining court jesters.
[事实] He describes comedy fashions changing during his career, from political comedy to Vic and Bob-style absurdity to later forms of edginess.

[40:36] **Comedy Shaping Popular History**

[事实] A listener asks how much comic works shape popular views of history, citing *Blackadder* and Monty Python.
[事实] Al says *Blackadder Goes Forth* is especially influential in shaping ideas about the First World War.
[事实] He says historians do not own history, because national stories and popular discourse shape historical understanding.
[事实] The speakers agree that the final minutes of *Blackadder Goes Forth*, where the characters die, gave the series unusual emotional power.

[43:59] **Monty Python As Historical Shorthand**

[事实] Al says he would rather watch *Life of Brian* than *Gladiator* to get a sense of the Roman Empire.
[事实] Tom says *Life of Brian* works like *’Allo ’Allo!* in parodying earlier dramatic forms.
[事实] The hosts note that lines from *Life of Brian* about Roman achievements and factionalism remain common shorthand in historical and political debates.
[事实] They also praise *Monty Python and the Holy Grail* for deconstructing Arthurian legend.

[事实] Dominic asks whether histories of comedy overemphasize what comedians or cultural elites admire, rather than what mass audiences enjoyed.
[事实] He contrasts *The Young Ones* with *Hi-de-Hi!*, noting that *Hi-de-Hi!* was far more widely watched.
[事实] Al says he likes both and accepts that histories often emphasize novelty, shock, and boundary pushing.

[事实] Dominic links comedy to a broader discussion about the fragmentation of popular culture since the 1990s.
[事实] Al says Britain has lost much of its collective comic culture because broadcasting has changed.
[事实] He cites Denis Norden’s view that post-war comedy could draw on shared wartime experience.
[事实] Al suggests lockdown might produce a similar shared comic reference point, though many comedians may avoid it.

[事实] Al argues that the age of everyone watching or listening to the same comedy was historically unusual and created by radio and television.
[事实] The speakers note that earlier music hall culture was more regional, with jokes not always travelling between cities.
[事实] They mention Charlie Chaplin being mistaken for French because people in the Lake District could not understand him.
[事实] The episode closes with a reference to *Carry On Cleo* and a preview of a future episode on Americanisation.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode is strongest when it treats comedy as historical evidence: jokes reveal power structures, class assumptions, technological change, trauma, and shifting boundaries of taste. Al Murray’s examples, especially on court fools and Spike Milligan, give the conversation more depth than a simple list of famous comedy moments.

Its most useful insight is that comic history can outlast academic correction. *Blackadder*, Monty Python, and *1066 and All That* are presented as works that do not merely joke about the past, but actively shape how many people imagine it.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners interested in British comedy, public memory, and the cultural history of humour. [推测] Its limitation is that it is heavily centered on British examples, so listeners looking for a global history of comedy will get only occasional glimpses beyond Britain.
