# Advice Line with Carlton Calvin of Razor

## 概览

This episode of *How I Built This Lab: Advice Line* features Guy Raz and Carlton Calvin of Razor USA advising three founders across games, stationery, and craft spirits.

The recurring theme is focus: know whether you want to be an inventor or an operator, prove demand before seeking licensing or scale, and concentrate scarce time and cash on products that already resonate.

Carlton repeatedly emphasizes founder visibility, retail distribution, sales reps, and trend awareness. His closing lesson is that doing everything yourself can feel satisfying, but delegation is what ultimately transformed his business.

## 分段落总结

[00:17] **Introducing Carlton Calvin and Razor**

[事实] Guy Raz introduces the Advice Line format and brings on Carlton Calvin, founder and CEO of Razor USA.
[事实] Razor’s original collapsible aluminum scooter is still described as the company’s largest-selling item by volume, close to a million units a year.
[事实] Carlton says he is trying to institutionalize his trend-seeking ability inside Razor.
[推测] The episode frames Carlton’s value as less about one past hit and more about repeatable pattern recognition.

[03:34] **Trend Spotting and Product Instinct**

[事实] Carlton says he used to be more passive about trends, expecting to spot them when they appeared.
[事实] He says actively looking for trends has helped him see more opportunities.
[事实] He mentions hacky sacks coming back and says Razor moved quickly to ship them to stores.
[推测] Carlton’s approach suggests trend spotting can be trained through deliberate practice, even if the best opportunities remain rare.

[05:30] **Patents, Copycats, and Brand Defense**

[事实] Guy asks about Razor winning a patent infringement case involving copycat competitors.
[事实] Carlton says patent cases can cost millions and may produce little financial recovery when copycats have shallow pockets.
[事实] He says the lawsuit helped clean up the market but did not necessarily create a large damages payout.
[推测] For smaller consumer-product companies, brand strength, speed, and product quality may be more practical defenses than litigation alone.

[07:28] **Sean’s Party Game, Eulogy**

[事实] Sean Barassa from Saskatoon describes a party game called Eulogy, where players create a character’s life story and then deliver a eulogy.
[事实] The game began during COVID, was funded on Kickstarter in 2024, and product arrived in 2025.
[事实] Sean says he has sold just shy of 1,000 copies and has about 500 units in inventory.
[事实] His question is how to make the game a household name.

[11:12] **Licensing Versus Building the Business**

[事实] Carlton says one traditional path for toy and game inventors is licensing to a publisher or toy company.
[事实] He warns that game buyers look at hundreds or thousands of games and buy very few.
[事实] Guy says Sean likely needs more proof of demand, such as much higher unit sales or retailers asking to carry it.
[推测] The advice points Sean toward treating licensing as unlikely until the market gives stronger validation.

[13:56] **Grassroots Growth for a Game**

[事实] Carlton says Sean may need to go to toy conventions, get into small stores, and run game nights.
[事实] Guy notes that people are looking for community and that game cafes are doing well in some places.
[事实] Carlton says the game has a unique-sounding concept and a story that could gain traction on Instagram or in media.
[推测] Eulogy’s unusual premise may be an advantage if Sean can turn live play and player reactions into shareable proof.

[18:17] **Shireen’s Stationery Business**

[事实] Shireen Tippett from Houston runs Honey Bespoke Stationery and Paper Goods.
[事实] She sells personalized stationery, birthday invitations, birth announcements, and collegiate stationery licensed from schools.
[事实] Her revenue was nearly $120,000 the prior year, mostly through Etsy, though the current year has been slower.
[事实] Her question is how to move customers toward her own website and stand out in a crowded stationery market.

[20:59] **Retail Channels for Stationery**

[事实] Carlton says he is not an expert in driving traffic to direct-sales websites because Razor sells through retail.
[事实] He recommends trying to get into stationery stores, especially stores connected to schools for which Shireen has licenses.
[事实] Guy notes that some of her products are personalized, while others could work as universal cards.
[推测] Retail could help Shireen reduce dependence on Etsy while using her collegiate licensing as a clear sales hook.

[22:12] **Advertising and Founder-Led Content**

[事实] Shireen says Meta ads boosted her sales for a month or two, but she felt she was spending without a clear strategy.
[事实] Guy suggests using short videos showing her sketching, designing, and being the face of the brand.
[事实] Shireen says competitors often show their faces online, while she has avoided doing that because she is uncomfortable on camera.
[事实] Carlton and Guy encourage her to use social video, including voiceover and behind-the-scenes content.

[25:11] **Markets, Reps, and Social Presence**

[事实] Carlton says paid advertising is challenging in her space and that building an organic following could matter a lot.
[事实] He also recommends going to markets and getting sales reps.
[事实] Shireen mentions Dallas and Atlanta markets and says the Dallas market had contacted her.
[推测] The combined advice is to pair digital trust-building with old-fashioned wholesale selling.

[27:28] **Mark’s Craft Distillery**

[事实] Mark from Little Water Distillery in Atlantic City says the business has operated since 2016.
[事实] The distillery makes products including gin, vodka, rum, bourbon, espresso martini, chocolate martini, and a barrel-aged Old Fashioned.
[事实] The business began after Mark and his brother bought their father a copper pot still in 2013.
[事实] Mark says they later applied for an early New Jersey craft distillation license and left their careers to launch the company.

[29:20] **Distribution Complexity and Debt**

[事实] Mark explains that New Jersey rules allow manufacturing, a tasting room, retail sales from the tasting space, direct distribution, or distributor relationships.
[事实] He says direct distribution preserved margin but required manufacturing, marketing, sales, vehicles, staffing, hospitality, and retail operations.
[事实] He says the business did well during COVID by producing hand sanitizer, but has since taken on debt and seen sales contraction.
[事实] The company recently decided to work through distributors.

[31:27] **Focusing on the Espresso Martini**

[事实] Mark says 48 Blocks Espresso Martini is a premium product and one of the company’s best sellers.
[事实] He wants to move from a small local reputation to becoming the industry standard for the best espresso martini.
[事实] Guy identifies 48 Blocks Espresso Martini and Chocolate Martini as the products that appear most central.
[推测] The strongest path may be to build around the product line already showing pull rather than spreading effort across many SKUs.

[32:36] **Story, Ingredients, and Endorsement**

[事实] Carlton advises Mark to tell the story of the product and be the face of it on Instagram and TikTok.
[事实] He suggests showing the distilling process and what makes the product better.
[事实] He also suggests seeking endorsement from someone known for judging quality.
[事实] Mark says the espresso martini uses La Colombe cold-press Brazilian espresso, and the chocolate martini uses custom-roasted chocolate from a small chocolatier in North Carolina.

[34:42] **Cutting SKUs and Conserving Cash**

[事实] Guy says companies often fail because they run out of cash, not necessarily because demand disappears.
[事实] He advises simplifying the product line, focusing on high-margin channels, and leaning into the espresso and chocolate martinis.
[事实] Mark says reducing products is emotionally hard and could limit the tasting-room experience.
[事实] Carlton says that when one or two things resonate with consumers, founders should focus because there is no time for products that do not sell.

[37:35] **Cans, Trends, and Culture**

[事实] Mark asks whether he should follow the trend of canned cocktails, even though he worries cans may signal lower quality.
[事实] Carlton says founders should not fight the culture and should pay attention when a trend is visible in their industry.
[事实] Guy asks about possible martini line extensions, and Mark says combining chocolate and espresso would require little R&D.
[推测] The advice does not require abandoning premium positioning, but it does suggest testing formats consumers already want.

[39:53] **Carlton’s Closing Lesson**

[事实] Guy asks Carlton what advice he would give his younger self.
[事实] Carlton says he made many mistakes by trying to avoid giving up margin to sales reps.
[事实] He says sales reps are better at selling, founders cannot do everything, and delegation transformed his business.
[推测] This closing lesson ties together the callers’ challenges: growth requires letting specialized partners handle work the founder cannot scale alone.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode is especially useful for early-stage consumer-product founders who already have some traction but are unsure whether to license, advertise, wholesale, or narrow their product line.

Its strongest moments come from concrete tradeoffs: licensing versus building, Etsy versus owned channels, direct distribution versus reps, and product breadth versus focus. Carlton’s advice is practical and grounded in retail experience.

[推测] The limitation is that some recommendations remain directional rather than operational; callers get strategic priorities, but not detailed execution plans for ads, wholesale terms, distributor economics, or SKU-level financial modeling.

[推测] The best audience is founders selling physical products who need a sharper sense of distribution, storytelling, and focus before spending more cash or time.
