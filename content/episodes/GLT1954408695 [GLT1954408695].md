# Lord Byron’s Pilgrimage: Greece, Albania, and Byromania

## 概览

This episode follows Lord Byron’s 1809-1811 travels through Portugal, Spain, Malta, Greece, Albania, the Aegean, and Constantinople, showing how the journey shaped both his poetry and his public persona. The hosts connect *Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage* to Byron’s own self-fashioning as a tormented Romantic hero.

A central thread is Byron’s attraction to places he imagined as freer, more dangerous, and more sensuous than Britain. The discussion repeatedly contrasts his hostility to British repression and militarism with his fascination for Napoleon, the Ottoman world, Ali Pasha, Greek antiquity, and sexual cultures beyond English norms.

The episode also explains how Byron’s experiences fed directly into later poems, including his anti-war writing, his attacks on Lord Elgin, and his mythologizing of Greece. It ends with his return to England, personal losses, political debut, and sudden fame after the publication of *Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage*, giving rise to “Byromania.”

## 分段落总结

[01:18] **Childe Harold and Byron’s Self-Mythology**

[事实] The episode opens with lines from *Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage*, the poem Byron began in autumn 1809 while in Albania.
[事实] The hosts describe Childe Harold as a tormented nobleman fleeing dissipation and travelling through the Mediterranean toward Constantinople.
[事实] Byron claimed Harold was imaginary, but the hosts note that the poem closely mirrors Byron’s own travels and that he initially called the figure “Childe Byron.”
[推测] The poem matters because it lets Byron turn his own anxieties, ambitions, and experiences into the model of the Romantic hero.

[04:03] **War-Torn Europe and Byron’s Departure**

[事实] Byron left Falmouth on July 2, 1809 with his Cambridge friend John Cam Hobhouse, heading first for Portugal.
[事实] Europe was in the middle of the Napoleonic Wars, and Britain had been at war with France off and on for 16 years.
[事实] Byron admired Napoleon partly from hostility to Tory Britain and partly because he identified with Napoleon as a self-invented figure rising above mediocrity.
[事实] The hosts also note that Byron admired Nelson, whose victory at Trafalgar helped make sea travel possible for Britons.
[推测] Byron’s journey was both a Grand Tour substitute and a deliberate escape into danger, politics, and self-invention.

[07:51] **Sexual Repression, Greece, and the Ottoman World**

[事实] The hosts discuss Byron’s intense same-sex desires and the extreme legal danger of sodomy in Britain at the time.
[事实] Byron wrote provocatively to a former schoolmaster about studying the “morals of the Turks” and about ancient and modern defenses of pederasty.
[事实] The episode links Byron’s classical education and fascination with the Ottoman Empire to his belief that sexual taboos were culturally relative.
[事实] Byron saw the East as liberating compared with what he regarded as the repressive monotony of England.
[推测] His “cultural relativism” was strongly shaped by his own desires and by Western fantasies about the Orient.

[11:38] **The Peninsular War and Modern Violence**

[事实] Byron travelled through Portugal and Spain while the Peninsular War was raging and Sir Arthur Wellesley was preparing for the Battle of Talavera.
[事实] Byron was unimpressed by Wellesley and disliked what he saw as the character of industrial warfare.
[事实] The travellers saw French soldiers and Spaniards accused of treachery being taken away to be hanged.
[事实] The hosts cite Benita Eisler’s view that Byron became the first major poet of modern warfare without seeing a battle.
[推测] Byron’s anti-war sensibility anticipates later artistic responses to mass violence, including comparisons made in the episode with Goya.

[14:19] **Malta, Naval Adventure, and First Sight of Greece**

[事实] Byron and Hobhouse left Spain, sailed via Sardinia to Malta, and Byron had a fling with Mrs. Spencer Smith.
[事实] They then travelled toward Greece on the British brig of war *Spider*, which was guarding merchant ships.
[事实] The *Spider* captured a merchant ship and fitted it out as a privateer; Byron and Hobhouse joined actions in which a Turkish vessel was captured.
[事实] They first saw Greece, landed at Patras, and later passed Missolonghi while sailing up the northwestern coast.
[推测] These adventures fed Byron’s taste for danger and his later theatrical sense of himself as an actor in historical drama.

[16:14] **Greece Under Ottoman Rule**

[事实] Greece was then part of the Ottoman Empire, with mosques, Turkish soldiers, and Ottoman authority visible in the landscape.
[事实] The hosts say Greece had been under Ottoman rule since the collapse of the Byzantine Empire, with some parts ruled for around 400 years.
[事实] Byron’s classical education made it painful to him that ancient Greece, once victorious over Persia, was now subject to the Turks.
[事实] *Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage* repeatedly laments Greece as fallen from ancient greatness.
[推测] Byron’s sympathy for Greek liberty combined revolutionary ideas of national freedom with a literary fantasy of ancient Greece.

[17:48] **Albania and Ali Pasha**

[事实] Byron wanted to visit Albania partly because it was obscure, mountainous, and little known to British travellers.
[事实] Albania was formally part of the Ottoman Empire but effectively ruled by Ali Pasha, a former bandit chief and despot.
[事实] Ali Pasha was then pro-British, which helped Byron and Hobhouse travel under protection.
[事实] Byron loved Albania immediately, while Hobhouse and Byron’s servant Fletcher disliked the food, flies, plumbing, and discomfort.
[推测] Albania gave Byron the mixture of beauty, danger, strangeness, and aristocratic violence that most excited his imagination.

[20:16] **Byron’s Meeting with Ali Pasha**

[事实] Byron wrote vividly to his mother about Albanian costumes, horses, couriers, drums, minarets, and the palace at Tepelena.
[事实] Ali Pasha greeted Byron warmly, complimented his looks, his small ears, and his delicate white hands, and told Byron to regard him as a father.
[事实] The hosts describe Ali Pasha as outwardly jolly but also as a ruler associated with rape, murder, impalement, roasting enemies, and other atrocities.
[事实] Ali Pasha gave Byron a bodyguard or servant named Vasili, who had participated in a notorious atrocity against Greek women.
[推测] Byron appears both horrified and fascinated by Ali Pasha, treating brutality differently when it appears exotic and glamorous rather than British.

[24:12] **Hints, Gender, and Byron’s Greek Destiny**

[事实] The hosts say Byron drops hints that something more may have happened between him and Ali Pasha, but they state that it is not clear.
[事实] Byron stayed excited by Albania while Hobhouse and Fletcher continued to struggle with discomforts.
[事实] As Byron travelled toward Athens via Delphi and Thebes, he was working on *Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage*.
[事实] In an early version of the poem, Byron repeatedly wrote his own name as the answer to who would call Greece’s scattered children forth.
[推测] Byron was already imagining himself as a future liberator or symbolic savior of Greece.

[28:19] **Athens and The Curse of Minerva**

[事实] After the break, the episode turns to Athens with lines from Byron’s *The Curse of Minerva*.
[事实] Byron and Hobhouse arrived in Athens on Christmas Day, 1809.
[事实] They were disappointed by the city’s run-down condition, narrow streets, and small population, though Byron soon became happy there.
[事实] Byron and Hobhouse rented a house with a courtyard and lemon tree, and Byron settled into expatriate life with sexual opportunities and antiquities around him.
[推测] Athens intensified Byron’s sense that modern Greece was both degraded and romantically charged.

[31:32] **Lord Elgin and the Parthenon Marbles**

[事实] Byron became obsessed with Lord Elgin’s removal of the Parthenon friezes.
[事实] Giovanni Battista Lusieri had worked for Lord Elgin, first making drawings and casts, then helping remove the friezes after reporting their beauty and decay.
[事实] The hosts say Elgin obtained permission through bribes, as was common in the Ottoman system, and that no formal objection came at the time from the Ottoman authorities or Greeks.
[事实] Byron and Hobhouse saw the Acropolis on January 8 and found it used as an Ottoman garrison.
[推测] Byron’s anger at Elgin was partly political and aesthetic, but also personal, tied to his resentment of Scotland, Britain, and his own background.

[36:01] **Two Reactions to the Acropolis**

[事实] Hobhouse believed the marbles were in danger because local people were burning marble for lime and because the Parthenon was decaying.
[事实] Hobhouse therefore thought Elgin was rescuing and preserving the marbles.
[事实] Byron instead portrayed Elgin as worse than earlier plunderers of Athens, attacking him fiercely in *Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage*.
[事实] Byron mocked Elgin’s appearance and personal life, including incorrectly attributing damage to Elgin’s nose to syphilis.
[推测] The hosts interpret Byron’s attack as projection: Elgin represented the British establishment intruding on Byron’s imagined Greece.

[39:28] **Ruins, Troy, and Swimming the Hellespont**

[事实] Byron spent 10 weeks in Athens before crossing the Aegean and visiting ancient sites including Ephesus and the Troad.
[事实] He rejected doubts about Troy’s existence and later wrote about standing on Achilles’ tomb.
[事实] On May 3, 1810, Byron swam the Hellespont with Lieutenant William Ekenhead in one hour and ten minutes.
[事实] Byron loved swimming partly because his club foot did not limit him in the water.
[推测] Swimming the Hellespont allowed Byron to merge physical achievement, classical myth, and personal legend.

[42:22] **Constantinople, Exoticism, and Gender Play**

[事实] Byron and his party reached Constantinople on May 13 and stayed in Pera, across the Golden Horn from the old city.
[事实] The hosts describe Byron seeing both disturbing scenes, such as dogs eating a corpse, and familiar comforts, such as a hotel with a billiard table.
[事实] On July 10, Byron was received by Sultan Mahmud II, who was struck by his youthful appearance and dress.
[事实] The Sultan reportedly thought Byron was a woman dressed as a man, and the hosts connect this to Byron’s interest in dressing in female clothing and later writing gender disguise into *Don Juan*.
[推测] Constantinople offered Byron a setting for literary, sexual, psychological, and gender exploration.

[45:30] **Hobhouse Leaves and Byron Stays**

[事实] Hobhouse returned home, while Byron stayed abroad for another nine months.
[事实] Thomas Moore later portrayed Byron as seeking solitude, but Hobhouse disputed that explanation in a marginal note.
[事实] The hosts infer that Byron preferred not to have an Englishman constantly near him because of his sexual conduct and Hobhouse’s ambivalence.
[事实] Byron wrote that England had pipes and beer while Turkey had opium and boys, presenting the difference as culturally relative.
[推测] Byron’s extended stay suggests he felt freer abroad than he could in English society.

[47:33] **Return to England and Personal Losses**

[事实] Byron eventually returned because he was running out of money and his friends were exposed to his debts in England.
[事实] He sailed from Athens to Malta on a ship carrying the last consignment of the Parthenon friezes, with Lusieri also aboard.
[事实] Byron arrived back in England at Sheerness on July 11, 1811, aged 23.
[事实] Soon after his return, his mother died, his friend Matthews drowned, and he learned that John Edleston, his first great love, had also died.
[推测] The return compressed financial anxiety, sexual repression, and bereavement into a bleak period after the freedom of travel.

[51:02] **Childe Harold Finds a Publisher**

[事实] Byron’s distant relative Robert Dallas asked whether he had written anything abroad and was deeply impressed by the manuscript of *Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage*.
[事实] Publisher John Murray took on the poem, beginning a strong editorial relationship with Byron.
[事实] Murray was a Tory, while Byron was suspicious of Tories, but they nevertheless worked well together.
[事实] Byron still affected contempt for literary work and considered a political career.
[推测] The publication succeeded partly because Byron’s personal myth, aristocratic status, scandal, and travel narrative all reinforced one another.

[52:56] **Whig Politics and the Maiden Speech**

[事实] By early 1812, Byron was moving in Whig salons, especially Holland House.
[事实] Lord Holland admired Napoleon, and Lady Holland became strongly attached to Byron.
[事实] On February 27, 1812, Byron gave his maiden speech in the House of Lords against making loom-breaking a capital offence.
[事实] The hosts describe the speech as powerful and moving, but more like a poet’s speech than a politician’s.
[推测] Byron used politics partly as a public stage and partly as promotion before the publication of his poem.

[55:35] **The Birth of Byromania**

[事实] John Murray published *Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage* on March 3, 1812.
[事实] The poem sold out within three days, and booksellers were mobbed first in London and then around the country.
[事实] Byron famously said he woke to find himself famous.
[事实] Annabella Milbank later coined the term “Byromania” for the public obsession with him.
[推测] The episode presents Byron’s fame as the result of a perfect fusion of poetry, scandal, aristocratic glamour, erotic danger, and self-created myth.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s main value is how it ties Byron’s literary breakthrough to travel, war, sexuality, imperial politics, and classical imagination. Rather than treating *Childe Harold’s Pilgrimage* as isolated poetry, the hosts show how Byron’s lived experiences became material for a public identity.

A major strength is the episode’s attention to contradiction: Byron is anti-war yet drawn to violent figures, anti-British yet a British aristocrat, devoted to Greek liberty yet fascinated by Ottoman power, and performatively masculine while also exploring gender ambiguity. These tensions make the discussion more than a simple travel narrative.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners interested in Romantic literature, European history, the Ottoman world, Greek independence, or the construction of literary celebrity. Its limitation is that the hosts often move quickly through morally disturbing material, so some claims about Byron’s sexual conduct and inner motives remain interpretive rather than fully provable.
