# Italian Food: Cities, Myths, Pasta, and Pizza

## 概览

This episode uses Italian food as a way into Italian history, with Professor John Dickie arguing that “Italian food” is real, but not in the rustic, timeless, peasant-kitchen sense often sold by advertising and nationalism.

The main argument is that Italian cuisine developed through cities: Palermo, Venice, Rome, Ferrara, Naples, Milan and others created, exchanged, branded and mythologised foods. Pasta, pizza, spices, banquets, tomatoes, tortellini and the “Mediterranean diet” all appear less as fixed traditions than as products of trade, politics, class, urban life and modern marketing.

A recurring theme is myth-busting. The episode challenges the Marco Polo pasta story, the royal origin story of pizza Margherita, the idea of naturally healthy ancient peasant food, and the belief that modern Italian cuisine simply preserves old rural customs.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Pizza Begins as a Disgusting Street Food**

[事实] The episode opens with Carlo Collodi’s 1886 description of pizza as a “patchwork of greasy filth,” showing that pizza was once seen by some Italians as dirty and low-status.

[事实] Collodi, author of *The Adventures of Pinocchio*, is presented as a Tuscan who despised pizza rather than as someone who saw it as a national treasure.

[事实] The hosts introduce John Dickie, professor of Italian studies at University College London and author of *Delizia: The Epic History of Italians and Their Food*.

[02:37] **Italian Food Exists, but Not as Simple Regional Tradition**

[事实] Dickie argues that Italian food does exist, but as a dialogue between cities rather than as a neat map of regional cuisines.

[事实] He says the modern administrative regions of Italy are relatively recent and too simple a framework for understanding food history.

[事实] Naples, Rome and other cities had different political and cultural histories, which shaped their food traditions.

[03:35] **The Peasant Kitchen Myth Is Challenged**

[事实] Dickie rejects the romantic image of Italian food coming from rural mothers preserving ancient recipes.

[事实] He says historical documentation after Italian unification in 1861 shows peasants generally ate badly and often lacked the means or knowledge to cook well.

[事实] He links improved nutrition after the postwar economic miracle to the fact that average Italian height rose by four centimetres from the late 1950s to the mid-1970s.

[推测] The episode treats the rural-origin story of Italian cuisine as a powerful modern myth rather than a reliable historical account.

[05:09] **Classical Cities Matter More Than Roman Recipes**

[事实] Dickie says he tried to trace modern Italian food directly back to ancient Rome but could not do so convincingly.

[事实] He argues that the classical world’s main legacy was Italy’s network of strong urban centres, sometimes called the “hundred cities of Italy.”

[事实] Many famous Italian foods carry city names, such as pizza Napoletana, bistecca Fiorentina, risotto Milanese and Parmigiano from Parma.

[06:17] **Palermo and the First Documentary Evidence for Dried Pasta**

[事实] The discussion turns to Palermo in 1154 under Roger II, where the first documentary evidence for dried pasta on Italian soil appears.

[事实] The evidence comes from the Arabic-speaking geographer al-Idrisi, who described large-scale production near Trabia, close to Palermo.

[事实] The pasta described was made from durum wheat, dried, exported widely and called *ytria*, a term connected to later Italian pasta words.

[事实] Dickie distinguishes dried pasta from other foods now grouped under “pasta,” such as tortellini and lasagne.

[09:45] **Marco Polo Did Not Bring Pasta from China**

[事实] Dickie says Marco Polo came about 150 years after the Palermo evidence and did not mention pasta or noodles.

[事实] The story that Marco Polo introduced spaghetti to Italy was first invented by the Pasta Manufacturers Association of the United States in 1929.

[事实] Dickie suggests the American pasta industry used the Marco Polo story to make pasta seem less narrowly Italian and more universal.

[推测] The myth helped make pasta easier to sell in the United States by detaching it from negative stereotypes about Mediterranean immigrants.

[10:57] **Medieval and Renaissance Food Was Not “Authentic Italian” as We Imagine It**

[事实] Medieval Milanese and Venetian sources discussed foods such as fruit, vegetables and spices, while pasta was not yet emblematic of all Italian cuisine.

[事实] Sicilians became known from about the 15th century as *mangia maccheroni*, or macaroni eaters.

[事实] Venice’s cuisine was strongly shaped by the spice trade, and Dickie says older Venetian food could seem closer to heavily spiced dishes than to modern stereotypes of Italian simplicity.

[事实] Spices were valued for taste, status and Galenic medical ideas about balancing the humours.

[13:37] **Platina and Renaissance Food as Medicine, Status and Humanism**

[事实] The hosts discuss Platina, a 15th-century Vatican librarian whose food writing linked ingredients to health, libido and classical learning.

[事实] Dickie presents Platina as a humanist scholar using food to display Renaissance cultural ambition.

[事实] Platina’s world valued foods such as blancmange, then made with pounded capon, almonds, sugar and spices, rather than the modern dessert of the same name.

[事实] Platina was later arrested over an alleged plot against the pope and, after becoming Vatican librarian, destroyed records relating to his own trial and imprisonment.

[17:23] **Renaissance Italy Becomes Aware of Its Food Prestige**

[事实] Dickie says Italians became aware in the Renaissance that they ate in a style that distinguished them from foreigners.

[事实] He connects this to Italian leadership in European courtly fashion, literature, Rome’s international papal role and the wider prestige of Italian culture.

[事实] The discussion uses a 1529 Ferrara banquet as an example of elite cuisine filled with sweet-savoury combinations, sugar, cinnamon, eels, capons and elaborate display.

[推测] This cuisine was “Italian” in the sense of elite court culture, but it had little resemblance to everyday modern Italian restaurant food.

[19:47] **Banquets, Eels and Religious Food Rules**

[事实] Dickie explains that Renaissance rulers used food to show magnificence, wealth and the ability to bring produce from many places to the table.

[事实] Eels were important because Christian food rules distinguished between lean days and fat days, restricting meat on certain days.

[事实] Eels were practical because they could be stored and preserved, then dressed up in many ways for elite tables.

[事实] The episode highlights that old food categories, such as fish versus meat or lean versus fat, no longer map neatly onto modern eating habits.

[22:01] **France, the Atlantic World and the Decline of Italian Courtly Leadership**

[事实] Dickie says Italy became marginalised at a courtly level from the 16th century as food fashions were increasingly driven from Paris.

[事实] He says modern Italian cuisine would not exist without French influence.

[事实] New World foods such as tomatoes and chocolate arrived, but Italians took a long time to work out how to use tomatoes in dishes such as pizza and pasta.

[事实] The shift of economic gravity from the Mediterranean to the Atlantic helped make Italy a relatively marginal player in this food revolution.

[23:44] **Naples Turns Pasta into Mass Urban Food**

[事实] After the break, the discussion moves to Naples, the largest Italian city in the 1700s.

[事实] Dickie says dried pasta’s capital became Naples in the 17th and 18th centuries, after the *mangia maccheroni* nickname shifted from Sicilians to Neapolitans.

[事实] Naples had a vast urban population to feed, and pasta became an easy way to feed the masses.

[事实] Pasta also took on political and symbolic meaning through the Lazzaroni, urban poor figures associated with eating macaroni by hand.

[26:42] **Macaroni and Monarchy in Naples**

[事实] Grand Tour visitors often wrote about the Lazzaroni and their apparently idle, ragged public life.

[事实] King Ferdinand also ate macaroni with his hands, even at the opera.

[事实] Dickie says macaroni came to symbolise a bond between the monarch and the masses, with the Lazzaroni acting as a kind of proletarian elite loyal to the monarchy.

[推测] In this telling, pasta was not just food but a political performance of Neapolitan absolutism.

[28:18] **Pork Fat, the Mediterranean Diet and American Reinvention**

[事实] Dickie says Neapolitan pasta was largely eaten with pork fat before tomato-based sauces became central.

[事实] He says the modern “Mediterranean diet” was invented by American scientist Ancel Keys in the 1950s and popularised through a cookbook in the 1970s.

[事实] Keys removed or downplayed elements such as pork fat to create something closer to what people now associate with healthy Italian food.

[事实] The episode links modern Italian cuisine to large-scale Italian migration to the New World and cultural exchange between Italy and America.

[30:05] **Pizza Becomes Visible as Poor Neapolitan Street Food**

[事实] Dickie says pizza existed in Naples, but not in the modern form people now imagine.

[事实] He describes it as part of a very old flatbread tradition that became associated in the 19th century with street eating in poor, overcrowded Neapolitan quarters.

[事实] Alexandre Dumas is cited as one of the early writers to notice pizza, including versions with tomato and anchovy as flavourings.

[事实] Pizza was street food, but the episode stresses that this association was not then glamorous or positive.

[31:49] **Pizza Margherita and the Cholera Context**

[事实] Dickie says 19th-century Naples inspired fear and disgust because of cholera, overcrowding and poor hygiene.

[事实] A major cholera outbreak occurred in Naples in 1884, after which King Umberto I and Queen Margherita visited the city.

[事实] Dickie says the name pizza Margherita seems to have emerged around that time as a homage to the queen and monarchy.

[事实] He rejects the famous story that Raffaele Esposito created three pizzas for the queen and that she chose the tomato, mozzarella and basil version.

[33:04] **The Pizza Margherita Origin Story Is Fake**

[事实] Dickie calls the Raffaele Esposito story fake.

[事实] He says the famous letter supposedly thanking the pizzeria for the pizzas is also fake.

[事实] He notes that Italian food history contains many such origin myths.

[推测] The persistence of the story shows how modern food culture often prefers clear founding legends over messier social history.

[34:17] **Food, Politics and Fascist Nostalgia**

[事实] Dickie says his book became more controversial as Italian political identity became increasingly attached to food.

[事实] He describes the current Italian government as using ideas of traditional Mediterranean peasant food.

[事实] Discussing original fascism, he says Mussolini and the fascists were not especially interested in pleasurable food culture and preferred ideals of austerity and self-sufficiency.

[事实] Fascists promoted rice and the “battle for grain,” while large-scale Italian pasta culture depended on imported grain from places such as Crimea.

[35:58] **The Rural Housewife Myth Gains Ground**

[事实] Dickie says the fascist period is when traces of rustic eating nostalgia begin to appear.

[事实] He says earlier traditions had pastoral poetry and opera, but little serious association of good eating with countryside life.

[事实] A 1938 fascist exhibition in Rome included a supposed rustic village, but its cuisines were still named after cities such as Rome, Venice and Milan.

[推测] Even fascist rural nostalgia could not escape the urban foundations of Italian food identity.

[37:04] **Postwar Plenty Creates Modern Rustic Nostalgia**

[事实] Dickie says rustic food nostalgia became powerful after the postwar economic miracle, especially in the 1970s.

[事实] Italy rapidly shifted from a peasant society to an industrial society in the late 1950s, 1960s and 1970s.

[事实] The 1970s were also marked by terrorism, corruption concerns and environmental crises.

[事实] Dickie says brands such as Mulino Bianco built on invented ideas of ancient peasant food traditions.

[38:31] **Giovanni Rana Turns Tortellini into Trusted Convenience Food**

[事实] Tortellini are described as medieval “little pies” cooked by boiling.

[事实] In the postwar period, tortellini became associated with Sunday lunch and domestic labour.

[事实] Giovanni Rana built a successful modern business by turning tortellini into a convenience food for urban, supermarket-era consumers.

[事实] Rana put his face and signature on packaging to solve the trust problem of what was inside the filling.

[推测] The example shows that modern Italian food success depends as much on industrial trust and marketing as on inherited recipes.

[40:24] **Slow Food and the Productive Power of Myths**

[事实] Dickie says Italy has a long urban tradition of connoisseurship around good eating.

[事实] He connects the Slow Food movement to the politics of food and to Carlo Petrini’s 1980s rebellion against fast food and commercialisation.

[事实] Dickie says many myths about peasant tradition are false, but Italians have used them effectively in commercial and cultural terms.

[推测] The episode’s final position is not that Italian food is fake, but that its authenticity is historically constructed and commercially powerful.

[42:10] **Italy’s Difference Comes from Its Network of Cities**

[事实] The hosts note a statistic from Dickie’s book that Italy has far fewer fast food outlets than Britain, France or Germany.

[事实] Dickie says Italy’s long food history excluded most Italians for much of the past, but still left a model of sophisticated urban eating.

[事实] He argues that food has been attached to Italy’s sense of living among ancient stones, artistic treasures and city identities.

[事实] He also notes that Italians now eat more sushi per head than anyone else in the world.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s main value is its clear demolition of comforting food myths. Rather than treating pasta, pizza and the Mediterranean diet as timeless expressions of national character, it shows how they emerged through trade, poverty, monarchy, migration, marketing and politics.

Its strongest sections are the city-based case studies: Palermo for dried pasta, Naples for macaroni and pizza, Rome and Ferrara for Renaissance elite food, and postwar Italy for industrial brands and rustic nostalgia. These examples make the argument concrete rather than abstract.

A limitation is that the episode moves quickly through a huge subject, so many claims depend on Dickie’s synthesis rather than extended evidence within the transcript itself. [推测] Listeners wanting recipe-level detail or a celebration of Italian food may find the myth-busting tone bracing.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in food history, Italian history, nationalism, urban culture and the gap between tradition as lived practice and tradition as modern invention.
