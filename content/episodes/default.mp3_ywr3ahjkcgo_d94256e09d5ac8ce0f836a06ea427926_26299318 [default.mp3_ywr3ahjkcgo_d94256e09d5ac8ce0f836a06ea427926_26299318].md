# Reviving Prize Law: How the U.S. Could Sell Seized Oil Tankers

## 概览

The episode investigates what happens after the U.S. military seizes oil tankers linked to Venezuela or Iran. Under the current civil asset forfeiture system, selling a vessel and its cargo can require lengthy litigation while the government bears enormous maintenance and custody costs.

The Trump administration is exploring an alternative rooted in centuries-old maritime “prize law.” Historically, prize courts rapidly determined whether captured enemy ships and cargo could be auctioned, while privateers—effectively state-authorized pirates—received part of the proceeds.

Officials are not proposing a return to privateering. Instead, the Justice Department, Navy, and a federal court in Texas have worked on modern procedures for processing captured vessels more quickly. The central unresolved issue is whether prize law can operate without a formal declaration of war by Congress.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **The mystery of seized oil tankers**

[事实] The U.S. military has seized tankers carrying fuel from Venezuela and vessels departing Iran as part of an effort to disrupt illicit networks.

[事实] After seizing the vessels, the government attempts to liquidate both the ships and their cargo, but the existing legal process can take years.

[事实] The Navy confirmed that prize law provides a framework for condemning, returning, or disposing of maritime property captured during conflict.

[02:12] **An ancient alternative to modern litigation**

[事实] During the age of wooden sailing ships, countries treated captured enemy vessels as “prizes” and could auction them and their contents within days or weeks.

[事实] Justice Department officials confirmed that they were working with the Navy on potentially restoring a modern prize-court process.

[事实] The administration’s objective is to dispose of captured ships more efficiently and avoid repeated, protracted court proceedings.

[05:25] **How civil asset forfeiture works today**

[事实] Under civil asset forfeiture, the government files a lawsuit against the seized property itself.

[事实] Vessel owners, cargo buyers, victims, and others asserting an interest in the property can enter the proceeding and argue for compensation or a share of the sale.

[事实] These procedural protections make it difficult to auction a captured vessel quickly.

[06:48] **The Majestic X exposes the cost of delay**

[事实] Two days after U.S. forces seized the Majestic X, the Justice Department initiated proceedings to sell the tanker and its oil.

[事实] A judge rejected an attempt to conduct a rapid private sale before potential claimants could object, requiring notice to the Iranian National Oil Company and the vessel’s suspected owners.

[事实] Hundreds of victims of Iranian terrorism subsequently sought part of the proceeds and requested a jury trial.

[事实] The government reported spending $42 million on custody, maintenance, and repairs during the first month and a half, although it valued the vessel itself at less than $3 million.

[事实] At the time of recording, the case had continued for nearly seven months, with both the ship and oil still held by the government.

[09:08] **Why the slow system exists**

[事实] Civil forfeiture is deliberately cumbersome because it is intended to prevent the government from taking private property without due process.

[事实] From the government’s perspective, however, the process is lengthy, expensive, and inefficient, particularly when seized commercial vessels must be maintained while idle.

[推测] The extraordinary cost of holding the Majestic X provides a strong practical incentive for officials to seek a faster legal mechanism.

[10:34] **Privateering and the origins of prize law**

[事实] Prize taking reached its height during the seventeenth and eighteenth centuries, when European states had limited naval capacity and supplemented it with privateers.

[事实] Privateering resembled piracy, but captains operated with government authorization and were required to follow rules, obtain financial guarantees, and carry an official commission.

[事实] An authorized captain could seize enemy ships—and sometimes neutral vessels connected to an enemy—and retain part of the resulting proceeds.

[事实] Prize law imposed legal structure on this activity and could penalize privateers who captured neutral ships or stole property improperly.

[12:24] **From capture to prize court**

[事实] A privateer would stop a suspected enemy vessel, inspect its papers, question its crew, and evaluate its connections to the enemy.

[事实] If the vessel appeared to be a valid prize, a prize crew would take it to port along with the collected evidence.

[事实] A prize-court judge used standardized questions to determine whether the ship and cargo could be auctioned.

[事实] Sale proceeds were divided among the captain, crew, investors, and the state; historical cargo could also include enslaved people.

[15:18] **A commercial practice designed to avoid combat**

[事实] Successful privateering generally relied on intimidation rather than fighting because armed engagements endangered both crews and valuable captured property.

[事实] Maritime historian Nathan Perl-Rosenthal said historical prize taking was comparatively bloodless, though not entirely without violence.

[推测] Reviving the institution after its customs have faded could make modern seizures more dangerous or unpredictable than their historical advocates expect.

[16:17] **What the administration actually wants to revive**

[事实] Justice Department officials are not proposing private mercenaries or a civilian “bonus navy.”

[事实] Their focus is the prize-court mechanism, which could also process vessels captured by the regular Navy.

[事实] Prize law remains on the books, but modern procedures for applying it are largely absent and would need to be reconstructed.

[事实] New procedures could face debate and litigation rather than delivering immediate certainty.

[18:02] **The threshold question: Is the United States at war?**

[事实] Prize taking traditionally requires a state of war.

[事实] Congress has not formally declared war against Iran, and the administration’s descriptions of the conflict have not been consistent.

[事实] Courts may therefore have to decide whether an undeclared conflict is sufficient to activate prize law.

[推测] Litigation over the meaning of “war” could undermine the administration’s goal of creating a faster and simpler process.

[20:12] **A federal judge builds modern prize rules**

[事实] The Justice Department approached Chief Judge Randy Crane of the Southern District of Texas about preparing to handle a possible prize case.

[事实] The district was considered a suitable venue because its Gulf Coast jurisdiction contains the nation’s largest port capable of handling major oil deliveries.

[事实] Judge Crane initially knew little about prize law but assembled a team after being told that such a case could arrive at any time.

[事实] The team produced a 30-page set of rules for prize proceedings in the Southern District of Texas.

[22:51] **Modernized procedures and faster oil sales**

[事实] The Texas rules retain traditional requirements for collecting evidence and questioning relevant parties while adding references to agencies and systems such as the FBI, NCIS, and AIS vessel tracking.

[事实] The rules allow the government to sell perishable or hazardous property, explicitly including crude oil, without waiting for a final ruling.

[事实] The resulting procedure appears more streamlined than civil asset forfeiture.

[24:14] **Judges leave the war question unresolved**

[事实] Justice Department officials argued that an armed conflict, rather than a formally declared war, should be enough for prize law to apply.

[事实] Judge Crane’s team rejected proposed language favorable to that position because the governing statute uses the word “war.”

[事实] The issue will instead be decided by the judge assigned to the first relevant case.

[事实] The rules have been released, while several seized ships—including the Majestic X—remain in the Caribbean only a few days’ sailing distance from Texas.

[推测] The first modern prize proceeding could become a consequential test of executive wartime authority as well as maritime property law.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode makes an obscure legal subject accessible by connecting maritime history to the concrete financial problem created by seized tankers. Its strongest example is the Majestic X, whose reported custody costs dramatically illustrate why officials are searching for an alternative to civil forfeiture.

It also draws an important distinction between reviving prize courts and reviving privateering. Interviews with a maritime historian, a law professor, Justice Department sources, and the federal judge drafting new rules give the discussion both historical depth and contemporary relevance.

[推测] The episode’s principal limitation is that no actual modern prize case has yet tested these rules, so its conclusions about speed, legality, and effectiveness remain provisional. It is particularly suitable for listeners interested in maritime law, executive war powers, sanctions enforcement, and the hidden economic consequences of military seizures.
