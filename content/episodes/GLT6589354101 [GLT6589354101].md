# Robert F. Kennedy: The Last Campaign and Assassination

## 概览

This episode examines Robert F. Kennedy’s final campaign in 1968, beginning with his Indianapolis speech after Martin Luther King Jr.’s assassination and ending with Kennedy’s own assassination at the Ambassador Hotel in Los Angeles.

The hosts frame Kennedy as a compelling but conflicted figure: a grieving brother, a celebrity politician, a champion of the underdog, and an ambiguous ideological actor whose romance often exceeded the clarity of his policy program.

The central conclusion is deliberately unsentimental: although Kennedy became a liberal martyr and symbol of “what might have been,” the speakers argue that he was unlikely to win the 1968 Democratic nomination, and even less likely to defeat Richard Nixon.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **The Indianapolis Speech and the Theme of Assassination**

[事实] The episode opens with Robert F. Kennedy’s speech in Indianapolis after Martin Luther King Jr. was shot, where he invoked his own brother’s murder and quoted Aeschylus.
[事实] The hosts use the speech to introduce the episode’s subject: the assassination of Robert Kennedy.
[推测] The opening positions RFK’s rhetoric as unusually elevated for mass politics and as central to his later myth.

[04:14] **Robert Kennedy’s Early Life and Role Beside JFK**

[事实] Kennedy was born in 1925, the seventh Kennedy child, attended boarding school and Harvard, and briefly worked for Joseph McCarthy.
[事实] He made his name investigating union corruption and organized crime, then became John F. Kennedy’s campaign manager and attorney general.
[事实] The hosts describe him as JFK’s loyal “bad cop,” especially useful on civil rights, organized crime, and during the Cuban Missile Crisis.

[06:24] **Family, Grief, and Hatred of Lyndon Johnson**

[事实] Kennedy married Ethel Skakel, and the couple had ten children.
[事实] After JFK’s assassination, RFK’s grief was described as extreme: he wore mourning clothes, became withdrawn, stopped eating, and built a shrine-like office around his brother.
[事实] RFK and Lyndon Johnson intensely disliked each other; Kennedy refused to call Johnson “the president,” while Johnson resented Kennedy’s hostility.

[10:36] **Senator, Celebrity, and Restless Public Figure**

[事实] Kennedy became senator from New York in January 1965 and quickly became one of the most famous political figures in America.
[事实] The press covered him romantically, and he received huge numbers of letters and speaking invitations.
[事实] The hosts describe his wealthy Virginia household, large staff, many children, and numerous animals as part of a gilded but not necessarily happy life.

[12:47] **A New Moral Persona: Greeks, Camus, Poverty, and South Africa**

[事实] Jackie Kennedy gave him Edith Hamilton’s *The Greek Way*, and RFK became interested in Greek tragedy, Camus, and moral seriousness.
[事实] He visited Mexican migrant workers, the Mississippi Delta, and Bedford-Stuyvesant, presenting himself as a champion of poor and marginalized people.
[事实] In South Africa, he gave the “ripple of hope” speech at the University of Cape Town, which the hosts praise as meeting the moral challenge of apartheid.

[17:06] **Coolness, Ambiguity, and Vietnam**

[事实] By 1967, Kennedy was seen by many journalists as a dark, romantic, late-1960s figure.
[事实] The hosts say his ideology was ambiguous, partly because his advisers came from different wings of the Democratic Party.
[事实] On Vietnam, he shifted from Cold War hawk toward opposition to bombing, support for negotiations, a coalition government in Saigon, and troop withdrawal.
[推测] His anti-war stance was complicated by personal hostility toward Johnson, making his motives easy for critics to question.

[20:10] **Entering the 1968 Race After Eugene McCarthy**

[事实] Eugene McCarthy challenged Johnson in New Hampshire and damaged him badly, after which Kennedy announced his own campaign on March 16.
[事实] Critics accused Kennedy of opportunism because McCarthy had taken the initial risk against Johnson.
[事实] When Johnson later withdrew, Kennedy faced both McCarthy and Hubert Humphrey, Johnson’s vice president and the favorite of many party insiders.

[23:13] **Humphrey, McCarthy, and the Democratic Party Fight**

[事实] Humphrey had a strong liberal civil rights record but remained publicly loyal to Johnson on Vietnam.
[事实] McCarthy despised Kennedy, calling him cowardly and mocking his intellect and toughness.
[事实] One host says he found evidence in Humphrey’s papers that Humphrey’s backers secretly helped fund McCarthy’s campaign against Kennedy.
[推测] The Democratic race became not just a policy contest but a bitter struggle over personality, legitimacy, and who owned the anti-war liberal vote.

[28:51] **Kennedy Mania and the Risk of Violence**

[事实] Kennedy’s path to the nomination depended on winning primaries and overwhelming the convention with celebrity, rallies, emotion, and public pressure.
[事实] He was also intensely hated by conservatives and others who saw him as arrogant, disorderly, or threatening.
[事实] The hosts cite public and private death wishes directed at Kennedy, including from far-right columnist Westbrook Pegler and Clyde Tolson.

[32:05] **Indiana: A Win With Warning Signs**

[事实] After King’s funeral and the unrest that followed, Kennedy resumed campaigning in Indiana.
[事实] Indiana was difficult terrain for him because it was conservative, industrial, and full of white ethnic working-class voters.
[事实] Kennedy adjusted his image by cutting his hair, wearing cheaper suits, and emphasizing his record as attorney general.
[事实] He won Indiana, but his margin depended heavily on Black voters, revealing weakness among white voters.

[37:04] **Oregon: Kennedy’s First Electoral Defeat**

[事实] Oregon was well-educated, very white, progressive, and better suited to McCarthy’s cerebral anti-war appeal.
[事实] McCarthy attacked Kennedy as part of the military-industrial complex and linked him to the origins of the Vietnam problem.
[事实] McCarthy beat Kennedy 45 to 39 percent, the first public election defeat for a Kennedy.
[推测] Oregon exposed a split between McCarthy’s middle-class liberal supporters and Kennedy’s poorer, Black, Hispanic, and Camelot-linked coalition.

[39:21] **California as the Decisive Showdown**

[事实] California’s June 4 primary was crucial because it had recently overtaken New York as the largest state and sent the most delegates to the convention.
[事实] The hosts describe California as a microcosm of 1960s America, with Berkeley protests, Watts riots, hippies, and Reagan-era backlash politics.
[事实] McCarthy campaigned as the calm philosopher, while Kennedy doubled down on huge rallies in poorer urban areas.
[事实] Their televised debate drew 32 million viewers, despite the hosts saying the candidates did not differ much on many issues.

[43:03] **The Final Day of Campaigning**

[事实] On June 3, Kennedy traveled about 1,200 miles across California and gave speeches focused on Vietnam and poverty.
[事实] He was exhausted, had lost his voice, and his hands were bruised and bleeding from shaking so many hands.
[事实] His team worried about assassination threats and poor cooperation with local police, but Kennedy resisted hiding from the public.
[推测] The hosts present Kennedy’s visibility as tied to his need to prove courage, especially after McCarthy’s attacks.

[45:55] **Election Night at the Ambassador Hotel**

[事实] On June 4, Kennedy stayed in Malibu, swam with his family, rescued his son David from nearly drowning, and later went to the Ambassador Hotel.
[事实] Returns came in slowly, but Kennedy won Los Angeles County strongly and appeared to win California narrowly, perhaps 46 to 42 percent.
[事实] Despite the victory, delegate counts still showed Humphrey well ahead, and Kennedy considered offering McCarthy secretary of state if McCarthy withdrew.

[50:19] **Victory Speech and the Kitchen Route**

[事实] Just after midnight, Kennedy addressed a packed ballroom, declared victory in California, called for racial reconciliation, domestic reform, and peace in Vietnam, then said, “on to Chicago.”
[事实] Because the room was crowded and it was late, his team skipped a second rally and directed him through the hotel kitchen toward a press conference.
[事实] In the crush, Kennedy became separated from his bodyguard while Ethel was being helped off the platform.

[52:19] **The Shooting**

[事实] In a narrow kitchen corridor, Kennedy was speaking to a journalist while reaching to shake hands with Mexican busboy Juan Romero.
[事实] A young man pushed through the crowd and fired a gun; Kennedy was shot three times, once in the head and twice in the back.
[事实] Five other people were shot, though less severely.
[事实] Kennedy asked whether everybody was OK, received rosary beads from Romero, and later said “oh no, don’t lift me,” which the hosts identify as his last words.

[54:59] **Death and Sirhan Sirhan**

[事实] Kennedy died after a day of national waiting; his press secretary announced the death at about 2 a.m. on June 6.
[事实] The hosts identify the gunman as Sirhan Sirhan, a Palestinian Christian born in Jerusalem in 1944 who moved to the United States at age 12.
[事实] Sirhan later said he shot Kennedy because of Kennedy’s support for Israel and proposed fighter jet sales, and his diary included the line that Kennedy must die before June 5.
[事实] He was sentenced to death, later commuted to life imprisonment, and the hosts reject conspiracy theories that he was brainwashed by the CIA.

[58:31] **The Myth of the Once and Future King**

[事实] The hosts say graffiti near the scene read “the once and future king,” reinforcing Kennedy’s Camelot-linked afterlife as a liberal martyr.
[事实] One host argues Kennedy was unlikely to become the Democratic nominee because Humphrey led among delegates and party insiders.
[事实] He also argues Nixon probably would have beaten Kennedy in 1968 or 1972 because many Americans wanted order and stability.
[推测] The episode’s final interpretation is that Kennedy’s death mattered enormously as myth, but may not have changed the presidency as much as admirers believe.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its balance between drama and demythologizing. It treats RFK’s assassination as a gripping human and political tragedy, while also questioning the assumption that he was destined for the presidency.

[推测] Its strongest sections connect personality, rhetoric, electoral mechanics, and the atmosphere of 1968. The rivalry with McCarthy and Humphrey prevents the story from becoming only a martyr narrative.

[推测] Its limitation is that some judgments, especially about whether Kennedy could have won, depend on counterfactual reasoning. The hosts acknowledge that listeners may disagree, and the transcript presents this as interpretation rather than settled fact.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in 1960s America, presidential politics, political mythmaking, and the gap between public memory and electoral reality.
