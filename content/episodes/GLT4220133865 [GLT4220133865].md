# The Riotous History of British Election Campaigns

## 概览

This episode of *The Rest Is History* traces the long, unruly history of British election campaigns, using Dickens’s *The Pickwick Papers* as an entry point into a world of public voting, bribery, drink, processions, violence, and political theatre.

The hosts move from medieval parliaments and uncertain early voting customs through rotten boroughs, Whig and Tory party politics, and eighteenth-century electoral disorder. A recurring argument is that elections were not always the calm, private, administrative exercises modern voters expect; they were often public festivals mixed with coercion, corruption, and physical intimidation.

The second half focuses on vivid case studies: Westminster in 1698, Coventry in 1705, Bristol in 1830, Cheltenham in 1865, Bolton in 1868, and Islington West in 1886. The episode ends by contrasting the comic absurdity of historic election violence with the serious reality of modern threats against MPs.

## 分段落总结

[00:31] **Dickens and Election Theatre**

[事实] The episode opens with a passage from Charles Dickens’s *The Pickwick Papers*, describing Samuel Slumkey being coached to shake hands, pat children, and kiss babies during an election campaign.

[事实] The hosts say Dickens wrote the novel in 1836, four years after the Great Reform Bill widened the franchise.

[事实] They describe Dickens as contemptuous of politicians, MPs, Parliament, and elections.

[推测] The Dickens example is used to frame elections as public performance, with staged gestures and crowd management already recognizable in modern campaigning.

[02:35] **The Episode’s Main Question**

[事实] The hosts say they are in the middle of a parliamentary election and want to look at the history of election campaigns.

[事实] They describe older elections as colourful, carnival-like events with booze, ribbons, crowds, babies, bribery, hooliganism, and violence.

[事实] They say this episode will focus on the deep history of general elections, with a later episode planned on more recent elections.

[推测] The episode’s central contrast is between bland modern campaigning and the more chaotic, communal, and dangerous election culture of the past.

[04:49] **Medieval Origins of Representation**

[事实] The hosts say early parliaments in 1236 or 1237 did not include a Commons and were mainly gatherings of barons approving taxes.

[事实] They identify Simon de Montfort’s 1265 parliament as an early moment when burgesses from major towns were summoned to London.

[事实] They say it is unclear how these representatives were chosen, and that the process was not democratic in the modern sense.

[事实] They connect representation to taxation, including the later American slogan “no taxation without representation.”

[推测] The episode presents elections as emerging from disputes over consent to taxation rather than from a fully developed democratic ideal.

[07:59] **Who Could Vote**

[事实] The hosts cite a 1429 Commons petition complaining that too many people of little or no means were helping choose MPs.

[事实] They say a county vote became tied to owning a freehold worth 40 shillings or more.

[事实] They say it is possible, and generally considered likely, that some women such as rich widows could vote under the Tudors and Stuarts.

[事实] Borough voting rules varied widely by local custom.

[推测] The franchise was shaped less by equal citizenship than by property, status, local practice, and attempts to limit participation.

[09:33] **Borough Franchises and Electoral Oddities**

[事实] The hosts describe scot-and-lot boroughs, where people liable for local payments such as church and poor relief rates could vote.

[事实] They describe potwalloper boroughs, where householders with their own hearth could vote if they were not receiving poor relief.

[事实] They describe corporation boroughs, where a closed town corporation could choose MPs without regard for ordinary residents.

[事实] They explain faggot voting as the subdivision of land into nominal freeholds to create extra voters controlled by a landowner.

[事实] They note that Oxford and Cambridge had university MPs into the twentieth century.

[12:55] **Old Sarum and Rotten Boroughs**

[事实] Old Sarum had the right to send two MPs to Parliament from 1295, even after it had declined into a place with virtually no inhabitants.

[事实] In 1802, its election was held in a booth in a cornfield under a tree, with five electors.

[事实] The Earl of Caledon had bought the land and controlled the electors, reportedly paying £60,000, which the hosts say was roughly equivalent to £80 million.

[事实] At the same time, Manchester had no MPs at all.

[推测] Old Sarum is used as the clearest example of how pre-reform representation could preserve historical privilege long after its practical justification had disappeared.

[15:25] **How Early Modern Elections Worked**

[事实] By the rise of Whig and Tory party politics, the hosts say about 200,000 people could vote.

[事实] Elections followed the dissolution of Parliament and the issuing of writs, but voting happened locally and could occur at different times and over multiple days.

[事实] Candidates used “treating,” meaning feasts, beer, pies, and public hospitality, as a central campaign practice.

[事实] Campaigns used ribbons, cockades, banners, colours, bands, taverns, and processions.

[事实] Voters declared their choices orally in public, so everyone knew how they had voted.

[推测] Public voting made bribery, intimidation, and social pressure much easier than under a secret ballot.

[19:44] **Tallies, Long Polls, and Election Rituals**

[事实] Voters could arrive in groups called tallies, led by tally captains wearing a candidate’s colours and sometimes accompanied by banners or bands.

[事实] Some elections could last for weeks, until a 1696 law required polls to be completed within 40 days.

[事实] At the end of each day, candidates addressed crowds and led processions back to taverns.

[事实] Winning candidates were carried through town on chairs, after which the crowd ritually tore the chair apart.

[推测] Elections functioned as public rituals as much as decision-making procedures.

[22:06] **Violence as a Built-In Feature**

[事实] The hosts say elections created enormous scope for fighting and bad behaviour.

[事实] They mention attacks on taverns, stone throwing, halberds, grenadiers, and drunken crowds.

[事实] They compare the atmosphere to football fans and describe the tradition as carnivalesque.

[推测] The combination of alcohol, public voting, partisan identity, and local rivalries made disorder structurally likely.

[24:25] **Whigs and Tories**

[事实] The hosts introduce the 1690s under William III as the beginning of recognizable Whig and Tory party politics.

[事实] They describe Whigs as linked to the Protestant succession, the Glorious Revolution, mercantile interests, and a softer attitude toward dissent and nonconformism.

[事实] They describe Tories as associated with the established church, a slight Jacobite tinge, and appeals to Old England.

[事实] They stress that Whig and Tory identities evolved and were not always fixed.

[推测] The simplified Whig-Tory contrast gives listeners a framework for following later election stories, even though the hosts acknowledge it is broad and comic.

[26:06] **Westminster 1698**

[事实] In the 1698 election, 104 of 269 seats were contested.

[事实] Westminster had a large electorate of about 15,000 to 20,000 people and was often contested.

[事实] Whig candidates Charles Montague and James Vernon opposed Sir Henry Dutton Colt.

[事实] Vernon and Montague arrived with several thousand horsemen, charged Colt’s supporters, and one of Colt’s men later died from wounds.

[事实] Colt petitioned Parliament, alleging use of off-duty grenadiers and pistol threats, but Parliament rejected his complaint.

[推测] The case shows that very high levels of force could still be treated as acceptable electioneering.

[29:13] **Norfolk 1698 and the Fatal Duel**

[事实] Sir Henry Hobart lost his Norfolk seat to two Tories in 1698.

[事实] Hobart blamed Oliver Le Neve for spreading claims that he had acted cowardly at the Battle of the Boyne.

[事实] Hobart challenged Le Neve to a duel, but his sword tangled in Le Neve’s coat and Le Neve ran him through.

[事实] Le Neve fled to Holland, returned after two years, and was acquitted.

[推测] Electoral defeat, reputation, and masculine honour could become inseparable enough to turn politics into lethal personal conflict.

[31:10] **Coventry 1705**

[事实] The hosts describe early eighteenth-century politics as involving taxation, war, religion, and culture-war conflict.

[事实] Coventry was a Whig stronghold because of its Presbyterian and dissenting political culture.

[事实] Before the 1705 election, hundreds of Tory supporters entered the town armed with halberds and seized the Guildhall.

[事实] Tory voters were admitted freely, while Whig voters were intimidated and abused.

[事实] In 1707, Parliament declared the election void and the Whigs won.

[推测] Unlike Westminster’s violence, the Coventry seizure of the voting process crossed a line that Parliament was willing to punish.

[34:20] **Nineteenth-Century Missiles and Bristol 1830**

[事实] The hosts say nineteenth-century candidates could be pelted with stinking fish, dead dogs, cats, rats, rotten eggs, and similar missiles.

[事实] In Bristol in 1830, rival Whigs Edward Prothero and James Bailey fought for support.

[事实] Prothero supported Catholic votes and abolition, while Bailey was described as more moderate and gradualist on slavery.

[事实] Bailey’s procession was attacked by intoxicated men and boys armed with bludgeons, and later 27 people had to be taken to the infirmary.

[事实] Prothero was hit on the head with an oak ladder and lost by 500 votes.

[推测] The Bristol story shows that intraparty battles could become violent when local economic interests and moral causes collided.

[39:02] **Reform Acts and the Secret Ballot**

[事实] The Great Reform Act of 1832 expanded the electorate to about 650,000.

[事实] The 1867 Reform Act raised the electorate to about 2 million, and the 1884 reform raised it to about 6 million.

[事实] Even after these reforms, women still could not vote, and only about 60% of men in Britain could vote.

[事实] The 1872 Ballot Act introduced secret ballots.

[事实] The hosts say violence declined with the expansion of the franchise, stronger party organization, secret ballots, and Gladstonean rectitude, but did not disappear.

[推测] Secret voting weakened the older system of bribery and coercion because candidates could no longer easily verify voters’ choices.

[40:43] **Cheltenham 1865**

[事实] The hosts cite Durham University’s Electoral Violence Project, which records 3,000 Victorian incidents and 96 deaths.

[事实] In Cheltenham in 1865, liberal messenger William Lines sang in support of the liberals and Colonel Barclay.

[事实] Conservative chemist John Thomas Glass confronted Lines and shot him in the mouth with a revolver.

[事实] Lines died, leaving a pregnant wife and four children.

[事实] Glass was charged with murder, convicted of manslaughter, and sentenced to 15 years’ penal servitude.

[推测] The discussion of “election excitement” suggests that contemporaries sometimes treated campaign frenzy as a partial explanation for extreme violence.

[44:48] **Bolton 1868**

[事实] In Bolton in 1868, the hosts say liberals discovered where Conservative voters lived, kidnapped them, detained them in Thomas Barlow’s mill, and supplied them with drink until their senses were stupefied.

[事实] Some detained voters shouted from the mill windows for help.

[事实] Rescuers found the mill guarded by armed roughs, said to be Irishmen, and a pitched battle followed before the men were rescued.

[事实] Thomas Barlow’s brother was the mayor, a Wesleyan Methodist temperance campaigner, and a Sunday schoolteacher.

[事实] The hosts say the mayor had supposedly paid the roughs involved.

[推测] The story contrasts respectable civic and religious identities with organized electoral coercion behind the scenes.

[46:40] **Islington West 1886**

[事实] In Islington West in 1886, the Liberal Party was split between liberal unionists and liberals.

[事实] Liberal Unionist Richard Chamberlain planned a large meeting, and his former Liberal allies advertised it in the *Pall Mall Gazette* as an event where people could come to see disorder.

[事实] Men rushed the stage, and Dr Slater, who had come because he was genuinely interested in politics, was hit with a chair and punched.

[事实] Arthur Brighty, an 18-year-old joiner, said he had no interest in politics and went because he expected a fight.

[事实] Brighty was sentenced to a month’s imprisonment with hard labour because the judge believed others would pay a fine for him.

[推测] By this point, election violence could attract participants motivated by entertainment as much as political conviction.

[49:25] **Modern Coda**

[事实] The hosts acknowledge that electoral violence is not just comic history, noting that two MPs had lost their lives in recent years.

[事实] They also mention an MP standing down because of threats of violence and Rosie Duffield cancelling hustings because of concerns about violence.

[事实] They then return to comic regret over the disappearance of beer drinking, ribbon wearing, chairing candidates, and other older election rituals.

[事实] They say the next episode will cover recent elections, including John Prescott punching a man and Neil Kinnock’s Sheffield rally.

[推测] The ending balances nostalgia for political theatre with an explicit warning that intimidation and violence remain serious democratic problems.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its vivid reconstruction of elections as lived public events: noisy, physical, local, and often coercive. It makes institutional history memorable by tying abstract reforms to concrete scenes of taverns, processions, rotten boroughs, armed crowds, and public voting.

[推测] Its strongest feature is the use of sharply chosen anecdotes, especially Old Sarum, Westminster, Coventry, Cheltenham, Bolton, and Islington West. These examples show that electoral reform was not just about expanding votes, but also about changing the social experience and safety of voting.

[推测] Its limitation is that the comic tone can compress complex issues such as class, religion, slavery, Irish migration, and political violence into fast-moving anecdotes. The hosts do acknowledge the seriousness of modern threats, but the episode is primarily narrative and entertaining rather than a systematic academic account.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in British political history, historical oddities, electoral reform, and the continuity between old and modern campaign theatre.
