# England vs Italy: Football Is Coming Home, or Going to Rome?

## 概览

The episode uses England’s football final against Italy as a playful frame for a much longer history of English-Italian relations. The hosts argue that Italy, especially Rome, has had an unusually deep influence on English identity, from Roman Britain and Anglo-Saxon Christianity to Renaissance culture, finance, travel, food, fashion, and football.

The first half presents England as culturally and spiritually subordinate to Rome: Anglo-Saxon kings define themselves through Roman memory, the papacy converts and disciplines England, and medieval English culture and finance are deeply shaped by Italian models. The Reformation then changes the tone, turning Rome from sacred centre into Protestant antagonist.

The second half shifts toward Britain’s later confidence and condescension: the Grand Tour, macaroni fashion, Garibaldi mania, and football links show admiration and imitation, while the world wars expose mockery, hostility, and darker treatment of Italians in Britain. The episode ends by returning to the football final, with both hosts predicting an Italian victory.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Football as the Frame**

[事实] The hosts open after England’s tense win over Denmark and ask whether football is “coming home” or “going to Rome.”
[事实] They describe Italy as a very different opponent from Denmark: not northern kin, but a Mediterranean cultural opposite with a long intertwined history with England.
[推测] The match provides a comic pretext for discussing how England has defined itself through admiration, rivalry, and suspicion of Italy.

[02:24] **Defining Italy Before Modern Italy**

[事实] The hosts argue that Italy can be discussed before the 19th-century kingdom because Romans had a coherent sense of “Italia” by the early second century BC, possibly earlier.
[事实] They say Italian peoples rebelling against Rome in the early first century BC minted coins bearing the name Italia.
[事实] Julius Caesar’s expeditions to Britain in 55 and 54 BC are presented as the first description of what would eventually become England.

[04:13] **Anglo-Saxon England and Rome**

[事实] The hosts decide to begin English history with the Anglo-Saxons rather than with figures such as Boudicca or Caratacus.
[事实] They note that Rome was central to Anglo-Saxon imagination and that works like the Anglo-Saxon Chronicle and Bede situate Britain in relation to Rome.
[推测] This creates a “colonial” or peripheral relationship in which England sees Rome as the centre and itself as the edge.

[07:30] **English Kings and Roman Imperial Models**

[事实] The hosts discuss East Anglian kings, Sutton Hoo, the Wolfingas, and claims of descent from Caesar through Woden.
[事实] Alfred’s supposed papal anointing, Athelstan’s use of Roman sites, and Edgar’s coronation at Bath are used as examples of English rulers imitating Roman imperial authority.
[推测] The hosts present this as evidence of an English “cultural cringe” before Roman grandeur.

[10:04] **Rome as the City of the Popes**

[事实] Rome is described not only as an imperial capital but as the city of Peter, Paul, and the bishops of Rome.
[事实] Gregory the Great sends missionaries to convert the pagan Anglo-Saxons, and the hosts retell the famous “Angles/angels” pun story.
[事实] English pilgrims establish a hostel in Rome, and the name of Borgo Santo Spirito is linked to this English presence.

[12:12] **Papacy, Power, and Medieval Tension**

[事实] In the Anglo-Saxon period, the pope is described as a kind of spiritual godfather to the English.
[事实] Gregory VII supports William the Conqueror, but his claims for papal superiority over earthly kings help create later tension.
[事实] Henry II’s penance after Thomas Becket’s murder and King John’s submission of England as a papal fiefdom are cited as examples of papal dominance.
[推测] The hosts suggest these conflicts helped establish a legacy of English resentment toward Rome.

[14:48] **Italy as Medieval Cultural and Economic Powerhouse**

[事实] Chaucer goes to Italy to negotiate wool trade deals and visits Genoa and Florence.
[事实] The hosts say Chaucer may have met Boccaccio and may have seen Giotto’s paintings, making Italian culture important to the Canterbury Tales and English literature.
[事实] Italian banking families lend heavily to English kings, and Edward III’s defaults damage both Italian banks and England’s economy.
[事实] Lombard Street is linked to Lombard goldsmiths given land in London.

[19:40] **The Reformation Recalibrates the Relationship**

[事实] The popes Henry VIII falls out with are Italian, and the pope cannot grant Henry’s divorce because he is under Charles V’s control.
[事实] Henry’s claim that his realm is an empire is framed as a rejection of papal authority.
[推测] The hosts see the Reformation partly as England rebelling against its old sense of marginality before Rome.

[21:47] **Shakespeare and Italy**

[事实] The hosts discuss Shakespeare’s many Italian settings, including Venice, Rome, and Verona.
[事实] They mention theories that Shakespeare’s Italian knowledge has led some to speculate about missing years in Italy or alternative authorship involving John Florio.
[事实] Shakespeare’s geography is also described as unreliable, with the Bohemian coast in The Winter’s Tale used as an example.
[推测] Italy still signifies sophistication, culture, and dramatic richness for English audiences, despite Protestant hostility to Rome.

[23:41] **Jacobean Italy and Anti-Catholic Imagination**

[事实] Shakespeare’s sympathetic friar in Romeo and Juliet is contrasted with more propagandistic Jacobean tragedies.
[事实] In those plays, Italy is portrayed as a place of cardinals, poison, depravity, murder, rape, and incest.
[推测] The hosts treat this as a turning point where Italy becomes both admired and morally suspect in English culture.

[25:03] **Milton, Galileo, and Protestant Mythmaking**

[事实] Milton travels to Italy, speaks Italian, knows Italian poetry, and is deeply influenced by Italian culture.
[事实] He is said to have visited Galileo and to reference him in Paradise Lost.
[事实] The hosts argue that Milton helped popularize the idea of Galileo as a victim of Roman Catholic repression, while also saying Galileo was not tortured or imprisoned.
[推测] They suggest English Protestant anti-Catholicism later shaped secular and atheist hostility toward Christianity.

[28:05] **Mary of Modena, Jacobites, and Rome as Anti-England**

[事实] Mary of Modena, wife of James II, is mentioned in connection with the warming-pan baby controversy before the Glorious Revolution.
[事实] The Jacobite court and later Jacobite claimants are associated with Rome, including a monument in St Peter’s.
[推测] The hosts frame this as a tipping point where Rome becomes linked with defeated anti-English or anti-Protestant causes while British power rises.

[30:03] **The Grand Tour and Rome’s Ruins**

[事实] The second half begins with the Grand Tour, described as a late 17th- and 18th-century journey by wealthy young men through Europe, usually ending in Italy.
[事实] Gibbon’s emotional arrival in Rome and inspiration for The Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire are discussed.
[事实] Dr Johnson is quoted as saying a man who has not been to Italy feels an inferiority because he has not seen what a man ought to see.
[推测] By this point, Britain admires Italy’s past while increasingly visiting it as a wealthy observer of ruins.

[32:54] **Macaroni Culture and Italianate Fashion**

[事实] The hosts say grand tourists brought back enthusiasm for macaroni and Italian-style dress.
[事实] “Macaroni” becomes a term for fashionable, extravagant men wearing tight clothes, colourful stockings, large wigs, scent, and short coats.
[事实] The hosts connect this to English anxieties about effeminacy, foreign taste, opera, and later Italian influence on football culture.
[推测] Italian style becomes both aspirational and mockable in British culture.

[36:22] **Gender, Sexuality, Mods, and Italian Style**

[事实] The hosts discuss satirical writing that presents the macaroni as vain, performative, and sexually suspect.
[事实] They connect this tradition to 20th-century mods, who wore Italian fashions, rode scooters, and went to coffee bars.
[推测] The episode suggests that British suspicion of Italianate style often carried anxieties about masculinity and class.

[37:56] **Yankee Doodle and the Macaroni Insult**

[事实] The hosts explain that “Yankee Doodle” began as a British insult toward Americans during the American War of Independence.
[事实] The line about sticking a feather in a cap and calling it macaroni is explained as mocking Americans for trying and failing to look fashionable.
[推测] Macaroni becomes a symbol not just of Italian influence but of insecure imitation.

[38:46] **Byron and Italy’s Humiliation**

[事实] Byron is presented as another English aristocrat drawn to Italy’s ruins and beauty.
[事实] The hosts say he also mourns Italy’s weakness, fragmentation, and subjection to the church and Austrians.
[推测] Romantic admiration for Italy depends partly on seeing it as beautiful but politically fallen.

[39:15] **Garibaldi, Biscuits, and British Support for Unification**

[事实] Garibaldi is introduced through the Garibaldi biscuit, which the hosts say was invented in 1861.
[事实] Garibaldi visits Newcastle in 1854 and receives a golden sword from the people of Tyneside, who support European freedom.
[事实] British working men and liberals support Italian unification as a cause of liberty.

[41:27] **The Expedition of the Thousand and Garibaldi Mania**

[事实] Garibaldi’s 1860 expedition to Sicily attacks the Bourbon Kingdom of the Two Sicilies.
[事实] The hosts say the Royal Navy helps make his landing possible by sending HMS Hannibal and two gunboats.
[事实] When Garibaldi visits London in 1864, huge crowds greet him, and servants sell soap suds from his wash basin.
[推测] In this period, Garibaldi makes Italy a popular British liberal and anti-Catholic cause.

[44:29] **Garibaldi’s Legacy and Football Links**

[事实] The hosts say Garibaldi’s name survives in British roads, pubs, merchandise, and biscuits.
[事实] Nottingham Forest’s red shirts are linked to Garibaldi’s red shirts.
[事实] AC Milan is said to have begun as a cricket and football club founded by English people, explaining the English “Milan” rather than “Milano.”
[事实] Juventus’s black-and-white colours are linked to Notts County.

[46:17] **World War I and British Distrust of Italy**

[事实] The hosts say Italy began the First World War aligned with Germany and Austria, then broke that alliance and joined Britain and France after bargaining for territory.
[事实] British leaders including Asquith, Churchill, Lloyd George, and Jackie Fisher are quoted or paraphrased expressing contempt for Italy.
[推测] Italy’s wartime diplomacy feeds a British stereotype of Italian slipperiness and unreliability.

[47:34] **World War II Mockery and Stereotypes**

[事实] In the Second World War, Mussolini’s Italy fights against Britain and again enters the war late.
[事实] The hosts read a Wavell comment portraying Italy as Germany and Japan’s foolish companion.
[事实] British jokes and descriptions portray Italian soldiers as overdressed, picnic-ready, or needing pasta during war games.
[推测] Older stereotypes about macaroni, style, and effeminacy shape British mockery of Italian military performance.

[49:53] **Italy’s Brutal Campaign and the Partisans**

[事实] The hosts state that the Allied invasion of Italy was brutal and bloody.
[事实] They say the British came to admire and respect Italian partisans, while also being somewhat frightened of them.
[事实] After the war, they note that British resentment toward Italy did not last like resentment toward Germany or Japan.
[推测] They attribute this partly to the idea that Italians helped liberate themselves and were seen as having been led astray.

[50:41] **Italian Civilians in Britain During the War**

[事实] The hosts describe Italians in Britain, especially Scotland, being badly treated after Italy entered the war.
[事实] Italian shops were smashed and looted, one shop owner in Edinburgh is said to have been shot dead, and some employers dismissed Italian staff.
[事实] Churchill’s “collar the lot” policy led to Italians being rounded up and interned.
[事实] The Arandora Star, carrying mostly Italians and some Germans to Canada, was torpedoed by a German U-boat, killing about 800 people.

[52:28] **Postwar Italian Culture and British Aspiration**

[事实] The hosts say postwar relations were generally good and that Italy’s 1950s economic miracle allowed it to overtake Britain economically.
[事实] Italian films, including Fellini and Antonioni, are described as highbrow; Italian fashion, shoes, trousers, and mod style are also highlighted.
[事实] Tuscany is presented as shorthand for upper-middle-class elite aspiration.
[推测] Italian cultural prestige returns after the war through lifestyle, cinema, design, travel, and fashion.

[53:49] **Rome, Renaissance, Naples, and Tourism**

[事实] The hosts debate whether Italy’s modern appeal is rooted more in Rome or in the Renaissance.
[事实] Rome and Pompeii are described as major sources of excitement for tourists, while Tuscany and Florence are associated with Renaissance art and landscape.
[事实] Naples is described as a thrilling city, with jokes about rubbish, coffee, and Maradona’s shrine.
[推测] The episode presents Italy’s appeal as a layered combination of ancient ruins, Renaissance beauty, urban chaos, and football mythology.

[55:34] **Italy’s Artificiality and Football Identity**

[事实] The hosts mention Maradona urging Neapolitans to identify against Italy during Argentina’s 1990 World Cup match in Naples.
[事实] They refer to David Gilmour’s argument that Italy is a “fake country” that perhaps should not exist as one state.
[事实] They note that similar arguments could be made about Britain and that the final is England versus Italy, not Britain versus Italy.
[推测] National identity is treated as unstable and contested on both sides.

[56:17] **Predictions for the Final**

[事实] Both hosts predict that Italy will beat England, one suggesting 2-1 and the other 1-0.
[事实] They argue Italy looked stronger in the semifinals and that England may struggle against Italian defensive craft.
[事实] They joke that the Italian team look like figures from Italian history and embody national stereotypes.
[推测] The historical sweep of the episode is folded back into football, casting Italy as stylish, wily, and historically formidable.

[58:04] **Closing Reflections on the Euros Series**

[事实] The hosts say they have enjoyed using England’s Euro fixtures as opportunities to discuss relations with Germany, Ukraine, Denmark, and Italy.
[事实] They thank the England team for reaching the final and making the Italy episode possible.
[事实] They close by wishing England good luck and repeating “It’s coming home.”

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s strength is its broad historical range. It moves quickly from Roman identity and Anglo-Saxon Christianity to Chaucer, Shakespeare, Milton, the Grand Tour, Garibaldi, wartime internment, postwar cinema, fashion, and football, while keeping the England-Italy final as a comic through-line.

Its best moments come from connecting high politics and culture with everyday objects: Lombard Street, macaroni, Garibaldi biscuits, football shirts, scooters, coffee, and Tuscany all become ways to explain how Italy lived in the British imagination.

[推测] The main limitation is that the tone relies heavily on national stereotypes and jokes, even when the hosts sometimes acknowledge the darker side of that history. Listeners looking for a systematic diplomatic history may find it too conversational and digressive.

[推测] It is especially suited to listeners who enjoy history told through cultural memory, humour, sport, and odd details rather than a formal chronological lecture.
