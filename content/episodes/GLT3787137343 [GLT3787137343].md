# The Man in the Iron Mask: Evidence, Myth, and Eustache Degé

## 概览

This episode investigates whether the Man in the Iron Mask was a real prisoner, how the legend grew from scattered records, and why later writers turned him into a symbol of royal secrecy under Louis XIV.

The discussion moves from Alexandre Dumas’s romanticized version to contemporary evidence: a 1687 report of a masked state prisoner, Bastille records from 1698 and 1703, and later claims by Voltaire. The hosts repeatedly separate what the records say from what later literary and political imagination added.

The central conclusion is that the masked prisoner most likely was Eustache Degé, described in documents as a valet arrested in Calais in 1669 and tracked through several prisons until his death in the Bastille in 1703. The hosts stress that his identity is more secure than the reason for his imprisonment.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Dumas and the Historical Question**

[事实] The episode opens with a dramatic passage from Alexandre Dumas’s *The Man in the Iron Mask*, presenting a prisoner in black, wearing a steel visor and guarded by Saint-Mars.

[事实] The hosts explain that Dumas’s story belongs to *The Vicomte of Bragelonne*, part of the wider Three Musketeers sequence.

[事实] D’Artagnan and Saint-Mars are described as real historical figures, leading to the episode’s core question: whether the masked prisoner was also real.

[05:01] **The Contemporary Evidence for a Masked Prisoner**

[事实] The hosts identify three contemporary pieces of evidence for a prisoner wearing a mask, though not always specifically an iron one.

[事实] A Jansenist bishop’s gazette from 4 September 1687 reports an unknown state prisoner transported by Saint-Mars from Exilés to Saint-Marguerite under extreme secrecy and wearing a “mask of steel.”

[事实] A Bastille register entry from 18 September 1698 records Saint-Mars arriving as the new governor with an unnamed prisoner who was always masked.

[事实] A later Bastille entry records the prisoner’s death on 19 November 1703 and burial in an unmarked grave; this source describes the mask as black velvet and later adds the name Monsieur de Marchiel in the margin.

[09:48] **Louis XIV and Absolutist France**

[事实] The hosts place the story in the late seventeenth and early eighteenth centuries, during the long reign of Louis XIV, the Sun King.

[事实] They describe French royal absolutism as a long-building trend that intensified under Louis XIII and Louis XIV, aided by ministers such as Richelieu and Mazarin.

[事实] The Fronde of 1648 is presented as a formative crisis for Louis XIV, leaving him determined never again to face a similar challenge to royal authority.

[12:12] **Versailles, Fouquet, and Saint-Mars**

[事实] Versailles is presented as a symbol of Louis XIV’s resolve and power, partly inspired by the palace of Nicolas Fouquet.

[事实] Fouquet, superintendent of finances, alarmed Louis with his wealth and was arrested by D’Artagnan before being sent to Pignerol.

[事实] Saint-Mars is described as a trusted jailer who commanded Pignerol, Exilés, Saint-Marguerite, and eventually the Bastille.

[推测] Saint-Mars’s repeated presence makes him the key administrative thread connecting the known records of the masked prisoner.

[15:42] **The Darker Side of Royal Power**

[事实] The hosts describe Louis XIV as glamorous and powerful but also capable of making offenders disappear into the prison system.

[事实] They discuss lettres de cachet, royal orders that did not need to specify either the crime or the length of imprisonment.

[事实] The chain of command is described as short: prisoner, jailer, minister of war Louvois, and the king.

[推测] The Man in the Iron Mask became so powerful as a symbol because he represented the hidden coercion behind the visible splendour of Versailles.

[18:52] **Voltaire Creates the Legend**

[事实] Voltaire learned stories about the masked prisoner while imprisoned in the Bastille in 1717, fourteen years after the prisoner’s death.

[事实] In *The Century of Louis XIV*, published in 1751, Voltaire describes an iron mask with springs that allowed the prisoner to eat.

[事实] Voltaire claims the prisoner was treated with extraordinary respect, including Louvois standing while the prisoner sat and the governor serving him meals.

[事实] Voltaire also notes that no person of major consequence in Europe was reported missing when the prisoner was sent to Saint-Marguerite.

[23:18] **Royal Brother and Twin Theories**

[事实] Voltaire later stated that the prisoner was an elder brother of Louis XIV.

[事实] The hosts connect this theory to rumors surrounding Louis XIV’s birth, which occurred twenty-three years after his parents’ marriage.

[事实] Another theory, reported before Dumas, held that the prisoner was Louis XIV’s identical twin brother.

[推测] The twin theory is compelling as fiction because it creates a direct threat to royal legitimacy, but the episode presents it as a dramatic theory rather than a proven identity.

[28:52] **The Father Theory and Sensational Explanations**

[事实] Lord Quickswood proposed in the twentieth century that the prisoner was Louis XIV’s real father, supposedly used by Richelieu to impregnate Anne of Austria.

[事实] In this theory, the father later tried to blackmail Louis XIV and was imprisoned and masked.

[事实] The hosts state that Quickswood admitted there was no evidence for the theory.

[推测] This section uses the theory to show how easily the mystery attracts explanations that are satisfying as stories but weak as history.

[31:17] **Marchiel, Mattioli, and English Candidates**

[事实] The name Monsieur de Marchiel appears in a later marginal note, but the hosts say there is no supporting evidence for such a person in the records.

[事实] Count Ercole Mattioli is presented as a strong former candidate because he double-crossed Louis XIV, was abducted to Pignerol, and was held under close watch.

[事实] Newly found Saint-Mars letters indicate Mattioli was probably never taken to the Bastille, so the hosts rule him out.

[事实] The hosts also reject theories involving Oliver Cromwell’s son and the Duke of Monmouth, noting that the masked prisoner was heard speaking and was not reported to have an English or Italian accent.

[35:09] **Eustache Degé as the Likely Prisoner**

[事实] The hosts say that since the 1960s, close study of the documentation has enabled scholars to identify the likely prisoner.

[事实] Letters from July 1669 by Louis XIV and Louvois to Saint-Mars, along with a lettre de cachet, name Eustache Degé.

[事实] Degé is described in the documents as a villain or wretch, arrested in Calais and ordered to be kept in strict isolation.

[事实] The hosts say Degé can be tracked through Pignerol, Exilés, Saint-Marguerite, and the Bastille from 1669 to 1703, making him the only plausible match.

[37:49] **A Valet, Not a Prince**

[事实] The documents describe Eustache Degé as a valet, not as a noble or royal figure.

[事实] The hosts emphasize that Degé was made to work as Fouquet’s valet in Pignerol, which would have been unthinkable if he had been noble or royal.

[事实] Voltaire’s claims about high-ranking men standing before the prisoner are treated as late and possibly exaggerated.

[推测] The strongest explanation offered is that the prisoner’s danger lay in what he knew, not in his birth.

[40:48] **What Degé May Have Known**

[事实] The hosts note that valets could be close to powerful people and might witness sensitive matters.

[事实] Degé was arrested in Calais in 1669, across from Dover, during secret talks between Charles II and Louis XIV that led to the Secret Treaty of Dover.

[事实] The treaty involved English support for Louis XIV against the Dutch, French subsidies to Charles II, and Charles’s promise to convert publicly to Catholicism, which he never did.

[推测] Degé may have been connected to these negotiations as a valet, agent, or witness, but the hosts state that the exact reason for his imprisonment remains unknown.

[43:37] **The Cover-Up That Made the Mystery Famous**

[事实] The hosts cite historian Georges Mangredien as the scholar who compiled the documentation in the 1960s and appeared to favor Eustache Degé as the answer.

[事实] They say further correspondence might one day reveal what Degé actually did.

[事实] Saint-Mars wrote that his method of guarding and transporting the prisoner made everyone try to guess who he was.

[推测] The episode’s final point is that secrecy itself helped create the legend: the attempt to erase the prisoner’s identity made him famous for centuries.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its careful movement from legend to documentation. It gives listeners the pleasure of a mystery while repeatedly checking each theory against the surviving evidence.

[推测] Its strongest feature is the balance between entertainment and historical discipline: Dumas, Voltaire, royal scandal, and conspiracy theories are all discussed, but the final emphasis remains on archival records and probability.

[推测] The limitation is that the ultimate reason for Eustache Degé’s imprisonment remains unresolved. The episode can identify the likely man behind the mask, but not with certainty explain what secret made him dangerous.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in historical mysteries, Louis XIV’s France, the boundary between literature and history, and how state secrecy can create myths more durable than the facts it tried to hide.
