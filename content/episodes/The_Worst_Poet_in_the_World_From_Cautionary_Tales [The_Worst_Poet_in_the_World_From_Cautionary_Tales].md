# The Worst Poet in the World

## 概览

William McGonagall is remembered as the world’s worst poet, especially for “The Tay Bridge Disaster,” whose awkward rhymes and blunt moralizing transformed a real catastrophe into an enduring literary curiosity.

The episode traces McGonagall’s improbable career from weaver and amateur Shakespearean actor to self-proclaimed poetic genius. Despite mockery, cruel hoaxes, failed journeys, hostile audiences, and poverty, he continued writing and performing for 25 years.

Tim Harford first presents McGonagall as a lesson in creating without fear of failure. He then advances a more provocative possibility: perhaps the poet’s incompetence was partly a comic persona, and the supposed victim was actually an astute entertainer who understood his audience.

## 分段落总结

[00:09] **Introducing the “Worst Poet”**

[事实] The episode introduces *Cautionary Tales* as a podcast about historical mistakes and what can be learned from them.

[事实] William McGonagall is described as the world’s worst poet, but the introduction suggests that his reputation may deserve reconsideration.

[01:34] **The Tay Bridge Disaster**

[事实] On a stormy night in 1879, a northbound train entered the Tay Bridge while signalmen John Watt and Thomas Barclay watched from their cabin.

[事实] The train’s tail lamps disappeared after several flashes, and the signal box on the far side stopped responding.

[事实] Moonlight later revealed that roughly a thousand yards of the bridge had collapsed, taking the train and everyone aboard with it.

[05:47] **A Catastrophe Turned into Verse**

[事实] McGonagall’s “The Tay Bridge Disaster” recounts the collapse through strained rhymes, repetitive phrasing, and a concluding lesson about supporting structures with buttresses.

[事实] The poem is widely regarded as one of the worst ever written, yet Tim Harford recalls it as vividly as the childhood image through which he first encountered the disaster.

[事实] Harford frames the central questions of the episode: who McGonagall was, what he can teach us about art, and how poetry could become so memorably bad.

[08:37] **Childhood, Shakespeare, and an Unusual Macbeth**

[事实] McGonagall gave conflicting accounts of his birth year, while describing an interrupted education and claiming that, like Shakespeare, he learned more from nature than from school.

[事实] He worked as a weaver in Dundee, memorized major Shakespearean roles, and entertained his fellow workers with performances.

[事实] During a paid appearance as Macbeth, he refused to die on cue, prolonged the sword fight, and was eventually tackled by the actor playing Macduff.

[事实] The chaotic performance delighted the audience and earned McGonagall a standing ovation.

[14:17] **The “Spirit of Poetry” Arrives**

[事实] When mechanized looms undermined his livelihood at about age 50, McGonagall concluded that fortune had endowed him with poetic genius.

[事实] He said that in June 1877 he felt physically inflamed and heard a voice urging him to write.

[事实] A Dundee newspaper printed his first poem but rejected a later work, “Bonnie Dundee,” and publicly welcomed his threat to stop submitting poetry.

[17:14] **The Failed Pilgrimage to Balmoral**

[事实] After receiving a fraudulent letter claiming Queen Victoria wished to patronize his work, McGonagall walked 59 miles to Balmoral Castle.

[事实] A constable told him the letter was not genuine and reminded him that Tennyson—not McGonagall—was the Queen’s actual poet laureate.

[事实] McGonagall returned to Dundee, publicized the adventure, and was mocked throughout Britain.

[事实] Rather than stop, he continued writing, including war poetry apparently inspired by Tennyson’s success.

[23:04] **More Hoaxes, Travels, and Public Performances**

[事实] Another prank invited McGonagall to meet playwright Dion Boucicault, although Boucicault later sent him five pounds after learning what had happened.

[事实] McGonagall traveled to London and New York but failed to establish the literary career he wanted.

[事实] He accepted another dubious honor naming him “Knight of the White Elephant of Burma” and proudly wore its medal.

[事实] His later performances attracted audiences who threw insults, eggs, and vegetables while he dramatically recited poems in a kilt with a sword and shield.

[25:37] **Death and the Conventional Critical Verdict**

[事实] McGonagall died in poverty in 1902 after writing poetry for 25 years and enduring mockery throughout his career.

[事实] Critics cite his plain statements, clichés, poor meter, trivial ideas, and excessive length as examples of how not to write poetry.

[事实] Scottish literature scholar Gerard Carruthers argued that continuing to republish McGonagall was cruel and that his work should be left behind.

[27:32] **Creativity Without Commercial Value**

[事实] Harford rejects the idea that McGonagall matters only as a negative example, arguing that bad creative work is normal and should not prevent people from making art.

[事实] Easy access to great artists can create an unrealistically high benchmark against which ordinary creative efforts appear worthless.

[事实] Harford distinguishes the market value of art from the personal value gained through trying to express oneself.

[事实] He argues that generative AI, like cameras and record players, may change the economics of creative work without making human creativity impossible.

[30:11] **The Courage to Fail**

[事实] Harford observes that adults often become less creative because they fear failure.

[事实] McGonagall appeared immune to that fear and continued producing and performing despite relentless public ridicule.

[事实] Harford then goes beyond praising persistence and proposes that McGonagall may have been a kind of genius.

[31:00] **Was McGonagall a Brilliant Clown?**

[事实] Harford compares McGonagall to the clown Pagliacci and asks whether his apparent pomposity concealed a deliberately comic persona.

[事实] McGonagall’s chaotic Macbeth performance played especially well to the cheap seats occupied by fellow workers who had helped finance his appearance.

[事实] Several poems contain lines that can be read as deliberate jokes, including an ode celebrating moonlight’s usefulness to thieves, poachers, and lovers.

[推测] These comic details may indicate that McGonagall understood the absurdity of his writing and was entertaining audiences more knowingly than his reputation suggests.

[34:10] **Bad Poetry or Satirical Truth-Telling?**

[事实] Before the Tay Bridge collapsed, McGonagall wrote a poem praising it while pointedly hoping that no accident would befall its passengers.

[事实] As a Dundee resident, he could have known that part of the bridge had already blown down during construction.

[事实] In another disaster poem, he praised a company’s £1,000 donation as something that would fill bereaved hearts “with glee.”

[推测] Harford interprets such lines as possible evidence that McGonagall operated like a court jester, using apparent foolishness to expose uncomfortable truths.

[推测] The fraudulent letters attributed to prominent figures may even have been part of McGonagall’s own construction of his public legend, although the episode provides no conclusive proof.

[36:28] **A Failure Who Knew How to Draw a Crowd**

[事实] McGonagall’s appearances drew such unruly crowds that police excluded him from a Robert Burns ceremony and Dundee eventually banned his recitals.

[事实] He reportedly earned 15 shillings per performance, roughly an ordinary laborer’s weekly wage.

[事实] Harford argues that his poverty resulted partly from the authorities suppressing performances that had become too riotous.

[推测] His public career can therefore be interpreted not simply as artistic failure but as commercially effective clowning whose success became socially unmanageable.

[37:35] **The Mask Remains Unresolved**

[事实] Harford acknowledges that it is impossible to know whether McGonagall was oblivious, knowingly endured humiliation to survive, or orchestrated the entire spectacle as comedy.

[事实] Whatever his intentions, his poems have entertained generations of readers.

[事实] At an Edinburgh auction, a collection of McGonagall poems reportedly sold for more than signed first editions of *Harry Potter* books.

[事实] The episode concludes that the poet mocked as an enduring failure has, in fact, been remembered for a very long time.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s strongest feature is its gradual reversal of perspective. What begins as an amusing biography of an incompetent artist becomes an inquiry into creative courage, commercial value, technological disruption, class-based entertainment, and the unstable boundary between failure and performance.

Its use of McGonagall’s poetry is especially effective because the same awkward lines can support opposite readings: naïve incompetence or deliberate comic mischief. The uncertainty is preserved rather than resolved, inviting listeners to reconsider how confidently audiences infer an artist’s intentions.

[推测] The “comic genius” interpretation is persuasive as a thought experiment but remains speculative; the evidence can also be explained by sincere self-belief and accidental humor. The episode openly recognizes this limitation.

This installment is particularly suited to listeners interested in literary history, creative practice, public humiliation, technological change, and unconventional definitions of artistic success. Its central lesson is generous: creative work can have personal and cultural value even when it fails every conventional test of quality.
