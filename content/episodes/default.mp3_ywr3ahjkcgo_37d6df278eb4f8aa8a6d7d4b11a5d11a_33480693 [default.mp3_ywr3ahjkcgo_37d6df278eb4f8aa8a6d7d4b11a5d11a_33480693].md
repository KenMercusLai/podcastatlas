# Can 100 Random Citizens Fix Connecticut’s Property Taxes?

## Overview

Connecticut recruited 100 randomly selected residents to tackle a problem elected officials had failed to resolve for decades: a property-tax system that places heavy financial pressure on households and local governments. The experiment resembled jury duty for tax policy, combining expert instruction, public deliberation, and collective decision-making.

The assembly learned how Connecticut’s reliance on local property taxes is connected to limited state redistribution, fragmented municipal services, and 169 largely independent school systems. Members debated possible responses ranging from highway tolls and shared school services to higher taxes on million-dollar earners.

The process showed that ordinary citizens can absorb complicated policy information, deliberate across political and social differences, and produce concrete recommendations. It also exposed a fundamental limitation: the assembly could only make proposals, leaving implementation to the same political system that had previously resisted reform.

The final controversy reinforced that tension. State Comptroller Sean Scanlon initially sought to delay publication until after an election, while other organizers argued that the citizens had been promised a timely release. The recommendations were ultimately published, but the dispute damaged trust and demonstrated how quickly an experiment in alternative democracy can encounter conventional political pressures.

## Segmented Summary

[00:26] **A Jury-Duty Model for Tax Policy**

[Fact] About 100 Connecticut residents were randomly selected to participate in a citizens’ assembly focused on reforming the state’s property-tax system.

[Fact] Participants were expected to study civics, property taxes, school funding, and consensus-building before submitting a proposal to the state legislature.

[Fact] The central question was whether ordinary citizens could address property taxes, avert a future budget crisis, and potentially offer a new model of democratic decision-making.

[Speculation] The experiment treated demographic variety and collective deliberation as possible substitutes for professional political expertise.

[04:50] **Why Connecticut’s Property-Tax Problem Persisted**

[Fact] State Comptroller Sean Scanlon said Connecticut had struggled with its property-tax system for roughly 70 years.

[Fact] Some middle-class homes face annual property-tax bills of $20,000 to $30,000 because municipalities depend heavily on local revenue to fund schools, police, water services, and other services.

[Fact] Connecticut contributes less state funding to municipalities than some other states, leading towns and cities to increase property taxes when local budgets fall short.

[Fact] Previous reform efforts often produced reports but no lasting action because meaningful change could require unpopular tax increases or spending cuts.

[Speculation] The assembly was partly intended to break a political stalemate created by elected officials’ concern about reelection and voter backlash.

[07:53] **The Democratic Case for Random Selection**

[Fact] Political scientist Hélène Landemore helped design the experiment and drew inspiration from ancient Greece, where many public officials were selected by lottery rather than election.

[Fact] Landemore argued that elected politicians are disproportionately male, college-educated, attracted to power, and unusually confident.

[Fact] Citizens’ assemblies have previously addressed electoral reform in the Netherlands, constitutional changes in Ireland, and climate policy in France.

[Fact] Organizers sent more than 200,000 invitations, received about 1,500 positive responses, and selected 100 participants using demographic configurations and a lottery machine.

[Fact] Participants were scheduled to meet over four weekends and two all-day online sessions, receiving $1,200 each.

[11:10] **Can Ordinary Citizens Master Complex Policy?**

[Fact] Critics questioned whether 100 randomly chosen residents were qualified to make complicated tax-policy decisions.

[Fact] Landemore responded that collective intelligence is difficult to understand until it is observed in practice.

[Fact] Organizers sought a mix of homeowners, renters, parents, non-parents, and residents from different parts of Connecticut.

[Speculation] The design assumed that a carefully composed group could produce knowledge and judgment beyond what any individual participant initially possessed.

[13:55] **Creating Rules Before Creating Policy**

[Fact] The participants first had to decide their own rules concerning speaking turns, voting, quorum, and shared values.

[Fact] Early discussions became heated, with interruptions and louder participants dominating the room.

[Fact] The group eventually adopted a hand-raising system intended to give quieter and more reserved members opportunities to speak.

[Speculation] Establishing fair discussion procedures was itself a practical lesson in democratic governance and a prerequisite for meaningful deliberation.

[14:54] **A Crash Course in Connecticut’s Fiscal System**

[Fact] Professors and government officials taught the participants about sales taxes, income taxes, business taxes, and local property taxes.

[Fact] Connecticut taxes homes and vehicles, and its municipalities rely more heavily on property taxes than municipalities in states that redistribute more statewide tax revenue.

[Fact] Connecticut has 169 independent towns and cities, nearly all with separate school districts and duplicated services such as transportation, food programs, and special education.

[Fact] Participants who initially knew little about property taxes began reading relevant articles and described themselves as tax-policy enthusiasts.

[Speculation] The educational phase helped turn personal frustration with taxation into a more systemic understanding of revenue, spending, and municipal fragmentation.

[16:49] **Possible Reforms and Ideological Conflict**

[Fact] The assembly considered new taxes, modifications to existing taxes, spending reductions, efficiency improvements, and fairer revenue-sharing among municipalities.

[Fact] Ideas included highway tolls, higher state-park fees for out-of-state visitors, greater taxation of nonprofit hospitals and universities, and consolidation of school or municipal services.

[Fact] A proposed tax on very high earners became especially contentious; opponents warned that wealthy residents might leave Connecticut or exploit loopholes.

[Fact] Participants sometimes strongly disagreed, but the debates also included humor and generally good-natured exchanges.

[Speculation] The assembly reproduced many of the same ideological disagreements found in elected politics, but its structure encouraged members to keep working together instead of campaigning against one another.

[20:09] **Friendship as a Product of Deliberation**

[Fact] Participants began forming friendships, exchanging messages, sharing meals, and socializing after sessions.

[Fact] Landemore said citizens’ assemblies often begin with wariness and conflict before members start helping one another and building on shared ideas.

[Fact] She argued that people divided by party, age, or race can develop deep friendships when given appropriate conditions and sufficient time.

[Fact] Landemore described this as the “love dimension” of politics, drawing on the idea that friendship and civic affection lie at the heart of political life.

[Speculation] The experiment’s social value may have extended beyond its policy recommendations by reducing distrust among people who might otherwise encounter one another only as political opponents.

[23:47] **Ten Recommendations, but No Direct Power**

[Fact] The assembly approved approximately ten recommendations, generally presented in concise documents of one to one-and-a-half pages each.

[Fact] Participant Stacey McGiver said many recommendations were not new and expressed frustration that politicians had previously encountered similar ideas without enacting reforms.

[Fact] The assembly had no authority to pass laws; its proposals had to go through the comptroller and state legislature.

[Fact] Citizens’ assemblies elsewhere have faced the same limitation because their recommendations usually remain subject to conventional political processes.

[Speculation] Without a guaranteed legislative response, such assemblies risk becoming another source of reports that officials can acknowledge without implementing.

[26:28] **Disputes Over the Final Proposal**

[Fact] Some participants objected that recommendations had been altered without sufficient discussion, including a proposed tax on million-dollar earners that changed from 4% to 1%.

[Fact] Despite those concerns, the group voted to include all ten recommendations, including the 1% tax.

[Fact] The planned press conference was canceled after Scanlon raised concerns about releasing the report shortly before an election.

[Fact] Scanlon argued that the proposals might be ignored, denied proper credit, or used as a partisan political weapon.

[Speculation] The episode illustrated the very political risk-aversion that the citizens’ assembly had been created to bypass.

[29:17] **Publication, Political Pressure, and Lost Trust**

[Fact] Landemore and other organizers opposed delaying publication because participants had been promised that their recommendations would enter the public discussion once voting was complete.

[Fact] Following an emergency meeting involving organizers and several citizens, Scanlon withdrew his objection and the report was released before the election.

[Fact] Assembly members chose official spokespeople and stopped speaking individually to the press so the group could maintain a consistent message.

[Fact] The final recommendations included a 1% tax on people earning more than $1 million annually, consolidation of some school services, and interstate tolls intended to reduce or eliminate vehicle taxes.

[Fact] Scanlon did not attend the online release event but said he would support the proposals in the legislature.

[Speculation] The citizens’ decision to centralize communications showed that participation in politics can lead even non-politicians to adopt conventional political tactics.

[31:00] **The Limits of Reinventing Democracy**

[Fact] Assembly spokesperson Riley Janow said the attempted last-minute change to the release plan undermined the premise of the citizens’ work.

[Fact] Landemore acknowledged that the controversy cost the organizers goodwill among participants.

[Fact] She maintained that citizens have a right to know what an informed, deliberative sample of the public thinks about an important issue.

[Fact] The program concluded that new democratic processes must still navigate established institutions and political calculations.

[Speculation] The assembly demonstrated that citizen deliberation can generate informed recommendations, but its long-term significance will depend on whether elected officials translate those recommendations into policy.

## Podcast Review/Summary

The episode’s strongest contribution is its concrete depiction of deliberative democracy. Rather than discussing citizens’ assemblies only as an abstract theory, it follows participants through confusion, education, disagreement, friendship, compromise, and frustration.

Its treatment of the experiment is notably balanced. The assembly is neither presented as a simple cure for dysfunctional politics nor dismissed as amateur policymaking. Participants gained substantial knowledge and produced actionable proposals, yet they also encountered procedural conflict, contested revisions, and limited formal power.

A key limitation is that the episode ends before the legislature’s response is known. It therefore establishes that citizens can deliberate and recommend, but not whether this model can deliver durable policy change.

The episode is especially valuable for listeners interested in public finance, democratic reform, local government, political polarization, and participatory policymaking. [Speculation] It may also appeal to audiences looking for institutional reforms that preserve representative government while giving ordinary citizens a more direct role.
