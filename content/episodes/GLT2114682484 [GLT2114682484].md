# China’s War with Japan and the Beginning of World War II in Asia

## 概览

This episode argues that the Second World War should be understood not only through the familiar European chronology of 1939, but also through the war in East Asia, where China and Japan entered full-scale conflict in 1937. Rana Mitter makes the case that the fighting at Wanping near Beijing on 7 July 1937 marked the beginning of World War II in Asia, and arguably of the wider global war.

The discussion traces the deeper roots of the conflict to the 19th century: China’s weakening under Western imperial pressure, the Opium Wars, loss of sovereignty, internal warlordism, and the rise of Chinese nationalism. It contrasts this with Japan’s rapid modernization after the Meiji Restoration and its turn toward imperial expansion in Taiwan, Korea, Manchuria, and finally deeper into China.

A central theme is that China in the 1930s was not a unified, strong state. Chiang Kai-shek’s Nationalist government held real power around Nanjing and parts of eastern and southern China, but warlords, communists, foreign concessions, and Japanese military footholds complicated any claim to national control. The episode ends with the outbreak of full-scale war, the uneasy Nationalist-Communist united front, the fall of Beijing, and Chiang’s decision to make a major stand at Shanghai.

## 分段落总结

[00:54] **War as Disorder, Not Strategy**

[事实] The episode opens with W.H. Auden’s 1939 BBC reflections after returning from China, describing war as chaotic, inefficient, obscure, and shaped by chance rather than tidy military maps.
[事实] The hosts frame the Chinese war against Japan as either a horrific prelude to World War II or the opening stage of the war itself.
[事实] The conflict is described as little known in the West despite around 14 million deaths and possibly 80 to 100 million refugees.
[推测] Auden’s quotation is used to shift the listener away from abstract military chronology and toward the lived disorder of war.

[03:45] **The Case for 1937 as the Start of World War II**

[事实] Rana Mitter argues that the war between China and Japan, often called the Sino-Japanese War, should be pulled back into the history of the global war.
[事实] He identifies 7 July 1937, when Japanese and Chinese troops fired on each other near Wanping outside Beijing, as the moment when World War II had effectively begun, though nobody involved knew it.
[事实] He contrasts this with the conventional European date of 3 September 1939 and the American emphasis on Pearl Harbor in December 1941.
[推测] The episode’s framing challenges a Eurocentric or America-centered chronology of World War II.

[06:18] **China’s 19th-Century Crisis and the Rise of Nationalism**

[事实] Mitter traces the roots of the conflict back to the mid-19th century, when industrialized Western powers forced their way into Qing China through the Opium Wars.
[事实] Britain, France, and other outside powers imposed territorial losses and infringements on Chinese sovereignty, including Hong Kong and foreign control over tariff structures.
[事实] China became Asia’s first republic in 1912 after the overthrow of the last emperor, Puyi, but remained weak, unstable, and divided.
[事实] Young Chinese nationalists in the 1910s and 1920s protested against imperialism from outside and warlordism from inside, invoking “Mr. Science” and “Mr. Democracy” as possible answers.

[11:12] **Chiang Kai-shek, the Nationalists, and the Communists**

[事实] The Nationalist Party, also called the Guomindang or Kuomintang, was founded by Sun Yat-sen, who died in 1925.
[事实] Chiang Kai-shek succeeded Sun and led an expedition that tentatively reunited China under a Nanjing-based government in 1928.
[事实] Chiang turned against the communists, who had temporarily been his allies, and slaughtered them because he saw them as a threat.
[推测] The prewar Chinese state was already shaped by a civil conflict that would later resume after Japan’s defeat.

[12:05] **Japanese Imperialism and the Expansion into Asia**

[事实] Mitter identifies Japanese imperialism as the second major ideology shaping the conflict, alongside Chinese nationalism.
[事实] Japan modernized rapidly in the Meiji Restoration, transforming its army, education system, railways, and imperial ambitions.
[事实] Japan conquered Taiwan in 1895, colonized Korea in 1910, fought Russia on Chinese territory in 1904-05, and seized Manchuria in 1931.
[事实] In Manchuria, Japanese junior officers helped create the puppet state of Manchukuo, using the deposed Chinese emperor Puyi as figurehead from 1932 to 1945.

[14:26] **Manchuria, False Flags, and Semi-Colonial China**

[事实] The 1931 Manchurian incident is described as a false flag operation involving a bomb near a railway, which Japan used to justify taking over Manchuria.
[事实] Mitter explains that China was often described as “semi-colonial”: not a formal colony as a whole, but with important territories, ports, and rights controlled by outside powers.
[事实] China remained formally sovereign over most of its territory and was a member of the League of Nations, but foreign powers held extensive privileges inside China.
[推测] This semi-colonial condition created openings that Japan could exploit while claiming to protect its own interests.

[15:52] **Shanghai as a Colonial and International City**

[事实] The Treaty of Nanjing in 1842 opened five Chinese ports after the Opium War, and Shanghai became the most successful of them.
[事实] Shanghai contained a French Concession and a larger International Settlement, which Mitter describes as essentially British-run though governed through a municipal committee rather than a colonial governor.
[事实] The settlement included British, Japanese, and American ratepayers, while Chinese residents had few rights until the 20th century.
[事实] Mitter emphasizes Shanghai’s importance as a British colonial city and points to the Bund and the old Hong Kong and Shanghai Bank building as evidence of that legacy.

[20:01] **Why Japan Wanted More Than a Foothold**

[事实] Mitter says the primary driver of Japanese expansion was emotional: Japan had risen from near-colonial vulnerability in the 1850s to imperial power status half a century later, but felt late to the imperial game.
[事实] Japanese leaders resented that Britain and France could keep vast empires while Japan was told further expansion was no longer acceptable.
[事实] The Great Depression and American tariffs damaged Japan’s export markets, especially textiles such as silk stockings, worsening rural economic distress.
[事实] Japan used China’s internal weakness to demand rights for troops on Chinese territory, ostensibly to protect Japanese interests and civilians.

[23:43] **China and Japan’s Ambivalent Cultural Relationship**

[事实] The hosts ask whether Japan still felt cultural inferiority toward China, given China’s deep influence on Japanese civilization.
[事实] Mitter says there was an emotional rebalancing: by the early 20th century Japan was more powerful politically and militarily, while China’s elites often looked to Japan as a model of successful modernization.
[事实] He notes that many Chinese studied in Tokyo on Japanese scholarships, and that Tokyo became a metropolitan meeting point for Chinese, Koreans, Southeast Asians, and Japanese.
[事实] Despite later hatred and violence, there was also cultural interaction and a shared East Asian intellectual space.

[27:00] **Why Not 1931 as the Start of the War?**

[事实] Mitter says modern China officially treats 1931 as the start of World War II because of the Manchurian incident, and textbooks have been changed to reflect that position.
[事实] He notes that Japanese left-wing historians have sometimes referred to the period from 1931 to 1945 as the “15-year war.”
[事实] Mitter personally argues for 1937 because Chinese contemporaries in 1945 described the conflict as an eight-year war of resistance, not a 14-year war.
[事实] He also argues that after Manchuria, the 1933 Tanggu Truce created an uneasy status quo, so the path from 1931 to full-scale war was not a straight line.

[32:10] **Wang Jingwei and Collaboration**

[事实] Wang Jingwei was a major anti-Qing revolutionary who once threw a bomb at a Qing prince, failed to kill him, and was sentenced to death before his sentence was commuted.
[事实] Mitter describes Wang as one of the most prominent figures in the Nationalist Revolution, second in prominence only to Chiang Kai-shek.
[事实] Wang became known in the 1930s as more open to a deal with Japan than harder-line nationalists.
[事实] Mitter compares him to European collaborators such as Laval, Pétain, and Quisling because he later became the most notorious Chinese collaborator with the Japanese occupation.

[35:47] **How Divided Was China Before 1937?**

[事实] Mitter rejects the extreme claim that China was merely a “geographical expression,” but says Nationalist power weakened the farther one moved from Nanjing.
[事实] The Nationalist heartland included the Yangtze Delta area around Shanghai and Nanjing, plus Guangdong in the south.
[事实] Chiang Kai-shek relied on a mix of bribery, coercion, and conquest to manage warlords who often paid nominal allegiance while acting independently.
[事实] The Nationalist government’s reliance on Nanjing and the east-central heartland helps explain why the later wartime move inland destabilized it.

[40:03] **China’s Political Geography and the Communists**

[事实] The hosts identify Beijing, Nanjing, and Shanghai as crucial cities: Beijing as former imperial capital, Nanjing as Nationalist capital, and Shanghai as commercial port.
[事实] Beijing was called Beiping at the time because Nanjing was the capital and “Beijing” means northern capital.
[事实] The communists had been driven from Jiangxi by Chiang Kai-shek and undertook the Long March from 1934 to 1935.
[事实] By the late 1930s, the communists had established themselves in a remote northwestern base that was difficult for Chiang to reach.

[42:39] **The Wanping Incident and the Marco Polo Bridge Crisis**

[事实] On 7 July 1937 near Wanping outside Beijing, Japanese troops stationed on Chinese territory took roll call and found some soldiers missing.
[事实] The missing soldiers later returned, but Japanese commanders accused Chinese troops and the confrontation escalated.
[事实] Japan demanded greater control in the area, including railway junctions that were vital for freight, troop movements, and communications in northern China.
[事实] Chiang Kai-shek had previously followed public non-resistance toward Japan while resisting in smaller ways behind the scenes.

[45:20] **Chiang Kai-shek’s Decision to Fight**

[事实] Chiang wrote in his diary asking whether this was the moment for confrontation with Japan and whether it was the turning point.
[事实] Advisers warned that China’s large army was controlled by warlords, poorly trained, and part of a largely agrarian society, while Japan’s army was heavily mechanized.
[事实] Chiang decided that after Manchuria and further provocations, China had to push back against Japan’s new territorial demands.
[事实] A war council transformed the crisis from a local skirmish outside Beijing into a war between the Japanese Empire and the Republic of China.

[46:19] **Was the War Inevitable?**

[事实] Mitter says Chiang believed war was clearly coming, either immediately or soon.
[事实] Some advisers argued China needed until around 1940 to rearm properly.
[事实] Mitter says Japan gave no indication that it would stop expanding its control over large parts of China.
[推测] Chiang’s choice is presented less as a simple decision for war than as a calculation that further retreat would hand northern China, including culturally symbolic Beijing, to Japan for a generation.

[48:15] **The Communists, Stalin, and the United Front**

[事实] In December 1936, Chiang Kai-shek was kidnapped in Xi’an by warlords including the Young Marshal of Manchuria and Yang Hucheng.
[事实] The communists initially wanted to try and execute Chiang, but Stalin intervened and told them not to do so.
[事实] Stalin feared threats from both Germany in the west and Japan in the east, and did not want China’s most credible anti-Japanese national leader removed.
[事实] After war broke out in July 1937, Nationalists and Communists entered an uneasy united front to oppose Japan, despite hating each other.

[51:05] **Beijing Falls and Shanghai Becomes the Main Stand**

[事实] Beijing fell in the summer of 1937 within weeks, defended largely by local warlord troops rather than Chiang’s central Nationalist army.
[事实] Chiang chose Shanghai as the place to make a major stand and opened a second front there in August 1937.
[事实] One reason was publicity: the world’s press was in Shanghai, and Western observers such as Auden, Isherwood, and Robert Capa were watching China’s war.
[事实] Another reason was military: Chiang’s best German-trained troops were stationed in and around Shanghai.

[53:45] **The Battle of Shanghai and the Move Inland**

[事实] Chinese troops held Shanghai for about three months, and trenches were dug in the streets of the Chinese-controlled parts of the city.
[事实] The foreign-controlled French Concession and International Settlement remained neutral and did not fall until Pearl Harbor.
[事实] More than 100,000 Chinese troops fought at Shanghai, but by November 1937 many had been killed and the rest were ordered to retreat.
[事实] Shanghai fell to the Japanese in November 1937, while Chiang Kai-shek’s government and much of the army moved 800 miles upriver to Chongqing, China’s temporary wartime capital from 1937 to 1946.

[55:50] **Nanjing as the Next Target**

[事实] The episode ends by noting that after Shanghai, Nanjing was next in Japan’s sights.
[事实] The hosts say many listeners will know that events in Nanjing did not unfold well for its people, and they reserve that story for the next episode.
[推测] The ending sets up the Nanjing episode as a continuation of the military collapse and human catastrophe already introduced through Shanghai.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it reframes World War II as a conflict whose origins cannot be understood only through Europe in 1939 or Pearl Harbor in 1941. By centering China in 1937, it makes the Asian theater foundational rather than secondary.

[推测] Its strongest feature is the layered historical setup: the hosts and Rana Mitter connect Qing decline, Western imperialism, Japanese modernization, Manchuria, warlord politics, Nationalist-Communist rivalry, and Shanghai into one coherent path toward war.

[推测] A limitation is that the episode is heavily focused on elite politics, imperial structures, and strategic decisions; ordinary Chinese civilians appear mainly through casualty, refugee, and battlefield references rather than sustained personal stories.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in World War II, modern China, Japanese imperialism, and the global history of empire. It also works well for listeners who already know the European chronology of the war and want a broader Asian framework.
