# Romans in Space: Star Wars, Rome, and Science Fiction

## 概览

This episode uses *Star Wars* as a way into a broader question: why Roman history, imagery, and political anxieties keep resurfacing in science fiction. The hosts begin with the original *Star Wars* crawl, Dave Prowse’s Darth Vader, and childhood memories of the film before turning to its 1970s American setting.

The discussion argues that *Star Wars* is not a direct allegory of Rome, but a mixture of historical signals: the Roman Republic and Empire, Nazi iconography, Cold War anti-totalitarianism, British imperial villainy, and post-Watergate suspicion of authority. The Roman thread appears most clearly in ideas of a republic corrupted from within, an emperor dissolving representative institutions, and liberty dying through elite weakness or popular applause.

In the second half, the conversation widens beyond *Star Wars* to Isaac Asimov’s *Foundation*, Frank Herbert’s *Dune*, *Star Trek*, and *The Hunger Games*. These works use Rome’s decline, frontier collapse, charismatic conquerors, gladiatorial spectacle, and imperial iconography as narrative templates for futuristic worlds.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Star Wars Opening and the Historical Angle**

[事实] The episode opens with the famous *Star Wars* crawl about civil war, Rebel victories, stolen Death Star plans, and Princess Leia fleeing the Empire.

[事实] Tom identifies the opening voice as his own impression of Dave Prowse, the bodybuilder and actor who physically played Darth Vader.

[事实] The hosts say the episode will examine *Star Wars* from a distinctive historical angle, joking that Tom wanted to call it “Romans in Space.”

[推测] The opening frames *Star Wars* not just as nostalgia, but as a cultural artifact whose political and ancient-history references can be unpacked.

[02:07] **Star Wars as a Product of the 1970s**

[事实] Dominic situates *Star Wars* in the era of Vietnam, Watergate, Nixon, and political disillusionment.

[事实] George Lucas is described as a suburban, slightly disaffected, idealistic baby-boomer film-school figure, comparable in some ways to Steven Spielberg.

[事实] Lucas’s earlier success, *American Graffiti*, is described as nostalgic, looking back to the 1950s.

[事实] The hosts discuss claims that Emperor Palpatine reflected Nixon, while noting that Lucas gave many interviews and may have retrofitted or varied his explanations.

[推测] The Empire’s evil authority may draw less from one fixed historical source than from a broader 1970s American suspicion of corrupt power.

[04:49] **Myth, Flash Gordon, and the First Film’s Plot**

[事实] Lucas is said to have described Joseph Campbell’s hero-myth framework as an influence, though Dominic suggests this may have become exaggerated later.

[事实] Dominic says Lucas originally wanted to remake *Flash Gordon*, a 1930s comic and film serial about adventures against an evil empire.

[事实] The first *Star Wars* film follows Princess Leia, the stolen Death Star plans, Luke Skywalker, the droids, Obi-Wan Kenobi, Han Solo, and the mission to destroy the Death Star.

[事实] Luke is compared to King Arthur, with the lightsaber functioning like the sword in the stone and Obi-Wan as a Merlin-like figure.

[推测] The first film’s power comes partly from combining pulp adventure, westerns, medieval myth, and film-school references rather than from a single historical model.

[08:11] **Nazi and Roman Imagery in Star Wars**

[事实] The hosts note that the medal ceremony at the end of *Star Wars* has echoes of Leni Riefenstahl and Nazi mass-rally imagery.

[事实] They connect Nazi iconography itself to Roman imperial symbols such as eagle standards, massed ranks, and ideas of imperial order.

[事实] The Empire’s “stormtroopers” are identified as an obvious Nazi allusion, while the idea that they are also Roman legions is treated more cautiously.

[推测] Rome reaches *Star Wars* partly through twentieth-century visual languages of dictatorship, especially Nazi and cinematic representations of imperial power.

[09:47] **Leia, the Senate, and Classical Hints**

[事实] The hosts identify several classical signals in the first film: the Empire, the Senate, Leia’s white robe, and comparisons between her hairstyle and the Lady of Elche.

[事实] Leia says the Imperial Senate will not tolerate her capture, establishing that some senatorial institution still exists.

[事实] Luke’s clothing is compared to costumes from biblical or ancient-market scenes in films such as *Cleopatra*.

[事实] Obi-Wan tells Luke that the Jedi guarded peace and justice in the Old Republic before “the dark times” and the Empire.

[推测] These details imply a fallen republican order, but the first film leaves most of that history suggestive rather than fully explained.

[12:00] **The Collapse of the Republic**

[事实] Grand Moff Tarkin announces that the Emperor has dissolved the Imperial Senate and that regional governors will control territories through fear of the Death Star.

[事实] The hosts compare this to both Hitler’s destruction of the Weimar Republic and the Roman Republic’s replacement by imperial rule under Augustus and his heirs.

[事实] They note that in the first film, this is almost the only explicit political exposition.

[事实] Dominic says the first film succeeds because it hints at deeper history without showing it, creating a Tolkien-like sense of a larger past.

[推测] The political backstory works best in the original film because it remains atmospheric rather than bureaucratically detailed.

[15:31] **Novelization, Palpatine, and American Republican Anxiety**

[事实] The *Star Wars* novelization, ghostwritten by Alan Dean Foster, gives more detail about the Old Republic rotting from within.

[事实] The novelization describes ambitious Senator Palpatine rising through promises to reunite disaffected people and restore the Republic’s remembered glory.

[事实] The hosts compare Palpatine to both Julius Caesar and Augustus, especially as a figure who turns republican institutions into autocracy.

[事实] Tom links this fear to American republican thought, including the anxiety that a republic can collapse into tyranny like Rome.

[推测] *Star Wars* taps into a long American fear that corruption, luxury, ambition, and elite weakness can destroy liberty from within.

[19:03] **The Prequels and the Roman Republic**

[事实] The hosts discuss Lucas later branding the original film as Episode IV, adding to the sense of a larger backstory.

[事实] Dominic says the Roman allusions become much more overt in the prequel trilogy, especially through Palpatine’s emergency powers.

[事实] Palpatine says he loves democracy and the Republic and promises to lay down his powers once the crisis passes.

[事实] The hosts compare this to Roman dictatorship and especially to Augustus’s affected modesty while consolidating power.

[事实] In *Revenge of the Sith*, Amidala says liberty dies “with thunderous applause” as the Republic becomes the Galactic Empire.

[22:54] **Patricians, Princesses, and Roman Film Traditions**

[事实] Dominic cites scholar Michael Charles on similarities between Roman republican nostalgia and *Star Wars*.

[事实] The hosts note that Roman defenders of republican tradition were often rich patrician elites, while *Star Wars* defenders of liberty include queens and princesses.

[事实] They compare this to *Gladiator*, where the Senate is portrayed as guardian of Rome’s ancient liberties against Commodus.

[事实] Tom suggests *Star Wars* may draw less from Gibbon directly and more from Roman epics such as *Ben-Hur*, *Spartacus*, and *The Fall of the Roman Empire*.

[推测] The franchise inherits popular cinematic Rome: tyrannical emperors, noble aristocratic dissenters, and moralized struggles between liberty and oppression.

[25:04] **Language, Accents, and Imperial Villainy**

[事实] Scholar Martin Winkler is mentioned for arguing that *Star Wars* shares a linguistic pattern with toga films of the 1950s and 1960s.

[事实] Alec Guinness, who plays Obi-Wan Kenobi, had earlier played Marcus Aurelius in *The Fall of the Roman Empire*.

[事实] Han Solo speaks like a modern American rogue, while Governor Tarkin speaks in a clipped British style.

[事实] The hosts compare Tarkin’s speech to British villains in Roman epics and American films about tyranny.

[推测] Accent coding makes American-style informality feel free and heroic, while British formality becomes associated with empire, cruelty, and oppression.

[28:11] **Cold War Layers and Mixed Historical Allegory**

[事实] The hosts say Roman epics were Cold War stories about democracy and freedom versus totalitarianism.

[事实] They also note that *Spartacus* was written by Dalton Trumbo, who had been blacklisted, complicating simple anti-Soviet readings.

[事实] Dominic argues that *Star Wars* is not a straightforward allegory of Roman history.

[事实] The villains are described as a mix of Nixon’s Republicans, Nazis, Soviets, British imperial oppressors, and Romans.

[推测] Rome gives *Star Wars* historical grandeur because it sits behind multiple western stories about republics, empires, corruption, and decline.

[30:50] **Isaac Asimov, Foundation, and Gibbon**

[事实] After the break, the hosts introduce Isaac Asimov’s comment that *Star Wars* contains “more than a whiff” of *Foundation*.

[事实] Asimov is described as a Russian-born American scientist and science-fiction writer, famous for the three laws of robotics and the *Foundation* novels.

[事实] *Foundation* is said to be directly inspired by Edward Gibbon’s *Decline and Fall of the Roman Empire*.

[事实] The novels begin in a vast Galactic Empire centered on Trantor, near the point when decline is about to begin.

[推测] *Foundation* translates Rome’s imperial collapse into galactic scale more directly than *Star Wars* does.

[33:43] **Psychohistory and Predicting Decline**

[事实] The Foundation is established by Hari Seldon, a psychohistorian who uses mathematics and historical data to predict the future.

[事实] Seldon foresees imperial collapse and creates the Foundation as a beacon during the coming darkness.

[事实] Tom links this to the American tradition of applied history, including ideas such as the Thucydides Trap.

[事实] He mentions attending a Stanford conference organized by Niall Ferguson about historical prediction.

[推测] The discussion treats psychohistory as a science-fiction version of recurring hopes that history can reveal rules for future power politics.

[36:23] **The Mule, Islam, and Unpredictable Historical Shocks**

[事实] In the second *Foundation* novel, Seldon’s predictions fail because of the Mule, a mutant telepath who conquers the galaxy and the Foundation.

[事实] Tom interprets the Mule as an echo of Muhammad and the rise of Islam, though he calls it a hostile portrayal.

[事实] The hosts discuss how this reflects older historiography that treated Islam’s emergence as a startling event from outside predictable imperial patterns.

[事实] They contrast this with later scholarship that explains Islam’s rise through Roman-Persian conflicts and Arabian religious contexts.

[推测] The Mule functions as a narrative challenge to deterministic history: a single charismatic or anomalous figure can disrupt grand models.

[38:04] **Dune, Spice, Oil, and Islamic History**

[事实] The hosts move to Frank Herbert’s *Dune*, set on the desert planet Arrakis, which produces spice that enables space travel.

[事实] Tom says spice is clearly connected to oil, especially given the period just before OPEC’s rise.

[事实] He also links it to older ideas that Mecca was a major center of the spice trade, an idea he says Patricia Crone later rebutted.

[事实] The plot of *Dune* is described as involving aristocratic houses competing over control of spice.

[推测] *Dune* draws on orientalist, Islamic, oil-age, and imperial-collapse motifs to create a future history with ancient and modern political resonances.

[40:15] **Paul Atreides, Muhammad, and Arthur**

[事实] Paul Atreides becomes Muad’Dib, a prophetic figure who launches a jihad that consumes the Galactic Empire.

[事实] Tom sees parallels with Muhammad, while Dominic also notes Arthurian echoes in Paul’s providential destiny.

[事实] The hosts say stories of Arthur and biographies of Muhammad emerge around the same broad post-Roman historical context.

[事实] Both Arthur and Muhammad are described as charismatic figures on the margins of former Roman imperial territory.

[推测] Science fiction can combine post-Roman frontier myths because galactic settings allow ancient historical dramas to be reimagined at enormous scale.

[42:42] **Star Trek, Gladiators, and Bread and Circuses**

[事实] Tom identifies *Star Trek* as another major science-fiction franchise with Roman elements, including the Romulans.

[事实] In the 1968 episode “Bread and Circuses,” Kirk, Spock, and McCoy encounter a civilization that resembles Rome in the mid-twentieth century.

[事实] Spock and McCoy are forced to fight as gladiators, and the spectacle is televised in color.

[事实] The episode ends with a pun revealing that worshippers are devoted not to the sun but to the Son, meaning Christ.

[推测] The episode turns Roman spectacle into a Cold War-era television allegory about violence, mass media, empire, and Christianity.

[45:35] **The Hunger Games and Futuristic Rome**

[事实] The hosts identify *The Hunger Games* as a recent franchise with overt Roman echoes, though it is set in the future rather than in space.

[事实] The story takes place in a collapsed North America where districts send boys and girls as tributes to fight to the death.

[事实] Its capital is called the Capitol, the country is called Panem, and characters have classical names such as Coriolanus, Plutarch, and Cato.

[事实] Tom says his daughters loved the films and that he has seen them many times.

[推测] *The Hunger Games* uses Roman names and gladiatorial spectacle to give a young-adult dystopia historical weight and immediate political symbolism.

[47:34] **Why Rome Keeps Returning**

[事实] Dominic asks why Rome, rather than Greece or Persia, occupies this central place in the western imagination.

[事实] Tom says Rome is glamorous, visually recognizable, and able to stand in for sinister empires such as the British Empire, the Nazis, or the Soviet Union.

[事实] Roman customs such as slavery, gladiatorial combat, violence, and whipping are described as unsettling but exciting for cinematic storytelling.

[事实] The hosts say the Roman Republic’s transformation into empire and the Empire’s later decline and fall are foundational events in political imagination.

[推测] Rome remains useful in science fiction because it combines class, spectacle, tyranny, moral drama, and recognizable warnings about power.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it treats popular science fiction as a historical archive of anxieties: fear of dictatorship, nostalgia for republics, suspicion of empire, and fascination with collapse. It is strongest when showing that *Star Wars* does not map neatly onto Rome but absorbs Rome through films, American political culture, Nazi imagery, and Cold War storytelling.

[推测] A major highlight is the breadth of comparison. Moving from *Star Wars* to *Foundation*, *Dune*, *Star Trek*, and *The Hunger Games* shows how flexible Roman history has become as a cultural language for future worlds.

[推测] The limitation is that some links are necessarily interpretive. The hosts often distinguish between explicit evidence and possible over-reading, especially around Lucas’s intentions and whether particular Roman echoes were conscious.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in ancient history, science fiction, film history, and the political afterlife of Rome rather than listeners looking for a straightforward plot recap of *Star Wars*.
