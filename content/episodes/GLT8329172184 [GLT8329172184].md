# Is Christmas Really a Pagan Festival?

## 概览

This Christmas episode tests the popular claim that Christmas is essentially a pagan festival borrowed from Mithras, Sol Invictus, Saturnalia, and other ancient cults. The hosts frame the discussion through a joking return to Dan Brown and *The Da Vinci Code*, then ask whether the familiar “Christianity stole Christmas” argument survives contact with ancient evidence.

Their main conclusion is that many famous parallels are either unsupported or far more complicated than popular summaries suggest. The transcript says there is no ancient source for Osiris, Adonis, or Dionysus being born on December 25, no evidence that baby Krishna received gold, frankincense, and myrrh, and no evidence that Roman Mithras died and rose again after three days.

The discussion then turns to Sol Invictus and Saturnalia. The hosts accept that December was a Roman season of festivity and that a December 25 festival of “the unconquered one” probably existed, but they argue this does not prove Christmas was copied from it. They end by presenting a Christian explanation for December 25: March 25 as the date of Christ’s incarnation, plus nine months.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Dan Brown and the Pagan Christmas Claim**

[事实] The episode opens with a passage from *The Da Vinci Code* claiming that Christian icons and Nativity motifs were borrowed from older religions.
[事实] The quoted claims include Mithras being born on December 25, dying, being buried in rock, and rising after three days.
[事实] The hosts use the passage as a festive setup for examining whether Christmas is really pagan in origin.

[02:00] **The Central Question**

[事实] The hosts say the idea that Christmas was stolen from Mithraism, Sol Invictus, or other pagan cults is widely repeated.
[事实] They distinguish between Jesus probably not being born on December 25 and the stronger claim that Christmas is a pagan construction.
[推测] The episode’s argument is designed to separate uncertain historical dating from unsupported claims of direct borrowing.

[04:00] **Claims About Osiris, Adonis, Dionysus, and Krishna**

[事实] The transcript states that there is no ancient source saying Osiris, Adonis, or Dionysus was born on December 25.
[事实] Osiris is said to have had an important birth festival, but the speaker places it around early September after calendar conversion.
[事实] The transcript says there is no evidence that Krishna was presented with gold, frankincense, and myrrh as a baby.

[06:13] **Mithra and Roman Mithras**

[事实] The hosts distinguish the ancient Persian god Mithra from the Roman cult of Mithras.
[事实] Plutarch mentions pirates practicing mysteries of Mithras before Christ, but the speaker says this is probably not the same as the later Roman officers’ cult.
[事实] Roman Mithraism is described as a first-century AD cult that originated in Rome and borrowed exotic elements.

[09:09] **Mithras and the Missing Resurrection Parallel**

[事实] The speaker says Mithras was not buried in a rock; rather, Mithras appears to emerge fully formed from a rock.
[事实] The transcript states that the speaker found no ancient references to Mithras dying and being reborn on the third day.
[事实] The hosts joke that the error could belong to the fictional Sir Leigh Teabing, though they do not treat that as the likely explanation.

[10:00] **Sol Invictus Is Not Mithras**

[事实] The phrase Sol Invictus means “the unconquered sun” and appears as a title connected with Mithras in inscriptions.
[事实] The speaker argues that Mithras and Sol Invictus are separate gods, and that Mithraic images can show them as separate figures.
[事实] The transcript rejects the idea that sun worship was introduced to Rome only by Elagabalus, citing evidence for a Roman cult of Sol much earlier.

[13:07] **The Source Chain for December 25**

[事实] Ronald Hutton is cited as saying the first certain record placing Christmas on December 25 is the Calendar of Philocalus from 354.
[事实] A commonly cited passage says Christians chose December 25 because people were already drawn to a sun festival.
[事实] The speaker says that passage is not from a fourth-century author but from a 12th-century Syrian scribe.

[16:00] **The Syrian Writer’s Context**

[事实] The 12th-century Syrian writer was commenting in a context where Eastern Christians celebrated Christmas on January 6 or 7, while Latin Christians used December 25.
[事实] The speaker says the writer assumed the Eastern date was authentic and was trying to explain why Western Christians differed.
[推测] The anti-Western or polemical context weakens the passage as evidence for fourth-century Christian decision-making.

[17:44] **The Calendar of Philocalus Evidence**

[事实] The Calendar of Philocalus includes an entry interpreted as “30 chariot races were ordered to mark the birthday of the unconquered one.”
[事实] The speaker says it is not completely certain that “the unconquered one” in this entry means Sol.
[事实] Even if it does refer to Sol, the transcript says the evidence does not prove that the December 25 sun festival predates Christmas.

[19:00] **How Important Was Sol’s December Festival?**

[事实] The speaker says Sol had a major feast on August 28, with evidence going back at least to Augustus.
[事实] The Calendar of Philocalus also records 36 chariot races for Sol on October 22, more than the 30 races listed for December 25.
[推测] The December 25 Sol festival may have existed, but the transcript presents it as too uncertain and possibly too minor to explain Christmas by itself.

[21:24] **Saturn and the Golden Age**

[事实] After the break, the hosts introduce Saturn through Justinus, who describes Saturnus as a just ruler under whom no one lived as a slave and property was held in common.
[事实] Saturn is linked with the area around Rome, Janus, the Capitoline hill, and traditions about a lost golden age.
[事实] The speaker says Saturn’s origins and meaning are difficult because Roman divine traditions are old, contradictory, and unevenly sourced.

[29:07] **Saturn’s Darker Side**

[事实] The transcript says Christian writers claimed gladiatorial deaths during Saturnalia were offerings to Saturn.
[事实] Saturn’s cult statue had its ankles bound with wool, which the speaker interprets as a sign of his dangerous power.
[事实] Saturn was also equated with the Greek Kronos, who devoured his children before being overthrown by Zeus.

[31:50] **What Saturnalia Was**

[事实] Saturnalia’s great feast day was December 17, not December 25.
[事实] By the first century BC, the Saturnalia had expanded into a festive season lasting roughly a week, until December 23.
[事实] Its rituals included sacrifice, a public banquet, the loosening of Saturn’s bonds, and cries of “Io Saturnalia.”

[34:07] **Social Inversion at Saturnalia**

[事实] The hosts describe Saturnalia as a time when Roman social conventions were temporarily overturned.
[事实] Men could wear the synthesis instead of the toga, and free people could wear the liberty cap associated with freed slaves.
[事实] Slaves could talk back to masters, be served dinner by them, and eat first.

[35:51] **Mock Rulers, Drinking, Gambling, and Gifts**

[事实] Saturnalia involved riotous drinking, gambling, and the selection of a princeps or rex to organize jokes and commands.
[事实] Lucian is cited for describing party games in which a temporary ruler could order embarrassing or absurd performances.
[事实] People exchanged candles and sigillaria dolls, which could be cheap, mass-produced, or expensive.

[39:09] **Limits and Reactions**

[事实] The hosts note that Saturnalia’s temporary freedom for slaves was limited because normal hierarchy returned afterward.
[事实] Seneca disliked Saturnalia and complained about its excess and its challenge to Roman dignity.
[事实] Pliny the Younger is described as withdrawing to read while others celebrated, while poets such as Catullus and Statius enjoyed the festival.

[42:29] **Did Saturnalia Become Christmas?**

[事实] The speaker says Saturnalia and Christmas were celebrated separately in late antiquity, including into the fourth and fifth centuries.
[事实] The transcript says Christmas gift-giving is much later, around the 15th century, rather than inherited directly from Saturnalia.
[事实] The Lord of Misrule is presented as a medieval tradition with no demonstrated link back to Saturnalia.
[推测] The similarities between Saturnalia and Christmas are treated as seasonal and convergent rather than proof of direct descent.

[44:54] **Why December 25?**

[事实] The speaker says early Christians were slow to celebrate birthdays because birthdays were seen as pagan.
[事实] Origen is cited as saying scripture records no birthday celebrated by a righteous man.
[事实] After the Council of Nicaea in 325, debates over Christ’s humanity and divinity made the identification of Jesus’ birthday more important.
[事实] The speaker argues that Christians calculated December 25 from March 25, the date associated with Christ’s incarnation, plus nine months.

[49:21] **A Roman Christian Ending**

[事实] The hosts say early Christmas celebration is not well documented, though it probably involved hymns and prayer.
[事实] They close with a fourth-century hymn by Saint Ambrose, “Veni Redemptor Gentium,” performed by the choir of Saint Bartholomew the Great.
[推测] The ending reinforces the episode’s conclusion that Christmas is Roman in historical setting, but not simply pagan in origin.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s strongest value is its source criticism: it takes a familiar internet-level claim and tests it against dates, texts, calendars, and later scholarly assumptions.

[推测] Its highlight is the way it keeps the argument lively while still distinguishing between “possible,” “probable,” and “actually evidenced.” The discussion of the 12th-century Syrian writer is especially useful because it shows how a popular claim can rest on a misdated or decontextualized source.

[推测] A limitation is that listeners who want full citations will need to follow up on the names and works mentioned, including Hutton, Heemans, Justinus, Virgil, Tacitus, Lucian, Macrobius, and Origen.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in Roman religion, early Christianity, Christmas traditions, and the difference between appealing historical parallels and demonstrable historical transmission.
