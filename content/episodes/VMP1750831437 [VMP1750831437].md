# From $1,500 to $1.5 Billion: How Dr. Squatch Built a Men’s Soap Brand

## Overview

Dr. Squatch founder Jack Halldrup traces the company’s journey from a $1,500 side project to a consumer brand acquired by Unilever in 2025 for a reported $1.5 billion. The idea began with his dissatisfaction with consulting, a psoriasis diagnosis, and an unexpectedly positive experience using cold-process soap from a farmers market.

The business initially grew through e-commerce, family-assisted fulfillment, subscription boxes, and early Facebook advertising. A forced rebrand from Sasquatch Soap to Dr. Squatch ultimately strengthened the company’s identity, while a humorous 2018 video advertisement gave the brand a distinctive voice and dramatically accelerated customer acquisition.

Halldrup also discusses the less visible costs of growth: separating from a childhood friend and business partner, cutting staff during a cash-flow crisis, raising outside capital, and eventually recognizing that he was no longer the right person—or sufficiently motivated—to lead a 300-person consumer-products company.

His final assessment is that the outcome was roughly half execution and half luck. Starting, surviving, and executing well created the opportunity, but favorable timing, emerging digital advertising channels, a recurring product, and a category ready for disruption helped determine the scale of the result.

## Segment Summary

[02:28] **A Tiny Soap Business Becomes a Billion-Dollar Brand**

[Fact] A humorous 2018 advertisement helped transform Dr. Squatch from a small online business into a brand selling millions of soap bars per month.

[Fact] Halldrup launched the business with approximately $1,500, and Unilever acquired it in 2025 in a deal reportedly worth $1.5 billion.

[Fact] The original product idea came from a bar of soap Halldrup bought at a farmers market while looking for products that might help with his psoriasis.

[03:52] **Leaving the Consulting Track**

[Fact] Halldrup grew up in Carmel, Indiana, studied business at Indiana University, and began his career as a consultant in Chicago.

[Fact] He disliked advising businesses without being directly involved in building them and experienced some imposter syndrome in the role.

[Fact] Seeing the travel-heavy lifestyle of senior partners convinced him that the conventional career destination was not one he wanted.

[Fact] Rather than immediately quitting, he immersed himself in entrepreneurship books and podcasts, including The 4-Hour Workweek and The Lean Startup.

[07:17] **Health Problems Lead to a Product Discovery**

[Fact] Frequent travel made it difficult for Halldrup to maintain healthy habits, and around 2012 he developed psoriasis.

[Fact] The condition prompted him to learn more about diet, natural foods, and personal-care products.

[Fact] After using cold-process soap from a farmers market, he noticed that his skin felt softer and less dry than it did after using body wash.

[Fact] His research showed that cold-process soap differed fundamentally from the mass-produced, high-temperature soap commonly sold in stores.

[09:14] **Turning an Overlooked Product into an Online Opportunity**

[Fact] Halldrup had roughly $10,000 in savings and viewed that money as potential startup capital.

[Fact] He believed many men like his friends would appreciate artisan soap but were unlikely to discover it at farmers markets.

[Fact] His initial opportunity was not to reinvent soap, but to use e-commerce and branding to introduce an existing product to a new audience.

[Fact] He experimented with making soap himself, then decided that customer acquisition and brand building were more important than becoming the manufacturer.

[17:27] **Launching Sasquatch Soap in 90 Days**

[Fact] Halldrup set a 90-day deadline to find a manufacturer, modify a website template, handle basic legal work, and launch the company.

[Fact] His sister designed a Sasquatch character reflecting the desired combination of masculinity, humor, whimsy, and outdoor culture.

[Fact] The company launched as Sasquatch Soap with four or five products and an initial inventory of roughly 400 to 500 bars.

[Fact] Each bar cost about $1.50 to produce and sold for approximately $6, while Halldrup stored and shipped the inventory from his bedroom closet.

[20:45] **Early Sales and Family-Powered Fulfillment**

[Fact] In its first partial year, the company generated about $12,000 in sales through word of mouth, friends and family, Facebook posts, and a small Google AdWords budget.

[Fact] Halldrup moved through New York, Colorado, and San Francisco while continuing to operate the business remotely.

[Fact] His mother took over fulfillment from his childhood bedroom and assembled gift sets using cigar boxes sourced from local stores.

[Fact] Sales reached approximately $80,000 in 2013, eventually creating enough volume to justify using a professional fulfillment center.

[26:19] **A Friendship Becomes a Business Partnership**

[Fact] Halldrup brought in his childhood friend AJ Fountain, who initially sold soap door to door for a commission of $1 per bar.

[Fact] Fountain later invested family money, received an ownership stake, and became Halldrup’s business partner.

[Fact] The pair approached small West Coast retailers, although Halldrup believed many early store owners bought because they personally liked the young founders rather than because they considered the concept exceptional.

[Fact] Subscription services such as Bespoke Post and Birchbox became important early marketing channels, sometimes converting about 10% of sampled customers into buyers.

[Fact] The value of those services declined as their terms shifted from paying for samples to demanding free products, fees, and control over subsequent sales.

[32:03] **Going All-In and Discovering Facebook Advertising**

[Fact] Halldrup went full-time on the company after his employer failed in the second half of 2014.

[Fact] With about $5,000 left in savings, he spent approximately $3,500 on a General Assembly digital-marketing course.

[Fact] The course showed him that Facebook advertising was more suitable than Google search advertising because few customers were actively searching for men’s natural soap.

[Fact] Facebook allowed the company to present soap as an entertaining impulse purchase, and revenue nearly tripled in the month after Halldrup applied what he learned.

[35:25] **A Trademark Crisis Produces a Better Name**

[Fact] Another soap seller had prior rights to the name Sasquatch Soap, forcing Halldrup’s company to reconsider its identity.

[Fact] The team struggled to find an acceptable replacement and viewed the dispute as a potentially existential threat.

[Fact] They ultimately chose Dr. Squatch, making the Sasquatch more of a character while using “Doctor” to give the brand a more elevated position.

[Fact] The new trademark was accepted, and Halldrup concluded that the forced rebrand produced a stronger name and encouraged the company to develop the character’s persona.

[40:50] **Facebook Ads Establish a Repeatable Growth Engine**

[Fact] One early Facebook advertisement used a free stock image of a Sasquatch figurine, a digitally added bar of soap, and a simple “soap for men” message.

[Fact] The ad remained one of the company’s best performers for roughly six months and acquired customers for about $15 each.

[Fact] Revenue reached approximately $750,000 in 2015 and $1.8 million in 2016.

[Fact] Growth increasingly depended on an integrated e-commerce system that included paid advertising, subscriptions, merchandising, email marketing, and customer retention.

[45:00] **Conflicting Visions End a Business Partnership**

[Fact] Halldrup believed the company was still in its early stages and could become much larger, while Fountain wanted a business that supported an outdoor-focused lifestyle.

[Fact] Both ambitions were considered legitimate, but their diverging goals made the partnership unworkable.

[Fact] The separation placed significant emotional strain on their childhood friendship and required a financially complex buyout.

[Fact] Because the company had limited cash despite $1.8 million in revenue, the buyout used upfront, monthly, and performance-based payments spread across several years.

[49:22] **Overexpansion Creates a Survival Crisis**

[Fact] Soon after the partner buyout, the company added employees, office costs, and agency expenses faster than it could sustainably support them.

[Fact] Marketing performance declined when Halldrup stepped away from direct oversight and outside agency relationships failed to produce the expected results.

[Fact] In 2018, he reviewed bank statements line by line, eliminated expenses, and dismissed two employees.

[Fact] The retrenchment felt like the company’s first major backward step after several years of continuous growth.

[52:14] **A $15,000 Video Changes the Company’s Trajectory**

[Fact] Inspired by Dollar Shave Club-style advertising, Halldrup considered hiring the Harmon Brothers but could not afford their proposed $250,000 production fee plus $250,000 in managed advertising.

[Fact] Dr. Squatch instead worked with San Diego agency Raindrop to produce a roughly three-minute video for about $15,000.

[Fact] The advertisement used humor, an outdoor shower, and a bearded spokesperson to argue that men should replace conventional soap with natural cold-process soap.

[Fact] The campaign acquired some customers for $3 to $4, making it profitable enough for the company to spend as much as its supply chain could support.

[Fact] Dr. Squatch generated a little over $5 million in 2018 sales, with Halldrup attributing roughly 30% to 50% of that revenue to the video.

[Fact] Beyond immediate sales, the campaign established the brand’s voice and provided a template for future content.

[59:32] **Outside Capital Accelerates a Proven Model**

[Fact] In 2019, Halldrup raised $1.3 million from Guild Capital after deciding he wanted to pursue a larger outcome rather than operate the company indefinitely as a lifestyle business.

[Fact] The investment valued the company at $5.7 million after the financing and gave the investor slightly more than a 20% stake.

[Fact] The new capital was directed primarily toward hiring talent and increasing advertising expenditure.

[Fact] Revenue rose from about $5 million in 2018 to roughly $20 million to $23 million in 2019 and $113 million in 2020.

[Fact] Product-line expansion increased average order value and customer value, enabling the company to spend more on acquisition.

[65:04] **Pandemic Tailwinds and Majority Investment**

[Fact] The company initially slowed its plans when COVID-19 began because management was uncertain about demand and inventory risk.

[Fact] Demand soon accelerated as consumers spent more time online and hygiene products received greater attention.

[Fact] Halldrup said the company was already growing rapidly before the pandemic but acknowledged that COVID-19 provided a substantial tailwind.

[Fact] In 2021, a private-equity group purchased a majority stake in Dr. Squatch.

[67:13] **Recognizing the Limits of Founder Leadership**

[Fact] Halldrup concluded that his strengths and interests were better suited to an earlier-stage company than to managing a large organization.

[Fact] Sustaining the pace of work and the emotional burden accumulated over approximately a decade had become increasingly difficult.

[Fact] He stepped down as CEO in 2023, when Dr. Squatch had roughly 300 employees, and transitioned to a board role.

[Fact] He described the large-company CEO position as feeling increasingly like another job, echoing the employment structure he had originally left behind.

[70:06] **Stepping Away and Reorienting Around Family**

[Fact] Halldrup did not leave with another business already planned; he primarily wanted to remove the weight of day-to-day responsibility.

[Fact] Remaining on the board allowed him to retain some connection with Dr. Squatch without being its principal operational driver.

[Fact] He has three children and said that being present while they are young became a major priority.

[71:31] **Unilever Acquisition and the Shift to Retail**

[Fact] Unilever acquired Dr. Squatch in June 2025 for a reported $1.5 billion, ending Halldrup’s board involvement and giving him a clean break from the company.

[Fact] Dr. Squatch built its own manufacturing facility to produce cold-process soap at scale, reaching output of approximately five million bars per month.

[Fact] The brand evolved from an almost entirely direct-to-consumer business into one whose majority of sales came through retailers such as Target, Walmart, and grocery stores.

[Fact] Halldrup said the retail business alone had grown to well over $300 million, while e-commerce remained significant.

[74:24] **Execution, Timing, and Luck**

[Fact] Halldrup attributes the outcome approximately equally to skill and luck.

[Fact] On the execution side, he identifies three essential elements: starting so that opportunities can occur, surviving difficult periods, and executing well enough to extend the company’s growth.

[Fact] On the luck side, he cites the timing of e-commerce and Facebook advertising, the recurring nature of soap, favorable category conditions, and serendipitous relationships.

[Fact] He does not believe Dr. Squatch could have been built in the same way ten years earlier because traditional retailers would have been unlikely to accept the product without online proof of demand.

## Podcast Commentary/Summary

This episode offers a detailed case study in discovering a market through personal experience rather than through abstract opportunity analysis. Its strongest material concerns the gradual construction of a business model: matching an unfamiliar product with e-commerce, recognizing the limitations of search advertising, using entertainment to generate demand, and reinvesting only while customer acquisition remained profitable.

It is also unusually candid about founder psychology and the costs of entrepreneurship. The partner separation, cash-flow pressure, layoffs, and decision to step down as CEO complicate the conventional story of a $1,500 investment becoming a billion-dollar exit. The discussion shows that creating a company and operating a mature organization can require different skills, motivations, and personal tradeoffs.

[Inference] The episode is particularly useful for founders, direct-to-consumer marketers, and operators interested in brand positioning, paid acquisition, and scaling transitions. Its central lesson is not that viral advertising alone creates a major company, but that a breakthrough campaign becomes transformative only when the product, margins, fulfillment capacity, retention systems, and willingness to reinvest are already in place.

[Inference] A limitation is that the conversation gives less detail about manufacturing economics, product claims, competitive responses, and the mechanics of the later retail expansion. The account is therefore strongest as a founder and marketing narrative rather than as a complete operational or financial analysis of the company.
