# Fashion Victims: The Dangers of Dress Past and Present

## 概览

This episode uses Alison Matthews David’s *Fashion Victims* to examine how clothing, cosmetics, dyes, and accessories have injured or killed both wearers and makers across history.

The discussion moves from flammable stage costumes and crinolines to hobble skirts, painful footwear, foot binding, corsets, mercury-treated hats, arsenical green dyes, lead cosmetics, and modern garment-worker hazards.

A recurring conclusion is that dangerous fashion was rarely simply ignorance. In many cases, risks were known, but social pressure, class aspiration, gender expectations, profit, weak regulation, and workers’ lack of power kept harmful practices alive.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Postman’s Park and the opening image of deadly dress**

[事实] Tom Holland opens with Postman’s Park in central London, where George Frederick Watts created a memorial to heroic self-sacrifice.

[事实] The first memorial mentioned is Sarah Smith, a pantomime artist who died in 1863 after trying to extinguish flames that had engulfed a companion’s inflammable dress.

[事实] Tom says this story led him to Alison Matthews David’s book *Fashion Victims: The Dangers of Dress Past and Present*, recommended by his daughter Eliza.

[03:42] **Alison Matthews David’s route into fashion hazards**

[事实] Alison Matthews David says she has worked as a fashion, dress, and textiles historian for about 20 years.

[事实] She traces the subject back to field trips to Manchester, where textile machinery, deafening work, tuberculosis transmitted through shuttles, and child injuries revealed the harms of textile production.

[事实] The hosts introduce her work on *Fashion Victims*, her academic position in Toronto, and her later project *The Fabric of Crime*.

[05:34] **Flammable tutus and theatre fires**

[事实] The episode cites a statistic of 10,000 deaths worldwide from theatre fires between 1797 and 1897, with many victims being dancers.

[事实] Alison explains that machine-made tulle, first mass-produced in 1808 by John Heathcote’s bobbinet machine, helped create the light romantic ballerina costume.

[事实] Starched, open-weave tulle combined with gas lighting created a severe fire hazard on stage.

[事实] Ballet girls often came from working-class backgrounds, and theatres lacked consistent fireproofing, flameproofing, or even basic water buckets.

[推测] The discussion frames stage costume danger as an occupational hazard shaped by gender and class, not merely by aesthetic choice.

[10:23] **Crinolines, fire, and social pressure**

[事实] The Lancet described crinoline-related fire deaths as a “holocaust” in the 1860s, using the older sense of burnt offering.

[事实] Crinolines were steel hoop skirt supports that replaced heavy horsehair petticoats and made the bell-shaped skirt lighter and easier to wear.

[事实] Alison says crinolines could function as a physical boundary, helping women claim space and repel unwanted touching.

[事实] Their shape also acted like a chimney flue: air could rush under the skirt, causing flames to blaze upward.

[事实] Victims included upper-class women such as Archduchess Matilde and Oscar Wilde’s sisters, while cooks and maids also wore crinolines and faced similar risks.

[15:53] **Hobble skirts and restricted mobility**

[事实] Alison describes the hobble skirt as a very narrow, ankle-length skirt that became fashionable around 1908 to 1910.

[事实] She compares its silhouette to the traditional Coca-Cola bottle and says the skirt forced women to hobble.

[事实] One origin story links the fashion to Edith Berg, a publicist for the Wright brothers, who tied her skirt during an early airplane flight.

[事实] The skirt caused real mobility hazards, including broken ankles, falls from streetcars, drowning after falling into a lock, and one woman’s inability to escape a horse.

[推测] The hobble skirt shows that fashion danger could come from clothing being too tight and restrictive, not only from being loose or flammable.

[19:02] **Shoes, foot binding, and elite immobility**

[事实] The discussion turns to painful footwear, including narrow European shoes and Chinese foot binding.

[事实] Alison says European men and women also bound or stacked their toes to fit into very narrow fashionable shoes.

[事实] In parts of China, mothers bound girls’ feet from around age six to create the small “lotus foot,” with the aim of beauty and social advancement.

[事实] Alison stresses that women with bound feet could walk, and that they embroidered slippers to display the shaped feet.

[推测] The shared logic across European shoes and Chinese foot binding is that limited mobility could signal elite status because it suggested the wearer did not need to perform labor.

[23:42] **High heels, corsets, comfort, and power**

[事实] The hosts compare historical foot binding with modern high heels, noting that many people still wear painful shoes for fashion.

[事实] Alison says the pandemic made dress more casual and comfort-focused, but warns against judging earlier wearers too simply because fashionable clothing also made people feel stylish or good about themselves.

[事实] Corsets are discussed as another example of discomfort and fashion, but Alison notes that some wearers find corsets supportive and comfortable.

[事实] High heels are described as having masculine and military histories, including Persian and British riding contexts, before becoming strongly gendered as feminine.

[事实] Examples of male heel-wearing include cowboys, French presidents, and Elton John.

[28:14] **Mercury hats and the “mad hatter”**

[事实] Alison explains that fur-felt hats were fashionable from the medieval period into the 1960s.

[事实] Mercury and nitric acid were used to treat rabbit and hare fur so it would felt more effectively after beaver fur became scarcer.

[事实] Hat-making exposed workers to mercury through treated fur and through vapors from processes such as the planking kettle.

[事实] Alison says mercury pollution from hat-making persisted in places such as Danbury, Connecticut, and that the hats themselves can still be toxic.

[事实] British hatters were still using mercury in the late 1960s, and the process was never simply banned but faded as hats went out of fashion.

[34:44] **Arsenical green and poisonous color**

[事实] After the break, the hosts introduce the theme of color through Giacomo Leopardi’s 1827 dialogue between fashion and death.

[事实] Alison explains that vivid green was difficult to create with natural dyes and became newly achievable through chemistry.

[事实] Scheele’s green, created in the 1770s, used arsenic trioxide and copper; by the 1820s, arsenical greens appeared in paper, wallpaper, paint, toys, candies, and fashion goods.

[事实] The hosts discuss claims that some green ball gowns could contain enough arsenic to kill many people, especially because large crinoline-era dresses used many yards of pigment-dusted fabric.

[事实] Punch joked that women wearing Scheele’s green should be marked dangerous or labeled with poison warnings.

[39:34] **Consumer warnings, Chanel, and green symbolism**

[事实] Alison says advice columns told women to test green fabrics with ammonia, which could turn blue because of copper and signal arsenical green.

[事实] She says suspicions about green persisted into theatre wardrobe culture and that Chanel considered green unlucky.

[事实] Alison links Chanel’s avoidance of green to her training as a milliner and seamstress whose teachers may have encountered arsenical greens.

[事实] She also discusses Oscar Wilde’s green carnation as an artificial, coded symbol associated with homosexuality, with some references describing such flowers as dipped in arsenic.

[41:27] **Workers, unions, and arsenic regulation**

[事实] Alison says artificial flower makers, dressmakers, and dyers were especially exposed to arsenic dust and suffered chronic illness or early death.

[事实] Doctors saw both fashionable consumers with rashes from green gloves or wreaths and poor flower workers harmed by the same materials.

[事实] In France, workers protested dangerous conditions, and arsenic was banned in France, Scandinavia, and Germany.

[事实] In the UK, after 19-year-old flower maker Matilda Scheurer died of arsenic poisoning, a public outcry and parliamentary inquiry followed, but arsenic was not banned because it was considered too important to industry.

[推测] The contrast suggests that regulation depended not only on medical evidence but also on political pressure, industrial priorities, and workers’ ability to organize.

[45:41] **Modern toxins, radioactive fashion, and cosmetics**

[事实] The hosts mention an ASOS belt inspired by Alexander McQueen whose studs contained cobalt-60 and were radioactive.

[事实] Alison says Elizabeth I’s white cosmetics likely harmed her face, and she connects skin whitening to class, race, and continuing cosmetic risks.

[事实] Lead white was used because it created an opaque, light-reflecting surface, and 19th-century pearl white creams could cause lead palsy.

[事实] A 2011 FDA study found lead in all 400 lipsticks tested.

[事实] Alison says lead can appear as a contaminant in pigments and may not be listed on cosmetic labels, even though lipstick can be ingested.

[50:06] **Contemporary labor harms and the true fashion victims**

[事实] Alison argues that modern clothing may be more comfortable for consumers while still harming workers.

[事实] She gives examples including sandblasted jeans causing silicosis and trainers assembled with neurotoxic adhesives.

[事实] In response to a listener question, she agrees that sweatshop workers are true fashion victims.

[事实] She says fashion’s labor, environmental, and health problems continue globally and are often unseen by consumers in the global north.

[事实] The hosts close by praising *Fashion Victims* and inviting Alison back for her future crime-focused book.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s strongest value is that it turns fashion history into a history of technology, labor, gender, class, medicine, and regulation. The examples are vivid, but the main point is structural: dangerous clothing persisted because beauty, profit, and social expectation often outweighed safety.

[推测] A major highlight is Alison Matthews David’s ability to connect famous images, such as tutus, crinolines, high heels, top hats, green dresses, and Elizabethan makeup, to concrete materials and production processes. The result is accessible without becoming merely anecdotal.

[推测] The main limitation is that the conversation ranges widely, so some topics receive only brief treatment, especially modern fast fashion, cosmetics regulation, and global supply chains.

[推测] This episode is especially suited to listeners interested in social history, fashion, material culture, labor history, and the hidden costs behind everyday objects.
