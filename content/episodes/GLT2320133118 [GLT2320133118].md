# Sitting Bull, the Ghost Dance, and the Road to Wounded Knee

## 概览

This episode traces the aftermath of Little Bighorn through Sitting Bull’s return to Standing Rock, the disappearance of the buffalo, the spread of railroads and settlers, and the reservation system imposed on the Lakota.

The hosts frame the Ghost Dance not as an isolated religious craze, but as a response to hunger, confinement, political dispossession, and the collapse of an older world. They connect Sitting Bull’s personal humiliation with wider Lakota dependency on agents, rations, boarding schools, and federal policy.

The episode ends before Wounded Knee itself, with the press, local agents, party politics, and military mobilization converging on Sitting Bull as the supposed instigator of a crisis.

## 分段落总结

[01:30] **A Poem Points Toward Wounded Knee**

[事实] The episode opens with a 1927 poem celebrating American place names and ending with “Bury my heart at Wounded Knee.”
[事实] The hosts explain that this phrase later became associated with a major history of Native Americans and with Wounded Knee as a tragic endpoint.
[事实] They say Wounded Knee will be the climax of their wider story, but this episode mainly sets up the Ghost Dance and the events leading toward it.

[04:00] **Sitting Bull Returns to a Changed World**

[事实] After Custer’s death at Little Bighorn in 1876, the Lakota dispersed and were pursued by U.S. Army officers including Crook and Miles.
[事实] Crazy Horse was killed, while Sitting Bull fled to Canada, returned, was held at Fort Randall, and arrived at Standing Rock in May 1883.
[事实] Sitting Bull hoped he might be left alone, but found that “Indian country” had been transformed while he was away.

[05:13] **Railroads, Buffalo Hunters, and the End of the Plains World**

[事实] Between 1878 and 1887, about 2,000 miles of railroad track were built across South Dakota, bringing settlers, supplies, and buffalo hunters.
[事实] The hosts say that in 1876 there were still about two million buffalo in Wyoming and Montana, but six years later a rancher reported seeing dead buffalo everywhere and no live ones.
[事实] Cattle, cowboys, barbed wire, farmers, and Washington legislation followed the destruction of the buffalo herds.
[推测] The discussion presents environmental destruction, settlement, and political dispossession as one connected process rather than separate developments.

[08:31] **Reservations as Social Engineering**

[事实] Sitting Bull found about 17,000 Lakota confined in what is now South Dakota, divided around agencies such as Pine Ridge, Rosebud, Standing Rock, and Cheyenne River.
[事实] Each agency distributed rations and included institutions such as schools.
[事实] The hosts describe the Great Sioux Reservation as a “sealed laboratory for social engineering.”
[事实] Reformers and officials sought to Christianize Native people, break down tribal structures, suppress ceremonies such as the Sun Dance, and send children to boarding schools.

[11:34] **McLaughlin, Gall, and the New Agency Order**

[事实] At Standing Rock, Sitting Bull came under the authority of Major James McLaughlin, a former blacksmith whose wife spoke Lakota and served as an interpreter.
[事实] McLaughlin is described as principled and religious, but also ruthless in dividing and ruling among Native people.
[事实] He recruited Gall, one of Sitting Bull’s former lieutenants, into the agency order.
[事实] Former warriors could gain new status by joining agency police known as the “metal breasts” because of their badges.

[15:36] **Sitting Bull’s Disorientation and Humiliation**

[事实] McLaughlin dismissed Sitting Bull as mediocre, vain, boastful, and inferior to other Lakota leaders.
[事实] Sitting Bull felt deeply diminished and is quoted as saying he had once been a man but was now a “pitiful wretch.”
[事实] The hosts compare his condition to other Indigenous societies shattered by European intrusion, disease, violence, and industrial modernity.
[推测] Sitting Bull’s suffering is presented as spiritual as well as political: the reservation confined both his body and the worldview that had sustained him.

[18:16] **Sitting Bull as Spectacle**

[事实] McLaughlin arranged for Sitting Bull to appear in St. Paul, Minnesota, through a deal involving the Northern Pacific Railroad.
[事实] Sitting Bull was shown schools, the fire department, the post office, and a telephone, and thousands came to see him.
[事实] In 1885, agents connected him with Buffalo Bill Cody’s Wild West show, where he appeared before crowds that treated him as a villain.
[事实] Gall refused to participate in this kind of performance, while Sitting Bull did so and later tried to settle into farming.

[25:17] **The Ghost Dance Appears as a Promise of Salvation**

[事实] Sitting Bull heard rumors of a new movement beyond the mountains that seemed to offer salvation for Native people.
[事实] The hosts identify the Ghost Dance as a cult, religion, or movement that promised a change in the narrative for the Lakota.
[推测] Its appeal is tied to the despair created by confinement, hunger, and the collapse of older Lakota life.

[25:37] **Statehood Politics and the Breaking of the Great Sioux Reservation**

[事实] Benjamin Harrison became president in March 1889 after winning the Electoral College but losing the popular vote.
[事实] Republicans wanted western states, including North and South Dakota, admitted quickly because they expected them to strengthen Republican power.
[事实] South Dakota’s viability as a state was complicated by the Great Sioux Reservation, so federal policy moved toward breaking it into smaller reservations.
[事实] In 1889 Congress approved the breakup, General Crook negotiated with Lakota chiefs, and the Lakota lost half their land.

[29:04] **Hunger, Disease, and Black Elk’s Winter**

[事实] After the reservation breakup, Congress cut rations despite promises that this would not happen.
[事实] During the winter of 1889-1890, hunger, measles, whooping cough, and influenza swept through the reservations.
[事实] Black Elk described people as heavy, dark, hungry, and surrounded by lies they could not eat.
[推测] The episode treats material deprivation as the immediate soil in which the Ghost Dance took root.

[29:51] **Wovoka and the Messiah Rumor**

[事实] Lakota emissaries including Kicking Bear and Short Bull traveled west and returned in March 1890 saying a Messiah had appeared.
[事实] The figure was Wovoka, also known to white acquaintances as Jack Wilson, a Paiute man in his thirties who spoke English, worked for a rancher, and had absorbed Christian ideas.
[事实] The hosts say he reportedly had a vision during a solar eclipse in 1889 and brought news that Jesus was coming to help Native people.
[事实] The accounts are described as garbled, and the hosts say Lakota emissaries may have distorted Wovoka’s message.

[32:03] **The Dance, the Dead, and Religious Fusion**

[事实] Wovoka’s message, as described by the hosts, emphasized peace, love, not taking up arms, and above all dancing.
[事实] The Ghost Dance involved communal dancing, visions, contact with dead family and friends, and white shirts decorated with moons and stars.
[事实] The hosts say the bulletproof nature of the shirts may have been a later addition or a distorted detail.
[事实] They describe the Ghost Dance as a fusion of Christianity, Native spirituality, and probably some Mormon influence.

[34:07] **Communion With a Lost World**

[事实] The hosts compare the Ghost Dance’s longing to commune with the dead to later spiritualism after the First World War and visions such as Fatima.
[事实] They say the dead represented not only lost relatives but also an older world that had disappeared.
[事实] The promised return of the buffalo is singled out as especially moving.
[推测] The Ghost Dance is interpreted as a religious language for grief after catastrophe.

[35:20] **Drought and the Militant Turn**

[事实] In the summer of 1890, drought and burning winds destroyed crops and hay on South Dakota reservations.
[事实] The hosts cite the view that if people had been well fed, the Ghost Dance would likely not have taken root in the same way.
[事实] By that summer, the reservation version of the Ghost Dance had become more militant, with stronger claims about bulletproof shirts and the removal of white settlers.
[事实] As autumn and winter came, dances became more fervent, with collapses, visions, screaming, and speaking in tongues.

[37:22] **Pine Ridge Panic and Daniel Royer**

[事实] The Pine Ridge agent in 1890 was Daniel F. Royer, appointed through Republican patronage.
[事实] Royer is described as inexperienced, politically connected, and deeply unsuited to the crisis.
[事实] He panicked when Lakota dancers laughed at him, demanded troops, and told the press that an uprising was imminent.
[推测] Royer’s fear turned a religious movement into a security crisis.

[39:41] **Harrison, Miles, and Army Mobilization**

[事实] Senior military figures initially viewed the Ghost Dance as a passing craze rather than a major threat.
[事实] President Harrison wanted to appear strong and to impress South Dakota voters at a politically important moment.
[事实] General Nelson A. Miles was sympathetic to Native grievances but also saw the crisis as a way to show the army’s importance.
[事实] Miles mobilized about 5,000 men, a quarter of the U.S. Army’s fighting strength and the largest mobilization since the Civil War.

[42:12] **The Press Chooses Sitting Bull as Villain**

[事实] Although Miles handled the situation carefully and many Lakota were persuaded to return to reservations, the arrival of troops created a press frenzy.
[事实] Reporters found little fighting to report and instead amplified stories of imminent massacre.
[事实] Sitting Bull was blamed as the famous Native figure available to cast as a supervillain and was called the “high priest” of the Messiah craze.
[事实] Miles considered using Buffalo Bill to persuade Sitting Bull, but McLaughlin insisted on handling the matter himself, with tragic consequences left for the next episode.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its layered explanation of how Wounded Knee became possible: not through one sudden decision, but through hunger, media panic, patronage politics, military incentives, and the destruction of Lakota autonomy.

The strongest material is the connection between Sitting Bull’s personal decline and the larger transformation of the Plains. The hosts make the Ghost Dance understandable as a movement of grief, hope, and desperation rather than simply a bizarre religious episode.

[推测] A limitation is that this episode stops just before the decisive events around Sitting Bull and Wounded Knee, so listeners wanting the full climax need the next installment. It is best suited to listeners interested in Native American history, the U.S. West, religious movements, and the politics behind military escalation.
