# Stephen Fry on Troy and the Enduring Power of Greek Myth

## 概览

This episode of *The Rest Is History* welcomes Stephen Fry as the podcast’s first guest to discuss his new book on the Trojan War, the third in his sequence retelling Greek myths. The conversation begins with why Troy felt both exciting and daunting to Fry: it brings the Greek mythic cycle toward closure, from the birth of the gods to humans returning home from war.

Fry, Tom Holland, and Dominic Sandbrook explore how Greek mythology moves from elemental divine figures to rounded, contradictory characters. The Trojan War becomes a meeting point between myth and history, with Helen, Achilles, Hector, Odysseus, Athena, and the gods used to examine ambiguity, violence, grief, cunning, and human motive.

A central theme is why these stories still reach modern readers, especially children, even without the older culture of classical education. Fry suggests Greek myth occupies a special space between reality and symbolism, while the hosts argue that its strength lies in endless retelling and reinterpretation.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Introducing Stephen Fry and the Return to Greek Myth**

[事实] The hosts introduce the episode as a broader return to Greek mythology after a previous episode on the Trojan War.
[事实] Stephen Fry is presented as the podcast’s first guest, described as a comedian, actor, novelist, polymath, and lover of ancient Greece.
[事实] Fry joins from California and has a new book out on the Trojan War.

[01:43] **Troy as the End Point of Greek Myth**

[事实] Fry says he looked forward to writing about the Trojan War but also felt dread because it leads toward the end of the Greek mythic story.
[事实] He describes Greek myth as seeming to begin with the birth of the gods and end with the homecomings after Troy, especially those of Odysseus, Agamemnon, Helen, and Menelaus.
[推测] Fry treats Troy not only as a war story but as a symbolic transition from a world governed by divine intervention to one centered more on human affairs.

[03:44] **The Iliad’s Violence and Nobility**

[事实] Fry says he did not expect to enjoy writing the intense hand-to-hand fighting of the *Iliad*.
[事实] On rereading Homer, he found the battle scenes beautiful, poetic, strong, and unexpectedly noble.
[事实] He names Ajax, Hector, Achilles, and Diomedes as warrior figures who still speak across thousands of years.
[推测] The discussion frames Homeric violence as morally difficult but artistically and emotionally powerful.

[05:45] **Between Myth and History**

[事实] Dominic asks whether the Trojan War required a different voice because it stands closer to history than Fry’s earlier books on gods and heroes.
[事实] Fry says the Trojan War begins to “leak into historicity,” especially after Schliemann’s nineteenth-century excavations.
[事实] Fry also notes that Schliemann was seen as a showman and fraud, and that his excavation methods damaged Troy.
[推测] The episode presents Troy as a liminal subject: neither simple fantasy nor straightforward history.

[07:03] **Greek Myth and Increasing Character Depth**

[事实] Fry compares the development of Greek myth to generations of computer graphics, moving from blocky simplicity to richer resolution.
[事实] He says early gods such as Uranus represent elemental or abstract forces, while Titans and Olympians gain more personality and complexity.
[事实] By the Trojan stories, Fry says the drama is driven by flaws, contradictions, and conflicting motives in both gods and humans.
[推测] This metaphor helps explain why later Greek myth can feel psychologically modern.

[10:23] **Helen and the Ambiguity of Blame**

[事实] Fry identifies Helen of Sparta, later Helen of Troy, as the apparent cause of the war.
[事实] He says the sources do not settle whether Helen went willingly, was abducted, was influenced by Aphrodite, or was initially glamorized by Paris.
[事实] Fry argues that Homer sketches Helen’s ambiguity lightly but powerfully.
[推测] Helen becomes an example of how Greek myth resists a single moral explanation.

[12:16] **Blending Sources and Choosing a Perspective**

[事实] Dominic asks how Fry blends multiple ancient sources into unitary characters.
[事实] Fry says it is impossible not to take sides, and that many classically educated readers naturally side with the Greeks.
[事实] He says he increasingly admires Priam, Hector, and Andromache while finding Odysseus twisted and Agamemnon pompous.
[事实] Fry often uses Odysseus as a kind of point of consciousness, giving ironic distance from the events.
[推测] Fry’s method is less scholarly synthesis than narrative judgment shaped by character and perspective.

[17:07] **Ancient Codes and Modern Readers**

[事实] Dominic raises the challenge of retelling a story from a violent honor culture to modern readers who often find violence repellent.
[事实] Fry compares this to modern viewers struggling to understand emotional codes in older British films.
[事实] He argues that ancient practices around bodies, burial, guest-friendship, and honor should not be reduced to simple functional explanations.
[推测] Fry warns against judging ancient cultures too quickly through modern assumptions.

[21:36] **Small Human Moments in Homer**

[事实] Fry highlights Achilles’ grief when he hears of Patroclus’s death and the scene of Hector, Andromache, and Astyanax as moments of emotional contact.
[事实] He says these small moments make Homer’s larger world of violence and martial conquest feel real.
[事实] Fry compares this storytelling principle to advice from screenwriter William Goldman about using brief reminders of death beneath comedy.
[推测] The episode suggests Homer’s emotional force depends on restraint rather than constant sentiment.

[26:17] **Children and the Appeal of Violent Myths**

[事实] Tom recalls Robin Lane Fox weeping while reciting Priam begging Achilles for Hector’s body.
[事实] The hosts note that Greek myths include death, slaughter, rape, and horror, yet often serve as children’s entry point into antiquity.
[事实] Fry says many communications he receives about his myth books come from children or their parents.
[推测] The paradox is that children may be drawn not despite the intensity of myth, but because myth gives extreme experience a symbolic shape.

[28:04] **Myth, Fantasy, and Double Determination**

[事实] Fry says Greek mythology differs from modern fantasy even though both can contain gods, monsters, and superhuman figures.
[事实] He describes “double determination”: an event can be read as both human action and divine intervention, such as Paris shooting Achilles with Apollo guiding the arrow.
[事实] He also uses Athena calming Achilles as an example of a god who may be read literally or symbolically.
[推测] Greek myth’s power partly comes from allowing sacred and psychological explanations to coexist.

[32:16] **Putting the Gods Back into History**

[事实] Tom says he is writing a sequel to Troy: a history of ancient Greece in which the gods continue to play roles.
[事实] He gives Herodotus’s story of Philippides meeting Pan before Marathon, after which the Persians experience panic.
[事实] Tom describes this as “rewilding history,” because the gods make aspects of the ancient worldview intelligible.
[推测] The hosts treat belief in gods as historically important even when modern readers do not share that belief.

[34:12] **Modern Mythic Characters**

[事实] Dominic asks whether figures such as Sherlock Holmes have become mythic in a way comparable to Homeric characters.
[事实] Fry says Holmes is an unusually recognizable example, identifiable through a magnifying glass, deerstalker, and pipe.
[事实] Fry compares Holmes and Jeeves as figures people might desire: wise companions who can advise or solve things without being tyrants.
[推测] The discussion broadens myth from antiquity to any character that gains symbolic life beyond its original author.

[37:00] **Odysseus, Trickery, and Human Victory**

[事实] Fry argues that the Trojan War is not won by heroic fighters such as Ajax, Diomedes, Hector, Penthesilea, or Memnon.
[事实] He says it is won by deceit, cunning, trickery, lying, and storytelling, embodied by Odysseus.
[事实] Fry links Odysseus to Hermes, god of storytellers and liars.
[推测] The episode presents trickery as one of the most distinctively human forces in the Trojan story.

[40:03] **Greek Self-Understanding and Human Complexity**

[事实] Fry says the Greeks seem to have been unusual in believing in progress, adaptation, and change.
[事实] He contrasts Greek development with what he describes as the long stability of Egyptian civilization.
[事实] He says Greek plays and philosophy examined human motive, ambiguity, contradiction, and competing ways of life.
[推测] Fry sees Greek myth and later Greek culture as deeply connected by a fascination with what humans are.

[43:36] **Athena, Athens, and Civilized Intelligence**

[事实] Tom calls Athena the greatest deity of all time and links her to Odysseus and Athens.
[事实] He describes Athena as both goddess of the battle line and goddess of the loom and arts of civilization.
[事实] Fry recounts a version of Athens’ origin in which Athenians choose Athena’s olive over Poseidon’s gifts.
[推测] Athena functions in the discussion as a symbol of intelligence that joins war, craft, politics, and civilization.

[46:03] **The Hearth and the Origins of Myth**

[事实] Fry says he did not set out to write children’s books, but he remembered falling in love with myth as a child.
[事实] He imagined early humans gathered around fire, using language to explain wind, fruit, seasons, and other forces.
[事实] He describes gods as agencies given to forces whose causes people did not know.
[事实] He says such stories bound families, clans, tribes, and communities together.
[推测] Fry’s account presents myth as both explanation and social glue.

[49:56] **Hesiod, Homer, and Official Versions**

[事实] Fry says Hesiod and Homer gave lasting shape to Greek myths around roughly the same period.
[事实] Hesiod’s *Theogony* gave clear accounts of the birth and genealogy of the gods.
[事实] Homer gave stories an authoritative shape that later writers and philosophers could refer back to.
[事实] Fry compares this process to later official versions of Arthurian legend and Robin Hood.
[推测] The conversation suggests that myths become canonical when oral traditions are fixed by influential literary tellers.

[52:03] **Retelling Without Forcing Modernity**

[事实] Fry says he feels free to retell Greek myths because they are folk stories and public-domain stories in the deepest sense.
[事实] He also feels humbled because the stories have been shaped by great writers before him.
[事实] He mentions Thomas Bulfinch and Nathaniel Hawthorne as major American transmitters of Greek myth for children.
[事实] Fry says he avoids forced modern slang, such as making mythological figures sound like contemporary “dudes.”
[推测] Fry’s preferred style tries to be accessible without making the myths artificially fashionable.

[54:29] **Children, Libraries, and the Appeal of the Old**

[事实] Fry argues that many children are not simply absorbed by Instagram, Snapchat, or TikTok.
[事实] He says some children love discovering old, musty things in libraries.
[事实] Fry imagines that if he were fifteen now, he might enjoy going off-grid, using real telephones, or producing an old-fashioned magazine.
[推测] The point supports his wider claim that old stories can attract young readers without being aggressively updated.

[55:44] **Closing the Interview**

[事实] The hosts thank Fry for discussing ancient stories from California while they speak from England.
[事实] Fry praises both hosts’ work and says he has listened to Dominic’s books as audiobooks.
[事实] The conversation ends with thanks and good wishes for Fry’s book.
[推测] The ending reinforces the episode’s theme that ancient stories continue through modern media and conversation.

[事实] After Fry leaves, Tom says the conversation was especially useful because he is working on a sequel to the Trojan War in which the gods are real.
[事实] Dominic asks whether people might one day lose interest in Greek myth.
[事实] Tom argues that the success of Fry’s books shows readers do not need deep classical education to become absorbed by the stories.
[事实] Tom cites Dan Simmons’s science-fiction novel *Ilium* as a favorite reinterpretation of the Trojan War.
[推测] The hosts conclude that Greek myth is likely to endure because it keeps inviting new forms, genres, and historical settings.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s strength is its combination of literary enthusiasm and historical caution. Fry is especially effective when explaining why the Trojan War feels both ancient and familiar: its violence belongs to a distant honor culture, but its grief, ambiguity, and human contradiction still feel immediate.

The discussion is valuable for listeners interested in Greek myth, Homer, retelling classical material, or the relationship between myth and history. It also gives a useful view into Fry’s method: he does not present himself as a historian, but as a storyteller trying to preserve ambiguity while making old material alive.

A limitation is that the episode moves quickly across many large subjects, including Homer, Hesiod, Schliemann, Athena, Holmes, children’s reading, and the nature of fantasy. [推测] Listeners seeking detailed historical argument about the archaeology of Troy or close textual analysis of ancient sources may find the treatment suggestive rather than exhaustive.
