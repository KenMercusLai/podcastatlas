# Helen, Clytemnestra, and the Search for Mycenae

## 概览

This episode examines Heinrich Schliemann’s attempt to turn the Homeric world into archaeological fact, moving from his famous claim to have found Troy to his later excavations at Mycenae. The hosts use Schliemann’s ambitions and self-mythologizing as a way into the larger question of whether figures such as Helen, Agamemnon, and Clytemnestra preserve memories of real Bronze Age people and societies.

A major part of the discussion retells the mythic background of Agamemnon’s cursed family, especially the stories dramatized by Aeschylus in the *Oresteia*. Clytemnestra emerges as the episode’s central figure: wronged by Agamemnon’s sacrifice of Iphigenia, murderous in her revenge, and symbolically important in Greek tragedy’s movement from blood feud to civic justice.

The second half weighs Schliemann’s discoveries against later archaeological scholarship. While Schliemann’s identification of the “face of Agamemnon” is rejected, the hosts argue that modern evidence from Mycenae, Linear B tablets, Hittite records, and Bronze Age material culture makes the Homeric world look less purely fictional than some twentieth-century scholars assumed.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **Schliemann and the Dream of Finding Troy**

[事实] The episode opens with a poem about Heinrich Schliemann and frames him as the archaeologist famously associated with “discovering” Troy.

[事实] The hosts say Schliemann’s fame depends on the idea that stories of the Trojan War, Helen, and perhaps even the wooden horse might have some historical basis.

[事实] Schliemann is described as the son of a Lutheran pastor, gifted with languages, and later a very wealthy businessman.

[推测] The episode presents Schliemann as both a genuine archaeological pioneer and a self-mythologizing figure whose discoveries were shaped by literary obsession.

[02:37] **Sophia, Helen, and Schliemann’s Personal Mythology**

[事实] Schliemann later claimed he had loved the story of Troy since childhood, but the hosts say this was probably not true.

[事实] In 1869, after divorcing his Russian wife, Schliemann sought a Greek wife who could embody his idea of Helen and married a 16-year-old Greek girl named Sophia.

[事实] Schliemann asked Sophia to recite Homer before the marriage went ahead.

[推测] Sophia’s role in Schliemann’s story shows how he blurred the boundary between archaeological discovery, literary fantasy, and personal performance.

[03:55] **The Excavation of Hisarlik and “Priam’s Treasure”**

[事实] In 1870 Schliemann began excavating Hisarlik in northwestern Turkey, the traditional site of Troy.

[事实] In 1872 he announced that he had found a burned city, which he identified with the Troy destroyed by the Greeks.

[事实] In May 1873 he found a hoard of gold and silver, including jewelry he called the jewels of Helen and the treasure of Priam.

[事实] Schliemann claimed Sophia had carried the jewels away in her skirts, but the hosts say she was actually in Athens at the time.

[04:58] **From Helen to Clytemnestra and Mycenae**

[事实] After claiming to have found Troy and Helen’s jewels, Schliemann turned his attention to Mycenae, the city associated with Agamemnon and Clytemnestra.

[事实] Clytemnestra is introduced as Helen’s sister and Agamemnon’s wife.

[事实] Schliemann began digging at Mycenae illegally in 1874, but after paying the Greek Archaeological Society he received permission to continue.

[推测] The hosts suggest Schliemann’s excavation at Mycenae was driven by the same desire to prove mythic figures real that had motivated his work at Troy.

[07:15] **Clytemnestra in Aeschylus and Greek Tragedy**

[事实] Clytemnestra is described as a powerful figure in Greek mythology and especially in Aeschylus’s trilogy, the *Oresteia*.

[事实] Aeschylus was a veteran of the Persian Wars and won major dramatic competitions in Athens.

[事实] His play *Agamemnon*, first performed in 458 BC as part of the trilogy, gives a bloody account of Clytemnestra’s relationship with Agamemnon.

[推测] The episode treats Clytemnestra not as a minor companion to Helen’s story, but as one of the most forceful female figures in Greek literature.

[09:02] **The Curse of Agamemnon’s Family**

[事实] Agamemnon’s family line is traced back to Tantalus, who served his son Pelops to the gods and was punished in the underworld.

[事实] Pelops later won a bride through cheating in a chariot race, and his line was cursed again.

[事实] Pelops’s sons Atreus and Thyestes continued the family violence, including murder, exile, cannibalism, rape, and revenge.

[事实] Agamemnon and Menelaus inherited this history of monstrous behavior before reclaiming Mycenae.

[推测] The family history functions as an explanation for why Agamemnon’s reign is powerful but morally doomed.

[14:02] **Iphigenia and Clytemnestra’s Motive for Revenge**

[事实] Agamemnon became king of Mycenae and was described by Homer as “lord of men.”

[事实] When the Greek fleet gathered at Aulis to sail for Troy, Artemis prevented the winds from changing after Agamemnon killed a deer in her sacred grove.

[事实] Agamemnon was told that he had to sacrifice his daughter Iphigenia to Artemis for the fleet to sail.

[事实] He deceived Clytemnestra by claiming Iphigenia would marry Achilles, then had Iphigenia sacrificed.

[推测] The episode presents Clytemnestra’s later murder of Agamemnon as revenge rooted in a specific betrayal rather than simple villainy.

[16:18] **Agamemnon’s Return and Murder**

[事实] While Agamemnon was at Troy, Clytemnestra took Agisthus as her lover and ally.

[事实] When Troy fell, watchfires announced Agamemnon’s return to Mycenae.

[事实] Agamemnon returned with Cassandra, Priam’s daughter, whom he had made his concubine.

[事实] Clytemnestra persuaded Agamemnon to enter the palace over purple robes, then killed him and Cassandra.

[推测] Agamemnon’s return with Cassandra intensifies the episode’s portrayal of him as a man who has invited vengeance through repeated violations.

[19:39] **Orestes, the Furies, and the Birth of Trial by Jury**

[事实] In the second play of the trilogy, Orestes is instructed by Apollo to avenge Agamemnon by killing Clytemnestra and Agisthus.

[事实] Because killing one’s mother is a terrible crime against the natural order, Orestes is pursued by the Furies.

[事实] In the third play, Athena creates a courtroom trial in Athens to judge Orestes.

[事实] The jury is split, Athena casts the deciding vote, and Orestes is acquitted.

[推测] Aeschylus uses the myth to dramatize a transition from inherited blood vengeance to civic legal order.

[23:26] **Aeschylus, Athens, and the Political Use of Myth**

[事实] The hosts argue that the *Oresteia* makes Athens and its democratic institutions look ancient and authoritative.

[事实] Aeschylus described his plays as slices from the great feast of Homer.

[事实] The episode asks whether Homer himself may also have adapted older stories and preserved traces of a real Bronze Age past.

[推测] The discussion positions Greek myth as a flexible cultural memory that later writers used to explain political and social change.

[27:25] **Schliemann at Mycenae and the “Face of Agamemnon”**

[事实] In 1876 Schliemann sent a telegram saying that a corpse resembled his imagined image of wide-ruling Agamemnon.

[事实] This was later condensed into the famous phrase that he had gazed on the face of Agamemnon.

[事实] At Mycenae, Schliemann excavated shaft graves near the Lion Gate and found bodies, treasure, and gold masks.

[事实] The famous gold mask is now commonly associated with Agamemnon, though the hosts state there is no evidence that it was his.

[推测] Schliemann’s discoveries were spectacular partly because he immediately translated finds into Homeric narrative.

[30:14] **Why the Mask Was Not Agamemnon’s**

[事实] The hosts say the remains and mask Schliemann found were older than he believed.

[事实] Schliemann thought the burial dated to around 1250 BC, the supposed period of the Trojan War, but it was actually about 400 years older.

[事实] Later archaeology professionalized after Schliemann and became more wary of identifying finds with legendary individuals.

[推测] Schliemann’s error illustrates the danger of beginning with a desired mythic answer and fitting evidence around it.

[31:34] **Twentieth-Century Reactions Against Heroic Archaeology**

[事实] The hosts say postwar archaeologists became embarrassed by attempts to find figures from legend.

[事实] They compare this discomfort to archaeology in Palestine around biblical sites and to questions about King Arthur.

[事实] The hosts say Nazi admiration for golden-haired Greeks and warrior rulers made this kind of heroic archaeology politically suspect after the Second World War.

[事实] The decipherment of Linear B revealed records of bureaucracy, farmers, potters, and weavers rather than Homeric heroic narratives.

[推测] The episode suggests that academic caution was shaped not only by evidence, but also by twentieth-century political and cultural anxieties.

[33:14] **The Pendulum Swings Back Toward Historicity**

[事实] The hosts argue that scholarship has moved away from the idea that Mycenaean society was mainly peaceful bureaucracy and trade.

[事实] They point to massive Cyclopean walls as evidence that Mycenaean cities were concerned with violence and defense.

[事实] Hittite texts from Anatolia describe a world of great-power politics that may fit with a conflict involving Troy.

[事实] The name Akiwawa in Hittite records may correspond to the Achaeans, Homer’s name for the Greeks.

[推测] The hosts do not claim Schliemann was simply right, but they argue that Homer’s world may rest on stronger historical foundations than once assumed.

[35:43] **Homeric Kings and Bronze Age Evidence**

[事实] Archaeology has shown that Homer’s portrayal of Greek kingdoms maps in some ways onto Bronze Age evidence.

[事实] The tablets show that kingdoms were ruled by a figure called a *wanax*, close to Homer’s term *anax*.

[事实] Agamemnon’s Homeric title as lord of men may therefore preserve a genuine Bronze Age term or memory.

[推测] The episode uses language as one of the strongest links between epic poetry and Bronze Age political structures.

[36:23] **Helen, Clytemnestra, and Bronze Age Queens**

[事实] Bettany Hughes’s book on Helen of Troy is cited as an accessible study of how Helen may preserve memories of Bronze Age queens.

[事实] The hosts say archaeological evidence shows women were present in Mycenaean citadels and were not segregated from men in the way later Greek women often were.

[事实] At Mycenae, evidence of weaving and arrowheads in the same room suggests a mixing of male and female spheres.

[事实] Female bodies were found in the shaft graves excavated by Schliemann, though the women had gold diadems rather than masks.

[推测] Helen and Clytemnestra may reflect memories of elite women whose status was more visible and politically meaningful in the Bronze Age than in classical Athens.

[39:03] **Women, Weaving, and Diplomacy**

[事实] Hittite tablets provide evidence that the abduction or transfer of high-ranking women could be politically significant.

[事实] The hosts say elite women could be used as diplomatic tools or bargaining pieces between kingdoms.

[事实] Homeric women such as Clytemnestra, Helen, and Penelope are associated with weaving and textiles.

[事实] Bronze Age tablets also show evidence for women overseeing groups of servant weavers.

[推测] The textile imagery in Greek epic may preserve a real association between elite women, palace production, and household authority.

[40:36] **Helen’s Beauty and Bronze Age Ideals**

[事实] Helen is described in Homer as golden-haired or possibly red-haired.

[事实] Bettany Hughes points to frescoes from Thera showing aristocratic women, including one red-haired figure close to a deity and wearing a necklace.

[事实] The hosts say red or golden hair may have implied semi-divine status in this visual culture.

[事实] White lead oxide found archaeologically may have been used as cosmetics, fitting traditions of Helen’s white or dazzling appearance.

[推测] The episode suggests Helen’s beauty may be rooted in Bronze Age ideals of elite, almost supernatural female display rather than being a purely later fantasy.

[42:26] **What Can Be Said About a Historical Helen**

[事实] The hosts do not claim that a specific person named Helen definitely existed.

[事实] They argue that the image of a golden-haired, powerful princess surrounded by servants and involved in international politics may fit a recognizable Bronze Age type.

[事实] Homer preserves fragments of language and authentic elements from the Bronze Age world.

[推测] Helen may be less a recoverable individual than a poetic condensation of memories about Bronze Age queenship, beauty, diplomacy, and danger.

[44:18] **Modern Readings of Helen and Clytemnestra**

[事实] The hosts discuss the modern trend of rewriting Greek myths from female perspectives.

[事实] They note that modern readers may focus on objectification, sexuality, agency, and sexual violence in these stories.

[事实] Clytemnestra is described as having clear agency because she kills Agamemnon herself.

[事实] Helen is also described as having agency if her story is read as choosing Paris rather than being only a rape victim.

[推测] The episode resists reducing Helen and Clytemnestra either to victims or villains, instead emphasizing their disturbing power within Greek myth.

[45:17] **Female Power and Athenian Anxiety**

[事实] Clytemnestra dominates Aeschylus’s tragedy and is described as the most powerful figure in it.

[事实] The hosts say women in fifth-century BC Athens had no political power but did have religious roles as mediators between the city and the gods.

[事实] Athena’s role in ending the cycle of bloodshed is important because she is female but also embodies civic order.

[事实] The hosts close by invoking Socrates’ idea that beauty is more powerful than strength.

[推测] Clytemnestra and Helen both expose Athenian anxieties about female power, sexuality, domestic authority, and the stability of the civic order.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode is valuable because it links myth, literature, archaeology, and modern historiography without flattening any of them. Schliemann is neither treated simply as a fraud nor as a heroic discoverer; instead, he becomes a way to explore why people want Homeric stories to be true.

The strongest sections are the retelling of Clytemnestra’s role in the *Oresteia* and the later discussion of Bronze Age evidence for elite women. The episode makes clear that even if Helen and Clytemnestra cannot be proven as historical individuals, the worlds around them may preserve real memories of power, diplomacy, violence, and palace life.

Its limitation is that the discussion moves quickly through a large amount of mythological genealogy and archaeological debate, so listeners unfamiliar with Greek myth may find the family relationships hard to track. [推测] The episode is best suited to listeners interested in ancient history, Homer, Greek tragedy, archaeology, or the long afterlife of myth in Western culture.
