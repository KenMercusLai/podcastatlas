# Politics Is for Power: Moving Beyond Political Hobbyism

## Overview

This episode of *Hidden Brain* explores political scientist Eytan Hirsch’s argument that many Americans engage with politics as a hobby. They closely follow national news, scandals, personalities, and partisan conflict, yet rarely take actions designed to influence elections, public policy, or other people.

Hirsch distinguishes political hobbyism from genuine political participation by asking whether emotion is an end in itself or a means to action. Real political work involves acquiring and exercising power: organizing communities, addressing practical needs, persuading opponents, turning out voters, and consistently participating even when candidates or issues are not exciting.

Examples involving Massachusetts elections, a Ukrainian immigrant community leader, political donations, media coverage, and deep canvassing illustrate how spectacle can displace effective participation. The episode concludes that ordinary citizens can exert meaningful influence by shifting their attention from national political entertainment toward sustained local organization.

## Segment-by-segment summary

[00:00] **From staying informed to consuming political entertainment**

[Fact] Hirsch’s father read newspapers and listened to the radio but also watched cable news at night, explaining that staying informed was a civic duty.

[Fact] Hirsch concluded that his father was already informed and watched additional political coverage largely because he enjoyed politics.

[Fact] The episode introduces the central paradox that constant exposure to political news can coexist with increasingly shallow political engagement.

[04:20] **Defining political hobbyism**

[Fact] Hirsch defines political hobbyism as engagement that satisfies emotional or intellectual needs without attempting to move voters, shape policy, or acquire power.

[Fact] Political hobbyists collect facts, discuss political gossip, share memes, and follow campaigns much as sports fans follow teams and athletes.

[Fact] Under Hirsch’s definition, political participation requires trying to influence electoral politics or public policy in a preferred direction.

[07:52] **Emotion as an end versus a tool for action**

[Fact] Serious organizers approach political opponents as people who might be persuaded rather than merely hated or dismissed.

[Fact] Partisan hobbyists often remain among like-minded people and never take the second step of identifying shared concerns that could move someone else.

[Fact] Hirsch says political anger becomes useful only when it is converted into action; when emotional identification is the final goal, it remains hobbyism.

[11:24] **Massachusetts elections reveal the gap between excitement and power**

[Fact] Massachusetts saw high participation in the 2008 presidential election even though Barack Obama’s victory in the state was never seriously in doubt.

[Fact] Turnout—especially in heavily Democratic areas—fell sharply during the later special Senate election between Martha Coakley and Scott Brown, even though control of a crucial Senate seat was at stake.

[Fact] Scott Brown won after many Democratic voters failed to participate when their party’s candidate and the election itself generated less excitement.

[Fact] At Tufts, only a few students agreed to travel to New Hampshire to canvass, while far more wanted to make the same trip for an opportunity to meet Pete Buttigieg and take a photograph.

[15:29] **Voting claims, declining local attention, and demographic differences**

[Fact] Hirsch reports that in some surveys, nearly half of confirmed nonvoters claimed that they had voted; college-educated news followers were especially likely to make this false claim.

[Fact] Interest and turnout in state and local politics have declined alongside shrinking local-news resources, while national politics receives increasing attention.

[Fact] Hirsch says people may profess concern about issues such as climate change or racial equality while ignoring state and municipal institutions capable of acting on those issues.

[Fact] Women predominate in many community-organizing and party groups, while men—especially college-educated white men—report greater political-news consumption and factual knowledge.

[Fact] African American and Latino Democrats were described as spending less time consuming political information but more time participating in community organizations than college-educated white liberals.

[20:45] **The “Ukrainian boss” and the practical construction of power**

[Fact] A community leader named Nock helped older immigrants study for citizenship tests after welfare reform threatened benefits relied upon by legal immigrants.

[Fact] Nock and his wife helped approximately 300 neighbors obtain citizenship and built trust through years of community service.

[Fact] Nock later organized voter turnout by distributing marked sample ballots; his precinct eventually recorded turnout two or three times higher than neighboring precincts.

[Fact] Politicians began courting him because he could mobilize roughly a thousand residents around issues including immigration, transportation, and snow removal.

[Fact] Hirsch argues that even advocates of large causes such as climate policy must first build relationships by respecting people and responding to their immediate needs.

[26:15] **Post-material issues and symbolic participation**

[Fact] An analysis of an Obama administration petition platform found that many petitions concerned jokes or relatively narrow matters rather than employment, education, or basic economic welfare.

[Fact] Hirsch connects this pattern to a broader shift from “bread-and-butter” concerns toward post-material issues.

[Speculation] The discussion suggests that low-cost symbolic participation can draw attention away from less glamorous problems that have more direct effects on people’s daily lives.

[28:02] **Political donations as access, entertainment, and outrage**

[Fact] In a survey experiment, nearly as many respondents were willing to pay $1,000 to dine with a politician when the money went to an event company as when it supported a political campaign.

[Fact] Hirsch interprets this as evidence that some large donations purchase self-expression, status, or proximity to a political celebrity rather than political influence.

[Fact] After Representative Joe Wilson shouted “You lie” during Barack Obama’s address to Congress, Wilson received about $2 million in online donations.

[Fact] Hirsch argues that small-dollar fundraising can reward provocative conduct and viral confrontation rather than constructive policymaking.

[30:51] **How media and politicians reinforce hobbyism**

[Fact] Hirsch uses a detailed television report about Paul Manafort’s prison and inmate number to illustrate information that feels substantive without helping viewers participate as citizens.

[Fact] He says media outlets encourage a sports-like model of politics organized around daily winners, losers, gossip, and outrage.

[Fact] Politicians respond to the same incentives by producing confrontational moments that generate attention and donations.

[Fact] A rally targeting Representative Ilhan Omar is presented as an example of partisan chanting resembling sports fandom, while the resulting online conflict consumed attention on both sides.

[Fact] Hirsch summarizes the pattern as rejecting “boring” local news, voting when politics becomes spectacle, and donating when a viral moment supplies emotional gratification.

[38:19] **Service can build power for constructive or destructive movements**

[Fact] In 2018, the Ku Klux Klan distributed material offering help to people with opioid addictions, which Hirsch interprets as an attempt to recruit through service rather than an immediate ideological appeal.

[Fact] The episode notes that organizations including Hamas and ISIS have also gained support by supplying social, educational, or welfare services alongside radical political goals.

[Fact] Earlier political parties similarly built relationships by providing food, vaccinations, and other forms of practical assistance.

[Speculation] These examples imply that community service is politically powerful but morally neutral as a method; its value depends on the movement and goals it ultimately empowers.

[41:14] **Political parties as providers of practical assistance**

[Fact] Hirsch proposes that parties offer services such as emergency childcare, eldercare, or addiction assistance to demonstrate concern for constituents before achieving their larger policy objectives.

[Fact] He contrasts tangible help with repeatedly sending advertisements or promotional claims about how much a party cares.

[Fact] Parties generally cannot exchange assistance for votes, but they may undertake legally permitted party-building activities, subject to differing state rules.

[Fact] Events such as breakfasts and carnivals already fall within the historical community-building role of political parties.

[43:49] **Deep canvassing through vulnerability and personal connection**

[Fact] Activist Dave Fleischer tells voters an intimate story about being gay and having a girlfriend in high school to explain the personal experiences informing his support for abortion rights.

[Fact] His method uses vulnerability to invite reciprocal openness, create mutual understanding, and potentially persuade people whose views are uncertain or mixed.

[Fact] Early research on deep canvassing was retracted after a graduate student fabricated data.

[Fact] Researchers who uncovered the fabrication subsequently conducted rigorous studies and found similar evidence that deep canvassing can change opinions.

[Fact] Hirsch describes deep canvassing as time-consuming, difficult to teach, and awkward to perform, but highly effective for the difficult task of persuasion.

[47:32] **Replacing national spectatorship with local action**

[Fact] Hirsch argues that citizens are not needed to monitor presidential tweets or publish immediate reactions on social media.

[Fact] Communities do need people to attend meetings, organize neighbors, influence local institutions, and help move policy.

[Fact] Even a global problem such as climate change has important local dimensions, including state and regional transportation systems.

[Fact] The episode’s closing recommendation is to talk with neighbors, build trusted communities, organize consistently, and mobilize people around shared goals.

## Podcast commentary/summary

The episode’s greatest strength is its clear distinction between caring intensely about politics and exercising political power. Its election, organizing, fundraising, and media examples make the concept of political hobbyism concrete and give listeners practical criteria for evaluating their own behavior.

The discussion is also valuable because it offers an alternative rather than merely criticizing news consumption: serve immediate needs, build trust, participate locally, persuade people outside one’s circle, and remain involved when politics is not exciting. The section on deep canvassing is especially useful because it acknowledges both the technique’s research scandal and the later evidence supporting it.

[Speculation] One limitation is that the episode emphasizes individual behavior and community organization more than structural barriers to participation, including limited time, money, access, and institutional responsiveness. Its argument may therefore be most directly useful to politically attentive listeners who have the capacity to redirect some of their news consumption toward sustained civic work.

[Speculation] This episode is particularly suited to heavy consumers of political news, campaign volunteers, party organizers, activists, and anyone frustrated that intense political attention has not produced visible change.
