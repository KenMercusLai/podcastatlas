# Smuggling: Myths, Violence, and the Golden Age

## 概览

This episode of *The Rest Is History* uses smuggling to separate romantic fiction from historical reality. The discussion begins with Rudyard Kipling’s “A Smuggler’s Song” and Alex Preston’s novel *Winchelsea*, then turns to the question of why British smuggling is so often imagined through Cornwall, lovable rogues, and adventure fiction.

The central argument is that the real heart of large-scale eighteenth-century smuggling was not Cornwall but the Kent and Sussex coast. Preston presents smuggling as a response to taxation, war finance, geography, poverty, and weak state enforcement, but also stresses that the most powerful gangs were not Robin Hood figures: they were wealthy, organized, violent criminal networks.

The episode moves from medieval wool smuggling and the Cinque Ports to excise, tea, brandy, tobacco, the Hawkehurst gang, Jacobite support, state repression, and local resistance. It closes by connecting historical smuggling to modern people smuggling, refugee routes, organized crime, and the persistent tendency to romanticize criminals.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Kipling, Fiction, and the Smuggling Myth**

[事实] The episode opens with a reading from Rudyard Kipling’s “A Smuggler’s Song,” which frames smuggling through secrecy, danger, and folk memory.

[事实] The hosts introduce the subject as smuggling in its prehistory, golden age, and modern forms.

[事实] Alex Preston joins the episode because his novel *Winchelsea* is about smuggling and has just been released.

[推测] The opening deliberately sets up the tension between romantic literary smuggling and the darker historical reality the episode wants to uncover.

[03:04] **Cornwall Versus Kent and Sussex**

[事实] Preston says most people associate smuggling with Cornwall, but argues that this is wrong for the major organized gangs of the eighteenth century.

[事实] He names *Moonfleet*, *Jamaica Inn*, *Poldark*, and Dr. Syn as works that shaped the popular image of smugglers.

[事实] He argues that fiction has filtered and distorted how people imagine smugglers, often turning them into lovable rogues.

[推测] The episode treats Cornwall as important to the myth of smuggling, while Kent and Sussex are presented as more central to its large-scale criminal history.

[05:12] **Smuggling as Local Resistance**

[事实] Preston links the height of smuggling to the beginning of the industrial age and the rise of more centralized government.

[事实] He says local communities felt increasingly managed from London and saw smugglers as people resisting distant authority.

[事实] Adam Smith is quoted describing the smuggler as blameworthy under national law but not necessarily in violation of “natural justice.”

[推测] The discussion suggests that sympathy for smugglers came partly from resentment of taxes and central power, not simply from admiration for crime.

[07:00] **Organized Gangs and the Southeast Coast**

[事实] Preston says Cornwall was a minor part of organized smuggling compared with the southeast coast.

[事实] He names the Hawkehurst gang, the Mayfield gang, and the Sea Salter Company as large organized criminal operations.

[事实] He says the head of the Sea Salter Company left wealth equivalent to about £50 million, and Arthur Gray of the Hawkehurst gang built an enormous house called Seacocks Heath.

[事实] Preston compares these smuggling networks to modern organized criminal gangs involved in people smuggling.

[08:30] **The Cinque Ports and a Semi-Independent Coast**

[事实] Preston emphasizes the importance of the Cinque Ports, a medieval cooperative of seafaring towns on the south coast.

[事实] The towns named include Hastings, New Romney, Hythe, Dover, Sandwich, Rye, and Winchelsea.

[事实] These towns had exemptions and local rights that allowed them to manage trade with limited royal interference.

[推测] Preston presents this tradition of local autonomy as a cultural background that later made smuggling easier to justify and harder to suppress.

[10:49] **Medieval Wool Smuggling and “Owling”**

[事实] Preston says smuggling was initially an export business based on wool rather than imported luxuries.

[事实] British wool was highly valued, and Edward I taxed wool exports in 1275, later increasing the charge.

[事实] By 1300, England was exporting about 30,000 sacks of wool a year, creating incentives to evade taxes.

[事实] Wool smuggling was known as “owling,” and smugglers were called “owlers.”

[13:15] **Bans, Customs, and Better Boats**

[事实] Edward III encouraged skilled weavers to come to England and then banned the export of wool entirely.

[事实] Preston says the ban left years of unsold fleeces rotting on docks while smuggling continued.

[事实] By the 1400s, early forms of customs enforcement began to appear.

[事实] Around 1475, fore-and-aft rigging made boats more maneuverable, helping smugglers use beaches, cliffs, and landing points more effectively.

[16:00] **Why Britain Became Fertile Ground for Smuggling**

[事实] Preston says Britain’s island geography made customs taxation attractive to the state.

[事实] He argues that Britain was economically weak in earlier centuries and became a major global player only later.

[事实] At the start of the eighteenth century, about 80% of the population lived outside towns and cities, and around 20-25% claimed parish relief.

[推测] Poverty, geography, taxation, and weak enforcement combined to make smuggling a practical option for coastal communities.

[18:05] **Wrecking and the Invention of Criminal Images**

[事实] The speakers discuss the myth of wreckers luring ships onto rocks with false lights.

[事实] Preston says Catherine J. Pierce’s work on Cornish wrecking shows there is almost no evidence that this actually happened.

[事实] He distinguishes between deliberately wrecking ships and looting ships after they had already wrecked.

[推测] The episode uses wrecking to show how public images of coastal crime could be constructed for moral, legal, or commercial reasons.

[23:03] **From Wool Exports to Tea, Brandy, and Excise**

[事实] After the break, Preston says that by 1600 about 120,000 sacks of wool were being smuggled annually.

[事实] In 1566, owling could be punished by cutting off the left hand and displaying it publicly.

[事实] Excise was introduced in the 1650s on goods including chocolate, coffee, tea, beer, cider, spirits, salt, leather, and soap.

[事实] Once excise and customs made imports more profitable to evade, smugglers carried wool out and returned with goods such as brandy, gin, lace, tobacco, and especially tea.

[26:11] **Weak Enforcement and Corruption**

[事实] Charles II kept and strengthened customs after the Restoration rather than abolishing excise.

[事实] In 1690 there were only eight riding officers in Kent, and the job was poorly paid and dangerous.

[事实] Riding officers came from the same communities they policed, where friends and family might be involved in smuggling.

[事实] Preston says bribery was common and enforcement was limited, with a small land guard and water guard covering the coast.

[29:06] **Continental Supply and Inland Networks**

[事实] Smuggled goods came through ports including Calais, Boulogne, Dieppe, Dunkirk, Ostend, Roscoff, Le Havre, and Saint-Malo.

[事实] Preston says continental suppliers were usually merchants rather than criminal gangs.

[事实] The Mayfield gang was the first major organized criminal smuggling gang mentioned as managing the process from origin to final sale.

[事实] Inland places such as Mayfield and Hawkhurst mattered because they were staging posts on the route from the coast to London.

[31:45] **The Golden Age and the Tax State**

[事实] The speakers identify rising demand for tea, tobacco, spirits, lace, and other goods as part of the growth of smuggling.

[事实] Preston says the tax on tea reached 129% at one point.

[事实] He argues that government pressure on smugglers helped organize them and made them more brutal.

[推测] The “golden age” label is presented as morally troubling because it can romanticize a violent criminal economy.

[33:31] **The Hawkehurst Gang’s Violence**

[事实] Preston compares the romanticization of smugglers to the later glamorization of criminal gangs such as the Krays.

[事实] He says the Hawkehurst gang were “total monsters,” not lovable rogues.

[事实] He describes a local story behind Dumb Woman’s Lane, where a woman who informed on the gang had her tongue cut out.

[事实] The gang ran protection rackets, coerced farmers into lending horses, burned crops, and terrorized local people.

[36:08] **Jacobitism and Smuggling**

[事实] Preston says Jacobite support helped smugglers reach their level of power.

[事实] He cites Paul Monod’s paper “Dangerous Merchandise” and says some landowning Jacobite families gave smugglers money, hiding places, and storage.

[事实] The Carol family in Sussex is mentioned as a powerful family connected to hiding or disposing of bodies.

[推测] This makes some smuggling look less like casual lawbreaking and more like a mix of organized crime, political sympathy, and local insurgency.

[37:23] **Poole Custom House and Retaliatory Murder**

[事实] In September 1747, customs officers seized a Hawkehurst shipment of tea and brandy intended for Christchurch Harbour and stored it in Poole Custom House.

[事实] The Hawkehurst gang attacked the customs house and took back the tea.

[事实] Daniel Chater identified Diamond, a gang member, and was then targeted along with customs official William Galley.

[事实] Galley and Chater were tortured; Galley was buried alive and Chater was thrown into a well.

[40:31] **State Response and the Gazetting Act**

[事实] The 1736 Act of Indemnity allowed smugglers to avoid punishment if they informed authorities about a planned run.

[事实] Preston notes that this was dangerous because there was no witness protection.

[事实] The 1746 Gazetting Act listed suspected smugglers in the *London Gazette* and gave them 40 days to surrender.

[事实] If suspects did not surrender, a bounty of £500 could be placed on them, and capital penalties applied to several smuggling-related acts.

[43:17] **The Battle of Goudhurst**

[事实] Preston dates the Battle of Goudhurst to 21 April 1747.

[事实] William Sturt, an ex-soldier, organized the Goudhurst Militia to resist the Hawkehurst gang.

[事实] The gang threatened the village, but the militia refused to disband, sent vulnerable residents away, armed themselves, and fortified the church.

[事实] About 25 militia members faced around 400 gang members; the militia held the high ground and did not lose a life.

[事实] Dragoons arrived, the gang scattered, Kingsmill and Gray were captured and executed, and Preston presents this as the end of the Hawkehurst gang’s dominance.

[48:52] **Women, Later Battles, and Smuggling Kings**

[事实] Preston says some women were involved in smuggling, often as landladies of inns, but cautions against overstating their role.

[事实] Anne Bonny, Mary Read, and Bessie Catchpole are mentioned in relation to women and smuggling or privateering.

[事实] The Battle of Mudeford in 1784 is discussed as a later violent smuggling clash near Christchurch.

[事实] John Carter, known as the King of Prussia, is discussed as a Cornish smuggler, but Preston says his operation was smaller than southeastern smuggling networks.

[50:34] **Napoleonic War, Spies, and Information**

[事实] The speakers discuss “guinea runs,” in which smugglers moved gold from Britain to France during the Napoleonic Wars.

[事实] Preston says smugglers could be bought and were involved in moving spies back and forth.

[事实] The episode notes the long-standing links between the southeast coast and France, including raids and French ownership of parts of Rye through the Abbey of Fécamp.

[推测] Smuggling during wartime blurred the line between economic crime, espionage, and national security.

[54:23] **Literary Nostalgia and Romantic Branding**

[事实] The speakers return to how smugglers acquired posthumous brands through fiction and legend.

[事实] Preston says *Moonfleet*, published in 1899, looks back nostalgically to a supposed golden age.

[事实] He praises *Moonfleet* as a beautiful novel despite challenging the romantic image of smugglers.

[推测] The episode argues that literary charm can preserve the emotional appeal of smuggling even when the historical facts are brutal.

[55:39] **Modern People Smuggling**

[事实] Preston says modern smuggling around Sussex and Kent is often people smuggling, and he connects this to present-day coastal life.

[事实] He describes volunteering with Kent Kindness, helping unaccompanied minors with English, bank accounts, and basic orientation.

[事实] He identifies routes through Libya and Italy, Turkey and Greece, and sometimes Belarus, Bulgaria, and Romania.

[事实] He says many recent arrivals he sees are from Kurdistan, and that some people pay up to £25,000 to smuggling gangs.

[59:47] **Dinghies, Organized Crime, and Exploitation**

[事实] Preston says migrants cross in inflatable dinghies because they are difficult to detect on radar.

[事实] He describes the people-smuggling trade as monstrous and says the gangs treat people as means rather than ends.

[事实] He discusses Refugee Tales, a project pairing writers with refugees to tell their stories.

[事实] He recounts the story of a refugee who was misled by a gang in London into paying money to go to France, only to be sent back and arrested.

[62:27] **Violence on the Modern Coast**

[事实] The speakers compare the shocking violence of eighteenth-century Kent and Sussex with modern tensions along the same coast.

[事实] Preston says a group of locals in Hastings recently physically stopped lifeboats from going out to rescue a refugee boat.

[事实] He describes parts of the Kent coast as darker and more violent than postcard images suggest.

[推测] The episode implies that coastal smuggling has changed its cargo but not entirely lost its connection to fear, exploitation, and local tension.

[62:47] **Goods Smuggling Today and the Glamour of Criminals**

[事实] The speakers ask whether goods smuggling by sea still exists, including drugs, weapons, and guns.

[事实] Preston says goods smuggling has largely moved to lorries, especially for drugs.

[事实] The speakers argue that modern culture still glamorizes criminals through shows and stories about figures such as Pablo Escobar, *The Wire*, and *Peaky Blinders*.

[事实] Preston says his own novel is also part of that problem, while emphasizing that history should try to get at the truth.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its myth-busting. It takes a familiar adventure-story subject and reframes it as a history of taxation, state-building, geography, poverty, organized violence, and public memory.

[推测] Its strongest moments are the concrete stories: Dumb Woman’s Lane, the Poole Custom House raid, the murders of Galley and Chater, and the Battle of Goudhurst. These details make the argument against romantic smuggling vivid rather than abstract.

[推测] A limitation is that the discussion is conversational and sometimes jumps between centuries, places, and jokes, so listeners looking for a tightly chronological survey may need to reconstruct the timeline themselves.

[推测] The episode is especially suitable for listeners interested in British coastal history, historical myth-making, crime, taxation, and the relationship between past and present forms of smuggling.
