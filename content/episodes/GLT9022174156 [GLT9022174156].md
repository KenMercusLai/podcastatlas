# The Day of the Dead: Catholic Roots, Indigenous Imagery, and Invented Tradition

## 概览

This episode of *The Rest Is History* uses Mexico’s World Cup connection as a route into the history of Día de Muertos. The hosts begin with the familiar global image of the festival from *Spectre* and *Coco*, then challenge the assumption that today’s Day of the Dead is simply an ancient Aztec survival.

The central argument is that many well-known elements of the festival have deeper roots in medieval and early modern Catholic practice, especially All Saints’ Day and All Souls’ Day, rather than in pre-conquest ritual. Sugar skulls, pan de muerto, cemetery visits, prayers for the dead and the timing around 1-2 November are treated as parts of a Catholic and Spanish-Iberian inheritance that later took on Mexican forms.

The episode then shifts to the twentieth century, arguing that post-revolutionary anti-clerical politics and state promotion of Indigenous identity reshaped Día de Muertos into a national symbol. It ends with the irony that the spectacular Mexico City parade seen in *Spectre* was fictional when filmed, but became a real annual parade after the film’s release.

## 分段落总结

[01:00] **Mexico, the World Cup, and the Day of the Dead**

[事实] The hosts introduce the episode as part of a World Cup-themed series and locate it in Mexico, mentioning Mexico City’s Estadio Azteca as one of only two stadiums to have hosted the World Cup final twice.

[事实] The autumn timing of the World Cup leads them to Día de Muertos, which they describe as the first thing many non-Mexicans associate with Mexico in autumn.

[事实] They bring in the opening sequence of the James Bond film *Spectre* and Pixar’s *Coco* as modern popular images of the Day of the Dead.

[推测] The opening frames the episode as a test of whether the international image of Día de Muertos is historical reality, tourist fantasy, or something in between.

[04:18] **The Popular Myth of Ancient Origins**

[事实] Dominic says that online descriptions often present the Day of the Dead as an ancient tradition, with TripAdvisor calling it 4,000 years old and *The Guardian* saying it dates back to the Aztec period.

[事实] Tom immediately raises the alternative possibility that it may be a Christian festival, anticipating the main argument of the episode.

[事实] Dominic says the grand parade shown in *Spectre* did not exist in Mexico City when the film was made, calling it a classic invented tradition created by filmmakers.

[推测] The hosts use the *Spectre* example to show how a powerful image can feel authentic even when its specific public form is very recent.

[05:26] **Names, Dates, Altars, Bread, Skulls, and Flowers**

[事实] The festival is called Día de Muertos, though Dominic notes that grammatically Día de los Muertos would be expected.

[事实] The usual dates are 1 and 2 November, though Dominic says the dates can be contested and may fall a day or two earlier.

[事实] The hosts list familiar practices: home altars called ofrendas, favourite foods and drinks for dead loved ones, cemetery visits, sugar skulls, pan de muerto, mock epitaph poems, and orange marigolds.

[事实] The marigolds are identified as cempasúchil, linked to a Nahuatl word meaning “twenty flowers,” and the Florentine Codex is cited as evidence that the Mexica used such flowers in religious rituals.

[08:20] **Pan de Muerto and Sacrificial-Origin Stories**

[事实] Pan de muerto is described as a sweet bread or pan dulce, often shaped like a bun and decorated with bone-like pastry.

[事实] Dominic says many websites claim the bread derives from Aztec human sacrifice, including stories of a maiden’s heart being replaced by bread after the Spanish conquest.

[事实] Tom connects this to a wider internet habit of claiming that Christian festivals are really pagan festivals in disguise.

[事实] Dominic notes that official Mexican government and Indigenous-institution sources also present the festival as pre-Hispanic or Indigenous in origin.

[推测] This section sets up a conflict between national or popular origin stories and the hosts’ preferred evidence-based reconstruction.

[11:05] **Sugar Skulls and Food History**

[事实] Dominic says Martha Stewart’s website claims sugar skulls can be traced back more than 3,000 years, while also noting that Aztecs did not exist 3,000 years ago.

[事实] He says other origin claims shift the tradition from Aztec to Maya or Toltec, pushing the supposed antiquity further back.

[事实] Dominic argues that sugar-skull origin stories fail because people were not making sweets out of sugar before the Spanish conquest.

[事实] He says most food historians place sugar skulls in eighteenth-century New Spain, connected to Spanish expertise in molded sugar, bread and marzipan.

[15:28] **Medieval Catholic and Iberian Roots**

[事实] Dominic quotes Elsa Malvido of the Institute of Anthropology in Mexico City as saying that treating the Day of the Dead as pre-Hispanic means not understanding it, because it is deeply Roman.

[事实] The hosts discuss medieval European practices around All Saints’ Day and All Souls’ Day, including special bread called pan de ánimas, or bread of souls.

[事实] Tom explains All Saints’ Day and All Souls’ Day in relation to saints, purgatory and prayers for the dead, and names St Odilo of Cluny as popularizing All Souls’ Day in the tenth century.

[事实] Dominic cites Stanley Brandes’s book *Skulls to the Living, Bread to the Dead*, saying that All Souls’ Day was a major Iberian festival in the 1500s and that a Catalan reference to Dia dels Morts appears in 1671.

[推测] The episode’s argument is not that Mexico simply copied Spain unchanged, but that the basic ritual calendar and many core practices came through Catholic Iberian tradition.

[19:34] **From Colonial Catholicism to a Nineteenth-Century Ritual**

[事实] After the break, Dominic says Spanish Catholic traditions, calendars and rituals arrived in Mexico in the sixteenth century and were inevitably colored by Indigenous traditions.

[事实] He says nineteenth-century Mexican newspapers mention Day of the Dead processions to graves and drinking parties.

[事实] He emphasizes that in the nineteenth century the festival was treated as one Catholic ritual among many, not as a uniquely Mexican expression of national character.

[推测] The modern idea of Día de Muertos as a defining symbol of Mexican identity appears, in the hosts’ account, to be later than the ritual itself.

[23:18] **Revolution, Anti-Clericalism, and Indigenous Nationalism**

[事实] Dominic identifies the Mexican Revolution of the 1910s as the turning point in the festival’s modern meaning.

[事实] He says post-revolutionary regimes, especially generals from Sonora, were often aggressively secular and anti-clerical.

[事实] These regimes cultivated Indigenous pride and rejected Hispanic or European cultural models.

[事实] Diego Rivera and Frida Kahlo are presented as central figures in the outside world’s image of Mexican art from the 1920s to 1940s, with murals celebrating Indigenous scenes and rejecting European influence.

[推测] The reshaping of Día de Muertos fits into a broader political effort to build a Mexican identity that looked Indigenous, modern and anti-European.

[25:18] **Calles, the Cristero War, and the Weakening of the Church**

[事实] Plutarco Elías Calles is described as a revolutionary general, late-1920s president, and founder of the Institutional Revolutionary Party, which held power in Mexico for 71 years.

[事实] Dominic says Calles stripped the church of power over education and barred priests from politics.

[事实] The Cristero War is described as an uprising by Christians in rural Mexico using the slogan “Viva Cristo Rey,” and Dominic says 100,000 people were killed.

[事实] The number of priests is said to have fallen from about 5,000 in the 1920s to 334 by 1934, with 17 Mexican states having no priest by 1935.

[事实] Dominic quotes Donald Mabry to the effect that by 1940 the Mexican church had no corporate existence, property, schools, monasteries, convents, foreign priests, or public legal right to defend itself.

[29:03] **Replacing Santa Claus with Quetzalcoatl**

[事实] Dominic says the post-revolutionary state promoted a “cult of the Indigenous” in place of Catholic influence.

[事实] Santa Claus was treated as a symbol of Yankee imperialism, and officials tried to replace him with Quetzalcoatl, the feathered serpent.

[事实] In December 1930, a pyramid was built inside Mexico City’s National Stadium, where children gathered and a man dressed as Quetzalcoatl distributed presents.

[事实] The attempt did not take off because people remained loyal to Santa Claus.

[事实] Dominic also mentions John Taylor’s Mormon claim that Quetzalcoatl and Jesus were the same being, and D. H. Lawrence’s *The Plumed Serpent*, which imagines Catholicism in Mexico being replaced by Quetzalcoatl worship.

[33:09] **The 1930s Reinvention of Día de Muertos**

[事实] Dominic says that under Lázaro Cárdenas, the state strongly promoted the Day of the Dead.

[事实] Elsa Malvido is cited as saying that home altars and other elements often imagined as ancient survivals were basically invented in the 1930s.

[事实] Tom links this to the same period in which theories about Christian festivals being disguised pagan festivals became popular.

[事实] The hosts say the 1920s and 1930s were an age of powerful states staging public rituals that blended ancient and modern elements.

[推测] The festival’s non-Catholic-looking features, especially skulls and Indigenous imagery, were amplified because they suited an anti-clerical political climate.

[35:16] **La Catrina, Posada, and Political Skeletons**

[事实] The skeleton costume in *Spectre* is traced not to ancient ritual but to nineteenth-century imagery.

[事实] Dominic connects it to Diego Rivera’s mural *Dream of a Sunday Afternoon in Alameda Central Park*, which satirizes pre-revolutionary society.

[事实] Rivera’s image drew on José Guadalupe Posada, a nineteenth-century cartoonist critical of Porfirio Díaz’s regime, who filled his work with skulls and skeletons.

[事实] Posada’s famous La Calavera Garbancera, later known as La Catrina, is described as an elegant upper-class woman in a French-style hat with a skull face.

[推测] The skeleton imagery carries anti-elite and anti-French political satire as much as religious or Indigenous symbolism.

[38:02] **A Film Parade Becomes a Real Tradition**

[事实] Dominic concludes that the *Spectre* procession did not exist in reality and that its authentic elements are medieval Christian rather than Indigenous.

[事实] He says the current flavor of Día de Muertos comes from late nineteenth- and early twentieth-century anti-clericalism.

[事实] After *Spectre* was released, the Mexican government launched the first Día de Muertos parade in central Mexico City the following year.

[事实] That first parade was attended by a quarter of a million people, and Mexico City has held Day of the Dead parades ever since.

[推测] This is the episode’s strongest example of invented tradition becoming real through cinema, tourism and state performance.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it dismantles a simple origin story without flattening the festival into a single cause. It shows Día de Muertos as a layered tradition shaped by Catholic theology, Spanish practice, Indigenous imagery, Mexican nationalism, anti-clerical politics, popular art and modern film.

[推测] Its strongest moments are the concrete reversals: sugar skulls depending on post-conquest sugar, pan de muerto looking more Iberian than Aztec, La Catrina coming from political cartooning, and the *Spectre* parade becoming real only after the movie. These examples make the broader point about invented traditions easy to grasp.

[推测] The limitation is that the conversation relies on a fast, comic narrative and mostly follows the hosts’ selected sources, so listeners wanting a Mexican scholarly or devotional perspective may want more voices from within the tradition itself. It is best suited to listeners interested in history, religion, nationalism, and how modern identities are made from older materials.
