# The Companies Changing Warfare Forever: Palantir & Anduril Execs on Drones, AI & the Future of War

## 概览

This episode centers on whether the United States can rebuild deterrence by reconnecting Silicon Valley, defense technology, and the broader industrial base. Trey Stephens and Sankar argue that war is bad, but credible strength and rapid production capacity are necessary to make war less likely.

The discussion moves from Palantir’s early post-9/11 mission to Anduril’s attempt to build a new kind of defense prime: product-led, software-defined, and supported by large-scale manufacturing. A recurring theme is that stockpiles alone do not deter adversaries; factories, supply chains, and replenishment capacity do.

The episode also examines procurement bureaucracy, venture capital in defense, autonomous weapons, AI ethics, surveillance concerns, and cultural distrust of defense work. The guests frame the stakes as both military and economic: if the U.S. fails to reindustrialize, they argue, it risks losing rule-setting power to China.

## 分段落总结

[00:00] **Palantir Origins and the Anduril Connection**

[事实] Sankar says Trey Stephens first encountered Palantir while working at an intelligence agency and later joined the company in early 2008.

[事实] Palantir was still small when Stephens joined, with roughly 25 to 30 people, and he helped build the government business.

[事实] Sankar says Palantir’s original post-9/11 idea was to improve both privacy and security rather than treat them as opposites.

[事实] Palantir initially focused narrowly on counterterrorism problems for a small number of institutions.

[03:13] **War, Deterrence, and Silicon Valley’s Shift**

[事实] Stephens says war is categorically bad, but not always avoidable.

[事实] He argues the goal of defense technology is deterrence: making adversaries believe challenging the U.S. is not worth it.

[事实] Sankar says Silicon Valley’s anti-defense posture partly came from a long peaceful period after the Cold War.

[事实] He says Russia’s invasion of Ukraine changed how many people in Silicon Valley viewed defense work.

[06:44] **From American Industrial Base to Defense Industrial Base**

[事实] Stephens argues the U.S. once had an American industrial base, not just a defense industrial base, citing Chrysler, General Mills, and Ford as historical examples.

[事实] He says major weapons spending shifted from mostly dual-purpose companies in 1989 to mostly pure-play defense specialists today.

[事实] The host cites Anduril’s reported $60 billion valuation, a $20 billion Army contract, and Palantir’s $400 billion valuation.

[推测] The guests frame today’s “Silicon Valley takeover of defense” narrative as a reversal of the older model where much of American industry supported national defense.

[08:54] **Readiness Gaps and the Factory Thesis**

[事实] The host cites a 10,000-to-one drone production gap versus China and a 223x shipbuilding capacity disadvantage.

[事实] Sankar says the U.S. joint force is still the best in the world, but adversaries are changing faster.

[事实] He argues deterrence is eroding, pointing to Crimea, the Spratly Islands, Iran, Israel, the Houthis, and Ukraine.

[事实] He says Ukraine consuming ten years of production in ten weeks should have shown that factories, not stockpiles, are the real basis of deterrence.

[10:39] **Reindustrialization and Arsenal One**

[事实] Stephens says globalization sent many manufacturing capabilities away and gutted American communities.

[事实] He describes Anduril building a five-million-square-foot factory campus in Columbus, Ohio.

[事实] Arsenal One is intended to support modular production rather than lock the factory into one product line.

[事实] Stephens contrasts this with the early Ukraine war, when the U.S. had to restart production knowledge for Stingers and Javelins.

[14:49] **Product-Led Defense and the Monopsony Problem**

[事实] Stephens says Anduril invests private R&D capital first and then sells the resulting products.

[事实] He says this differs from traditional primes, which mostly respond to government requirements.

[事实] Sankar describes defense as a monopsony, where one buyer has enormous power over what gets built.

[事实] He argues defense innovation often starts as “heresy” and is validated only when it works in exercises or combat.

[19:35] **Palantir’s Long Road and Anduril’s Opening**

[事实] Sankar says Palantir was validated from the field backward because people on deployment wanted tools that worked.

[事实] He notes Palantir had to sue its customer for the right to compete.

[事实] Stephens says Anduril reached $10 million in annual revenue in 22 months, helped by lessons learned from Palantir’s much longer struggle.

[推测] Anduril benefited from Palantir proving that nontraditional companies could eventually work inside government channels.

[23:03] **Hardware, Software, and Founding Anduril**

[事实] Stephens says he initially believed hardware would be less hard than software in defense because the government understood bills of materials and margins.

[事实] Sankar says government buyers still often want to pay for software on a cost-plus basis, which he argues makes little sense.

[事实] Stephens says he searched for defense startups while at Founders Fund and found almost nothing worth investing in.

[事实] He says that gap led to the idea of a software-defined, hardware-enabled next-generation prime.

[25:47] **Venture Capital, Cost-Plus Economics, and Valuation Discipline**

[事实] Sankar uses Fairchild, integrated circuits, Nvidia, Shuttle, Starship, SpaceX, and Palantir to argue that price-performance improvement requires escaping cost-plus incentives.

[事实] Stephens says many new defense-tech startups are raising large amounts at high valuations.

[事实] He advises companies that they can raise less money at lower prices to reduce execution pressure.

[事实] Stephens says Anduril tries to reduce its revenue multiple with each funding round as discipline before a potential medium-term IPO.

[30:51] **Strategic Capital and Lost Industrial Markets**

[事实] Sankar supports government strategic capital aimed at bottlenecks such as drone components and brushless motors.

[事实] He argues the U.S. lost the consumer drone market after drones were treated as tightly controlled military technology and constrained by regulation.

[事实] Sankar says pharmaceuticals are another risk area and claims 80% of generic drug APIs are produced by China.

[事实] Stephens says semiconductors are a major example of a U.S.-born industry where production leadership moved elsewhere, especially to Taiwan.

[34:16] **Decision Advantage and Interoperability**

[事实] Sankar describes the “third offset” as decision advantage: out-thinking and out-executing adversaries.

[事实] He says hardware and software must coexist and be interoperable.

[事实] He warns that process-heavy frameworks can destroy innovation if they try to avoid all messiness.

[35:46] **Procurement Reform and Leadership**

[事实] Sankar says the current department is rethinking munitions and drones as consumables that should be exercised, expended, and replenished.

[事实] Stephens says the Office of Strategic Capital is examining critical minerals, refining, and private-sector deals.

[事实] Stephens argues these reforms are less about party politics than about whether the right leaders prioritize the problem.

[事实] Sankar says bureaucracy naturally tends toward sclerosis unless leaders actively clear it away.

[41:46] **Readiness and Munitions Capacity**

[事实] Sankar says the “tip of the spear” is sharp, but the supporting shaft needs work.

[事实] He argues the Pentagon struggles because demand from real-world combatant commands and supply from services and industry are not well integrated.

[事实] The host cites Sankar’s prior claim that the U.S. has eight days of munitions for a major China conflict versus 800 days needed.

[事实] Stephens says unlimited cash could put the country on track for a sustainable industrial base within 18 months, but expects the effort to be slower without political urgency.

[45:46] **Autonomy and Attritable Mass**

[事实] Stephens says today’s wars are fought with yesterday’s weapons.

[事实] He says high-end systems such as B-2s, Patriot missiles, and precision bombs are impressive but expensive and hard to resupply.

[事实] He argues the U.S. needs to build low-cost attritable mass systems now for future conflicts.

[47:25] **AI Ethics and Democratic Accountability**

[事实] The host raises Anthropic’s reported refusal to let Claude be used in Maven without human oversight constraints.

[事实] Stephens says fully autonomous weapons are not new and cites ship-based systems that autonomously shoot down aerial threats.

[事实] He argues accountability can exist inside autonomous systems when a responsible human remains accountable for the weapon’s operation.

[事实] Stephens says abstaining from national security technology is not morally neutral.

[50:41] **Palantir and Surveillance Claims**

[事实] Sankar says Palantir does not collect data and compares it to Excel for customers’ lawful data.

[事实] He argues Palantir has cell-level security and auditability that make illegal use easier to detect.

[事实] Sankar says technology vendors should support lawful use rather than impose narrow private policy judgments on elected governments.

[推测] His position assumes democratic institutions remain legitimate enough to decide how the tools are used.

[53:27] **Illegal Use and Inspector General Channels**

[事实] When asked whether they would report illegal government use of their technology, both guests answer yes.

[事实] They explain that every agency has an inspector general that can receive complaints and investigate independently.

[事实] Sankar says this mechanism has also been used against people he sees as defense innovators.

[推测] The guests present oversight channels as the appropriate remedy for abuse, rather than vendor refusal to serve lawful agencies.

[56:00] **Anti-Defense Culture and Foreign Influence**

[事实] Sankar traces part of the anti-defense culture to Vietnam and a lasting split between academia and defense.

[事实] Stephens says many elite students now have little or no family connection to the military.

[事实] Sankar claims adversaries have funded or amplified domestic protest movements, including Soviet funding during Vietnam-era anti-war activity.

[事实] The speakers argue that distrust fills the gap when people lack direct experience with military institutions.

[61:01] **Patriotism, Institutions, and Internal Discord**

[事实] Sankar says his book Mobilize is meant to help recast defense and industrial mobilization as patriotic work.

[事实] He suggests future observers may see World War III as having already begun.

[事实] He argues the U.S. must make institutions function better because institutional failure breeds nihilism.

[事实] The guests discuss socialism as a symptom of internal discord and failed elite responsibility.

[64:43] **Communications Strategy and 2040 Scenarios**

[事实] Stephens says he disagreed with Palantir’s historically quiet communications strategy.

[事实] He says Anduril deliberately chose to tell its story publicly and believes that helped the company.

[事实] Sankar says if the U.S. gets the next decade wrong, 2040 could look like a Chinese century where other countries become vassals.

[事实] He says if the U.S. gets it right, 2040 could bring reindustrialization, a stronger middle class, and renewed belief in institutions.

[68:11] **Primacy Versus Multipolarity**

[事实] Stephens argues that if the U.S. is not the leader, it does not get to set the terms of engagement.

[事实] He says the post-World War II U.S. role let America shape semiconductors, supply chains, and trade-lane protection.

[推测] The guests view multipolarity as unstable because they believe rival powers would rewrite rules around their own incentives.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s strongest value is its integrated view of defense technology as a manufacturing, procurement, capital allocation, and cultural problem rather than only a weapons problem.

[推测] Its main limitation is that the argument is highly sympathetic to Palantir, Anduril, and U.S. defense institutions; critics of surveillance, procurement capture, civilian harm, or foreign policy choices are discussed mostly through the guests’ framing.

[推测] The episode is best suited for listeners interested in defense tech, industrial policy, U.S.-China competition, autonomy, AI ethics, and why venture-backed companies are trying to become new defense primes.
