# The History of Culture Wars

## 概览

This episode examines whether “culture wars” are a modern phenomenon or a recurring pattern across history. Dominic Sandbrook argues for deep continuities, seeing arguments over identity, tradition, values, and national character in Rome, the Reformation, British party politics, and modern disputes.

Tom Holland narrows the definition: he argues that modern culture wars are best understood as post-Christian theological conflicts, where both sides draw on Christian moral assumptions but only one side may recognize that inheritance. The discussion repeatedly returns to the tension between progress and tradition, universalism and local belonging, and moral purification versus pluralistic compromise.

The episode moves from late antiquity and the Altar of Victory to the Reformation, Whigs and Tories, American civil rights, gay rights, universities, patriotism, football, communism, and the possibility of compromise. Its central conclusion is that culture wars endure because they are not merely policy disputes; they are emotional contests over moral legitimacy, identity, and the story societies tell about themselves.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Opening Frame: Culture Wars Across English History**

[事实] The hosts open by parodying a familiar culture-war complaint: that a small metropolitan minority is undermining national history and traditional culture.

[事实] Dominic says the same pattern can be imagined in England in the 1530s, 1640s, 1700s, 1900s, 1960s, and 2020s.

[事实] The episode sets out to ask what culture wars are, whether they are confected, and where they come from historically.

[01:24] **First Culture War: Ancient Conflict or Bismarck’s Kulturkampf**

[事实] Dominic argues that culture wars are as old as history, citing Greeks and Persians, Roman identity debates, and arguments about becoming too Greek or Persian.

[事实] Tom rejects a broad definition that treats any clash between cultures as a culture war, saying that would make every war a culture war.

[事实] Tom links the modern category more specifically to Bismarck’s Kulturkampf and to conflict over religious authority in a secularizing state.

[推测] The disagreement establishes the episode’s main tension: Dominic favors continuity across history, while Tom wants a tighter conceptual definition.

[03:27] **Culture War as Sublimated Theology**

[事实] Tom argues that culture war is “sublimated theology” generated from a specifically Christian context.

[事实] He says modern American culture wars revolve around Christian theological issues, even when one side no longer recognizes itself as Christian.

[事实] Dominic challenges this by pointing to late Roman debates over moral decline, luxury, effeminacy, and whether Rome was betraying its ancestors.

[推测] Tom’s definition makes Christianity not just one historical example, but the conceptual engine of modern culture-war language.

[05:04] **Rome, Progress, and the Christian Challenge**

[事实] Tom says a culture war requires an idea of progress: one side sees itself as progressive, while the other sees progress as loss.

[事实] He argues that Caesar and Cato’s conflict was more about political style than a modern-style culture war.

[事实] Tom identifies the clash between Christianity and traditional Roman ideals as a closer ancient parallel, because Christians attacked some Roman practices as demonic and in need of abolition.

[事实] Dominic suggests that late Roman pagans may have felt that Christians were overturning inherited values and taking their world away.

[08:04] **The Altar of Victory as Proto-Culture War**

[事实] Tom describes the Altar of Victory in the Roman Senate House as a symbol of Rome’s martial glory, tied to Nike, Augustus, Antony, and Cleopatra.

[事实] Christians opposed the altar because sacrifice to pagan gods was seen as demonic.

[事实] Tom recounts that Constantius II removed it in 357, Julian restored it, and it was removed again in 382.

[事实] Symmachus argued for toleration, while St Ambrose insisted there could be no compromise with the demonic.

[推测] The hosts use this as an early model for later disputes over statues, memory, and whether past symbols can simply be tolerated.

[10:24] **Pagan Literature, Trigger Warnings, and Christian Ambivalence**

[事实] Tom discusses Christian unease with pagan literature, including a 10th-century dream in which Virgil, Horace, and Ovid appear as snakes.

[事实] He contrasts this with people in Ravenna who preferred studying Virgil to the Gospels.

[事实] Tom says some Christians wanted to cancel literature, but presents this as a minority impulse surrounded by wider ambivalence.

[推测] His comparison between Christian anxieties over Ovid and modern university trigger warnings suggests that moral suspicion of inherited texts has a long Christian genealogy.

[12:34] **Images, Iconoclasm, and Statues**

[事实] Dominic connects modern disputes over statues and plaques to older Christian arguments about images in Byzantium and the Reformation.

[事实] Tom distinguishes these from modern culture wars because both sides in Byzantine and Reformation image debates understood themselves as arguing theology.

[事实] Tom says modern European public statues reflect a revived classical habit, especially from the 17th and 18th centuries onward.

[事实] He argues that current opposition to statues of slave traders or imperialists draws on Christian moral assumptions about slavery, conquest, and evil.

[16:47] **The Reformation and Modern Parallels**

[事实] Dominic says reading about the English Reformation during the Black Lives Matter protests made the historical parallels feel uncanny.

[事实] He describes Catholics shocked by Protestants with imported European ideas telling them that old beliefs were wrong and churches must be purged.

[事实] Tom agrees there is continuity but says Reformation conflicts were explicitly theological, while modern arguments over trans rights, statues, abortion, or gay rights are often framed as Christians versus progressives.

[事实] Tom argues that progressives also draw on Christian history and theology, even if they do not recognize it.

[20:36] **British Politics, Whigs, Tories, and Culture War**

[事实] Dominic argues that British politics has always had a culture-war dimension, citing Robert Tombs and the religious divide between Tories, the Church of England, dissent, and liberal-left traditions.

[事实] He points to the Sacheverell case and the 1710 general election as an example of a religious-cultural dispute becoming a major political issue.

[事实] Tom accepts that Whiggery becomes a precursor to modern culture war because it turns theological ideas of progress into a more secular political language.

[推测] The episode presents early party politics as a mechanism that transformed religious conflict into enduring ideological competition.

[23:51] **Temperament, Progress, and Conservatism**

[事实] Dominic asks whether people are temperamentally inclined either toward tradition and conservatism or toward progress and fear of being on the wrong side of history.

[事实] Tom agrees that temperament matters, but says Christianity radically changes the terms of debate by making change for its own sake morally meaningful.

[事实] Tom contrasts ancient societies, where even radical change was justified as restoration, with modern societies shaped by belief in an arc of progress.

[事实] The hosts break after framing culture war as both a temperamental divide and an ideological inheritance.

[26:30] **America and the Re-Exporting of British Culture Wars**

[事实] Dominic argues that English and Scottish religious-political arguments traveled to America with settlers and Puritans, survived there intensely, and were later re-exported to Britain.

[事实] Tom agrees and says Britain catches American culture-war conflicts easily because the arguments originally came from Britain.

[事实] Tom says American culture wars are especially intense because America remains more committedly Christian than Britain.

[推测] The discussion frames American culture wars less as foreign imports than as intensified descendants of British religious and political conflicts.

[28:30] **Civil Rights and the American Christian Civil War**

[事实] Tom argues that the civil rights movement was deeply Christian, especially through Martin Luther King Jr.’s use of biblical narratives such as Exodus.

[事实] He says civil rights created a template for other sidelined groups, including gay rights and feminism.

[事实] Tom argues that feminism and gay rights rubbed against traditional Christian interpretations in ways that racial justice did not to the same extent.

[事实] He describes modern American battle lines as Christians opposing progressives and progressives opposing Christians, while both draw from Christian thought.

[31:30] **Orthodox and Progressive Politics**

[事实] Dominic cites James Davison Hunter’s 1991 book on culture wars, describing a clash between the orthodox and the progressive.

[事实] He says the orthodox side need not be Christian, but may value tradition, the nuclear family, country, and the military.

[事实] The hosts discuss how culture-war issues are often dismissed by political obsessives as confected, even though many voters find flags, statues, and identity more emotionally gripping than technical policy.

[推测] The episode suggests that culture wars endure politically because they connect abstract values to symbols people can immediately recognize.

[34:43] **Britishness, Patriotism, and National Self-Criticism**

[事实] Dominic says British culture wars often revolve around Britishness and patriotism, citing George Orwell’s criticism of left-wing intellectual attitudes toward national symbols.

[事实] He argues that Britain has a distinctive tendency toward self-adulation about its own sins.

[事实] Tom links this to Christianity’s tension between universal identity and local or national belonging.

[事实] The hosts discuss Theresa May’s “citizens of the world” versus “citizens of nowhere” language as part of this local-versus-universal conflict.

[38:22] **Metropolitan Universalism and Local Belonging**

[事实] Dominic says many historians and literary figures see themselves as citizens of the world and treat local attachment as parochial or xenophobic.

[事实] Tom connects this pattern to Whig admiration for foreign, especially French, ideals and suspicion of Tory patriotism.

[事实] Dominic recalls encountering students at Oxford in the 1990s who refused to support England’s national football team on principle.

[推测] The football example makes the abstract debate over universalism and local loyalty feel concrete and socially recognizable.

[41:35] **Football, the Super League, and Everyday Conservatism**

[事实] The hosts discuss the European Super League debate as an example of sudden widespread defense of local tradition.

[事实] Tom says the backlash showed that many people believe football clubs should be rooted in local communities rather than treated as global assets.

[事实] Dominic notes that some American soccer followers liked the Super League because they saw globalized clubs as more progressive.

[推测] The hosts imply that even people who see themselves as progressive may become conservative when something they value feels threatened.

[43:01] **Generations, Universities, and Moral Education**

[事实] A listener asks how much culture wars are generational, citing 1968, hippies, LGBTQ rights, and veganism.

[事实] Tom says universities have long trained people to articulate progressive orthodoxies, beginning with medieval efforts to create moral and legal frameworks.

[事实] He links this pattern to the Albigensian Crusade, the Protestant Reformation, the French Revolution, and modern educational systems.

[事实] Tom says every generation tends to produce new progressive standards that older generations struggle to absorb.

[47:23] **How Culture Wars Are Won**

[事实] Dominic asks whether culture wars are won by changing minds or by capturing power structures.

[事实] He says the English Reformation was broadly unpopular but succeeded because it had state backing and because younger generations grew up in a Protestant England.

[事实] Tom argues that universities matter more than direct state power and cites changing attitudes toward homosexuality as a dramatic example from his lifetime.

[事实] The hosts agree that culture, sport, fashion, and the acceptance of a cause’s justice can change public attitudes more powerfully than individual politicians.

[51:34] **Communism, Failure, and the Limits of Control**

[事实] Dominic raises communism as a 20th-century culture war against the old order that controlled schools, universities, and the state but still failed.

[事实] Tom says communism failed because it did not work economically.

[事实] He links this to their earlier discussion of communism as an attempt to create heaven on earth, which collided with practical impossibility.

[推测] The exchange suggests that institutional control alone cannot secure a culture-war victory if the wider system produces failure people can feel in daily life.

[52:37] **Compromise, Victory, and Parliamentary Coexistence**

[事实] Dominic asks whether culture wars must end in victory or whether compromise is possible.

[事实] Tom says Britain’s parliamentary and party system evolved as a dialogue between Whig and Tory traditions.

[事实] He argues that this system allows people with rival views to live alongside each other in the same country and continue arguing.

[事实] The episode closes by noting that future topics include the Seven Years’ War, the French Revolution, and food as another kind of culture war.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its historical depth: it refuses to treat current disputes over statues, identity, patriotism, sexuality, and progress as isolated media storms, and instead places them in long-running arguments over theology, memory, and belonging.

[推测] Its strongest feature is the productive disagreement between the hosts. Dominic keeps widening the field of comparison, while Tom keeps forcing a tighter definition around Christianity, progress, and unrecognized theology.

[推测] A limitation is that the argument is heavily shaped by British, American, and Christian history; listeners looking for non-Western frameworks for culture conflict may find the scope narrower than the title might suggest.

[推测] The episode is especially suited to listeners interested in intellectual history, British politics, religion, and why symbolic disputes often feel more emotionally powerful than ordinary policy debate.
