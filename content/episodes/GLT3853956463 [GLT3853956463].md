# Richard Nixon and the 1968 Election

## Overview

This episode closes the series on America in 1968 by focusing on Richard Nixon’s comeback: from defeated former vice president and failed California gubernatorial candidate to Republican nominee and eventual president-elect.

The hosts argue that Nixon’s 1968 campaign worked because it was deliberately controlled, emotionally pitched to “middle America,” and built around television, image management, and slogans such as “peace with honor” and “law and order.”

A major theme is Nixon’s ambiguity: he could speak in progressive-sounding language about poverty and racial division while also signaling to white Southern and suburban voters anxious about crime, school integration, and disorder. The episode also examines, and largely rejects, the theory that Nixon sabotaged Vietnam peace talks through Anna Chennault.

## Segmented Summary

[00:55] **Nixon’s Nomination Speech**

[事实] The episode opens with Richard Nixon’s 1968 Republican nomination acceptance speech in Miami Beach on August 8, 1968.

[事实] The hosts describe the speech as highly characteristic of Nixon: emotional, self-pitying, melodramatic, and politically effective.

[事实] They argue that while some listeners might find Nixon’s rhetoric manipulative or nauseating, many voters found it brilliant.

[推测] The opening frames Nixon’s success as partly rooted in his ability to turn personal grievance into mass political appeal.

[03:24] **The Overlooked Winner of 1968**

[事实] The hosts say Nixon is often overlooked in accounts of 1968 because the year is dominated by assassinations, Vietnam, protests, and Democratic chaos.

[事实] Nixon’s campaign is described as deliberately boring, but ultimately successful.

[事实] He is presented as the champion of middle America and as one of the great comeback figures in American political history.

[04:11] **Nixon’s Background and Early Career**

[事实] Nixon came from a poor Quaker family in Yorba Linda, California, and is described as a genuine meritocrat.

[事实] He attended Duke Law School, served in the Navy, entered Congress, and made his name as an aggressive anti-communist.

[事实] Democrats viewed him as underhanded, belligerent, and nasty.

[事实] He became Eisenhower’s vice president and gained public attention through the “Checkers” television address.

[05:18] **Defeats and Exile**

[事实] Nixon lost the 1960 presidential election to John F. Kennedy and the 1962 California governor’s race.

[事实] After the 1962 defeat, he told the press they would no longer have Nixon “to kick around.”

[事实] He moved to New York, became a Wall Street lawyer, worked with Pepsi, and lived among country club Republicans.

[推测] His post-politics life is portrayed as materially comfortable but psychologically unsatisfying.

[07:17] **Nixon’s Character and Resilience**

[事实] The hosts describe Nixon as insecure, brooding, vengeful, socially awkward, and unable to forget slights.

[事实] They also emphasize his resilience, seriousness, reading habits, and capacity for hard work.

[事实] Friends such as Jackie Robinson and Billy Graham encouraged him not to give up politics.

[事实] Nixon wrote personal resolutions about goals, rest, time use, and writing on national and international issues.

[09:34] **Rebuilding a Republican Base**

[事实] Nixon disliked Barry Goldwater’s politics but campaigned hard for Republican candidates in 1964.

[事实] He worked the “rubber chicken circuit,” speaking to many local groups, especially in the South.

[事实] By 1967, he had earned credit with ordinary Republicans as a defeated politician who had worked his way back.

[推测] The hosts present this unglamorous groundwork as essential to his 1968 nomination.

[12:27] **Loyalists and Campaign Control**

[事实] Nixon’s 1968 campaign was run by his personal loyalists rather than the traditional Republican Party apparatus.

[事实] H. R. Haldeman and John Ehrlichman are named as important California loyalists who later became associated with Watergate.

[事实] The hosts connect this staffing pattern to Nixon’s deep suspicion and need for personal loyalty.

[13:10] **Solving the Nixon Image Problem**

[事实] Nixon faced two campaign problems: he was seen as a loser after two defeats and as a divisive, unpleasant man.

[事实] He decided to enter every Republican primary to prove he could win.

[事实] His team tried to present him as “Nixon as nice guy” and as a centrist, normal, 1950s-style figure.

[事实] On Vietnam, he adopted the phrase “peace with honor,” which the hosts describe as effective but vague.

[15:07] **The Press Reassesses Nixon**

[事实] Some journalists and writers in 1968 concluded that Nixon had matured and become more relaxed or impressive.

[事实] Theodore H. White, Norman Mailer, and Hunter S. Thompson are discussed as examples of commentators reassessing him.

[事实] Thompson reportedly came away from a conversation with Nixon about American football impressed by his political mind.

[推测] The episode suggests Nixon’s public image in 1968 was more fluid than later Watergate-era memories imply.

[18:10] **Republican Rivals**

[事实] Nelson Rockefeller is described as impressive but too East Coast, wealthy, divorced, and liberal for conservative Republican grassroots voters.

[事实] George Romney’s campaign collapsed after he said he had been “brainwashed” by generals in Vietnam.

[事实] Ronald Reagan emerged as a threat because he appealed strongly to Southern delegates with a law-and-order message.

[19:59] **Winning the South**

[事实] Nixon enlisted South Carolina senator Strom Thurmond to help secure Southern delegates.

[事实] Nixon promised conservative judges, less federal interference, and opposition to forced school busing.

[事实] The hosts say Nixon avoided George Wallace-style racist language while still giving Southern conservatives much of what they wanted.

[事实] This strategy helped defeat Reagan’s challenge and secure Nixon’s nomination.

[22:04] **Spiro Agnew and Middle America**

[事实] Nixon chose Maryland governor Spiro Agnew as his running mate.

[事实] Agnew had gained attention after harshly criticizing Black leaders in Baltimore following the 1968 riots.

[事实] The hosts say Nixon selected Agnew partly because he appealed to border-state and law-and-order voters.

[事实] Nixon’s core audience is described as churchgoing, patriotic, ordinary “middle America.”

[24:24] **The Forgotten Americans**

[事实] Nixon’s acceptance speech appealed to “the forgotten Americans,” the “non-shouters” and “non-demonstrators.”

[事实] He presented these voters as decent people who worked, saved, paid taxes, and cared.

[事实] The hosts note that the speech offered few concrete policies but strong emotional identification.

[推测] The speech’s power came from making ordinary voters feel recognized amid national disorder.

[26:40] **Private Insecurity After Public Triumph**

[事实] After the convention speech, Nixon called speechwriter William Safire late at night and worried that elite journalists would dislike it.

[事实] He complained that critics hated him because they could not write such a speech.

[事实] The hosts use this story to illustrate Nixon’s self-pity even at a moment of triumph.

[事实] After the Democratic convention chaos in Chicago, Nixon held a double-digit lead in the polls.

[29:23] **Humphrey’s Weak Position**

[事实] After the break, the hosts return to a campaign in which Nixon is far ahead and Hubert Humphrey is struggling.

[事实] Humphrey is described as being heckled by anti-war protesters and called a murderer.

[事实] Nixon’s plan was to run a tightly controlled campaign and avoid throwing away his lead.

[30:39] **The Television Campaign**

[事实] H. R. Haldeman wanted to move the campaign into the “brave new world” of television.

[事实] The hosts compare this media awareness to the counterculture’s understanding of television’s power.

[事实] Roger Ailes designed televised events in which Nixon stood surrounded by voters and answered questions.

[事实] The hosts describe Nixon’s 1968 operation as a prototype for later presidential campaigns.

[33:02] **Selling the President**

[事实] Joe McGinniss received extensive access to Nixon’s campaign and wrote *The Selling of the President*.

[事实] The book exposed the cynicism and packaging behind the campaign.

[事实] Nixon’s advertising team privately mocked his stiffness and awkwardness.

[事实] The campaign’s ads used images of disorder, Vietnam, and social unrest to present Nixon as the answer.

[35:33] **Law and Order, Race, and Dog Whistles**

[事实] The hosts discuss whether Nixon’s law-and-order message was fundamentally about racism.

[事实] Nixon had supported civil rights measures before 1968, and his ads did not show Black rioters.

[事实] At the same time, Haldeman’s notes and Nixon’s own remarks reveal cynical racial calculations.

[推测] The hosts’ view is that Nixon’s message was not identical to Wallace’s open racism, but it clearly exploited racial fear.

[39:05] **Nixon’s Dual Messaging**

[事实] Nixon also spoke about poverty, ghettos, and the danger of America becoming two nations, one Black and one white.

[事实] He told affluent suburban voters that the country could not be good for them unless it was good for everyone.

[事实] The hosts describe Nixon as unusually skilled at sending different messages to different audiences at once.

[推测] This dual messaging made the campaign both effective and morally ambiguous.

[40:50] **Humphrey’s Comeback**

[事实] Humphrey began to recover in the autumn as the economy remained strong and Democratic organizations reactivated.

[事实] On September 30, he used network television time for a Salt Lake City speech and broke with Lyndon Johnson over Vietnam.

[事实] Humphrey said he would stop bombing North Vietnam.

[事实] This reduced anti-war protests against him, brought liberal donors back, and helped narrow Nixon’s lead.

[42:30] **Vietnam and Johnson’s Dilemma**

[事实] Vietnam remained central to the election, with 1968 described as the bloodiest year of the war for American servicemen.

[事实] Johnson was personally and politically torn between Humphrey and Nixon.

[事实] Nixon had promised Johnson he would uphold and not publicly criticize Johnson’s Vietnam policy.

[事实] The hosts say Johnson may have respected Nixon more than Humphrey.

[44:14] **The October Bombshell**

[事实] On October 31, 1968, Johnson announced a total bombing halt and said peace talks in Paris could begin.

[事实] This announcement came five days before the election.

[事实] The hosts present it as a potential “October surprise” that could have shifted the race toward Humphrey.

[45:02] **The Chennault Conspiracy Theory**

[事实] The conspiracy theory claims Nixon used Anna Chennault to tell South Vietnamese president Nguyen Van Thieu not to attend peace talks.

[事实] According to the theory, Nixon promised South Vietnam a better deal if he won.

[事实] The theory claims this sabotaged the peace process, violated the Logan Act, and helped Nixon win.

[事实] The hosts say they do not believe the full conspiracy theory is true.

[46:23] **Why the Hosts Reject the Theory**

[事实] The hosts accept that Nixon’s campaign was in contact with Anna Chennault and that she had links to South Vietnam.

[事实] They also accept that Johnson believed Nixon was involved in encouraging South Vietnam not to cooperate.

[事实] The hosts argue that Nixon’s contacts with South Vietnam were not surprising because he had longstanding ties there and was likely to become president.

[事实] They say Chennault probably exaggerated her own importance.

[47:34] **South Vietnamese Agency**

[事实] The hosts cite recent scholarship arguing that South Vietnam was never likely to accept Johnson’s peace proposal.

[事实] President Thieu opposed talks that included the Viet Cong and already preferred Nixon over Humphrey.

[事实] The hosts argue that the conspiracy theory wrongly treats South Vietnam as passive.

[推测] Their conclusion is that Nixon’s signals may have been improper but were probably not decisive in the failure of the peace talks.

[50:27] **Election Night**

[事实] Election night was November 5, 1968, with Nixon at the Waldorf Astoria in New York.

[事实] Nixon watched events largely alone, separated from Pat and his daughters.

[事实] George Wallace won five states and 10 million votes, but both Nixon and Humphrey squeezed his support in different regions.

[事实] The race became extremely close, with Humphrey briefly ahead in the popular vote around midnight.

[52:23] **Nixon Wins**

[事实] The election came down to key states including California, Ohio, Illinois, and Texas.

[事实] Humphrey won Texas, Pennsylvania, and New York, while Nixon led in California, Ohio, and Illinois.

[事实] Chicago’s late results from Mayor Daley ultimately helped put Nixon over the top in Illinois.

[事实] Nixon won by about half a million votes out of more than 70 million cast.

[53:43] **“Bring Us Together”**

[事实] Nixon told the press that a teenager’s sign reading “Bring us together” had touched him during the campaign.

[事实] He said the objective of his administration would be to bridge generation and racial gaps and bring America together.

[事实] The hosts close with irony, noting Nixon’s later downfall through Watergate.

[事实] They remind listeners that earlier episodes covered Watergate in more detail.

## Podcast Review / Conclusion

This episode’s value lies in showing Nixon’s 1968 victory as more than a simple reaction to chaos. It presents the win as a product of long preparation, disciplined media strategy, emotional appeal to middle America, and Nixon’s unusual ability to speak differently to different constituencies.

A major strength is the balance between character study and campaign analysis. The hosts make Nixon vivid as insecure, resentful, resilient, intelligent, and politically gifted, while also explaining the structural importance of television, law-and-order politics, and the South.

The episode is strongest for listeners interested in American political campaigning, the Republican Party’s late-1960s transformation, and the roots of modern media politics. Its limitation is that many judgments about Nixon’s motives depend on interpretation, though the hosts usually distinguish between documented evidence and contested claims.

[推测] The discussion is especially useful as a bridge between 1968 and Watergate: it shows how the same traits that powered Nixon’s comeback also foreshadowed the mistrust, cynicism, and paranoia that later defined his presidency.
