# Kalshi and the Prediction Market Boom

## 概览

This Planet Money episode follows NPR reporter Bobby Allen as he investigates the rapid rise of prediction markets, especially Kalshi, where users can bet on future events ranging from sports and pop culture to politics and war.

The core question is whether prediction markets create useful public information or simply repackage gambling as financial trading. Kalshi argues that its markets are derivatives-like tools that price future events and reward careful thinking; critics argue the industry is exploiting a legal loophole with weak oversight.

The episode moves from trader culture and hands-on betting experiments into Kalshi’s regulatory history, legal arguments, enforcement concerns, and possible social harms. It ends with the industry still growing quickly despite lawsuits, state bans, and unresolved questions about incentives.

## 分段落总结

[01:18] **The prediction market boom**

[事实] Bobby Allen has been reporting on the rapid growth of prediction markets, including Kalshi markets where people bet on events such as Taylor Swift’s wedding date, war in Iran, and whether famine would be declared in Gaza.  
[事实] CNN’s partnership with Kalshi made Bobby wonder why a news organization would pair reporting with betting-market odds.  
[推测] The episode frames prediction markets as a collision between news, finance, gambling, and public trust.

[02:29] **Inside trader culture**

[事实] Bobby entered Kalshi-related Discord chats looking for stories of addiction or financial ruin and was sharply criticized by traders who felt he was stereotyping them as “degenerate” traders.  
[事实] He learned trader vocabulary such as “degens,” “sharps,” “printing,” and “alpha.”  
[事实] The active traders he encountered often presented themselves as skilled, research-driven winners rather than problem gamblers.

[04:29] **Chasing alpha**

[事实] Logan Suddeth, 25, said he left a private credit job because he was making more trading on Kalshi after work than from his annual salary.  
[事实] Logan said his edge came from research, including a profitable Time Person of the Year bet.  
[事实] Kaiden Booth flew to San Francisco to listen outside a stadium rehearsal and time Charlie Puth’s national anthem performance before the Super Bowl.  
[推测] The trader examples show that prediction markets can reward extreme effort, fast information gathering, and niche expertise.

[06:16] **The episode’s central question**

[事实] The hosts say prediction markets are clearly useful for successful traders and for the companies operating the markets.  
[事实] Kalshi’s broader claim is that prediction markets add knowledge, make people more informed about the future, and are not gambling.  
[推测] The episode tests whether that public-benefit claim holds up once the legal and behavioral incentives are examined.

[08:00] **Trying the app**

[事实] Bobby explains that sports accounts for about 80% of activity on Kalshi and that these apps are described as a way to bet on sports in all 50 states.  
[事实] Mary Childs and Bobby bet on whether President Trump would say certain words during a speech about Iran.  
[事实] Mary lost five dollars betting that Trump would say “tariff.”  
[事实] Bobby says the experience felt like gambling because it produced a dopamine rush, encouraged loss-chasing, and always offered another market.

[11:36] **Gambling or derivatives**

[事实] Several states have sued or challenged Kalshi, accusing it of operating as gambling.  
[事实] Kalshi’s survival depends on courts accepting that its yes-or-no event contracts are derivatives or swaps rather than gambling products.  
[事实] Bobby says he wanted to understand the argument made by Kalshi CEO and co-founder Tarek Mansour.

[12:47] **Tarek Mansour’s defense**

[事实] Tarek argues Kalshi is not like a casino because there is no house that wins when bettors lose; Kalshi makes money from a percentage of trades.  
[事实] He says speculation is not the same as gambling, otherwise the whole financial market would be gambling.  
[事实] He argues Kalshi lets ordinary people trade on politics, culture, sports, and economic events they understand.  
[推测] Kalshi’s defense depends on presenting betting on events as market pricing rather than entertainment gambling.

[15:12] **Origins and CFTC approval**

[事实] Tarek says the idea began around 2016 when he was at Goldman Sachs and heard customers wanted ways to bet on events like Brexit and Trump’s election.  
[事实] He imagined a market that would price simple yes-or-no questions about the future.  
[事实] Kalshi went to the Commodity Futures Trading Commission and received approval in November 2020 to operate as a marketplace.  
[事实] The CFTC required safeguards such as resistance to manipulation and limits on insider trading.

[17:41] **Early limits and the election fight**

[事实] After Kalshi was approved as a marketplace, it still needed approval for each new market it wanted to list.  
[事实] Under the Biden administration, the CFTC was more skeptical and blocked markets it saw as lacking public-interest value or falling under prohibited categories such as war, assassination, terrorism, or gaming.  
[事实] In 2023, Kalshi proposed an election market on which party would control Congress, regulators blocked it, and Kalshi sued.  
[事实] Kalshi cited academic evidence from the Iowa Electronic Markets, which had outperformed polls in the 1988 presidential race.

[19:39] **The floodgates open**

[事实] Kalshi won the election-market legal fight, and Tarek says that victory “opened the floodgates.”  
[事实] After that, Kalshi interpreted the approval as allowing many other markets, including sports, awards, and pop-culture bets.  
[事实] Donald Trump Jr. became an adviser to Kalshi, and the episode says the CFTC shifted from default rejection toward default acceptance.  
[推测] The episode links Kalshi’s growth to both legal precedent and a more favorable political environment.

[21:52] **The public-good claim**

[事实] Tarek presents prediction markets as a “truth machine” that can be more accurate than polls because traders have money at stake.  
[事实] He argues prediction markets reward nuanced, unbiased, well-calibrated views, unlike social media.  
[事实] Mary and Bobby’s word-betting experiment made them focus on individual words rather than the substance of Trump’s Iran speech.  
[推测] The episode questions whether betting actually produces better citizenship in every market type.

[24:03] **The legal loophole critique**

[事实] Bobby concludes that Kalshi is using derivatives law instead of gambling law and describes it as gambling “buttoned up and in a suit.”  
[事实] Amanda Fisher, formerly chief of staff at the SEC and now at Better Markets, compares Kalshi’s strategy to the crypto industry’s legal-risk playbook.  
[事实] Amanda says the disruption is less technological than legal: companies take risks and wait to see whether regulators catch up.

[25:36] **Weak oversight and self-policing**

[事实] Amanda argues prediction markets are not serving the traditional futures-market purposes of supporting the real economy, price discovery, jobs, and stable markets.  
[事实] Sports betting apps are also launching prediction markets and seeking futures-market approval as a way to enter states where sports betting is illegal.  
[事实] Amanda says the CFTC is much smaller than the SEC and relies heavily on self-regulation by exchanges.  
[事实] Bobby discusses suspiciously timed bets on events such as Biden pardons, Iran strikes, and tariff-related announcements.

[29:06] **Perverse incentives**

[事实] Bobby raises the question of whether bets on future events can change the future they are supposed to predict.  
[事实] He cites traders submitting questions in a Federal Reserve Zoom meeting to try to make an official say words they had bet on.  
[事实] Prediction-market bettors threatened a journalist over reporting on the Iran war.  
[事实] Tarek says Kalshi avoids markets that create perverse incentives, while the episode notes that prediction-market companies largely decide those limits themselves.

[30:51] **Trader doubts and industry growth**

[事实] Evan Semet, a trader who said he was making close to $100,000 a month on Kalshi, questioned whether more accurate odds on many events add real public value.  
[事实] Evan said prediction markets have a real dark side and are profitable for people like him because others are “throwing away money.”  
[事实] The episode says Nevada recently banned Kalshi while a court in New Jersey struck down a ban.  
[事实] It says 70 new prediction-market companies started in the past year and the industry is projected to become a trillion-dollar industry in four years.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s strength is that it does not treat prediction markets as a simple novelty. It shows the appeal through real traders and a live betting experiment, then connects that appeal to regulatory structure, court strategy, and public-risk questions.

Its sharpest insight is the distinction between prediction markets as information tools and prediction markets as incentive machines. The transcript makes clear that some markets may produce useful signals, while others may encourage obsessive behavior, manipulation, insider-style advantages, or attempts to influence real-world outcomes.

[推测] The episode is best suited for listeners interested in financial regulation, gambling policy, tech startups, and the politics of market design. [推测] Its limitation is that it focuses heavily on Kalshi’s legal and social stakes but does not deeply quantify how accurate different categories of prediction markets actually are.
