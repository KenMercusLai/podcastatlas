# Memorial Day, 2020, Part 2: Bad Apples and the Fat Tail

## Overview

This episode of *Revisionist History* reexamines George Floyd’s murder through the perspective of Thomas Lane, the rookie Minneapolis police officer who first approached Floyd. Lane recognized signs of medical distress and repeatedly suggested rolling Floyd onto his side, but expressed himself in softened, deferential language and ultimately yielded to Derek Chauvin, a 19-year veteran who had previously advised him.

Malcolm Gladwell connects Lane’s conduct to “mitigated speech”: the tendency of subordinates to soften urgent warnings when speaking to authority figures. Drawing on an aviation disaster, the episode argues that recognizing danger is insufficient when hierarchy prevents someone from stating clearly that immediate action is necessary.

The discussion then broadens from individual behavior to the “fat tail” distribution of police misconduct. Complaint data and expert interviews are used to argue that a small group of officers accounts for a disproportionate share of abuse and can spread harmful behavior through professional networks.

The episode concludes that police unions, collective bargaining rules, delayed investigations, and arbitration systems often prevent chiefs from removing these problem officers. Its central argument is that reform should identify and remove the small number of persistently dangerous officers before their conduct harms colleagues, victims, and entire institutions.

## Segment Summary

[02:01] **Thomas Lane’s account of the initial encounter**

[Fact] Six days after George Floyd’s death, Thomas Lane gave investigators a statement about the arrest at 38th Street and Chicago Avenue.

[Fact] Lane and Alexander Kueng responded to a report that someone had used a counterfeit $20 bill at Cup Foods.

[Fact] Lane said the occupants of the parked Mercedes appeared to reach beneath their seats, prompting him to draw his gun and order Floyd to show his hands.

[05:38] **A rookie officer recognizes medical distress**

[Fact] Lane had been a fully fledged Minneapolis police officer for only four days when Floyd died.

[Fact] After Floyd struggled against being placed in the squad car and injured his mouth, officers requested an ambulance and later upgraded the request to the most urgent response level.

[Fact] Lane believed Floyd might be experiencing medical distress associated with what he had learned as “excited delirium,” although the episode notes that this is not an officially recognized clinical diagnosis.

[Fact] Lane told Chauvin that Floyd was not doing well, suggested placing him in the recovery position, and later said he thought Floyd was passing out.

[08:17] **Mitigated speech and failed intervention**

[Fact] Video of the arrest records Lane asking Chauvin whether Floyd should be rolled onto his side; Chauvin rejected the suggestion.

[Fact] Gladwell describes Lane’s phrasing—questions, softened warnings, and qualified language—as “mitigated speech.”

[Fact] The episode compares Lane with the first officer on the 1982 Air Florida flight who repeatedly hinted that ice on the wings posed a danger but did not directly challenge the captain before the aircraft crashed.

[Inference] The comparison suggests that hierarchy can make subordinates communicate urgent information so cautiously that a superior fails or refuses to act on it.

[12:33] **Chauvin’s authority over Lane**

[Fact] Lane told investigators that he deferred to Chauvin’s experience when deciding to continue restraining Floyd.

[Fact] Chauvin was not merely a 19-year veteran; he had also served as a training officer whom Lane had previously consulted for guidance.

[Inference] Lane’s prior dependence on Chauvin as a mentor likely intensified the difficulty of openly defying him.

[16:11] **Jerry Finnigan and the “fat tail” of misconduct**

[Fact] The episode introduces retired Chicago officer Jerry Finnigan, who acknowledged having the highest number of complaints in the city and described participating in the theft of drug money.

[Fact] The Citizens Police Data Project contained roughly 250,000 complaints against Chicago officers from 1988 through 2018.

[Fact] According to the episode, those complaints were highly concentrated: most officers had few or none, while a small number accumulated many; Finnigan had 175.

[Fact] Finnigan served ten years in prison for tax evasion and planning a murder-for-hire scheme against another officer, and his conduct cost Chicago more than a million dollars in settlements.

[19:36] **Why identifying the distribution matters**

[Fact] Gladwell uses repeat speeding offenders and people who emit unusually large amounts of virus while infected with COVID as additional examples of “fat tail” distributions.

[Fact] In 2023, 186 New York drivers reportedly received more than 100 speeding tickets each, with an average of $11,000 apiece in unpaid fines.

[Fact] The episode distinguishes a “skinny tail,” in which responsibility is broadly distributed, from a “fat tail,” in which a small number of extreme cases contributes disproportionately to the problem.

[Inference] The proposed policy lesson is that solutions should target the concentrated source of harm rather than treating every member of a population as equally responsible.

[21:31] **Bad conduct spreads through police networks**

[Fact] Criminologist Andrew Papachristos discusses Jason Van Dyke, the Chicago officer who shot Laquan McDonald 16 times, and says the involved officers were already among the department’s highest-risk group.

[Fact] Research based on Chicago police data found that officers connected to even a modest number of aggressive colleagues had a 26 percent higher chance of receiving a use-of-force complaint.

[Fact] The episode calls this effect “network spillover”: officers’ behavior becomes influenced by the conduct of the partners with whom they work.

[Fact] Gladwell places Chauvin in Minneapolis policing’s “fat tail,” citing his complaint history, seniority, and role as a training officer.

[24:08] **Lane’s responsibility and the limits of the rookie defense**

[Fact] Gladwell argues that Floyd would have lived had Chauvin not arrived, because Lane would have rolled him onto his side.

[Inference] This counterfactual cannot be confirmed from the events described and represents the host’s interpretation of Lane’s intentions.

[Fact] Federal prosecutor Amanda Sertich notes that Lane identified Floyd’s loss of consciousness and remained beside Alexander Kueng when Kueng twice said he could not find a pulse.

[Fact] Lane and Kueng continued restraining Floyd for more than two minutes after learning that he had no pulse; Sertich argues that failing to intervene was unacceptable at that point.

[Fact] Lane served two and a half years in federal prison for his role in Floyd’s death, while Gladwell states that he disagrees with assigning Lane that portion of the blame.

[26:35] **The broader lesson: remove the fat tail**

[Fact] Gladwell’s stated conclusion is that one persistently harmful officer can influence an entire group.

[Fact] He argues that institutions facing a fat-tail problem should identify and stop extreme repeat offenders before they cause catastrophic harm.

[Inference] The episode presents early removal of dangerous officers as more effective than relying on punishment only after a death or major scandal.

[29:23] **Defunding versus targeted institutional reform**

[Fact] After Floyd’s death, approximately 2,000 people gathered outside Minneapolis mayor Jacob Frey’s home and demanded that he commit to eliminating the police; Frey refused and was booed.

[Fact] Frey believed refusing to defund the police was the right decision.

[Fact] Gladwell interprets Frey’s position through the fat-tail framework: if a small number of officers causes disproportionate harm, abolishing the entire institution does not directly target that concentration.

[Fact] Frey instead identified the police union as a central obstacle to changing departmental culture.

[31:58] **How labor rules constrain police chiefs**

[Fact] Frey says that Minneapolis had exchanged limits on wage increases for provisions giving the police federation managerial authority that should have remained with the chief.

[Fact] Former police chief Daniel Oates says voters and governments created rules that prevent chiefs from simply firing officers who deserve dismissal.

[Fact] While leading the 650-officer Aurora Police Department, Oates sought to remove 16 officers; twelve departed with clean records under severance agreements, three of four firings were reversed, and only one was definitively removed from law enforcement.

[Fact] Because some departed officers retained clean records, they could seek police employment elsewhere.

[34:32] **Investigative protections and appeals**

[Fact] The episode says some police labor agreements delay questioning accused officers for days or weeks and require investigators to disclose evidence and witness information before the first interview.

[Fact] Oates argues that advance access to the evidence can allow accused officers to tailor answers around what management can prove.

[Fact] Even after termination, officers may receive a new hearing before an arbitrator whom the union helps select.

[Fact] The cited reinstatement rates range from 37 percent in Miami-Dade to 70 percent in San Antonio, with Denver at 67 percent and Philadelphia at 62 percent.

[36:02] **A system that protects the worst performers**

[Fact] Gladwell argues that procedural protections ostensibly created for the wider workforce are especially valuable to the small number of officers repeatedly accused of serious misconduct.

[Fact] Former chief Kathleen O’Toole says good officers often welcomed dismissals of problematic colleagues and told her those dismissals should have occurred years earlier.

[Fact] Frey says overturned discipline damages a chief’s ability to set standards because reinstated officers can show colleagues that serious misconduct may go unpunished.

[39:00] **Chauvin as the preventable failure**

[Fact] The episode recalls a 2017 incident in which Chauvin struck a child with a flashlight, used a chokehold, forced him to the ground, and placed a knee on his neck.

[Fact] Other officers did not intervene, and Chauvin remained in the department for another three years.

[Fact] Gladwell connects that earlier conduct directly to Chauvin’s restraint of Floyd and to Lane’s unsuccessful attempts to persuade him to change position.

[Inference] The closing argument is that Floyd’s murder was not an unforeseeable aberration but the culmination of an institutional failure to act on an established pattern of dangerous behavior.

## Podcast Commentary/Summary

The episode’s strongest contribution is its connection of three levels of analysis: Lane’s hesitant language, Chauvin’s influence within a police network, and the institutional rules governing discipline. This structure makes the case that misconduct cannot be understood solely as either an individual moral failure or a problem shared equally across an entire department.

Its use of complaint distributions, network-spillover research, examples from several cities, and testimony from experienced police chiefs gives the “fat tail” argument practical force. The aviation analogy also makes the danger of deference vivid, particularly when a junior employee recognizes an emergency but cannot overcome a rigid hierarchy.

The principal limitation is that Gladwell’s defense of Lane rests partly on an unprovable counterfactual: that Floyd would have survived if Lane alone had controlled the encounter. The prosecutor’s account complicates that defense by emphasizing that Lane continued participating after Floyd had no detectable pulse.

[Inference] This episode is especially valuable for listeners interested in policing, organizational culture, labor relations, risk management, and the psychology of authority. It offers a focused reform argument—identify and remove persistently dangerous officers—while leaving broader questions about systemic policing practices and the reliability of complaint data less fully examined.
