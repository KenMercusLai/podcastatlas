# Analysis, Parapraxis, Elvis

## Overview

Malcolm Gladwell investigates parapraxis—Freud’s term for slips of the tongue, misreadings, and other “faulty acts”—through Elvis Presley’s repeated lyrical mistakes. Psychoanalyst Michelle Press explains that such errors may allow suppressed or unacceptable ideas to surface when conscious control weakens.

The inquiry begins with Elvis changing “stroked” to “struck” in “Old Shep,” turning an act of comfort into an imagined act of violence. It then focuses on “Are You Lonesome Tonight?,” whose spoken bridge Elvis repeatedly mangled despite his otherwise formidable memory.

Conversations with Jack White and Nashville songwriters connect these mistakes to performance, emotional exposure, and autobiographical pain. The episode ultimately argues that a lapse is not necessarily a failure: it can reveal why a song matters, exposing vulnerabilities that polished performance conceals.

## Segment Summary

[02:19] **Parapraxis and the episode’s central question**

[Fact] Gladwell visits the New York Psychoanalytic Society and Institute to discuss parapraxis with its president, psychoanalyst Michelle Press.

[Fact] The investigation asks what it means when a singer repeatedly forgets the same part of a song he has performed many times.

[Fact] Press describes parapraxes as slips of speech, reading, or hearing that Freud believed could express unconscious ideas.

[Fact] Freud allowed that fatigue or illness might cause some errors, but argued that close investigation often reveals a meaningful psychological cause.

[04:09] **Freud’s theory of meaningful mistakes**

[Fact] According to Press, unconscious thoughts may emerge through slips when they are too unacceptable to receive direct expression.

[Fact] The concept of unacceptability is central to her account of parapraxis.

[Inference] Under this framework, the location and wording of a repeated error may matter more than the mere fact that someone made a mistake.

[05:31] **Elvis transforms “Old Shep”**

[Fact] “Old Shep” tells of a boy who cannot bring himself to shoot his dying dog and instead comforts him as he dies.

[Fact] The original lyric says the narrator “stroked” his best friend, but Elvis recorded “I had struck the best friend a man ever had.”

[Fact] Gladwell argues that this substitution reverses the scene’s meaning, replacing compassion and moral recovery with violence and remorse.

[Fact] Elvis knew the song intimately: it was the first song he learned on guitar, part of his first public performance, and a piece he continued performing later in life.

[09:46] **Loss, death, and intimacy in Elvis’s life**

[Fact] Gladwell connects the themes of “Old Shep”—love, betrayal, and loss—to Elvis’s preoccupation with his twin, who died before birth.

[Fact] Elvis’s mother, Gladys, died when he was 23; accounts quoted in the episode describe his intense grief at her funeral.

[Fact] Priscilla Presley recalled Elvis taking her to a morgue and spending long periods with her in a dark, cold bedroom.

[Fact] Priscilla also said Elvis controlled her appearance and wanted her to remain a virgin before marriage.

[Inference] Gladwell presents these details as context for interpreting “struck” as a potentially meaningful slip rather than a casual mishearing.

[13:33] **The troubled history of “Are You Lonesome Tonight?”**

[Fact] Elvis recorded the song in Nashville on April 4, 1960, after leaving the Army.

[Fact] The song was suggested because it was a favorite of his manager’s wife, and Elvis recorded it in darkness during the early morning.

[Fact] He initially disliked the takes, while the label waited roughly seven months before releasing the recording.

[Fact] Elvis normally possessed an extensive memory for songs, making his recurring problems with this particular piece conspicuous.

[16:50] **Gladwell’s own revealing slip**

[Fact] While speaking with Press, Gladwell says he is “worried” about Elvis’s mistakes when he intended to say he was “interested.”

[Fact] Press suggests the slip could reveal something Gladwell is working through while immersed in the subject.

[Fact] Gladwell identifies losing control in front of an audience as a profound anxiety for people doing creative work.

[Inference] His mistake makes him a participant in the episode’s argument, not merely an observer analyzing Elvis.

[18:27] **Elvis repeatedly loses the spoken bridge**

[Fact] In a late performance, Elvis changes words, jokes, and loses control during the spoken bridge of “Are You Lonesome Tonight?”

[Fact] Gladwell states that every taped live performance contains problems in the bridge, even though Elvis repeatedly returned to the song over many years.

[Fact] Archival versions have acquired labels such as “Laughing Elvis” and “Crazy Elvis.”

[Inference] The repetition and concentration of the errors suggest that the bridge itself, rather than the entire song or Elvis’s general memory, was the source of difficulty.

[23:30] **Jack White encounters the song**

[Fact] Gladwell visits Jack White, an Elvis admirer who owns the acetate of Elvis’s first recording, “My Happiness.”

[Fact] White performs “Are You Lonesome Tonight?” despite not having previously played it as a complete piece.

[Fact] The sung verses consist largely of questions directed at the absent lover, while the spoken bridge exposes the narrator’s own rejection, dependency, and loneliness.

[Fact] White observes that the song’s minor chords contribute to its melancholy character.

[30:02] **The bridge reverses emotional control**

[Fact] White argues that the title question is partly a diversion: the singer appears concerned about the lover but is actually worried about his own loneliness.

[Fact] In the sung sections, the narrator appears controlled and directs attention outward; in the bridge, he admits that he would prefer deception to life without his lover.

[Fact] White says performers sometimes avoid parts of songs that strike too close to home, and he generally writes from a character’s perspective to create emotional distance.

[Inference] Elvis could sing the controlled sections reliably but struggled when the lyric required him to inhabit helplessness and abandonment directly.

[32:05] **Why the spoken passage is technically difficult**

[Fact] White explains that the song’s waltz rhythm makes it awkward to deliver the prose-like bridge while also playing guitar.

[Fact] He says reciting the passage would be easier if another musician supplied the accompaniment.

[Fact] When Gladwell and White experiment with editing the bridge, White substitutes “carefully” for the correct word “cleverly.”

[Inference] Gladwell interprets this slip as evidence that performing his idol’s song is a high-stakes, almost sacred act for White—something that must be approached “full of care.”

[35:10] **The statistical pattern in Elvis’s errors**

[Fact] Alan Elms and Bruce Heller examined ten known live recordings made between 1961 and 1977.

[Fact] They counted 109 errors in the spoken bridges, including 29 concentrated in four lines about love at first sight, betrayal, unexplained change, and surrounding emptiness.

[Fact] Elvis performed the sung portions largely without error.

[Fact] The most problematic renditions came after the summer of 1972, when Priscilla left Elvis for another man.

[Inference] The timing strengthens the episode’s argument that the bridge’s themes of betrayal and abandonment became increasingly difficult for Elvis to contain.

[40:43] **Recitation as a musical form**

[Fact] Songwriter Bobby Braddock and singer-songwriter Casey Beathard discuss spoken recitations in country music.

[Fact] Braddock agrees with White that spoken passages are often easier when they could also be sung to the underlying music.

[Fact] He cites the recitation in “He Stopped Loving Her Today” and discusses the later talking style used in a Toby Keith song.

[Fact] Gladwell shows them Elms and Heller’s chart of Elvis’s live lyrical mistakes.

[45:39] **A songwriter confronts her own missing lyric**

[Fact] Casey describes a song about her mother that makes her cry and contains a particular passage she repeatedly cannot remember.

[Fact] The song reflects on her mother’s life as a daughter, lover, wife, and mother, and on whether she ever lived a life of her own.

[Fact] Casey connects the song’s emotional force to differences between her mother’s life and her own experiences with relationships and motherhood.

[Fact] When asked to perform it, she repeatedly stops, corrects herself, and says she is thinking about her mother.

[Inference] Her lapse closely reproduces the pattern attributed to Elvis: the words disappear at the point where the song becomes most personally charged.

[50:08] **From condemning mistakes to understanding them**

[Fact] Gladwell’s first response to Casey’s memory failure is embarrassment and concern that she has lost control.

[Fact] He reframes the season’s broader subject as the way people judge one another for mistakes and choices.

[Fact] He argues that condemnation is easy, while understanding what a mistake expresses is harder.

[Inference] Casey’s performance shifts the episode from a speculative diagnosis of Elvis toward an immediately observable example of emotion disrupting recall.

[51:28] **Parapraxis as a gift**

[Fact] Gladwell concludes that parapraxis is not necessarily failure and does not cheat an audience.

[Fact] He says Jack White’s substitution revealed the care with which White approached Elvis, while Elvis’s mistakes opened a window onto his pain after losing Priscilla.

[Fact] Casey’s inability to remember her song is presented as evidence of how deeply her mother matters to her, not evidence that she matters less.

[Fact] Gladwell states that mistakes reveal vulnerability and can make performances real.

[Inference] The episode’s final claim is that imperfect recall may communicate emotional truth more powerfully than flawless execution.

## Podcast Commentary/Summary

The episode’s strongest feature is its layered construction. Psychoanalytic theory, archival recordings, lyrical comparison, performance analysis, and live conversations all converge on one question: whether recurring mistakes deserve interpretation instead of automatic dismissal.

Its most effective turn comes when Gladwell’s own slip and Casey’s failed recall mirror the Elvis examples. These moments prevent the discussion from remaining a detached diagnosis of a dead celebrity and show how anxiety, reverence, and love can interfere with language in ordinary creative work.

[Inference] The central limitation is methodological: connections between particular mistakes and unconscious feelings cannot be conclusively demonstrated from lyrics, biography, and timing alone. Alternative explanations—including rhythmic difficulty, fatigue, intoxication, or deliberate stage play—remain possible even when the psychological reading is persuasive.

[Inference] The episode is especially suited to listeners interested in music, performance psychology, memory, psychoanalysis, or the emotional meaning of artistic imperfection. Its lasting value lies less in proving Freud correct than in encouraging a more generous response to mistakes.
