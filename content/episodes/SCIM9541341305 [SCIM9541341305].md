# Future-Oriented Thinking, Transgenerational Empathy, and Becoming Better Ancestors

## 概览

Andrew Huberman speaks with Ari Wallach about how humans perceive time, why modern technology pulls attention into short reward cycles, and how people can deliberately reconnect present choices with long-term futures.

The central idea is Wallach’s “Long Path” framework: transgenerational empathy, futures thinking, and telos, or ultimate aim. He argues that people need compassion for their past and present selves, empathy for ancestors and descendants, and concrete practices that make future selves and future generations emotionally real.

The discussion moves from neuroscience and psychology to family, institutions, social media, death, education, and storytelling. Rather than presenting the future as a single fixed destination, Wallach frames it as something people actively create through repeated behaviors, modeled values, and stories of better, though imperfect, tomorrows.

## 分段落总结

[00:00] **Episode Premise and Guest Introduction**

[事实] Huberman introduces Ari Wallach as an adjunct associate professor at Columbia University’s School of International and Public Affairs and host of *A Brief History of the Future*.
[事实] The episode focuses on how humans prepare the planet, culture, families, and society for future generations.
[事实] Huberman says Wallach’s work centers on long-term thinking and coordinated behavior at individual, organizational, and societal levels.

[06:13] **Time Perception and Short-Term Reward**

[事实] Huberman opens by asking how humans can think across past, present, and future while living amid anxiety, health concerns, and short-term reward schedules.
[事实] He describes modern life as strongly shaped by immediate goals, quick information access, and dopamine-linked reward cycles.
[推测] The setup frames the episode’s core tension: humans have long-range cognitive capacity, but current environments often train short-range behavior.

[08:38] **Mental Time Travel**

[事实] Wallach says humans excel at “mental time travel,” meaning they can imagine possible futures and collaborate to make preferred outcomes happen.
[事实] He cites Marty Seligman’s *Homo Prospectus* and links future projection to the hippocampus recombining episodic memories.
[事实] Wallach uses early human hunting as an example of future simulation serving survival, planning, and group coordination.

[12:16] **Presentism and Stimulus Response**

[事实] Wallach argues that notifications and modern cultural systems have narrowed people’s temporal horizon into “presentism,” a hall-of-mirrors state focused on immediate reaction.
[事实] Huberman connects this to concerns raised by Jonathan Haidt and James Hollis, including the need to exit stimulus-response mode for several minutes each day.
[推测] The discussion distinguishes mindful presence from reactive presentism: one supports awareness, while the other traps attention in urgent inputs.

[16:09] **Transgenerational Empathy Starts With the Self**

[事实] Wallach defines transgenerational empathy as beginning with self-compassion, then extending to past generations, future selves, and future generations.
[事实] He says empathy for self means recognizing that people do the best they can with what they have, without excusing harmful behavior.
[事实] He illustrates this with regret about how he responded as an 18-year-old during his father’s terminal cancer, later recognizing that his younger self had limited emotional capacity.

[22:22] **Empathy for Others and Connection**

[事实] Wallach distinguishes cognitive empathy from felt empathy, saying the goal is to feel another person’s state rather than merely think about it.
[事实] He links empathy to mirror neurons, group coordination, and the adaptive value of people being in sync with one another.
[事实] He argues that modern civilization has produced disconnection from self, others, nature, and the planet.

[27:02] **Progress, Negativity Bias, and Inflection Points**

[事实] Wallach says that in aggregate there is no better time to be alive, citing health, infant mortality, women’s status, education, calories, and medical knowledge.
[事实] He also says humanity is at an inflection point because decisions about climate change, artificial intelligence, and synthetic biology may shape centuries.
[事实] He describes negativity bias as an old survival mechanism that now interacts with media systems that reward negative attention.

[31:20] **Feeling Future Generations**

[事实] Wallach says asking what future descendants should “feel” is more powerful than listing abstract outcomes like shelter or health care.
[事实] He argues that desired futures must be connected to emotional states, not only intellectual visions.
[事实] Huberman compares this with Martha Beck’s exercises for recalling or creating emotional states that then open possible action steps.

[41:43] **Carob Trees and Cathedral Thinking**

[事实] Wallach tells a Talmudic story about an older man planting a carob tree whose shade and fruit he will not live to enjoy.
[事实] He calls this “cathedral thinking”: building things whose benefits will be experienced by later generations.
[事实] Huberman connects this to awe, architecture, science, and the need to act without expecting lasting personal credit.

[47:10] **Legacy Beyond Credit**

[事实] Wallach distinguishes egoic legacy, such as putting a name on a building, from behavioral legacy transmitted through family and culture.
[事实] He estimates that in 250 years he could have about 50,000 descendants and says his modeled behavior may affect them more than public recognition.
[事实] He emphasizes everyday interactions with children, partners, colleagues, and strangers as vehicles for cultural transmission.

[56:48] **Storytelling, Social Media, and Time Capsules**

[事实] Wallach describes caves in southern Spain containing Neanderthal and Homo sapiens hand paintings as examples of ancient storytelling.
[事实] He contrasts older “horticultural time” with modern mechanical time and says storytelling transmits cultural knowledge across generations.
[事实] Huberman questions whether social media can function as a real time capsule, given the short half-life of most posts.

[62:31] **The Casino in the Pocket**

[事实] Huberman compares social media and smartphones to Las Vegas casinos designed around short-term reward, intermittent reinforcement, and loss of temporal awareness.
[事实] He says algorithms can shorten users’ temporal windows by learning which emotional or reward loops hold attention.
[推测] This analogy supports the episode’s larger claim that attention systems can train people away from long-term thinking.

[69:29] **The Three Pillars of Long Path**

[事实] Wallach says Long Path rests on three pillars: transgenerational empathy, futures thinking, and telos.
[事实] He defines futures thinking as treating the future with an “s,” not as one fixed noun but as something people do.
[事实] He defines telos as ultimate aim or goal and argues that modern society has lost shared answers about what humanity is for.

[75:34] **Personal Tools for Future Awareness**

[事实] Wallach describes keeping family photos on a shelf with an empty frame representing his grandchildren and future generations.
[事实] He uses “long path” as a mantra when he feels pulled into short-term reaction.
[事实] He says the empty frame reminds him that he is one segment in a chain with generations before and after him.

[84:01] **Future Self as a Real Person**

[事实] Wallach cites Hal Hershfield’s research showing that people often process their future selves more like strangers or celebrities than like themselves.
[事实] He describes an intervention in which seeing an aged version of oneself increased hypothetical retirement savings.
[事实] Wallach keeps an aged photo of himself on his bathroom mirror to help guide daily decisions, including health behaviors like flossing.

[90:55] **Death, Mortality, and Future Thinking**

[事实] Wallach brings up Ernest Becker’s *The Denial of Death* and says awareness of mortality shapes culture, religion, and human striving.
[事实] Huberman argues that addictions are rooted in fear of death and attempts to shorten the time scale of reward.
[事实] Wallach describes working with death doula Alua Arthur and doing a death meditation for *A Brief History of the Future*.

[98:06] **Writing to the Future Self**

[事实] Wallach recommends writing a letter to one’s future self and says the change happens during the writing, not only when the letter is later received.
[事实] He says the letter can include hopes, dreams, desires, fears, and a private conversation with a future version of oneself.
[事实] He compares life to a relay race, where the baton is won or lost in the transition zone between generations.

[107:00] **Institutions, Family, and Inherited Stories**

[事实] Huberman raises distrust in universities, government, media, and traditional family structures as possible contributors to short-term thinking.
[事实] Wallach argues that institutions have become good at identifying what is wrong but weaker at articulating desired futures.
[事实] He says people should critically examine inherited stories, including social, family, and personal narratives, and decide what to keep or replace.

[117:18] **From Dystopia to Protopia**

[事实] Wallach notes that much youth fiction set in the future is dystopian and says dystopian stories can serve as early warning systems.
[事实] He introduces “protopia,” from Kevin Kelly, as a better tomorrow that is not perfect but shows progress.
[事实] He argues that stories set in better futures can act as emotional anchors that pull people through the current period of flux.

[122:23] **Scaling Long Path Through Behavior**

[事实] Wallach says optimizing society is not like scaling widgets but more like one person influencing another, repeatedly.
[事实] He points to social and emotional contagion as a way long-path behavior can spread through listeners and their networks.
[事实] He says the goal is not a march or bumper stickers but acting with awareness that daily behavior has consequences beyond the present.

[129:21] **Agency, New Stories, and Great Ancestors**

[事实] Wallach uses Tuesday voting in the United States as an example of an inherited system based on older travel, church, and market rhythms.
[事实] He encourages people to ask which inherited stories serve them, which do not, and what new stories they should write.
[事实] He ends by emphasizing agency, hope, awe, empathy, and seeing the future as something people create rather than something that simply happens to them.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s value is that it turns a broad subject, “the future,” into concrete personal practices: an empty frame, an aged self-image, a letter to the future self, biography reading, record keeping, and deliberate attention to modeled behavior.

Its strongest thread is the connection between neuroscience, emotion, and ethics. Wallach and Huberman repeatedly return to the idea that future-oriented action cannot stay purely intellectual; people need to feel connection to future selves and future generations.

[推测] The episode is best suited for listeners interested in personal development, parenting, leadership, education, social technology, and long-term decision-making. It may feel abstract for listeners seeking a narrower neuroscience protocol, but the conversation repeatedly grounds big ideas in actionable exercises.

[推测] Its main limitation is that the societal-scale solutions remain more philosophical than operational. Still, the episode offers a useful shift: legacy is presented less as fame or credit and more as the cumulative effect of everyday behavior carried forward through people, institutions, stories, and tools.
