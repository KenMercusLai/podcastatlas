# The Powerball Revolution

## 概览

This episode challenges a basic democratic assumption: that elections reliably identify the best leaders. Malcolm Gladwell examines Adam Cronkite’s experiments with selecting student councils by lottery in Bolivia, where removing campaigns and speeches brought more students into public life and produced more diverse governing teams.

Cronkite’s experience yields three central claims: elections can discourage participation, lotteries can produce broader representation, and people are poor at predicting leadership ability from confidence, charisma, or public performance. Students initially dismissed as disengaged sometimes became highly effective leaders once given responsibility.

The argument expands beyond schools through research on National Institutes of Health grants. NIH peer-review scores showed no strong correlation with the later citation impact of funded work, suggesting that expert evaluators also struggle to predict future success.

Gladwell finally tests the lottery proposal with students at the Lawrenceville School. Their concerns about competence and legitimacy lead to a hybrid model: first identify a qualified pool, then select the winner by lottery. The discussion also raises wider questions about political elections, research funding, and college admissions.

## 分段落总结

[02:16] **Questioning a “perfect” electoral system**

[事实] Gladwell visits the Lawrenceville School, a prestigious private school whose student leaders are chosen through campaign speeches and open elections.

[事实] He introduces Adam Cronkite’s alternative approach and asks students to reconsider whether their traditional electoral system works as well as they assume.

[事实] After nearly two hours of discussion, several students express dissatisfaction with the existing system.

[06:17] **Adam Cronkite’s path to democratic lotteries**

[事实] Cronkite studied at Queen’s University and initially wanted to enter politics, but worried that reaching positions of power would require serious compromises.

[事实] He joined Occupy Wall Street but became frustrated because the movement opposed a broken system without offering a practical method for reforming it.

[事实] A connection with Bolivian water activist Marcella Olivera eventually brought him to Cochabamba, where he proposed testing democratic lotteries in schools.

[09:43] **Selecting student councils with fava beans**

[事实] Participating Bolivian schools allowed interested students to volunteer for a lottery that assigned student-council positions.

[事实] Candidates drew green or purple fava beans from a covered pot, with purple beans representing the available council seats.

[事实] The process eliminated campaigns, speeches, posters, voting, and recounts, completing the entire selection in about 20 minutes.

[13:52] **Cronkite’s first law: elections discourage participation**

[事实] Participation varied by school, but many students volunteered when entering required only joining a lottery.

[事实] About three-quarters of students who served said they would not have run under a conventional electoral system, often because they were new, unpopular, or unwilling to campaign.

[事实] Many lottery winners enjoyed serving and entered the lottery again, despite saying they would still avoid a normal election.

[事实] Gladwell distinguishes being capable of holding office from being willing or able to campaign for it.

[16:38] **Cronkite’s second law: lotteries broaden representation**

[事实] Lottery-selected councils addressed human trafficking, obtained first-aid kits, organized field trips and competitions, opened a library, and created student identification cards.

[事实] The identification cards addressed a practical problem at a night school, where transportation workers doubted that attendees were students and charged them higher fares.

[事实] Cronkite says elected groups in Bolivia often consist of friends with similar perspectives, while lottery-selected councils bring together students from different social circles and interests.

[事实] Gladwell argues that a lottery can produce a more representative leadership group than elections that repeatedly draw candidates from the same narrow segment of a community.

[19:13] **Cronkite’s third law: leadership is difficult to predict**

[事实] Cronkite and his colleagues repeatedly misjudged students after their first meetings: some confident, articulate students failed to finish their terms, while seemingly disengaged students became essential team members.

[事实] Cronkite says shyer or less popular students can often acquire additional leadership skills more easily than highly charismatic students can learn to set aside ego, listen, and collaborate.

[事实] Teachers also made inaccurate judgments, sometimes praising students whom their council peers considered removing while dismissing students who later performed exceptionally well.

[事实] These experiences led Cronkite and Gladwell to conclude that charisma and confident presentation are unreliable predictors of effective leadership.

[23:22] **The NIH as an expert prediction system**

[事实] Michael Lauer, deputy director for extramural research at the National Institutes of Health, oversees a system distributing roughly $30 billion in research grants annually.

[事实] The NIH receives about 80,000 applications a year and can fund approximately 20 percent of them.

[事实] Reviewers eliminate weaker applications and score the remainder, effectively predicting which proposed research will be most successful.

[事实] Gladwell compares this process to an election because both ask evaluators to predict future performance from candidates’ presentations.

[26:04] **Peer-review scores fail to predict research impact**

[事实] Lauer examined the relationship between grant-review scores and the subsequent citation impact of funded research.

[事实] His analysis found no strong correlation between peer-review scores and later citation metrics.

[事实] Lauer had expected a reasonably strong relationship because distinguished scientists were evaluating other distinguished scientists.

[事实] Gladwell presents this result as evidence that Cronkite’s third law applies even to expert decisions involving billions of dollars in research funding.

[28:27] **A “Powerball” approach to research funding**

[事实] Following Lauer’s findings, some scientists proposed using reviewers only to remove clearly weak applications and then selecting from the qualified remainder by lottery.

[事实] Microbiologists Ferric Fang and Arturo Casadevall described this as a “Powerball approach,” arguing that formalizing the existing uncertainty could improve grant allocation.

[事实] Lauer notes that science is inherently unpredictable, making reliable forecasts about future discoveries especially difficult.

[推测] A qualified-pool lottery would acknowledge uncertainty openly while preserving a minimum standard of merit.

[32:55] **Lawrenceville students defend performance and readiness**

[事实] Lawrenceville students take the lottery proposal seriously but worry about leaving the selection of their student-body president to chance.

[事实] They emphasize that the president must immediately address the entire school and faculty during an important weekly meeting.

[事实] Some students therefore treat public speaking, sociability, and the ability to energize an audience as essential leadership qualifications.

[34:47] **The appeal—and bias—of a charismatic candidate**

[事实] Current president Tay says he was elected partly because he was highly visible, spoke with many people, and presented himself as someone willing to create change.

[事实] Knowing that his opponent was an excellent speaker, Tay ended his campaign speech with a rap to appear fun and relatable.

[事实] Gladwell finds Tay thoughtful and charismatic and says he probably would have voted for him.

[推测] Gladwell’s attraction to Tay demonstrates the very bias under examination: a compelling campaign performance can overshadow capable people whose strengths emerge only through actual work.

[36:38] **Hidden leaders emerge through responsibility**

[事实] Cronkite describes one initially bored and uncomfortable student who became dependable, volunteered for concrete tasks, followed through, and assumed substantial responsibility.

[事实] Another student, Richard, was new and highly introverted but became one of the most effective council members.

[事实] Cronkite told Richard that leadership did not require dominating conversations; it could mean developing leadership in others and working well within a team.

[事实] Gladwell argues that performance-based elections prevent such candidates from receiving the opportunity to demonstrate their abilities.

[39:01] **Legitimacy of outcomes versus legitimacy of process**

[事实] Lawrenceville students argue that elections give participants a sense of control and allow winners to say that their authority was earned.

[事实] Gladwell responds that legitimacy should be assessed not only by the final result but also by whether the selection process gives different groups a genuine chance to participate.

[事实] He suggests that a representative lottery could have produced far more female presidents and enfranchised people who feel excluded by conventional elections.

[推测] The discussion reframes randomness as potentially fairer than a familiar process that systematically rewards a narrow candidate profile.

[41:01] **The Summer proposal: qualification followed by lottery**

[事实] A student named Summer proposes an anonymous preliminary stage in which candidates submit a project, essay, video, or another piece of work.

[事实] Students would identify a pool of candidates they considered suitable to represent the community, and the final winner could then be chosen by lottery.

[事实] Gladwell notes that Summer pauses to seek feedback and reconsider her own idea—behavior that elections may penalize while rewarding candidates who project unwavering certainty.

[事实] Her proposal combines community judgment about minimum qualifications with random selection among acceptable candidates.

[43:05] **Lawrenceville reconsiders the status quo**

[事实] Gladwell asks the students to rate their happiness with the existing electoral system on a scale from one to ten.

[事实] They agree that a score below six should prompt consideration of a constitutional convention and rewritten rules.

[事实] The average score is 5.3, indicating that reflection on alternatives substantially weakens their confidence in the traditional system.

[44:14] **Extending the lottery argument to college admissions**

[事实] Gladwell asks whether colleges should select students by lottery, and several Lawrenceville students express interest in the idea.

[事实] He compares college admissions to elections and grant review because universities also attempt to predict future performance from polished applications.

[事实] Roughly half the students raise their hands in support of considering admissions lotteries.

[推测] The episode implies that a lottery among qualified applicants could reduce the influence of presentation skills and uncertain forecasts without abandoning standards altogether.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s strongest feature is its progression from a small Bolivian school experiment to elite education and biomedical research. That structure makes an initially radical proposal feel less like a rejection of competence and more like a response to the limits of prediction.

Its most valuable distinction is between campaigning and governing. By showing that the traits needed to win attention are not necessarily the traits needed to collaborate, follow through, or develop others, the episode offers a useful critique of how institutions define leadership.

[推测] The argument is persuasive as a challenge to conventional selection systems, but it provides limited systematic evidence about the long-term performance, accountability, or failure rates of lottery-selected councils. The NIH example also evaluates citation impact rather than every possible measure of scientific value.

[推测] This episode is especially suitable for listeners interested in democracy, institutional design, education, leadership, research funding, and decision-making under uncertainty. Its practical takeaway is not necessarily to replace every election with chance, but to consider qualified-pool lotteries when prediction is unreliable and conventional competition excludes capable participants.
