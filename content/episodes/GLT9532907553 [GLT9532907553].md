# Treason in Medieval and Tudor England

## 概览

This episode uses the National Archives exhibition on treason as a way into the long history of English treason law, from medieval kingship to Tudor religious conflict and the Gunpowder Plot.

The central thread is that treason began as a crime against the monarch’s person and authority, but over centuries came to be understood more broadly as an attack on the realm, the state, Parliament, religion, and public order.

The discussion repeatedly returns to the Treason Act of 1351/1352, treating it as the durable legal core around which later rulers, especially Henry VIII, expanded, manipulated, or temporarily distorted the definition of treason.

## 分段落总结

[00:20] **Modern Treason Laws and the National Archives**

[事实] The episode opens by referring to reports that British ministers were considering updating centuries-old treason laws to prosecute people who swear allegiance to hostile foreign powers.
[事实] The hosts connect this modern issue to cases such as British citizens who joined the Islamic State.
[事实] The episode is recorded at the National Archives, where an exhibition titled around treason, people, power, and plot is being prepared.
[推测] The modern reference frames treason as a still-relevant legal and political category rather than only a medieval curiosity.

[03:11] **What Treason Means**

[事实] Euan Roger explains that treason legislation draws together medieval and modern history because it concerns betrayal, war against authority, and plotting against rulers.
[事实] In England, ideas resembling high treason can be traced at least to Alfred the Great, where the crown begins to be distinguished from ordinary nobility.
[事实] Tom compares Roman ideas of *maiestas* with English treason, noting that Roman treason moved from offense against the people toward offense against individual emperors.
[推测] The episode presents English treason as moving in the opposite direction: from personal betrayal of the monarch toward betrayal of the country or state.

[06:00] **Edward I and the Spectacle of Punishment**

[事实] Edward I used treason in a personal way against enemies in Wales and Scotland.
[事实] Dafydd ap Gruffydd is discussed as a Welsh prince whose punishment helped make hanging, drawing, and quartering emblematic of treason.
[事实] “Drawing” is explained as being dragged behind a horse to the place of execution, not originally the same as disembowelling.
[事实] Quartered body parts were sent to different towns or regions and preserved with salt and wrapping so they could be publicly displayed.
[推测] The punishment was designed not only to kill the traitor but to turn the body into a public warning about rebellion and divided loyalty.

[10:08] **Why the Treason Act Was Needed**

[事实] Before the 14th century, treason was not clearly defined, which allowed kings and factions to use it against political enemies.
[事实] Edward I used the “king’s record” to present evidence and have nobles or Parliament confirm whether conduct counted as treason.
[事实] Under Edward II, factional politics and bills of attainder made treason a tool for destroying enemies and their bloodlines.
[事实] Attainder meant a taint on the bloodline: lands and goods were forfeited to the crown, and heirs could not inherit.
[推测] The Treason Act emerged partly because unrestricted accusations of treason made the charge politically dangerous and legally unstable.

[13:14] **The 1351/1352 Treason Act**

[事实] The National Archives exhibition includes the Parliament roll copy and the statute roll version of the Treason Act.
[事实] Euan Roger explains that lawyers often use 1351 while historians at the National Archives use 1352 because of different dating conventions.
[事实] The act was written in Anglo-Norman French on parchment, probably using iron gall ink.
[事实] The nobles asked the king to define treason, so the statute was not presented as a purely royal initiative.
[推测] The episode treats the statute as the central anchor for later English treason law.

[15:17] **Core Treason Clauses**

[事实] The act defined treason to include compassing or imagining the death of the king, queen, or heir.
[事实] It also covered violating the king’s companion, eldest unmarried daughter, or heir’s wife, a clause tied to protecting the royal bloodline.
[事实] The clause around royal succession was updated in 2015 to make it gender neutral.
[事实] Levying war against the crown and adhering to the king’s enemies are also discussed as clauses still relevant to later interpretations.
[推测] The broad phrase “compassing and imagining” made the statute especially adaptable and vulnerable to expansion.

[16:31] **Levying War and Foreign Enemies**

[事实] “Levying war” originally referred to noble military action, such as banners unfurled in open conflict.
[事实] The Peasants’ Revolt of 1381 caused legal difficulty because authorities wanted to treat it as treason, but it did not neatly fit the statute’s original wording.
[事实] “Adhering to the king’s enemies” was shaped by the fact that English kings and nobles also held claims and lands in France.
[事实] The hosts connect this clause to later examples such as broadcasting for the Nazis or joining the caliphate.
[推测] The statute’s medieval wording created later interpretive problems when treason shifted from feudal war to ideology, propaganda, and foreign allegiance.

[18:47] **Richard II, Henry IV, and Treasonous Words**

[事实] Richard II’s reign is described as marked by factionalism after he took the throne as a child.
[事实] Richard II was deposed by Henry Bolingbroke, who became Henry IV.
[事实] Parliament avoided simply declaring open treason against an anointed king by first forcing Richard to abdicate.
[事实] Under Henry IV, words claiming Richard II was still alive could become treasonous.
[事实] John Whitelock is described as claiming Richard II was alive in Scotland, offering to submit to imprisonment and investigation, then escaping from the Tower.
[推测] The discussion shows speech beginning to matter as evidence of treason, not just armed action.

[22:04] **Treasonous Magic and Eleanor Cobham**

[事实] The episode discusses astrology, magic, and necromancy as extensions of “compassing and imagining” the king’s death.
[事实] Eleanor Cobham, wife of Humphrey, Duke of Gloucester, was accused with associates of summoning spirits or demons to predict when Henry VI would die.
[事实] Because Eleanor was a noblewoman, there were procedural uncertainties about how to try her under rules requiring trial by peers.
[事实] Her associates were tried more directly; one was hanged, drawn, and quartered, while another died in prison.
[事实] Eleanor was also judged through a religious process and made to perform public walks of shame.
[推测] The case shows how treason could overlap with gender, status, religion, and fears about succession.

[26:47] **Richard III and Retrospective Treason**

[事实] The transcript discusses the attainder of Richard III after Henry Tudor defeated him at Bosworth.
[事实] Richard was already dead when the process was brought before Parliament.
[事实] Henry Tudor, once king, could present Richard as having committed treason against him.
[事实] The transcript notes that by the later 15th century these records begin appearing in English rather than Anglo-Norman French or Latin.
[推测] The case illustrates how victory could determine legality: the successful claimant could define the defeated king as the traitor.

[31:43] **Anne Boleyn and Tudor Treason**

[事实] Anne Boleyn is discussed in relation to charges of plotting the king’s death and inciting men involved with her.
[事实] The normal punishment for a woman convicted of treason was burning.
[事实] Henry VIII ordered that Anne be beheaded within the Tower instead, describing this as pity.
[事实] The relevant records were kept among the “bags of secrets,” a set of constitutionally important records stored in parchment bags.
[推测] The episode suggests that “leniency” in Anne’s execution should not be accepted simply at face value.

[33:48] **Henry VIII’s Expansions and Their Repeal**

[事实] Euan Roger says that most of Henry VIII’s additions to treason legislation were removed after his death by Edward VI and his council.
[事实] Later regimes repeatedly returned to the 1351/1352 act as the baseline.
[事实] Henry’s break with Rome created new forms of religious treason.
[事实] Thomas More is discussed as a figure who chose to lose his head rather than compromise his soul.
[推测] The Treason Act functioned almost like a treason-law equivalent of Magna Carta: a reference point later rulers expanded from but also returned to.

[36:31] **Thomas More, Cromwell, and Trial Process**

[事实] The hosts debate Thomas More through the lens of later portrayals such as *A Man for All Seasons* and Hilary Mantel’s work.
[事实] Thomas Cromwell is discussed as being executed by bill of attainder rather than through an ordinary trial.
[事实] Parliamentary attainder became a common way to prosecute treason from the Wars of the Roses onward.
[事实] Treason defendants did not receive defense lawyers until the 1690s.
[事实] More’s records preserve some of his own words in English.
[推测] The discussion emphasizes that Tudor treason trials were often legal performances shaped by power as much as by evidence.

[38:34] **Porridge, Poisoning, and Richard Roose**

[事实] Richard Roose, a cook in the household of Bishop John Fisher, is discussed in connection with poisoned porridge.
[事实] Fisher did not eat the porridge, but others in the household and poor people who received it as alms did.
[事实] Roose was first treated as a murderer, but Henry VIII’s legislation turned poisoning into high treason after the fact.
[事实] Henry VIII is described as especially fearful of poisoning, which was treated as a foreign or Italianate kind of crime.
[事实] Roose was publicly boiled to death at Smithfield.
[推测] Roose’s case shows Henry VIII using treason law to dramatize a feared method of killing and to intensify punishment.

[43:33] **Recusancy and the Gunpowder Plot**

[事实] Under Elizabeth and James I, Catholic plots were framed as treason rather than simply heresy.
[事实] The episode distinguishes private recusant worship from supporting foreign powers against the monarch.
[事实] The Monteagle letter warned Lord Monteagle not to attend Parliament shortly before the Gunpowder Plot.
[事实] The warning led to searches that eventually uncovered the gunpowder and Guy Fawkes.
[事实] After the plot, legislation made 5 November a public day of thanksgiving.
[推测] The Gunpowder Plot is presented as a major moment in the shift from treason against the crown to treason against institutions, Parliament, religion, and the state.

[47:04] **Choosing Traitors and Closing the Episode**

[事实] Euan Roger says he would save Richard Roose, partly because Roose may have been caught up in broader politics.
[事实] He says he would condemn Guy Fawkes because the Gunpowder Plot targeted institutions on a very wide scale.
[事实] The hosts emphasize that the National Archives exhibition includes rare documents brought together around treason.
[事实] They also mention a companion book on the bloody history of Britain through notorious traitors.
[事实] The episode is described as the first part of a two-part discussion.
[推测] The closing reinforces the episode’s main idea that treason is best understood through both legal documents and vivid human stories.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it makes a technical legal subject accessible by tying statutes to concrete cases: Dafydd ap Gruffydd, Eleanor Cobham, Anne Boleyn, Thomas More, Richard Roose, and Guy Fawkes.

[推测] Its strongest feature is the use of archival documents as narrative anchors. The discussion keeps returning to material records, rolls, warrants, indictments, and preserved trial documents rather than treating treason only as an abstract idea.

[推测] A limitation is that the transcript contains some corrupted or Welsh-rendered passages, especially around the late medieval and break sections, so parts of that discussion can only be summarized cautiously.

[推测] This episode is especially suited to listeners interested in medieval law, Tudor politics, state power, religious conflict, and how legal categories survive by being repeatedly reinterpreted.
