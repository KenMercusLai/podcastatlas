# Afghanistan: Empires, Crossroads, and Intervention

## 概览

The episode uses the aftermath of the Western intervention in Afghanistan to ask whether Afghanistan is really the “graveyard of empires,” or whether that phrase hides a more complicated history. The hosts argue that Afghanistan has also been a birthplace and corridor of empires, shaped by geography, trade routes, mountains, cities, and competing outside influences.

A major thread is that Afghanistan has never been simply lawless or isolated. The discussion moves from Persian rule, Alexander, Greek-Bactrian kingdoms, Buddhism, Islamic dynasties, Mongols, Timurids, Mughals, and the Durrani state to show Afghanistan as a crossroads of Persian, Central Asian, Indian, Buddhist, Greek, Islamic, and later imperial British and Russian worlds.

The modern part of the episode focuses on British interventions, the Durand Line, Afghan independence, modernization efforts, Cold War politics, the Soviet invasion, the mujahideen, and the Taliban. The hosts repeatedly return to tensions between cities and countryside, elites and rural communities, ethnic divisions, religious identity, corruption, and foreign-backed regimes.

The closing argument is cautious: the hosts do not claim a simple answer to whether intervention can ever work, but they frame the American-led war as part of a longer imperial pattern combining strategic interest with moral idealism.

## 分段落总结

[00:13] **The Question of Afghanistan and Empire**

[事实] The episode opens with Tony Blair’s 2 October 2001 remarks, made five days before the American and British invasion of Afghanistan.

[事实] The hosts say they want to explain the historical background to recent events and ask whether empires can intervene successfully in Afghanistan.

[事实] They challenge the cliché of Afghanistan as only a “graveyard of empires,” suggesting it has also been a “womb of empires.”

[推测] The episode frames Afghanistan not as an exception outside history, but as a place where imperial ambition repeatedly reveals its limits and contradictions.

[02:39] **Geography, Ethnicity, and the Crossroads**

[事实] The hosts emphasize that geography is crucial: Afghanistan has major mountain ranges through its center, with cities positioned around them.

[事实] They identify Bactria in the north, Arachosia in the south, and major cities including Herat, Kandahar, and Kabul.

[事实] They describe Afghanistan as ethnically mixed, with Pashtuns, Tajiks, Hazaras, Uzbeks, and other groups speaking different languages and often having competing interests.

[事实] Afghanistan is presented as a crossroads linked to India, China, Central Asia, and especially Iran/Persia.

[05:24] **Persian Rule and the First Imperial Framework**

[事实] The hosts say what is now Afghanistan first clearly emerges into history within the Great Persian Empire of Cyrus and Darius.

[事实] Bactria and Sogdiana are described as satrapies or provinces of the Persian king.

[事实] Greek communities were exiled into these eastern regions under Persian rule.

[事实] The hosts note that Afghanistan was not a graveyard for Persian imperialism, since these lands remained under Persian rule for about two centuries.

[07:06] **Alexander and the Greek Legacy**

[事实] Alexander pursued Bessus into Bactria after conquering the Persian heartland and after Darius was killed.

[事实] The hosts present Alexander’s campaign in the region as difficult but ultimately successful.

[事实] Alexander founded several cities, including Kandahar, which they identify with Alexandria in Arachosia.

[事实] After Alexander, Greek-ruled Bactrian kingdoms survived for centuries, even after Greece and Macedonian realms were conquered by Rome.

[08:42] **Ai Khanoum and Greco-Bactrian Culture**

[事实] The hosts discuss Ai Khanoum, a Greek city excavated by French archaeologists before work was interrupted by the Soviet invasion and later instability.

[事实] They describe the city as having Greek theatres and temples.

[事实] They discuss Greek cultural and artistic influence on Buddhist art, while noting that the subject is complex and debated.

[推测] This section is used to undermine the idea that ancient Afghanistan was culturally isolated or inherently hostile to “Western” influence.

[12:32] **Bamiyan and Buddhist Afghanistan**

[事实] The hosts describe Afghanistan as a major center of Buddhism in the early medieval period.

[事实] They discuss Bamiyan and its two famous Buddha sculptures, dating them to around 550 and 610 AD.

[事实] They cite Chinese Buddhist travelers who reported many monasteries in Bamiyan.

[事实] Bamiyan is described as significant because mountain routes connected it toward China.

[13:57] **Islamization, Ghazni, and Medieval Violence**

[事实] The hosts say Islamization of what became Afghanistan took a long time.

[事实] Ghazni is described as the focus of the first great Islamic kingdom in the region.

[事实] In 1150, Ghazni was sacked by a Tajik conqueror nicknamed the “World Burner.”

[事实] The hosts stress that medieval Afghanistan included both high civilization and extreme violence, while noting that violence was common elsewhere too.

[15:53] **Mongols, Hazaras, and Religious Diversity**

[事实] The hosts say Afghanistan’s northern flank was open to nomadic armies, including the Mongols.

[事实] They connect the Hazaras to Mongol origins and describe them as Shia Muslims living in the mountains.

[事实] They argue that the Shia presence helped the Bamiyan Buddhas survive because Shia traditions were more tolerant of visual representation.

[推测] The discussion presents Afghanistan’s later social divisions as rooted in long historical layers rather than recent politics alone.

[17:36] **Timurids, Herat, and Persian Civilization**

[事实] The hosts discuss Timur, or Tamerlane, as another conqueror whose armies passed through Afghan cities.

[事实] They describe Herat under Timurid rule as highly successful and culturally vibrant.

[事实] Robert Byron is cited as calling Herat the Florence of the eastern world.

[事实] The hosts frame Herat, Kandahar, and major monuments as signs of deep Persian cultural influence.

[20:13] **Babur, Kabul, and the Mughals**

[事实] Babur is described as the founder of the Mughal Empire and as claiming descent from both Genghis Khan and Tamerlane.

[事实] Babur first looked north toward Samarkand but ended up conquering Kabul.

[事实] He spent about two decades consolidating rule in Kabul before conquering Delhi.

[事实] After the Mughal center shifted to Delhi, Kabul became more peripheral, sometimes serving as a summer capital.

[23:18] **Nadir Shah, Ahmad Khan, and the Birth of an Afghan Polity**

[事实] The hosts describe the 18th century as a period when Tsarist Russia, Persia, the declining Mughals, and British India shaped the region.

[事实] Nadir Shah defeated the Mughals at Karnal in 1739, captured Delhi, and took Mughal treasure back to Persia.

[事实] Ahmad Khan, a Pashtun from the Kandahar region, was part of Nadir Shah’s world and seized treasure including the Koh-i-Noor after Nadir Shah’s assassination in 1747.

[事实] Ahmad Khan used that treasure to consolidate power among warlords and establish a Pashtun kingdom.

[25:30] **Afghans, Pashtuns, and Origin Stories**

[事实] The hosts explain that “Afghan” was a Persian word for Pashtun.

[事实] They say inscriptions from the Sassanid Empire refer to Afghans as people living around what became Kandahar.

[事实] They discuss traditions claiming Pashtun descent from Israelites and from a figure called Afghana, described as a grandson of King Saul.

[事实] The hosts explicitly say this figure is made up, but the tradition itself endured in the 16th to 18th centuries.

[27:47] **The Durrani State**

[事实] Ahmad Khan took the title Durr-i-Durran, “Pearl of Pearls,” and his tribe became known as Durrani.

[事实] The hosts describe the Durrani kingship as an early Afghan polity wedged between Persia, the Mughal world, and Russia.

[事实] The Durrani realm was larger than modern Afghanistan and included a large part of what is now Pakistan.

[事实] The hosts say this is the first time Afghanistan starts to take on the lineaments of the modern state.

[29:15] **Sikhs, Lost Territory, and the Great Game**

[事实] The hosts say Durrani descendants lost territory to the emerging Sikh Empire.

[事实] Afghan rulers tried and failed to recover territory taken by the Sikhs.

[事实] This loss forms part of the context for the Great Game involving Persia, Tsarist Russia, and British India.

[事实] The hosts connect this rivalry to the first British invasion of Afghanistan, which they say will be discussed in a following episode with William Dalrymple.

[31:42] **The Second Anglo-Afghan War**

[事实] The hosts quote Lord Hartington writing in 1880 after the second Afghan war, saying British efforts had disintegrated the state they wanted strong, friendly, and independent.

[事实] They describe the Great Game as a contest between Tsarist Russia expanding south and British India fearing Russian influence through Afghanistan.

[事实] The second Afghan war is described as a relative British success compared with the first.

[事实] The British sought to force a mission onto the Afghan court so Russia would not dominate Afghan diplomacy.

[34:20] **The Durand Line and Pakistan**

[事实] In 1893, the Durand Line was drawn between Afghanistan and what later became Pakistan.

[事实] The hosts say the line divided the Pashtun people between Afghanistan and the northwest frontier of future Pakistan.

[事实] They argue that the Durand Line created a lasting sore in Afghan politics and remained a factor into the 1970s.

[事实] They connect the border question to later Pakistani intervention and influence in Afghanistan.

[36:52] **Kipling and the Imperial Image of Afghanistan**

[事实] The hosts discuss Kipling’s “The Man Who Would Be King” as probably the most famous literary work about Afghanistan.

[事实] The story is set partly in Kafiristan, which the hosts say was historically non-Muslim and is now Nuristan.

[事实] They say the story helped shape an image of Afghanistan as lawless and dangerous for Western adventurers.

[推测] The discussion suggests that literature helped reinforce imperial myths that still affect how Afghanistan is imagined.

[37:16] **The Third Anglo-Afghan War and Independence**

[事实] The hosts say the third Anglo-Afghan war was unusually started by the Afghans after the First World War.

[事实] Amanullah Khan launched an invasion of British India partly to shore up his domestic power base.

[事实] Britain used air power, including a bombing raid on Kabul by a single plane.

[事实] The war was a tactical British victory, but Afghanistan forced Britain to stop controlling Afghan foreign policy.

[38:27] **Modernization, Women, and Zahir Shah**

[事实] Amanullah is compared to Ataturk because he wanted to westernize Afghanistan.

[事实] The hosts identify women’s emancipation, education, and the veil as major fracture lines in modern Afghan history.

[事实] Amanullah was overthrown after civil war in the late 1920s and was airlifted out by the British.

[事实] Mohammed Zahir Shah ruled from 1933 to 1973, a period the hosts describe as more gradual and relatively successful modernization.

[40:33] **Daoud Khan and the End of the Monarchy**

[事实] In 1973, Mohammed Daoud Khan overthrew Zahir Shah while the king was abroad.

[事实] Daoud established himself as a dictator and saw himself as a modernizing father-of-the-nation figure.

[事实] He promoted policies such as removing the veil.

[事实] The hosts say Afghanistan balanced between the USA and USSR during the Cold War, illustrated by the saying that Daoud lit American cigarettes with Soviet matches.

[42:06] **Communist Factionalism and the PDPA**

[事实] Daoud lasted five years before local communists turned against him.

[事实] The People’s Democratic Party of Afghanistan took power and was divided by factionalism.

[事实] The Khalq faction is described as more popular among rural Pashtuns, while Parcham is described as more urban, university-educated, and associated with Tajiks, Uzbeks, and others.

[事实] The hosts say the party’s leaders plotted against and killed one another.

[43:39] **Amin and Soviet Reluctance**

[事实] Taraki was suffocated after a coup, and Hafizullah Amin then came to power.

[事实] Amin is described as a brutal ruler with a Columbia University MA in education who arrested tens of thousands and built a personality cult.

[事实] The hosts argue that the Soviet Union was not encouraging Amin’s extremism and repeatedly warned him not to provoke a massive reaction.

[事实] They say the KGB tried to poison Amin, but Soviet doctors saved him because the Soviet embassy had not been told.

[45:19] **Insurgency and the Soviet Invasion**

[事实] By late 1979, the United States and Pakistan were already funding the insurgency before Soviet tanks entered Afghanistan.

[事实] The hosts say Islam helped bind opposition across tribal and ethnic divisions against an infidel invader.

[事实] Soviet leaders and analysts understood the danger and compared intervention to Vietnam.

[事实] The hosts say the Soviet invasion was chosen as the least worst option: kill Amin, install a friendly government, and withdraw.

[48:21] **The Soviet War and the Mujahideen**

[事实] The Soviet Union had about 110,000 troops in Afghanistan at its peak in 1985.

[事实] The hosts compare the war to Vietnam: the Soviets held cities and launched search-and-destroy missions but struggled in the countryside.

[事实] Muslim volunteers, including Osama bin Laden, came in with support from Saudi Arabia and Pakistan.

[事实] The United States put in up to $600 million a year at the peak, and Reagan welcomed mujahideen to the White House.

[50:15] **Casualties, Najibullah, and Collapse**

[事实] The hosts say at least one million Afghans died in the Soviet war, with some estimates at two million.

[事实] Official Soviet figures put deaths around 14,000, though the hosts say the real number was probably higher.

[事实] They note that the Soviet client regime lasted longer after Soviet withdrawal than the later coalition-backed Afghan government did after Western withdrawal.

[事实] Najibullah was toppled in 1992, remained in Kabul for a period, and was killed by the Taliban in 1996.

[51:26] **Taliban Origins and Appeal**

[事实] The hosts reject the description of the Taliban as simply medieval and argue that they emerged from the chaos of the Soviet war and postwar warlordism.

[事实] The Taliban had appeal among ethnic Pashtuns and among people angered by corruption and warlord abuses.

[事实] They provided basic services and presented themselves as a force against predatory elites.

[事实] The hosts discuss a founding story in which Mullah Omar executed two men accused of raping a boy.

[54:17] **Why the Taliban Endured**

[事实] The hosts cite a 2009 Carnegie Endowment report by Gilles Dorronsoro arguing that the Taliban were being underestimated.

[事实] They list Taliban strengths as anti-corruption rhetoric, rural services, resistance to foreign invaders, distrust of cities and modernity, and appeals to rural ethnic identity.

[事实] The hosts say coalition forces repeated earlier colonial patterns by occupying cities while opponents melted into the countryside.

[事实] They argue that aid money and American money helped create perceptions of corruption, reinforcing Taliban claims.

[56:28] **Intervention, Idealism, and Imperialism**

[事实] The hosts say the Afghan intervention cannot be spun as a success.

[事实] Joe Biden is described as having opposed continuing the occupation from an early stage.

[事实] Tom says he does not feel qualified to say whether a stable Afghan government could have been built if the Americans had stayed.

[事实] He does say the intervention was an imperial venture and part of a continuum reaching back to the first Afghan war.

[60:00] **Anti-Imperialism and the Demand to Intervene**

[事实] The hosts describe a contradiction between strong anti-imperialist sentiment in the West and simultaneous demands that Western governments “do something” in Afghanistan.

[事实] They connect the Afghan withdrawal to themes from Vietnam and the Soviet invasion.

[事实] They predict that the image of a helicopter over the American embassy will become a textbook symbol of American and Western decline.

[推测] The ending suggests that Western powers remain trapped between rejecting empire rhetorically and expecting imperial-scale influence in practice.

[62:05] **Closing and Next Episode**

[事实] The hosts say the next episode will focus on the first Anglo-Afghan War with William Dalrymple.

[事实] They also mention a fundraising walk for Yazidi refugees in northern Iraq.

[推测] The closing positions this episode as a broad historical survey and the following episode as a deeper case study of imperial failure.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its long historical frame. It resists reducing Afghanistan to recent war, Taliban rule, or a single imperial cliché, and instead shows how geography, trade, ethnicity, religion, and outside powers have repeatedly shaped the region.

[推测] Its strongest sections are the challenges to familiar assumptions: Afghanistan was conquered by Persians and Alexander; it was once a center of Greek-influenced culture and Buddhism; and the Taliban’s durability is explained through social roots, corruption, rural politics, and anti-occupation rhetoric rather than caricature alone.

[推测] The limitation is that the episode moves very quickly across more than two millennia, so many complex topics are necessarily compressed. The hosts also explicitly avoid claiming expertise on whether a stable post-2001 Afghan state could have been built.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners who want a historical primer before thinking about modern Afghanistan, imperial intervention, the Taliban, and the longer pattern of outside powers trying to control Afghan politics.
