# From Product-Market Fit to Focus: Calendly’s Tope Awotona Advises Growing Founders

## Overview

Guy Raz is joined by Calendly founder and CEO Tope Awotona to advise four entrepreneurs facing different stages of growth. Their questions range from scaling a foldable bicycle helmet and temporarily stepping back after childbirth to expanding a nail salon and choosing among multiple sales channels.

Awotona argues that AI has lowered the cost of building early software products, but product-market fit remains the essential first milestone. As companies mature, competition intensifies, bureaucracy appears, and continued innovation becomes harder but more important.

Across the four cases, the recurring advice is to identify the real constraint before pursuing growth. That may mean sharpening a product’s positioning, delegating operations, perfecting one location, or concentrating resources on the few channels most likely to produce the next stage of revenue.

The episode concludes with a broader lesson from Awotona’s 13 years at Calendly: durable businesses should be organized around customers rather than products, because serving an existing customer in new ways is generally easier than repeatedly acquiring entirely new customer groups.

## Segment Summary

[00:09] **Calendly’s evolution and the falling cost of software creation**

[Fact] Calendly has more than 20 million users worldwide, and Awotona remains its CEO while also serving as chief product officer.

[Fact] Calendly has repositioned itself from a scheduling platform to a meeting-assistant platform, adding an AI assistant named Cali that can join email conversations, understand preferences, and coordinate meetings.

[Fact] Awotona says AI tools have made it cheaper and easier to build an initial software product, although capital-intensive frontier AI labs still require enormous funding.

[Fact] He emphasizes that an inexpensive prototype does not eliminate the need to validate a genuine customer problem and achieve product-market fit.

[06:12] **Why established businesses become harder to run**

[Fact] Awotona says businesses often become more difficult as they mature because established companies must protect existing revenue while continuing to pursue growth.

[Fact] He describes technology competition in 2026 as exceptionally intense and identifies innovation as the key to staying ahead.

[Fact] He added the chief product officer role to his CEO responsibilities so he could place greater attention on product innovation.

[Fact] Awotona believes founders can use their organizational credibility to make larger bets, move faster, and overcome bureaucracy.

[07:40] **Newlane: scaling a UK-made folding bicycle helmet**

[Fact] Newlane co-founder Dom Cotton describes a patented, safety-certified bicycle helmet that folds into a flatter shape for commuters and users of shared bicycles.

[Fact] The company spent six years developing and certifying the helmet, launched it in 2024, earned about $650,000 in its first year, and was approaching $1 million in annual revenue.

[Fact] Newlane manufactures in the UK to preserve quality control, but profitability is constrained by production costs, expensive paid media, and distributor and retailer margins.

[Fact] Raz recommends either strongly promoting the product’s British manufacturing as a premium feature or moving production abroad to reduce costs.

[13:52] **Targeting buyers, overcoming behavior, and creating physical virality**

[Fact] Awotona distinguishes between helmet users and the family members who may influence or purchase for them, suggesting that Newlane strengthen its gifting experience and safety-oriented marketing.

[Fact] Cotton says roughly 20% of sales during the previous gifting season were purchases made for someone else.

[Fact] Awotona suggests positioning the product as “the helmet you will actually use,” addressing the behavioral problem that many people own helmets but do not wear them.

[Fact] Drawing on Calendly’s growth through product virality, he encourages Newlane to make the helmet’s distinctive folded form a conversation starter that naturally generates awareness.

[19:12] **Playfield: a solo founder prepares for childbirth**

[Fact] Ivana, the founder of Playfield, sells a modular dog-walking bag with a waste-bag dispenser, retractable bottle pocket, leash attachment, and compartments for personal items.

[Fact] The company launched in April 2024, generated slightly more than $200,000 in its first full year, and sells through its own site, Chewy, independent boutiques, and an upcoming nationwide retailer.

[Fact] Eight months pregnant and one month from giving birth, Ivana explains that she still handles product design, customer service, and overseas manufacturing logistics herself.

[Fact] She has recently outsourced fulfillment, social media, and management of the brand’s ambassador program, but asks what else should be delegated so the company can continue without her constant involvement.

[23:37] **Permission to pause and prioritize family**

[Fact] Raz says parenthood has been more meaningful to him than anything in his career and encourages Ivana to be present for the birth of her first child.

[Fact] He cites the founder of Bogg Bag, who paused her company for about two years after a manufacturing setback and later rebuilt it into a business with more than $250 million in annual sales.

[Fact] Raz stresses that temporarily slowing or pausing a business does not necessarily eliminate the opportunity to return and grow it later.

[Fact] Both hosts acknowledge that caring for a newborn can be exhausting and advise Ivana to allow herself flexibility if the important fourth-quarter sales period does not meet her original expectations.

[26:55] **Delegate operations so the founder can preserve her unique value**

[Fact] Awotona recommends making business continuity the immediate goal and hiring for operational excellence rather than pursuing every possible growth initiative.

[Fact] He recalls that Calendly’s early bottleneck was not marketing but supporting the customers generated by the product’s rapid organic growth, leading a marketing hire to shift into customer success.

[Fact] Ivana says operations consume time she would prefer to spend designing products, building the brand, and developing strategic partnerships.

[Fact] Awotona argues that operational work is easier to delegate than Playfield’s distinctive combination of product vision, brand thinking, and strategy, which currently depends on Ivana.

[33:13] **The Nail Room: deciding whether a successful salon should scale**

[Fact] Dany, founder of The Nail Room in Greer, South Carolina, uses builder gel to strengthen natural nails and avoid repeatedly soaking off artificial tips with acetone.

[Fact] After four months, the salon had reached $100,000 in gross sales, employed six people, was profitable, and had demand exceeding its available appointments.

[Fact] Dany wants to build a chain comparable to Drybar but questions whether large-scale expansion is compatible with her responsibilities as a wife and mother of three.

[Fact] Awotona says expansion is ultimately a personal decision because its financial and social rewards come with sacrifices in time, relationships, and other parts of life.

[39:24] **Perfect the customer experience before opening more salons**

[Fact] Raz argues that builder gel alone is replicable and therefore may not be a sufficient long-term differentiator.

[Fact] He recommends perfecting an elevated experience that customers cannot easily reproduce, such as hospitality, drinks, convenience, and the overall feeling created during a visit.

[Fact] Dany says The Nail Room already serves seasonal craft lattes and has customers who anticipate that part of the experience.

[Fact] Awotona recommends maximizing revenue and capacity at the first location, potentially through a larger space, before opening a second salon.

[Fact] The hosts advise testing each new location locally and operating it long enough to refine the model before accelerating expansion into additional cities.

[44:31] **iSlide: choosing a growth engine without abandoning resilience**

[Fact] Justin Kittredge’s company, iSlide, produces customized slide sandals, slippers, and socks for professional leagues, colleges, local teams, companies, and individual customers.

[Fact] After 13 years in business, iSlide generates about $5.5 million in annual revenue with ten employees and aims to reach $10 million.

[Fact] Its channels include direct e-commerce, traditional wholesale, bulk team and corporate orders, and dropshipping through partners such as Fanatics.

[Fact] Kittredge is reluctant to eliminate channels because e-commerce and dropshipping performed strongly during the pandemic while wholesale recovered and performed better afterward.

[48:26] **Concentrate resources while retaining selective diversification**

[Fact] Awotona favors a focused model in which a small number of products or channels generate most of the revenue.

[Fact] He says moving from $5 million to $10 million will likely require different methods and tradeoffs than those that produced the company’s earlier growth.

[Fact] Rather than immediately eliminating channels, he suggests directing about 80% of leadership attention and resources toward the one or two strongest opportunities while deprioritized channels continue under other owners.

[Fact] Raz recommends seeking fewer, larger, more consistent partnerships instead of spreading effort across numerous small accounts.

[Fact] Kittredge says iSlide is exploring AI to increase the productivity of its existing sales representatives and streamline outreach and communication.

[53:14] **Build around the customer, not the product**

[Fact] Looking back on 13 years of Calendly, Awotona says his most important lesson is to build a business around a customer rather than around a product.

[Fact] He believes first-time entrepreneurs tend to think product-first, whereas his current approach begins with understanding and serving a particular customer.

[Fact] Once a company has acquired customers, served them well, and built a trusted brand, Awotona says it is easier to offer those customers additional products than to continually pursue entirely new customer groups.

[Fact] He acknowledges that companies eventually need both customer expansion and product expansion, but considers deeper service to existing customers the better initial path.

## Podcast Commentary/Summary

[Inference] The episode’s greatest value is the consistency of its diagnostic approach: each founder arrives asking how to grow, but the advice begins by identifying the actual bottleneck—positioning, operations, capacity, personal priorities, or channel focus.

[Inference] Awotona’s recommendations are especially useful for founders moving beyond initial product validation. His emphasis on constraints, delegation, customer-centered expansion, and concentrated resource allocation offers a practical framework for deciding what should happen next.

[Inference] A limitation is that much of the guidance remains strategic rather than financial. The discussions do not deeply model hiring costs, location economics, channel profitability, customer acquisition costs, or the risks associated with the proposed changes.

[Inference] This episode is best suited to bootstrapped founders, consumer-product entrepreneurs, local service operators, and small teams navigating the transition from early traction to repeatable growth.
