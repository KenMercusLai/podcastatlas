# Rat vs. Raccoon

## Overview

Toronto’s supposedly raccoon-resistant compost bins prompt an investigation into a larger question: why did psychology build so much of its understanding of human behavior around laboratory rats while largely ignoring raccoons?

The episode traces raccoon research back to psychologist Lawrence Cole, whose early-20th-century experiments suggested that raccoons were unusually curious and adept at solving puzzles. Yet behaviorism favored rats because they were cheaper, easier to breed and control, and more likely to produce predictable, repeatable results.

Modern research complicates the romantic image of the brilliant raccoon. Animal-cognition expert Suzanne MacDonald describes raccoons as impulsive “search and destroy” animals rather than reflective problem-solvers. This apparent flaw becomes the episode’s central insight: humans cannot be adequately represented by one orderly animal model, because they can exhibit both rat-like persistence and raccoon-like destructiveness.

## Segment-by-segment summary

[00:41] **Toronto’s war on raccoons**

[Fact] Reporter Amy Dempsey became interested in raccoons after they opened her supposedly raccoon-proof green compost bin.

[Fact] Toronto spent 31 million Canadian dollars on raccoon-resistant bins, but residents soon reported that raccoons were defeating them.

[Fact] The city’s raccoons have entered homes, supermarkets, attics, refrigerators, public transportation, and roadways.

[Inference] The failed bins establish the raccoon as both an urban nuisance and a challenge to human confidence in engineered systems.

[03:27] **A neglected question about raccoon intelligence**

[Fact] Historian of psychology Michael Pettit began investigating raccoon intelligence after raccoons opened his compost bin.

[Fact] He discovered that raccoons had received very little scientific study compared with rats and monkeys.

[Fact] The episode frames this neglect as a challenge to a century of psychological theory built heavily upon observations of rats.

[04:29] **What if psychology chose the wrong animal?**

[Fact] Much of what psychology has claimed about human behavior has been informed by experiments involving laboratory rats.

[Fact] The episode asks whether selecting rats as psychology’s dominant animal model distorted the field’s picture of humanity.

[Fact] Pettit is drawn to overlooked figures and subjects rather than the established heroes of scientific history.

[06:07] **The raccoon as an evolutionary and cognitive misfit**

[Fact] Raccoons are neither rodents nor primates and historically resisted easy classification.

[Fact] Their paws are especially sensitive in water, so behavior that resembles washing is actually tactile exploration.

[Fact] Pettit found only a small body of historical research on raccoon intelligence, including Lawrence Cole’s 1907 work, *Concerning the Intelligence of Raccoons*.

[07:55] **Lawrence Cole’s forgotten experiments**

[Fact] Cole studied psychology at Harvard during a period when researchers increasingly used animal behavior to investigate human psychology.

[Fact] After acquiring a small raccoon colony, he placed the animals in puzzle boxes with complicated locks over an academic year.

[Fact] Cole reported that the raccoons solved many puzzles, appeared curious, and might be capable of holding mental images.

[Fact] He proposed that their apparent intelligence made them potentially valuable models for understanding people.

[10:38] **Behaviorism confronts the raccoon problem**

[Fact] Behaviorism was becoming dominant as Cole published his findings and was uncomfortable with claims that animals possessed minds.

[Fact] A later trained raccoon repeatedly rubbed coins together instead of reliably depositing them in a box as instructed.

[Fact] Such species-specific behavior challenged the behaviorist assumption that animals were blank slates that could be shaped predictably through conditioning.

[Inference] Raccoons threatened behaviorism because their actions were difficult to standardize and therefore difficult to convert into clean experimental data.

[12:52] **Why rats displaced raccoons**

[Fact] Rats were smaller, more docile, easier to house and feed, reproduced rapidly, and supported experiments with larger sample sizes.

[Fact] Rats generally produced more predictable and repeatable behavior, while raccoons escaped cages, entered ventilation systems, and resisted control.

[Fact] Psychology consequently entered a “century of the rat,” with pigeons playing a smaller supporting role.

[Inference] The dominant animal model was selected partly for institutional convenience, not because researchers had established that rats best represented humanity.

[15:22] **The laboratory rat becomes psychology’s default**

[Fact] Rat researcher Kelly Lambert says rats were already established in biomedical research and were effectively the only available animal model during her graduate training.

[Fact] Lambert trained rats to drive small vehicles and found that they learned directional controls in pursuit of food rewards.

[Fact] She nevertheless questions how rats became the primary model for understanding human beings.

[17:09] **Engineering animals for reliable results**

[Fact] Laboratory-rat production relied on extensive inbreeding intended to make animals as genetically similar as possible.

[Fact] These breeding practices produced rats that were more docile and controllable than wild rats or raccoons.

[Fact] Researchers believed that similarities among the rats allowed differences between experimental and control groups to be attributed to the manipulated variable.

[Inference] The scientific system did not merely choose rats; it created specialized rats suited to the system’s demand for controlled, reproducible experiments.

[18:42] **From controlling rats to explaining people**

[Fact] Behaviorists used rewards, electric shocks, and other stimuli to train rats to perform actions or display aggression.

[Fact] Some researchers asserted that behavioral mechanisms observed in animals were identical to those operating in humans.

[Fact] Rat behavior was used to support the idea that human conduct could be predicted and controlled through suitable incentives and punishments.

[Inference] The model encouraged an overly mechanical conception of human nature in which social crises and individual behavior appeared manageable through conditioning.

[20:28] **The limits of generalizing from rats**

[Fact] The episode stresses that knowledge derived from inbred rats living in artificial cages is not automatically knowledge about humans.

[Fact] Lambert reflects that she only recently began seriously questioning whether other animal models might have been more appropriate.

[Fact] Rats became so closely identified with experimental science that “lab rat” has no equally familiar counterpart involving pigeons or monkeys.

[Inference] Reliable data from a convenient model can create false confidence when the model’s limitations are forgotten.

[22:21] **Raccoon research returns in Toronto**

[Fact] Pettit introduced animal-cognition researcher Suzanne MacDonald to the history of Lawrence Cole’s raccoon studies.

[Fact] MacDonald initially wondered whether raccoons occupied a cognitive role in North America comparable to that of primates elsewhere.

[Fact] She subsequently began studying raccoons and became a leading expert on Toronto’s urban population.

[Fact] MacDonald acknowledges that raccoon research remains difficult and that significant gaps in knowledge persist.

[24:04] **The myth of the brilliant raccoon collapses**

[Fact] MacDonald reports that baby and adult raccoons perform poorly on many standard measures of animal cognition and developmental ability.

[Fact] She describes their strategy as “search and destroy”: they knock objects over, break them open, and persist until they obtain what they want.

[Fact] In her account, raccoons rely more on action than on visibly pausing to reason through a problem.

[Inference] Their success in cities may arise from uninhibited persistence and physical exploration rather than exceptional general intelligence.

[26:00] **Why a foolish raccoon may resemble humanity**

[Fact] Malcolm Gladwell argues that raccoons’ mixture of cleverness, mischief, destructiveness, and poor judgment makes them a useful metaphor for human behavior.

[Fact] He compares Donald Trump to a raccoon rather than a rat or fox, emphasizing impulsiveness, disruption, and lack of caution.

[Fact] The speakers contrast diligent, wary, task-oriented rats with disinhibited raccoons that take substantial risks and often die young in the wild.

[Inference] The political analogy illustrates behavior that seeks to damage or overturn a system rather than rationally navigate it.

[28:51] **A rat-built world occupied by raccoon-like humans**

[Fact] Gladwell argues that modern institutions were designed around expectations of rational, rat-like behavior.

[Fact] Such systems may fail to anticipate actors who prefer to destroy the “maze” rather than work within it.

[Fact] The narrator concludes that no single animal can serve as a complete model of human behavior: humans are not simply rats or raccoons.

[Inference] Remembering the raccoon means accounting for impulsiveness, defiance, and institutional self-destruction alongside rational responses to incentives.

[30:17] **The laboratory rat gets the last word**

[Fact] Lambert continues to study laboratory rats while recognizing that their selective breeding can make them unsuitable for research on aggression or stress.

[Fact] During the visit, one rat showed unexpected curiosity about the reporter’s microphone despite rats’ reputation for caution toward unfamiliar objects.

[Fact] The episode ends with a comic fictional translation of the rat’s ultrasonic communication defending the laboratory rat against the raccoon’s return.

[Inference] The closing scene playfully reinforces the central warning that even familiar model animals can behave in unexpected ways.

## Podcast review/conclusion

The episode’s strongest feature is its use of a comic urban conflict to illuminate a serious methodological problem. The history of Lawrence Cole, the rise of behaviorism, and the industrial production of docile laboratory rats show how convenience and reproducibility can shape scientific theories.

Its reversal is especially effective: raccoons first appear to deserve rehabilitation because they are brilliant, only for current research to portray them as impulsive and destructive. That disappointment becomes the larger argument that intelligence is not the only trait relevant to modeling human behavior.

The episode’s rat–raccoon and political comparisons are deliberately provocative metaphors, not demonstrations that either species provides a comprehensive model of people. Its broad historical conclusions are persuasive as criticism of overgeneralization, but the transcript does not present enough methodological detail to evaluate individual studies in depth.

This episode is well suited to listeners interested in psychology, animal cognition, the history of science, urban wildlife, and the hidden assumptions behind behavioral research. It also offers a useful reminder that experimental reliability does not guarantee that a model captures the full complexity of its intended subject.
