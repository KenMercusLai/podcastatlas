# Greatness: From Alexander to Angela Merkel

## 概览

Dominic Sandbrook and Tom Holland open the first episode by choosing “greatness” as their theme: who gets called great, what the label means, and why modern societies seem less willing to use it.

Their discussion moves from Persia and Alexander the Great to Victorian nation-building, democracies, fascism, communism, Christianity, Churchill, Trump, Putin, sport, and contemporary leaders. The core thread is that “greatness” is not a neutral description; it depends on political culture, moral expectations, and the stories societies tell about themselves.

The episode’s main conclusion is that older “greatness” often meant conquest, state-building, martial success, or national embodiment, while modern greatness is more likely to be framed as moral inspiration. The hosts repeatedly note a tension between the human appetite for heroic figures and modern discomfort with leaders who place themselves above democratic or egalitarian norms.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Podcast Setup and Historical Range**

[事实] Dominic Sandbrook introduces the show as the first episode of *The Rest Is History*, with Tom Holland as co-host.
[事实] Tom presents himself as a historian of the ancient world, the early Middle Ages, and Christianity; Dominic identifies his work with modern Britain and some American history.
[事实] They frame the podcast as able to range widely across historical periods.

[01:35] **The Question of Greatness**

[事实] Dominic proposes beginning with “greatness”: who is great, what greatness is, and why people are not called great anymore.
[事实] Alexander is immediately raised as the obvious historical example.
[推测] The episode treats “greatness” less as a fixed quality than as a historically changing label.

[02:01] **Persian Origins and Alexander the Great**

[事实] Tom argues that the idea of someone being “great” is not natural but comes from specific cultural circumstances.
[事实] He links the idea to Persia, where the ruler was “the great king” or “king of kings.”
[事实] Alexander III of Macedon conquers the Persian Empire and, in Tom’s account, inherits that model of greatness.
[事实] Tom says the Romans later refer to Alexander as *Alexander Magnus*, Alexander the Great.
[推测] In this framing, “greatness” begins as an imperial and dynastic claim before becoming a broader historical judgment.

[04:26] **Victorian Historians and Nation-Builders**

[事实] Dominic says a Wikipedia list places Emperor Meiji of Japan, who died in 1912, among the most recent figures called “the Great.”
[事实] Peter the Great, Catherine the Great, and Frederick the Great are discussed as rulers associated with modernization and state-building.
[事实] Dominic connects the label to Victorian writers and 19th-century nationalists who searched for founding fathers and national origins.
[推测] The label “great” often appears retrospectively, when historians or national traditions need a figure to embody state formation.

[06:04] **Republics, Democracies, and Founding Fathers**

[事实] The hosts ask why George Washington is not called “George the Great” despite being a founding father.
[事实] They note that the United States honors figures such as Washington, Lincoln, and Jefferson through monuments, houses, and civic reverence, but does not usually use “the Great.”
[事实] They argue that republics and democracies have difficulty with the title because it conflicts with egalitarian assumptions and electoral disagreement.
[事实] Ataturk and Eamon de Valera are mentioned as modern founding figures who are not typically styled “the Great.”

[08:12] **Great Man History and Its Dark Associations**

[事实] The hosts connect “great men” driving history to Thomas Carlyle and Victorian historical thought.
[事实] Hitler’s admiration for Frederick the Great is discussed, including the idea that Hitler and Goebbels compared Roosevelt’s death to Frederick escaping defeat.
[事实] Frederick the Great is described as raising Prussia into a major European power.
[事实] The hosts say fascists were comfortable with the idea that great men shape destiny.
[推测] The association with fascism helps explain why modern audiences often find martial greatness suspect.

[10:02] **Communism and the Great Leader Paradox**

[事实] Stalin and Mao are discussed as hugely influential figures whose regimes still projected leader-centered imagery.
[事实] Dominic argues that Stalin’s iconography presented him as a father of the people and a great military leader.
[事实] Tom counters that Marxism officially treats individuals as “surface froth,” with history driven by economic forces and class struggle.
[事实] Lenin’s mummified body in Red Square is compared to Alexander’s body being taken to Alexandria as a source of legitimacy.
[推测] Communist systems, as described here, both reject and reproduce the emotional structure of greatness.

[12:18] **Moral Greatness and Secular Sainthood**

[事实] The hosts suggest humans have an inherent desire to acknowledge greatness, even when modern political cultures hesitate to say so.
[事实] Nelson Mandela, Martin Luther King, Gandhi, and Barack Obama are discussed as figures associated with moral greatness rather than conquest.
[事实] Their greatness is linked to being on the “right side of history” and the “arc of justice.”
[推测] The conversation implies that moral heroism has partly replaced military conquest as the acceptable modern form of greatness.

[14:01] **Christian Ambivalence Toward Martial Greatness**

[事实] Tom says that in medieval Europe, a Christian perspective created ambivalence toward kings and martial heroes.
[事实] Alfred the Great is discussed as a ruler whom monks could later celebrate partly because he was seen as a patron of the Church.
[事实] Alexander is contrasted as a pagan and blood-soaked figure.
[事实] Tom argues that Western discomfort with martial greatness reflects inherited Christian ambivalence.

[15:23] **Churchill and Contested British Greatness**

[事实] Churchill is presented as a figure who can be called great in the classical sense: flawed, but decisive in a national crisis.
[事实] Tom says Churchill’s greatness lies in rallying and saving his country.
[事实] The hosts discuss attacks on Churchill’s statue and the criticism of him as racist or reactionary.
[事实] They connect Churchill to the idea of “Great Britain,” noting that the term originally refers to geographic size but gained imperial resonance.
[推测] Churchill becomes a test case for whether greatness can survive when moral standards change.

[19:15] **China, Trump, and Modern Political Performance**

[事实] The hosts discuss whether Xi Jinping could one day be regarded as great, while noting the tension between China’s imperial legacy and its official communist ideology.
[事实] China’s First Emperor is described as a state-creating figure in the great-man mold.
[事实] Mao, Deng Xiaoping, and the absence of the emperor are discussed as part of China’s political inheritance.
[事实] Trump’s “Make America Great Again” is cited as evidence that greatness remains embedded in American political imagination.
[事实] Tom says many Americans are uncomfortable with Trump because he pushes the great-man model too far.

[21:43] **Sport, Hero Worship, and Children’s History**

[事实] Dominic says academic historians would once have rejected a discussion of greatness as unserious, even while debating sporting greatness informally.
[事实] Pele, Maradona, and Muhammad Ali are mentioned, with Ali cited as someone who created his own myth by saying he was the greatest.
[事实] Sport is described as a safe stage for hero worship.
[事实] Children’s history books are said to celebrate figures such as Amelia Earhart, Marie Curie, Agatha Christie, and Rosa Parks.
[推测] The hosts see popular history as preserving hero worship even when academic history resists it.

[25:07] **Living Candidates for Greatness**

[事实] Tom says he does not think the present is an age in which people are great.
[事实] Boris Johnson, Donald Trump, and Nigel Farage are discussed as populist figures who might like the idea of being called great.
[事实] Putin is presented as the strongest candidate for being remembered as “the Great” in Russia, because he frames himself within a lineage including Peter the Great and Catherine the Great.
[事实] Macron is linked to the French tradition of Napoleon and de Gaulle.
[事实] Merkel is described as powerful and respected, but her possible greatness is tied to competence, anonymity, and moral authority rather than Frederick-style martial greatness.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s value lies in turning a familiar historical label into a wide-ranging argument about power, memory, morality, and political culture. It is strongest when the hosts show that “greatness” can mean conquest, state-building, national symbolism, moral witness, or secular sainthood depending on the society using the word.

[推测] A major highlight is the contrast between the two hosts’ instincts: one repeatedly tests the category against modern examples, while the other pushes the conversation back toward ancient, Christian, and ideological roots. Their disagreements over Stalin, Mao, Churchill, and modern populists keep the discussion from becoming a simple list of famous people.

[推测] The main limitation is that many claims are conversational and impressionistic rather than systematically evidenced. The episode is best suited to listeners who enjoy historical argument, political culture, and big conceptual questions, rather than those looking for a tightly documented survey of every figure called “the Great.”
