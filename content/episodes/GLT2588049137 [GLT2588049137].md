# Paganism, Christianity, and the Limits of Historical Knowledge

## 概览

This episode continues a discussion of paganism with Professor Ronald Hutton, focusing on what can and cannot be known about pre-Christian religion in Europe, the Near East, and Britain. A central theme is evidential humility: classical paganism can be studied through texts and art, but prehistoric and northern European religions often leave only mute material remains.

Hutton argues that Christianity succeeded not simply by destroying pagan traditions, but also by absorbing forms, customs, sacred spaces, ritual structures, and festivals from older religions. Paganism is presented as local, ritual-centered, and rooted, while Christianity belongs to a powerful category of missionary “world religions” that convert rulers and reshape societies.

The episode then moves through possible survivals and revivals: the Green Man, sheela-na-gigs, Harran, Lithuania, Renaissance Platonism, Druids, Romano-British religion, Stonehenge, and the Red Lady of Paviland. Again and again, the discussion distinguishes between what evidence shows and what later imagination, scholarship, or modern paganism has supplied.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **How Much Can Be Known About Paganism**

[事实] Hutton says certainty depends on which pagan religion is being discussed: prehistoric religion is largely guesswork, while Mediterranean classical religions are much better documented.
[事实] He says northern Europe is especially difficult because written sources before Christianity are extremely scarce.
[事实] Greek and Roman evidence is stronger because texts, art, ritual images, and philosophical arguments survive.
[推测] The episode frames paganism less as one coherent system than as a broad category whose knowability varies sharply by time and place.

[03:42] **Defining Paganism**

[事实] Hutton says “pagan” is a Christian-era term, because before Christianity there was no need to distinguish pagans from Christians.
[事实] He rejects older ideas that the word simply meant a country-dweller or civilian, and explains it as connected to local Roman units of government.
[事实] He defines paganism as the old, rooted, local religions of Europe and the Near East, as opposed to Christianity’s translocal missionary character.
[推测] The definition emphasizes social and geographical rootedness more than a single doctrine or creed.

[05:34] **Why Christianity Replaced European Paganism**

[事实] Hutton places Christianity within “world religions”: missionary religions of salvation with a general message for humanity.
[事实] He says such religions often defeat indigenous religions by converting local rulers and offering them enhanced authority and an afterlife.
[事实] He also argues that indigenous religions lacked traditions for resisting missionary conversion because they had not needed them before.
[推测] This explanation treats Christianization as both political and psychological, not merely theological.

[08:12] **Paganism Without Scripture**

[事实] Hutton says paganism is not a religion of the book, but a religion of ritual and contact with the natural world.
[事实] He says modern pagan sacred writings are mostly books of ritual rather than theology or law.
[事实] He links modern paganism with feminism, environmentalism, and personal choice.
[推测] Modern paganism appears in the discussion as a reconstruction shaped by ancient models and modern values rather than a continuous inherited church-like tradition.

[09:56] **Green Men and Sheela-na-gigs**

[事实] The episode discusses medieval church images often treated as pagan survivals: Green Men and sheela-na-gigs.
[事实] Hutton says the idea that these were pagan images in churches came from 20th-century folklorists rather than medieval art experts.
[事实] He says the Green Man motif originated in India, traveled through the Arab Empire, reached Spain, entered Christian monastic manuscript decoration, and then spread into churches.
[事实] He says Green Men seem to have been decorative rather than meaningful in medieval churches.

[12:50] **The Sheela-na-gig and Irish Ambiguity**

[事实] Hutton says sheela-na-gigs begin in 11th-century France within Romanesque decorative traditions linked to pilgrimage churches.
[事实] He presents their usual moral lesson as a warning against sexual temptation.
[事实] He says Ireland complicates this interpretation because sheela-na-gigs appear more often there on secular buildings and in places not visible to the human eye.
[事实] He notes an Irish folk tradition in which a woman exposing herself could ward off evil or a curse.
[推测] Ireland may preserve a different or additional apotropaic meaning, though Hutton does not present this as settled proof of pagan continuity.

[14:13] **Christianity’s Absorption of Pagan Forms**

[事实] Hutton says Christianity, like other world religions, succeeded partly by absorbing large quantities from older traditions.
[事实] He lists basilican church forms, hangings, incense, altars, carvings, statues, icons, seasonal festivals, and ritual sacrifice as areas with pagan parallels or inheritances.
[事实] He compares saints to pagan gods and goddesses in their roles as patrons of places, needs, and aspects of life.
[推测] The discussion suggests that Christianity eradicated pagan institutions while preserving and redirecting many older ritual habits.

[16:43] **Harran and Medieval Pagan Survival**

[事实] Hutton identifies Harran and Lithuania as the two main places to examine if looking for medieval pagan survival in Europe and the Near East.
[事实] He says Harran, now politically in southern Turkey, had a community of surviving pagans until the 11th or 12th century.
[事实] These Harranian pagans, called Sabians by Arabs, seem to have practiced a developed paganism partly based on Greek philosophy.
[事实] Hutton says possible links to mystical Islam, such as Sufism, are uncertain and may be coincidental.

[19:33] **Lithuania as the Last Official European Paganism**

[事实] Hutton says Lithuania remained officially pagan until the end of the 14th century.
[事实] He says Lithuanian paganism survived because neighboring powers wanted to conquer Lithuania more than convert it.
[事实] The Grand Dukes converted to Catholicism when marriage alliances brought them the kingdom of Poland.
[事实] Hutton says early evidence points to diverse local cults, sacred snakes, and no clearly dominant universal pantheon.
[推测] Later Lithuanian scholarly reconstructions helped shape modern Lithuanian neo-paganism, but the transcript treats the early evidence as puzzling and retrospective.

[22:26] **Renaissance Platonists and Pagan Gods**

[事实] The hosts ask whether figures such as Plethon and Marsilio Ficino can be called pagans.
[事实] Hutton says they were operating within a Christian framework while trying to bring pagan deities back into it.
[事实] He says Greek scholars fleeing the Turks brought important ancient Greek and Roman literature into Italy at a crucial moment.
[事实] He explains that Olympian deities could be reimagined as planetary powers serving the almighty God.
[推测] These Renaissance figures revived pagan intellectual and symbolic resources without necessarily trying to replace Christianity.

[26:08] **What Can Be Known About Druids**

[事实] Hutton says Druids existed and were experts in religion, spirituality, and ritual in northwestern Europe at the dawn of history.
[事实] He says no known writings by Druids survive, and no artifact can definitely be linked to them.
[事实] Greek and Roman descriptions are suspect because some are admiring but distant, while others are hostile and may justify Roman conquest.
[事实] Medieval Irish texts add evidence, but they were written centuries after Christian conversion.
[推测] Druids are powerful historical subjects partly because the evidence is too thin to close down interpretation.

[28:38] **Merlin and the Druid Myth**

[事实] Hutton says Merlin is a poor fit for a Druid.
[事实] He notes that Merlin is portrayed as the child of a nun and a demon, with magical powers tied to his demonic ancestry.
[事实] Merlin also serves a king who is represented, from early versions until the late 20th century, as a devout Christian.
[推测] The Merlin-as-Druid idea appears to belong more to later imagination than to the medieval material discussed in the transcript.

[29:24] **Romano-British Religious Enigmas**

[事实] The episode discusses elderly women found at Kimmeridge in Dorset with their heads removed, lower jaws placed by their feet, and spindles provided.
[事实] Hutton says interpretations range from honored wise women to suspected witches silenced after death.
[事实] He says Romano-British religion is much harder to know than Greek or Roman religion because surviving inscriptions are brief and enigmatic.
[事实] He gives the Wasperton example of a stone slab, stag antlers, and the word “felicitare,” whose meaning remains uncertain.

[31:14] **Unknown Gods of Roman Britain**

[事实] The hosts discuss the hooded figures at Housesteads Fort on Hadrian’s Wall.
[事实] Hutton mentions the genii cucullati and the mother goddesses, or matres and matronae, as enigmatic categories of deity.
[事实] He says the mother goddesses are shown as three portly women with food such as fruit or bread on their laps.
[事实] They were popular with soldiers, but Hutton says the reason is unknown.
[推测] The attraction of these figures today comes partly from the gap between vivid images and lost mythologies.

[32:49] **Britain Before the Romans**

[事实] Hutton says Roman adoption of native deities and shrines gives a dim view of religion in Britain around the Roman conquest.
[事实] The Romans preserved names, imagined appearances, and shrine layouts for some pre-Roman gods and sacred places.
[事实] More than about 100 years before the Romans arrived, Hutton says “the lights just go out.”
[事实] He says technology can reveal ethnicity, diet, technology, living spaces, and crafts, but not thoughts, gender relations, or political structures.
[推测] Archaeology becomes strongest on physical life and weakest where religion depends on inner meaning.

[35:22] **Neolithic Monumental Change**

[事实] Hutton says Britain had little monument building before the Neolithic, apart from posts.
[事实] With farming, polished axes, and pottery, Britain became intensely focused on monument building for about two millennia.
[事实] For about a thousand years, major monuments were sealed stone or timber chambers under mounds, often containing human remains that were handled after burial.
[事实] Around 3000 BCE these monuments were abandoned or blocked, and circular forms became dominant in burials and worship spaces.
[推测] Hutton calls this a massive conceptual change, but says the story behind it belongs to poets or novelists rather than prehistorians.

[37:18] **Human Remains and Competing Instincts**

[事实] The episode discusses practices such as preserving ancestors’ bodies and displaying skulls or long bones around enclosures.
[事实] Hutton says interpretations often depend on whether scholars see humanity as essentially decent or essentially depraved.
[事实] One reading treats remains as revered ancestors or relic-like sanctifiers of space.
[事实] Another reading treats them as war trophies, human sacrifices, or discarded remains of marginal people or enemies.
[推测] The same material evidence can support morally opposite narratives when no direct testimony survives.

[39:16] **Gough’s Cave and Scholarly Disagreement**

[事实] The hosts raise Gough’s Cave in Cheddar Gorge, where skulls were turned into drinking cups.
[事实] They ask whether such evidence shows respect for elders or cannibalism.
[事实] Hutton says there is no consensus and that interpretations now often depend on individual archaeologists.
[事实] He criticizes the competitive “gladiatorial” model of scholarship in which one theory defeats another to become orthodoxy.
[推测] Hutton favors acknowledging a range of possibilities over pretending certainty where the evidence cannot support it.

[41:48] **Stonehenge and the Productive Unknown**

[事实] Hutton says scholars now know much more about when Stonehenge was built, how it was built, and by whom, but not why.
[事实] He notes that older theories linking Stonehenge to Merlin, King Arthur, Anglo-Saxons, Vikings, or Romans have been ruled out.
[事实] He says interpretations of what happened there still depend heavily on imagination and instinct.
[事实] The host argues that Stonehenge and its landscape clearly had sacred or spiritual significance.
[推测] The episode treats Stonehenge as valuable not because its meaning is solved, but because its unresolved meaning connects modern people with prehistoric makers.

[43:51] **Ignorance as a Resource for Imagination**

[事实] Hutton says ignorance about prehistoric religion can stimulate creative imagination.
[事实] He says growing knowledge of prehistoric people’s physical lives helps us visualize them, while their beliefs remain open to poets, novelists, and dramatists.
[事实] He describes this openness as healthy in a multicultural and multi-faith society.
[事实] He also says the Stonehenge landscape should be honored by not building a large tunnel through it.

[44:44] **The Red Lady of Paviland**

[事实] Hutton says the so-called Red Lady of Paviland was actually a young man, around five foot seven or five foot eight.
[事实] He says the burial is about 34,000 years old and is certainly the oldest ceremonial human burial in northwestern Europe, possibly in Europe.
[事实] The man was buried in a cave on the Gower Peninsula, in a bright red two-piece garment.
[事实] Broken ivory objects resembling ceremonial wands or sceptres were placed over his body.
[事实] Hutton says the burial looks like “heavy duty ritual” and may mark, with qualifications, the birth of British religion.

[47:07] **Closing Reflections and Hutton’s Work**

[事实] The hosts thank Hutton and describe the discussion as a tour de force.
[事实] They recommend Hutton’s books, including *The Stations of the Sun* and *The Making of Cromwell*.
[事实] The episode closes by previewing a future Gunpowder Plot special for 4 November.
[推测] The closing positions Hutton as a scholar whose appeal lies in combining academic range with accessible historical storytelling.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s main value is its disciplined handling of uncertainty. Rather than turning paganism into a single hidden tradition running continuously into the present, it repeatedly separates documented evidence, plausible reconstruction, modern revival, and imaginative projection.

Its strongest moments come when Hutton explains how Christianity both defeated and absorbed pagan religions, and when he uses British prehistory to show the limits of archaeological interpretation. The discussion is especially useful for listeners interested in Druids, Stonehenge, ancient religion, medieval survivals, or the history of modern pagan assumptions.

The main limitation is also the point of the episode: many questions cannot be answered conclusively from the surviving evidence. [推测] Listeners looking for a neat story of pagan continuity may find the argument deflationary, while those interested in how historians reason from fragmentary evidence will likely find it especially rewarding.
