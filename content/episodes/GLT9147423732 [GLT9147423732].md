# Portugal’s Long 20th Century: Fado, Fatima, Football and Salazar

## 概览

This episode traces Portugal from its 19th-century decline after the loss of Brazil into the long authoritarian rule of António Salazar. The hosts frame Portugal as poor, politically unstable, agricultural, nostalgic, and still attached to a scattered overseas empire it preferred to call a “pluri-continental nation.”

The discussion then turns to cultural and religious expressions of that national mood: saudade, fado, Fatima, and later football. These become ways of understanding both Portuguese identity and the imagery later used, tolerated, or appropriated by Salazar’s Estado Novo.

The central question is whether Salazar should be understood as a fascist dictator or as a different kind of reactionary authoritarian. The hosts emphasize his austerity, Catholicism, technocratic self-presentation, anti-communism, censorship, secret police, and repression, while also noting Portugal’s relative stability compared with other 20th-century dictatorships.

The episode ends with Portugal’s postwar place in NATO, the Delgado assassination, the use of fado and football as national symbols, the colonial wars in Africa, and Salazar’s bizarre final years after his removal from office was concealed from him.

## 分段落总结

[00:50] **Portugal After Brazil and the Mood of Saudade**

[事实] Portugal is introduced after the Napoleonic Wars and after losing Brazil in the 1820s, while still holding places such as Angola, Mozambique, East Timor, Goa, and Macau.

[事实] The hosts say Portuguese officials described these territories not as colonies but as parts of a “pluri-continental nation.”

[事实] Portugal is described as largely agricultural, politically turbulent, and far behind industrial powers such as Britain, France, Germany, Belgium, and the Netherlands.

[事实] Saudade is presented as a defining Portuguese mood of melancholy, nostalgia, and longing for something lost or perhaps impossible.

[推测] The hosts use saudade as a bridge between Portugal’s historical decline and its later cultural and political self-image.

[06:20] **Fado as the Sound of Portuguese Melancholy**

[事实] Fado is described as originating in Lisbon and port areas, with ballads full of nostalgia and street-level experience.

[事实] Maria Severa is named as the most famous 19th-century fado singer, and Amália Rodrigues is called the queen of fado and the best-selling Portuguese musical artist.

[事实] Amália Rodrigues is described as coming from poverty and performing in a black shawl, which reinforced an old-fashioned and nostalgic image.

[推测] Fado is treated as one of the clearest cultural expressions of the melancholy associated with Portuguese history.

[09:03] **Salazar’s Background and the First Republic**

[事实] António Salazar was born in 1889, came from a modest rural background, studied at a seminary, then studied law and finance at Coimbra.

[事实] He became an economics professor at Coimbra in his mid-twenties.

[事实] The First Portuguese Republic, founded after the monarchy was overthrown in 1910, is described as chaotic, with eight presidents, 44 cabinets, and 21 coups or uprisings in 16 years.

[事实] Portugal joined the First World War on the Allied side, partly because of its commercial and sentimental ties to Britain.

[事实] The hosts say about 8,000 Portuguese combatants died on the Western Front.

[14:00] **Fatima and a Search for Solace**

[事实] The Fatima visions are described as taking place during the First World War, when shepherd children reported appearances of the Virgin Mary.

[事实] Large crowds gathered on 13 October 1917, when many people claimed to see the sun behave strangely.

[事实] The hosts connect Fatima to later Catholic devotion, including John Paul II’s interest in Our Lady of Fatima.

[推测] The hosts suggest the visions reflected a turbulent political and economic landscape in which people were looking for solace and escape.

[17:22] **The 1926 Coup and Salazar’s Rise**

[事实] In 1926, the army staged a coup and later called on Salazar to help manage Portugal’s public debt and economy.

[事实] Salazar briefly became finance minister, left after five days, and repeatedly used resignation threats to gain more authority.

[事实] By 1928 he became finance minister more permanently and received veto power over other departments.

[事实] In 1932, Salazar became prime minister and effectively dominated the government.

[推测] His rise is presented less as a dramatic seizure of power than as a gradual accumulation of authority through financial competence and institutional dependency.

[20:53] **The Foundations of the Estado Novo**

[事实] Salazar is credited in the discussion with sorting out debt, running balanced budgets, and presenting himself as clever, hard-working, and austere.

[事实] His support base is described as including Catholic middle-class conservatives, big landowners, the army, and the Catholic Church.

[事实] The hosts say he protected those groups from socialism, Bolshevism, and anarchism.

[事实] Salazar remained prime minister rather than president, even while exercising quasi-dictatorial power.

[推测] The regime’s authority depended both on Salazar’s personal competence and on powerful institutions that saw him as useful.

[22:35] **Was Salazar a Fascist?**

[事实] Salazar wore a business suit, lived frugally, avoided mass rallies, had no major personality cult, and showed little interest in mass mobilization.

[事实] His National Union is described as a single party that he presented almost as a non-party.

[事实] The hosts say his constitution was corporatist and that some regime symbols resembled fascist iconography.

[事实] Robert Paxton is cited as saying Salazar copied some fascist language and imagery but lacked fascism’s dynamism, excitement, and radicalism.

[推测] The episode’s answer is that Salazar was authoritarian and reactionary, but not easily reducible to the fascist model of Hitler or Mussolini.

[25:18] **Repression, Censorship and Political Prisons**

[事实] Salazar’s regime used a secret police best known as the PIDE and maintained political prisons.

[事实] The hosts say dissidents were jailed, censorship was extensive, and some people were tortured, disappeared, or killed.

[事实] They estimate that perhaps 50 to 100 people died in Portugal over several decades, while noting that a definitive figure is hard to find.

[事实] Portugal had abolished the death penalty in the late 19th century, and Salazar did not reintroduce it.

[推测] The hosts portray the repression as real and grim, but smaller in scale than the mass terror of several other 20th-century dictatorships.

[26:48] **A Reactionary State That Wanted to Stop the Clock**

[事实] Salazar’s Estado Novo is described as seeking not a new revolutionary future but a return to, or preservation of, a Catholic and traditional social order.

[事实] The hosts say literacy improved, schools were built, and the economy was managed more competently than before.

[事实] Salazar is said to have reacted to the discovery of oil in Angola by saying, “what a pity.”

[推测] The oil anecdote is used to show how deeply suspicious Salazar was of disruptive modernization.

[28:54] **Salazar, Nazism and Other Dictators**

[事实] Salazar hated Bolshevism and socialism, but described Nazism as pagan and incompatible with Christian civilization.

[事实] He purged or denounced the overtly fascist Blue Shirts in Portugal.

[事实] He condemned the exaltation of youth, direct action, state supremacy over social life, and organizing masses behind a single leader.

[事实] The hosts contrast him with Mussolini’s theatrical swagger and Franco’s corruption and display.

[推测] Salazar appears in the episode as an unusually austere authoritarian whose personal seriousness makes him more morally complicated to discuss, not less repressive.

[31:46] **Personal Austerity and the Monkish Image**

[事实] Life magazine described Salazar as calm, silent, ascetic, puritanical, and a glutton for work.

[事实] He lived with a housekeeper, Dona Maria, whose relationship with him became the subject of speculation.

[事实] The hosts say Salazar was not personally very corrupt, though his regime included corruption.

[事实] He worked constantly and disliked publicity.

[推测] His personal austerity helped create the image of a ruler above normal political vanity, even while he presided over censorship and repression.

[34:19] **Portugal’s Relative Stability and the Second World War**

[事实] The hosts argue that life in Portugal in the 1930s and 1940s was likely preferable to life in Italy, Spain, Germany, or the USSR.

[事实] Portugal avoided civil war, mass terror, and entry into the Second World War.

[事实] Salazar coordinated neutrality with Britain, which accepted Portugal’s position and valued its continued alliance.

[事实] Britain courted Salazar diplomatically, including through Churchill’s personal letter and an honorary Oxford degree.

[推测] Portugal’s neutrality is presented as a careful balancing act shaped by fear of Germany and dependence on Britain.

[37:40] **Tungsten, the Azores and Wartime Refugees**

[事实] Portugal supplied tungsten, or wolfram, to both Britain and Germany during the war.

[事实] Britain wanted access to the Azores as Atlantic bases, and Salazar allowed use of them in 1943 under conditions that avoided openly implicating Portugal.

[事实] Britain had a contingency plan called Operation Brisk to seize the Azores if Salazar refused.

[事实] Portuguese visas helped refugees escape Hitler’s Europe, including the Habsburgs and many Jews in France.

[事实] Aristides de Sousa Mendes, the Portuguese consul in Bordeaux, is described as giving out thousands or possibly tens of thousands of visas.

[40:53] **Postwar Reputation and NATO**

[事实] Sousa Mendes later came third in a Portuguese vote on the greatest Portuguese figures in history.

[事实] Salazar came first in that 2007 vote with 41 percent.

[事实] Portugal became a founding member of NATO.

[事实] During the Cold War, the Americans and NATO valued Salazar as a staunch anti-communist.

[推测] Salazar’s wartime neutrality, anti-communism, and avoidance of civil war helped preserve his international standing after 1945.

[42:17] **Humberto Delgado and the Regime’s Brutality**

[事实] In 1958, Air Force general Humberto Delgado ran for president as an independent candidate.

[事实] Delgado said his first act if elected would be to sack Salazar as prime minister.

[事实] He officially received 25 percent of the vote, though the hosts say many believed he could have won in a genuinely free election.

[事实] Delgado was expelled from the army, exiled, and murdered by the secret police in 1965 after being lured to the Spanish border.

[推测] The hosts treat Delgado’s murder as a moment when the unpleasant nature of the regime became harder for outsiders to ignore.

[44:40] **Fatima, Fado and Football**

[事实] The phrase “Fatima, Fado and football” is used to describe key cultural supports or symbols in Salazar-era Portugal.

[事实] Fatima brought tourists and pilgrims.

[事实] Fado, originally street music with some radical associations, became tamer after the war and was used to project an image of old-fashioned Catholic Portugal.

[事实] Amália Rodrigues was not described as a Salazarist, and the hosts say Salazar despised her, but the regime still used her image abroad.

[事实] Football became a major asset to the regime, with Benfica winning the European Cup in 1961 and 1962 and Portugal reaching the 1966 World Cup semifinals.

[47:24] **Eusébio and the Colonial Contradiction**

[事实] Eusébio is described as probably the most internationally famous Portuguese person before Cristiano Ronaldo.

[事实] He was Black, born in Mozambique, and came from a poor family.

[事实] The hosts say his fame came as Portugal’s relationship with colonies such as Mozambique and Angola was becoming increasingly strained.

[推测] Eusébio’s status as an international symbol of Portugal highlighted the contradiction between Portugal’s imperial self-image and rising anti-colonial conflict.

[48:00] **Goa, African Wars and Late-Salazar Crisis**

[事实] Portugal lost Goa to India in the 1960s.

[事实] Uprisings took place in Angola, Mozambique, and what became Guinea-Bissau.

[事实] Portugal became increasingly aligned with South Africa and Rhodesia, and Mozambican trade helped Rhodesia bypass boycotts.

[事实] The hosts say around 8,000 Portuguese were killed fighting in Angola and Mozambique.

[事实] Portugal remained the poorest country in Western Europe, even though its economy had grown after the Second World War.

[推测] The colonial wars are presented as financially and politically unsustainable for a poor country led by an increasingly outdated ruler.

[50:44] **Salazar’s Fall and Caetano’s Succession**

[事实] On 3 August 1968, Salazar suffered a brain hemorrhage after apparently falling from a chair.

[事实] He went into hospital and then into a coma.

[事实] The president dismissed him as prime minister and appointed Marcelo Caetano, a former rector of Lisbon University and constitutional lawyer.

[事实] Salazar recovered consciousness and lived for another 23 months.

[事实] The hosts say nobody told Salazar he was no longer prime minister, though they doubt the more elaborate stories about fake newspapers being produced for him.

[52:49] **Legacy, Popular Support and Ambiguity**

[事实] The hosts ask how Salazar maintained support if his security apparatus was repressive but not comparable to the Gestapo.

[事实] They connect his later popularity to the 2007 poll in which he won 41 percent.

[事实] The American Conservative is mentioned as having published an essay called “Waiting for Salazar,” presenting him as relevant to post-liberal politics.

[事实] Dean Acheson is quoted as describing Salazar as the closest 20th-century figure to a Platonic philosopher king.

[事实] The hosts say Salazar was not overtly anti-Semitic and that Portugal was often noted in the 1930s and 1940s for having relatively little anti-Semitism.

[推测] The episode’s final judgment is that Salazar remains unsettling because he combined repression with austerity, stability, personal seriousness, and some enduring popular admiration.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it resists a simple label for Salazar while still making clear that his regime censored, imprisoned, and killed opponents. It distinguishes personal austerity from political virtue, which is one of the strongest analytical threads.

[推测] Its best material is the connection between culture and politics: saudade, fado, Fatima, football, and empire are not treated as decorative background, but as ways Portugal imagined itself and was presented to the outside world.

[推测] A limitation is that several claims are openly tentative, including death totals under the regime, details of Fatima, and the extent of Salazar’s racism or lack of anti-Semitism. The hosts acknowledge some uncertainty rather than pretending to settle every point.

[推测] This episode is especially suitable for listeners interested in 20th-century authoritarianism, Catholic politics, Portugal’s imperial afterlife, and the blurred line between reactionary dictatorship and fascism.
