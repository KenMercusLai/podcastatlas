# Sitting Bull, Wounded Knee, and the End of the Lakota Wars

## 概览

This episode concludes an 11-part series on Custer, Crazy Horse, Sitting Bull, the Lakota, and the wars on the Great Plains. The central arc moves from the death of Sitting Bull to the massacre at Wounded Knee, treating both as the bleak final acts of a larger story of conquest, resistance, assimilation, mythmaking, and show-business exploitation.

The hosts frame Sitting Bull’s death through the intense hostility of Agent McLaughlin, the panic around the Ghost Dance, and the divided position of Lakota agency police who were themselves Lakota but had aligned with the reservation system. They present the killing not as a heroic confrontation, but as a confused, tragic, intimate collapse in which family, old rivalries, assimilation, and humiliation all converge.

The second half turns to Wounded Knee, where Bigfoot’s starving and frightened band is surrounded by the Seventh Cavalry. A disarmament attempt spirals into mass killing, with women and children among the dead, and the hosts stress both the role of panic and the deeper atmosphere of revenge, myth, and racial violence.

The episode ends by asking how the story has been remembered: from triumphant frontier narratives and “battle” language to later sympathy for Native American perspectives, and from Buffalo Bill-style spectacle to modern cultural unease about how Native peoples are represented, commemorated, and economically forced to perform versions of their own past.

## 分段落总结

[00:45] **Baum’s editorial and genocidal rhetoric**

[事实] The episode opens with a long editorial published after Sitting Bull’s death by L. Frank Baum, later known as the author of *The Wizard of Oz*.

[事实] The hosts note that the editorial begins by acknowledging Sitting Bull’s stature and the wrongs done to his people, but ends by calling for the annihilation of remaining Native Americans.

[事实] They use the editorial to show the ambiguity of white American attitudes: admiration for the “nobility” of Plains Indians alongside social-Darwinist conquest and genocidal contempt.

[推测] The editorial functions as a moral frame for the episode, showing how romanticization and extermination could coexist in late 19th-century public discourse.

[05:52] **Recap: pressure, the Ghost Dance, and Sitting Bull as scapegoat**

[事实] The hosts recap the previous episode: railroad expansion, bison destruction, drought, settlers, reservation pressure, and the political push to admit North and South Dakota as states.

[事实] They describe the Ghost Dance as a millenarian movement that gained strength in 1890 amid Lakota social collapse and despair.

[事实] The press unfairly treated Sitting Bull as the ringleader of a supposed uprising, while troops were mobilized and Agent McLaughlin prepared to act against him.

[推测] The hosts imply that panic about the Ghost Dance created a pretext for authorities to target Sitting Bull even though he was not directing a coordinated rebellion.

[07:16] **Sitting Bull, Mrs Weldon, and declining authority**

[事实] Sitting Bull is described as living near the Grand River with his extended family and followers when Mrs Weldon, a Swiss woman involved in Indian rights activism, comes to live near him with her son.

[事实] Mrs Weldon admired Sitting Bull and warned him not to involve himself with the Ghost Dance because it could give authorities a reason to kill him.

[事实] Sitting Bull was interested in the Ghost Dance and presided over dances, but the hosts say he did not have Ghost Dance visions himself.

[事实] His authority had declined, especially compared with Gall, who had moved toward cooperation with reservation authorities and elements of Christian or Western assimilation.

[推测] The hosts suggest Sitting Bull may have embraced the Ghost Dance partly as a way to restore authority against more “progressive” chiefs.

[11:16] **McLaughlin’s hostility and the arrest plan**

[事实] Agent McLaughlin is presented as intensely hostile to Sitting Bull and suspicious of his relationship with Mrs Weldon.

[事实] McLaughlin planned to use Lakota agency police, called the “metal breasts” because of their badges, to arrest Sitting Bull.

[事实] The arrest was originally planned for a ration day, but the timetable was accelerated after word came that Sitting Bull might travel to another reservation.

[事实] Henry Bullhead, a Lakota agency policeman and Christian convert who had a feud connected to Sitting Bull’s circle, was chosen to lead the arrest.

[推测] The episode presents the agency police as men caught in a morally fraught position: Lakota by identity, but instruments of the reservation regime.

[14:52] **The agency police assemble**

[事实] Around 40 Lakota agency policemen gathered at Bullhead’s cabin on the night before the arrest.

[事实] Some were relatives or connections of Sitting Bull, and many did not speak English, so orders had to be translated into Lakota.

[事实] The hosts describe the men as anxious and unable to sleep, telling old war stories before Bullhead led them in Christian prayers before dawn.

[推测] This scene is treated as a symbol of assimilation: former warriors who once resisted the U.S. Army now preparing, under Christian forms, to arrest Sitting Bull.

[16:27] **The arrest turns deadly**

[事实] Before dawn, in freezing rain, the policemen crossed the Grand River and entered Sitting Bull’s cabin while he was in bed.

[事实] Sitting Bull initially appeared likely to go with them, but a crowd gathered outside and tensions rose.

[事实] His teenage son Crowfoot challenged him, saying he had called himself a brave chief but was allowing himself to be taken by the agency police.

[事实] The hosts say this challenge pushed Sitting Bull to refuse to go, after which Catch the Bear shot Henry Bullhead, Bullhead’s gun hit Sitting Bull in the chest, and Red Tomahawk shot Sitting Bull in the head.

[推测] The episode presents Crowfoot’s intervention as tragically decisive because it turned an arrest into a confrontation over courage, status, and humiliation.

[21:00] **Crowfoot’s death and the desecration of Sitting Bull**

[事实] During the chaos after Sitting Bull was shot, Crowfoot was found crawling and begging not to be killed.

[事实] After Bullhead said Crowfoot had caused the trouble, the boy was beaten with rifle butts, shot, and thrown from the cabin.

[事实] The hosts discuss a reported story that Sitting Bull’s horse, possibly given by Buffalo Bill and trained for show business, began dancing during the shooting.

[事实] Sitting Bull’s body was mutilated by agency policemen before U.S. soldiers intervened and told them to stop because he was already dead.

[事实] At burial, the agency policemen received military honors, while Sitting Bull was placed in a rough grave without prayers, service, or memorial.

[推测] The horse story is treated by the hosts as uncertain but symbolically powerful because it fuses frontier violence with entertainment spectacle.

[24:37] **Sitting Bull becomes a spectacle after death**

[事实] The hosts say Sitting Bull’s cabin was later bought, transported to Chicago for the World’s Fair, and presented as the place of his death.

[事实] The cabin then became part of Coney Island’s “Historic Sioux Indian War Village,” with staged ghost dancers, teepees, a Sioux dwarf, and taxidermy.

[事实] The hosts connect this to the broader transformation of the Indian Wars into mass entertainment as U.S. victory became assured.

[推测] The posthumous display of Sitting Bull’s cabin suggests that defeat did not end exploitation; it created new commercial forms for consuming Native history.

[26:43] **The symbolic effect of Sitting Bull’s death**

[事实] The hosts say some Lakota collaborators, including Gall’s circle, may have been relieved by Sitting Bull’s death.

[事实] They also argue that for many Lakota, his death marked another spiritual and cultural rupture, since he symbolized authenticity and resistance.

[事实] The hosts connect this feeling to Baum’s editorial, which treated Sitting Bull’s death as the end of a larger historical era.

[推测] Sitting Bull’s death is presented less as a military event than as the collapse of a living symbol of Lakota independence.

[29:52] **The final segment turns to Wounded Knee**

[事实] After the break, the hosts introduce Wounded Knee as the final confrontation of the 11-part series.

[事实] They refer to Dee Brown’s *Bury My Heart at Wounded Knee* as a transformative book in changing how the conflict between Americans and Native Americans was understood.

[事实] Sitting Bull’s followers fled after his death to Bigfoot, a chief associated with the Ghost Dance but described as moderate and peaceable.

[推测] The episode treats Wounded Knee as both an endpoint of the Plains Wars and a later psychological scar in American historical memory.

[31:48] **Bigfoot’s band and the army’s pursuit**

[事实] General Miles wanted Bigfoot’s village brought in and escorted to a fort so they could be monitored and returned to reservation control.

[事实] Bigfoot’s group, about 300 people, slipped away from a military escort before Christmas because they feared they were being lured into a trap.

[事实] They aimed to reach Pine Ridge, where Red Cloud was, but troops tracked them in freezing conditions near Wounded Knee Creek.

[事实] Bigfoot was suffering from pneumonia, and his people were hungry, ill, ragged, and frightened when they came in.

[推测] Their fear of a trap is presented as understandable in light of what had happened to Crazy Horse and Sitting Bull.

[33:48] **The Seventh Cavalry arrives**

[事实] Army reinforcements arrived, including the Second Battalion of the Seventh Cavalry under Colonel Forsyth.

[事实] The force numbered almost 500 soldiers and included Hotchkiss guns, described as machine-gun-like weapons mounted on carts.

[事实] The hosts say many Seventh Cavalry soldiers were raw recruits raised on stories of Custer, the Little Bighorn, and Indian savagery.

[事实] Lakota survivors later said drunken soldiers asked them whether they had been at the fight with Custer.

[推测] The hosts suggest that the Seventh Cavalry’s desire for revenge and redemption helped make the situation more dangerous.

[36:44] **Disarmament and Black Coyote’s gun**

[事实] The next morning, Forsyth surrounded Bigfoot’s people and demanded their weapons before moving them on.

[事实] The hosts stress that guns were expensive, deeply valued, and connected to male dignity and identity.

[事实] Some Lakota men hid weapons, and soldiers began searching people under blankets.

[事实] Soldiers struggled with Black Coyote, a deaf young warrior who said he had paid a lot for his Winchester and would not give it up without payment.

[事实] During the struggle, the gun fired into the air, and an army scout shouted that the Lakota were shooting.

[推测] The disarmament attempt turned a tense refugee-control operation into a panic because both sides were already primed for fear and humiliation.

[39:28] **The massacre at Wounded Knee**

[事实] After the shot, Forsyth ordered fire, and soldiers began shooting into the surrounded Lakota group.

[事实] Because the soldiers were positioned in a circle, some U.S. soldiers were hit by friendly fire.

[事实] Boys playing leapfrog were killed almost immediately, and Lakota who still had weapons or seized weapons tried to fight back.

[事实] Hotchkiss guns were fired into fleeing people and into a ravine where many had tried to hide.

[事实] Bigfoot was shot in the back, and his daughter was also shot after running to his body.

[事实] The hosts say about 300 of roughly 350 people were killed and placed in a mass grave cut into the frozen ground.

[推测] The hosts frame the violence as a massacre rather than a battle because the victims included starving refugees, women, children, and many people without arms.

[44:26] **Immediate aftermath and official denial**

[事实] One private found two surviving babies and refused a sergeant’s order to smash their heads against a tree.

[事实] General Miles wrote that Wounded Knee was an “abominable criminal military blunder” and a horrible massacre of women and children.

[事实] Early press reports described the Lakota as having attacked first and portrayed the Seventh Cavalry as heroic.

[事实] The New York Tribune argued that Sioux women and boys were also dangerous enemies.

[事实] Colonel Forsyth was cleared, and the Secretary of War praised the discipline, skill, coolness, discretion, and forbearance of the Seventh Cavalry.

[推测] The gap between Miles’s private horror and public praise shows how official and press narratives could convert atrocity into victory.

[48:11] **The Ghost Dance ends and becomes show business**

[事实] After Wounded Knee, the Ghost Dance movement quickly fizzled out.

[事实] Miles used a carrot-and-stick approach: tightening control around reservations while offering food, gifts, leniency, and incentives to those who returned.

[事实] Twenty-three of 27 arrested Ghost Dancers joined Buffalo Bill’s Wild West show in exchange for commuted sentences.

[事实] Kicking Bear and Short Bull, who had helped bring the Ghost Dance to the Lakota, joined the European tour.

[事实] Buffalo Bill advertised performers as prisoners from the “heroic battle at Wounded Knee,” even though the hosts say these men were not there.

[推测] The conversion of Ghost Dance leaders into stage performers is presented as one of the episode’s starkest examples of defeat being absorbed into spectacle.

[50:11] **Kicking Bear in Glasgow and unheard testimony**

[事实] At the final date of the tour in Glasgow, Kicking Bear stayed on stage after the show and spoke to the audience in Lakota.

[事实] The hosts say people think he may have been trying to tell the audience what really happened.

[事实] Nobody in the audience understood him, and nobody knows what he said.

[推测] The anecdote becomes a symbol of Native testimony being present but unintelligible within the entertainment system that framed it.

[50:56] **The frontier closes**

[事实] The hosts discuss Frederick Jackson Turner’s 1893 essay on the significance of the frontier in American history.

[事实] Turner argued that the frontier had closed and with it the first period of American history.

[事实] The hosts say Native Americans were rounded up and largely confined to reservations, while white settlement spread across the Great Plains and the states between the coasts.

[事实] They note that millions of people in the United States still identify as Native American or partly Native American, despite 19th-century expectations of disappearance or full assimilation.

[事实] They also mention continuing poverty, unemployment, alcoholism, opioid addiction, and poor health outcomes in many Native communities.

[推测] The episode suggests that military defeat did not erase Native peoples, but it left long-lasting structural damage.

[52:44] **How memory changed**

[事实] The hosts argue that Native American perspectives now have far more salience than they did when Wounded Knee was described as a glorious victory.

[事实] They discuss Dee Brown, Johnny Cash’s Native-themed music, claims of Indigenous ancestry by public figures, and the modern desire among some descendants of settlers to identify with the displaced.

[事实] They connect modern sympathy partly to identification with victims and partly to the enduring heroic image of the Lakota.

[推测] The hosts suggest that later moral sympathy represents a kind of symbolic victory, though not a material one in land, wealth, or power.

[55:15] **Representation, stereotypes, and modern unease**

[事实] The hosts discuss the Museum of the American Indian’s display of Native American imagery used across U.S. culture and commerce.

[事实] They mention the Washington Redskins football team being renamed the Washington Commanders in 2020.

[事实] They argue that Native American history is less central in public debate than slavery and African American history, despite being deeply significant.

[事实] They discuss tourism in places such as Monument Valley, where Native communities may earn money by performing versions of their ancestral culture.

[推测] The hosts describe this as a “woke Buffalo Bill” dynamic: more sympathetic than older spectacle, but still shaped by outsiders’ desire to see Native people fixed in a historical image.

[58:02] **Final interpretation of the Lakota story**

[事实] The hosts stress that the Lakota were not a timeless static culture; they had their own brief period of expansion and conquest before being overwhelmed by settlers.

[事实] They contrast the Great Plains with Mexico, where sedentary Indigenous societies and cities made cultural fusion with Europeans more possible.

[事实] They argue that the image of the Lakota warrior became the global image of the Native American.

[事实] They say the balance of sympathy in the series lies with those who lost, even while acknowledging the courage of some members of the Seventh Cavalry.

[推测] The episode closes on the idea that the Lakota lost materially and politically, but the tragedy and power of their story continue to shape memory.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s value lies in how it connects event narrative with memory, spectacle, and moral interpretation. It does not treat Sitting Bull’s death and Wounded Knee as isolated incidents, but as the culmination of pressure from settlers, military panic, reservation politics, press hysteria, racial ideology, and the transformation of frontier violence into entertainment.

Its strongest material is the close narrative reconstruction: Sitting Bull’s arrest, Crowfoot’s role, the agency police, the chaos around Black Coyote’s gun, and the killings at Wounded Knee are presented with vivid detail while still acknowledging uncertainty in some accounts.

[推测] A limitation is that the transcript gives the hosts’ historical synthesis rather than a fully documented scholarly apparatus, so listeners wanting source-by-source verification would need to consult the works behind the episode. The hosts also use comic asides and modern analogies, which make the discussion lively but may feel tonally jarring beside the severity of the subject.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in the end of the Plains Wars, the making of American frontier mythology, and how historical violence is remembered, commercialized, and reinterpreted over time.
