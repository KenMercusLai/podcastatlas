# Covering: The Hidden Cost of Hiding Who We Are

## Overview

Why do people soften, edit, or conceal parts of themselves even when those traits are already known? Legal scholar Kenji Yoshino calls this behavior “covering”: downplaying an identity so that others feel more comfortable. Unlike conversion, which attempts to change an identity, or passing, which hides it entirely, covering operates in plain sight.

Through examples including Franklin D. Roosevelt, Margaret Thatcher, Ben Kingsley, Helen Keller, working mothers, immigrants, and Yoshino’s own experience as a gay Japanese American, the episode examines how social pressure shapes self-presentation. Covering can harm self-worth, weaken workplace commitment, and make belonging feel conditional.

The discussion also rejects a simple equation between authenticity and goodness. Some forms of covering support cooperation, empathy, or safety. The crucial questions are whether covering causes harm, whether a legitimate competing value justifies it, and whether people are being pressured to conform merely for others’ comfort.

The episode concludes with practical approaches to “uncovering”: sharing stories, modeling openness, challenging unjustified demands, acting as an ally, and creating psychological safety without pressuring vulnerable people to disclose more than they choose.

## 分段落总结

[00:03] **D.B. Cooper and the identities we disguise**

[事实] The episode opens with the 1971 hijacking attributed to “Dan Cooper,” who demanded $200,000 and four parachutes before disappearing into the night.

[事实] Cooper’s false identity introduces the broader question of why people disguise themselves, although most do so to fit in or be taken seriously rather than to commit crimes.

[事实] The program asks what happens to people themselves when they pretend not to be who they are.

[03:41] **Managing identity in public life**

[事实] Franklin D. Roosevelt’s polio was publicly known, but he carefully minimized the visibility of his disability through staged photographs, seating arrangements, and a specially adapted car.

[事实] Margaret Thatcher received voice coaching that lowered her voice and softened her working-class accent, helping her project authority.

[事实] Actor Ben Kingsley, born Krishna Bhanji, adopted a stage name because he believed his birth name would restrict the roles available to him.

[事实] These figures were not necessarily hiding their identities completely; they were managing how prominently those identities appeared.

[14:12] **Yoshino’s early attempts to change or conceal himself**

[事实] Yoshino realized early that he was gay, but as a teenager he hoped this identity would disappear and dated a girl, a decision he later regretted because of its effects on her.

[事实] He redirected energy into academic achievement and used poetry to express himself without feeling fully exposed.

[事实] While studying in England, he became depressed and prayed for conversion to heterosexuality because he could not imagine having marriage and children as an openly gay man.

[事实] A psychiatrist transformed Yoshino’s thinking by describing his desires not as “perverted” but as “thwarted.”

[18:28] **From conversion to passing**

[事实] After accepting that he was gay, Yoshino stopped trying to change his underlying identity but remained deeply closeted during law school.

[事实] He hesitated to enroll in a sexual-orientation law course because publicly posted class lists might reveal his identity.

[事实] After beginning a relationship, he avoided public affection and concealed his boyfriend, creating tension because his partner felt hidden and devalued.

[事实] Yoshino defines this stage as “passing”: retaining an identity while preventing others from knowing about it.

[20:55] **The professional demand to “be gay, but don’t flaunt it”**

[事实] A trusted colleague advised Yoshino that tenure would be easier if he became a “homosexual professional” rather than a “professional homosexual.”

[事实] The advice meant that he could openly be gay but should avoid centering gay-rights scholarship, litigation, or advocacy in his career.

[事实] Yoshino experienced this as a further demand for identity management after believing that coming out had ended such pressures.

[23:39] **Naming the phenomenon of covering**

[事实] Erving Goffman’s book *Stigma* gave Yoshino the term “covering” for the pressure placed on openly stigmatized people to mute or downplay their identities.

[事实] Yoshino distinguishes three forms of assimilation: conversion changes the underlying identity, passing hides it, and covering allows it to remain visible while reducing its prominence.

[事实] Covering focuses on behavior and is therefore available to everyone, including people whose identities cannot be converted or hidden.

[事实] Yoshino argues that everyone has some outsider identity and is therefore likely to encounter a covering demand.

[29:02] **Covering at work and in appearance**

[事实] Working mothers may reduce the motherhood penalty by avoiding discussion of their children and repeatedly signaling unlimited commitment to work.

[事实] Helen Keller avoided photographs emphasizing her protruding eye and later replaced her eyes with glass eyes, despite being a prominent disability advocate.

[事实] Yoshino emphasizes that these examples should not be used to blame individuals; his concern is the social demand to appear more “normal” in order to receive equal dignity.

[事实] Cosmetic choices such as concealing baldness or gray hair can be forms of covering, but Yoshino says not every form of covering is problematic.

[32:00] **Distinguishing justified and unjustified covering**

[事实] Social rules requiring everyone to restrain obnoxious conduct can be legitimate because they support the functioning of a workplace or community.

[事实] A demand that mothers stop discussing their children to receive promotions is problematic if the same demand is not imposed on fathers.

[事实] Yoshino proposes community values as the standard for distinguishing defensible covering from discriminatory conformity.

[推测] Under this framework, authenticity is important but does not automatically override civility, safety, fairness, or other legitimate shared goods.

[35:38] **Family, advocacy, and the fear of public exposure**

[事实] Yoshino’s parents knew he was gay but worried when a *New York Times* profile was going to identify him publicly as a gay-rights scholar.

[事实] His mother asked why he needed to become a “Joan of Arc,” meaning an activist or standard-bearer rather than simply living privately as a gay man.

[事实] His parents feared that he would receive hatred, threats, and abuse; after becoming a father, Yoshino better understood their protective concern.

[推测] Yoshino believes his father’s experience of anti-Japanese prejudice may have shaped the family’s preference for assimilation, but he explicitly says this is speculation.

[41:42] **Assimilation, inheritance, and the melting pot**

[事实] Yoshino’s father embraced code-switching, seeking to be fully American in the United States and fully Japanese in Japan.

[事实] Yoshino prefers to remain the same person across environments and avoids places where his authentic identity cannot be accommodated.

[事实] He nevertheless credits his father’s assimilation and achievements with creating the security and opportunity that made his own stance possible.

[事实] Yoshino argues that society needs both “bridging” identities that unite people and “bonding” identities rooted in particular communities.

[事实] The melting-pot ideal can be unequal because marginalized groups are usually expected to conform to norms established by dominant groups.

[46:32] **The measurable cost of covering**

[事实] A Deloitte collaboration found that 61% of surveyed respondents reported covering.

[事实] Depending on the type of covering, 60% to 73% of those respondents said it was somewhat to extremely detrimental to their sense of self.

[事实] Another Fortune 500 survey of 3,129 people found that 53% believed leaders expected them to cover; half of that group said the expectation reduced their commitment to the organization.

[事实] These findings connect identity pressure not only to personal distress but also to weaker institutional attachment.

[47:40] **Challenging an honor tied to Earl Warren**

[事实] NYU offered Yoshino a newly endowed Earl Warren Professorship of Constitutional Law as part of its effort to recruit him.

[事实] Yoshino objected because Warren, as California attorney general, helped oversee the internment of more than 100,000 people of Japanese ancestry.

[事实] After researching Warren’s later civil-rights record and regret over internment, the dean revised the title to the “Chief Justice Earl Warren Professorship,” emphasizing Warren’s subsequent growth.

[事实] Yoshino accepted the revised chair because it represented a journey from injustice toward greater understanding rather than erasing the earlier harm.

[53:21] **Covering as a universal experience**

[事实] Deloitte found that 45% of straight white men also reported covering, often around age, socioeconomic background, religion, veteran status, or mental and physical health.

[事实] Yoshino argues that even people with many dominant characteristics usually possess some non-dominant trait they feel pressured to manage.

[事实] Introverts may imitate an extroverted leadership ideal, while people experiencing sadness or depression may hide their condition to appear promising at work.

[事实] Yoshino distinguishes these experiences from identities shaped by long histories of systemic subordination, but still treats them as worthy of attention.

[58:23] **Mental health, addiction, and truthful support**

[事实] Yoshino argues that acknowledging depression without devaluing the person creates better conditions for offering treatment and support than demanding cheerful performance.

[事实] Addiction crosses racial and socioeconomic boundaries but remains heavily stigmatized in professional and social life.

[事实] He frames uncovering as a challenge to stigmas unsupported by morality or sound policy.

[推测] Greater candor may make problems easier to identify and address, while shame may deepen cycles of addiction and depression.

[60:27] **Authenticity as the basis of belonging**

[事实] Yoshino connects covering to the human need for belonging.

[事实] Before coming out, he trusted his parents’ statement “we love you” but did not trust that the “you” referred to his real self.

[事实] He argues that acceptance directed toward a constructed persona cannot produce secure belonging.

[事实] Authentic belonging occurs when a person presents who they truly are and the community responds that they belong as that person.

[63:23] **Distinct and diffuse storytelling**

[事实] “Distinct storytelling” uses a defined personal story to demonstrate what happened when someone resisted a covering demand.

[事实] Yoshino’s objection to the Earl Warren chair is one such story because speaking up replaced anticipated shame with a title he could accept with integrity.

[事实] “Diffuse storytelling” consists of small, ordinary disclosures rather than formal speeches—for example, openly saying that one is leaving work for a child’s school event.

[事实] Such everyday modeling can give colleagues permission to acknowledge their own non-work responsibilities.

[68:06] **Making covering decisions consciously**

[事实] Yoshino recounts choosing not to tell a supermarket cashier that a tabloid was for his husband.

[事实] He concluded that he was protecting his husband’s taste in tabloids rather than concealing his sexual orientation.

[事实] The story illustrates that covering is not always rooted in shame and can sometimes be affectionate or protective.

[事实] Yoshino asks people to bring these rapid decisions into conscious awareness and evaluate whether they reflect their values.

[72:48] **Passing, covering, and compassion**

[事实] Passing prevents others from knowing that a person belongs to a group; covering occurs when the group membership is known but the person is pressured to mute it.

[事实] Only some people can pass with respect to a given identity, while everyone can cover behavioral aspects of identity.

[事实] Survey data showed covering rates of 79% among Black respondents, 83% among LGBTQIA+ respondents, and 45% among straight white men.

[事实] Yoshino treats covering as a non-zero-sum project in which greater authenticity for one group need not diminish authenticity for another.

[76:23] **Imposter syndrome and shared vulnerability**

[事实] Yoshino links imposter syndrome to covering because people may conceal feelings of inadequacy while performing confidence.

[事实] Openly discussing such insecurity often reveals that colleagues have experienced similar feelings, especially early in their careers.

[事实] He argues that communities are healthier when people can discuss vulnerability rather than privately pretending to be invulnerable.

[78:04] **Political identity and polarization**

[事实] A listener describes avoiding political discussion with friends despite viewing politics as central to her identity.

[事实] Yoshino previously believed organizations had a legitimate interest in asking employees to moderate political expression.

[事实] He now increasingly favors finding ways to uncover political identities so that people can communicate across divides and recognize their common humanity.

[推测] Political disclosure remains context-dependent because authenticity can conflict with an organization’s need to maintain cooperation and prevent hostility.

[81:39] **Covering as empathy across power differences**

[事实] A listener describes adjusting which parts of her identity she presents when speaking with people from different socioeconomic backgrounds.

[事实] Yoshino says covering can be an empathetic strategy when self-modulation helps a less powerful person feel safe and welcomed.

[事实] Speaking to a small child at the child’s level illustrates how people routinely suppress differences to create connection.

[事实] Authenticity and empathy are both goods, and a situation may require balancing them rather than treating either as absolute.

[84:25] **A practical test for evaluating covering**

[事实] Yoshino proposes asking first whether covering is occurring and then whether it causes harm.

[事实] If it is harmful, the next question is whether some overriding good justifies it.

[事实] When covering hurts and has no defensible justification, the final question is when and how to challenge the individual or social demand.

[事实] Because the experience is universal, Yoshino believes it can unite people around resisting conformity imposed merely for its own sake.

[88:14] **Authenticity is not a fixed statue**

[事实] A listener asks how to uncover an identity she cannot clearly identify after generations of cultural assimilation.

[事实] Yoshino rejects the idea that authenticity is a fully formed, stable self waiting under a covering; identities are fluid, narrative, and capable of changing.

[事实] He recommends focusing on “covering demands” rather than trying to determine from someone’s behavior whether that person is covering.

[事实] A person need not fully understand their identity to recognize that an environment is inhospitable or that a demand for conformity is unjustified.

[93:03] **Sexual-abuse survivors and the role of allies**

[事实] Disclosure can help survivors receive acknowledgment and support, yet stigmatizing reactions make disclosure unsafe and reinforce covering.

[事实] Yoshino argues that allies can challenge harmful comments without forcing survivors to expose themselves or carry the burden of responding.

[事实] Allies should acknowledge that they are not directly affected, explain why a comment troubled them, and invite reconsideration.

[事实] He favors “calling people in” through constructive intervention rather than merely condemning them.

[96:05] **Covering amid the backlash against DEI**

[事实] The discussion notes that the Trump administration ordered the end of DEI frameworks within federal and federally funded workplaces.

[事实] Yoshino distinguishes presidential authority over federal employees from the more limited reach of executive orders into private workplaces and universities.

[事实] He warns institutions to comply with applicable law without unnecessarily abandoning their values.

[事实] He argues that individual support for marginalized colleagues and challenges to inappropriate covering demands remain legitimate.

[事实] Yoshino suggests that the universal language of covering may reduce the zero-sum framing often associated with DEI by recognizing that everyone has identities they manage.

[102:31] **From melting pot to mosaic**

[事实] Yoshino proposes replacing the melting-pot model with a mosaic or mixed-salad model in which different histories and identities remain visible.

[事实] He argues that greater authenticity is not a scarce resource: one person’s openness need not disadvantage another person.

[事实] A Colombian immigrant describes trying to eliminate her accent to avoid being stereotyped as a drug dealer.

[事实] Yoshino says learning accent-free English is not inherently wrong, but the underlying stereotype is the illegitimate covering demand that needs to be challenged.

[105:04] **Creating safety without forcing disclosure**

[事实] Yoshino strongly rejects confronting others with claims that they are being inauthentic.

[事实] Drawing on Amy Edmondson’s work, he defines psychological safety as a climate of mutual respect and interpersonal trust in which people feel comfortable being themselves.

[事实] He compares encouraging disclosure to inviting a bird to eat from one’s hand: the task is to create safety, not to force the bird.

[事实] Leaders and colleagues can foster uncovering by sharing their own stories and welcoming personal experiences without demanding that anyone disclose.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s strongest contribution is its precise distinction among conversion, passing, and covering. That vocabulary makes subtle pressures visible: formal inclusion may exist while people are still expected to minimize disability, family responsibilities, class background, sexuality, ethnicity, mental health, or temperament.

Its breadth is another major strength. Historical examples, Yoshino’s personal narrative, listener questions, workplace studies, and practical decision tests show that covering is neither confined to one marginalized group nor reducible to a slogan about unrestricted authenticity. The insistence that some covering can be courteous, protective, or empathetic gives the discussion useful moral nuance.

A limitation is that the episode combines very different identities and pressures under one framework. Yoshino explicitly acknowledges that introversion, race, sexuality, disability, and other traits do not carry identical histories or consequences, but the universal framing can still obscure major differences in power and risk. Some legal and political claims in the listener-question segment are also presented conversationally rather than examined in depth.

The episode is especially valuable for managers, educators, workplace-culture practitioners, members of marginalized communities, and anyone who has wondered whether acceptance still requires performing a more socially comfortable version of themselves. Its central lesson is practical: do not force disclosure, but examine unjustified demands, model openness, support allies, and build environments where belonging attaches to the actual person rather than a carefully edited substitute.
