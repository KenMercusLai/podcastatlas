# How to Avoid History

## 概览

The episode discusses how history can be written for children: what makes it engaging, where narrative and facts matter, and how writers should handle violence, atrocity, moral complexity, and myth.

Dominic Sandbrook explains the origins of his *Adventures in Time* books on the Second World War and the six wives of Henry VIII, including how his son’s reactions shaped the writing. The hosts argue that children often respond strongly to story, vivid characters, danger, strange details, and memorable incidents.

The discussion also examines the problems of simplifying history: older children’s histories often reflected national myths, imperial adventure, and heroic framing, while newer approaches need to account for multiple perspectives without losing narrative momentum.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Nigel Molesworth and the problem of history for children**

[事实] The episode opens with a comic passage from Nigel Molesworth about enduring history lessons.
[事实] The hosts introduce the topic as the challenge of writing history for children and young adults.
[事实] Dominic says the episode is partly self-promotion because two of his children’s history books are being published.

[02:06] **Starting with Nazis and Tudors**

[事实] Dominic’s first two *Adventures in Time* books are about the Second World War and the six wives of Henry VIII.
[事实] He says the publishers suggested beginning with “Nazis and Tudors,” rather than less obviously marketable subjects such as the Industrial Revolution or the Seven Years’ War.
[推测] The choice reflects a belief that familiar, dramatic topics are easier entry points for young readers.

[03:04] **Writing Hitler for children**

[事实] Dominic originally began the Second World War book with Hitler as a boy and as a First World War soldier.
[事实] He wanted to show Hitler’s trajectory into darkness rather than present him as a monster from the beginning.
[事实] Dominic removed a detail about Hitler having a dog after his son said readers might think Hitler was the hero.
[推测] The anecdote shows how small narrative signals can strongly shape a child’s moral reading of a story.

[04:39] **The origin of the Second World War book**

[事实] Dominic’s son studied evacuees at school and wanted more about tanks, battles, and the larger narrative of the war.
[事实] A visit to the Imperial War Museum made Dominic notice a lack of a “rollicking narrative” history of the Second World War for children.
[事实] He tested chapters on his son, asking what was boring or unclear.
[事实] His son’s question about the word “economy” forced Dominic to rethink how to explain adult concepts to children.

[07:51] **Older children’s history books**

[事实] The hosts discuss Ladybird history books, which Dominic remembers as short, narrative-driven introductions to British history.
[事实] Tom says he was less attached to Ladybird books and preferred a world history by Plantagenet Somerset Fry.
[事实] Tom liked that Fry’s book focused on ancient history, Sumeria, the New Kingdom, medieval China, and the Roman Empire.
[推测] Their memories show two different gateways into history: national narrative and exotic ancient worlds.

[11:20] **Story as the hook**

[事实] Dominic argues that children become interested in history through unfamiliar worlds and through story.
[事实] He says children need narrative drama, jeopardy, and character.
[事实] Tom says he felt disappointed when a book promising Julius Caesar turned into a broader account of Roman Britain.
[推测] Both hosts treat momentum and expectation as central to making history work for younger readers.

[14:04] **Children, facts, and gruesome details**

[事实] Dominic says children like strong characters, pace, tension, and also strange facts.
[事实] He describes including details about Henry VIII’s medical treatments, which children who read the draft found especially memorable.
[事实] The hosts connect this appetite for grotesque facts to the appeal of *Horrible Histories*.
[事实] Tom recalls Peter Connolly’s illustrated books on ancient armies, which combined narrative with detailed information about weapons and battles.

[17:20] **Handling violence and the Holocaust**

[事实] A listener asks how writers should approach genocide, torture, and warfare for a broad child audience.
[事实] Dominic says the Holocaust required a whole chapter in his Second World War book.
[事实] He framed the chapter around Anne Frank’s experience.
[事实] He says the facts should be told plainly and starkly, without exploitative or excessive violence.

[20:00] **Finding light within dark history**

[事实] Dominic included stories of people who saved Jews, including Nicholas Winton and rescuers in Denmark.
[事实] He describes the Danish rescue of Jewish people to Sweden as especially moving.
[事实] He tells a story of a student who helped reunite a young girl with her parents before they escaped by boat.
[推测] Dominic uses rescue stories not to soften the Holocaust, but to give children individual moments through which they can understand moral action.

[23:03] **Violence at a historical distance**

[事实] After the break, Tom discusses *Asterix* and the way it presents Roman Gaul without real death.
[事实] He contrasts this with Plutarch’s claim that Caesar killed and enslaved huge numbers of Gauls.
[事实] Tom says ancient history felt exciting, glamorous, violent, and safe to him as a child because it was distant.
[事实] Dominic agrees that both children and adults often feel less troubled by violence the further back it is in time.

[26:39] **Children’s stories and violence**

[事实] Dominic compares historical violence with deaths in *Star Wars* and Tolkien, where large numbers die without necessarily producing deep pity.
[事实] He says many boys enjoy violence in stories and play.
[推测] The hosts suggest that historical distance and fictional framing can make violence feel like adventure rather than suffering.

[28:01] **Revision, perspective, and imperial history**

[事实] Listener questions raise whether children’s histories will focus more on disadvantaged groups and what children teach adults about historical terms.
[事实] Tom notes that a Ladybird illustration of Walter Raleigh changed between editions, shifting the perspective from Raleigh to the people seeing him arrive.
[事实] The hosts say imperial history was often framed as British adventurers going to exotic places.
[事实] Dominic says a future book on the Conquistadors would need both the voyage of the Spaniards and the perspective of the people they encountered.

[31:46] **National myths and children’s history**

[事实] The hosts discuss *Our Island Story* as shorthand for a style of children’s history rooted in British achievement.
[事实] Dominic recalls an old history book with repeated chapters called “Great Men,” focused on male figures.
[事实] Tom argues that making history accessible to children often involves mythologizing it to some degree.
[事实] Dominic says history books always reflect their times.

[33:57] **Bunk, debunking, and Dunkirk**

[事实] Dominic says some Ladybird stories about Oliver Cromwell begin with claims that are not true, including Cromwell being stolen by a monkey and fighting Charles I as a child.
[事实] He argues that such stories can interest children, with time later for debunking.
[事实] In his Dunkirk chapter, Dominic chose to focus on the famous “little ships” story rather than immediately correcting the myth by emphasizing destroyers.
[推测] Dominic prioritizes narrative entry points first, while accepting that historical correction can come later.

[36:01] **Is children’s history propaganda?**

[事实] A listener asks whether children’s history is always propaganda.
[事实] Dominic says no, because propaganda allows only one interpretation.
[事实] He argues that children’s history can include hints of disagreement, uncertainty, and debate.
[事实] He also says children are generally not interested in historiography or sources and want the story to move forward.

[37:34] **Henry VIII and unresolved judgement**

[事实] Tom reads a passage from Dominic’s Henry VIII book asking whether Henry was a fun-loving boy, a brooding invalid, a sportsman, an art lover, a bully, a strong ruler, or a selfish tyrant.
[事实] Dominic does not give a final answer in the moment, leaving the question open.
[推测] The passage illustrates Dominic’s preferred balance: a strong story with enough ambiguity to avoid simple moral instruction.

[38:26] **What history should schools teach?**

[事实] The hosts briefly address a listener’s question about what history should be taught in schools.
[事实] Tom jokingly suggests Christianity, the Roman Republic, and Persia.
[事实] Dominic jokes about the 1970s and 1980s before saying children should study the Romans, the 17th century, and the 20th century.
[事实] They conclude that history and education deserves a whole separate episode.

[40:56] **Final plug and dedication**

[事实] Tom again mentions Dominic’s *Adventures in Time* books on the six wives of Henry VIII and the Second World War.
[事实] He reads from Dominic’s dedication to his son, who marked battles, massacres, severed heads, hangings, drawings, and quarterings while reading drafts.
[推测] The closing joke reinforces the episode’s recurring claim that children can be enthusiastic, demanding, and sometimes bloodthirsty readers of history.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode is valuable because it treats children’s history as a serious craft problem rather than a simplified version of adult history. The strongest parts are the practical writing examples: Hitler’s dog, Anne Frank, Danish rescuers, Dunkirk, Henry VIII’s medical details, and the question of whose eyes a story is told through.

Its main insight is that children need story, but story is never neutral. The hosts are alert to the way narrative can create heroes, villains, myths, and emotional distance, while still arguing that children should not be denied drama, danger, and memorable detail.

The limitation is that the discussion is largely shaped by British childhood reading, British historical memory, and Dominic’s own publishing project. [推测] Listeners looking for a systematic account of classroom history, curriculum design, or non-British children’s history publishing may find the episode suggestive rather than comprehensive.

[推测] The episode is especially suitable for listeners interested in public history, history education, children’s books, narrative nonfiction, or the ethics of explaining violence and atrocity to younger audiences.
