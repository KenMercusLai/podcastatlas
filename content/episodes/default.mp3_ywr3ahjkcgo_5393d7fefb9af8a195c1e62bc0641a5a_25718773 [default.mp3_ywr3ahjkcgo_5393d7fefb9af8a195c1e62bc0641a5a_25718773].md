# How Yemeni Coffeehouses Built on an Immigrant Business Tradition

## Overview

Yemeni coffeehouses have rapidly expanded across the United States, meeting demand for late-night, family-friendly gathering places—especially among Muslim and Arab American communities. Their growth also draws on Yemen’s historic connection to coffee and the business experience Yemeni Americans developed through decades of running neighborhood bodegas.

Through Brooklyn entrepreneur Hakeem Soleimani and his father, Tarek, the episode shows how immigrant communities create industry clusters. Family ties provide newcomers with training, practical knowledge, financing, business contacts, and employment—advantages that can make specialization financially rewarding.

Hakeem’s story illustrates how a second generation can transform rather than simply inherit that playbook. He combined his family’s community network and retail knowledge with specialty coffee, Yemeni sourcing, and a modern café concept, ultimately building a profitable business despite skepticism and severe import challenges.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **The Rise of Yemeni Coffeehouses**

[事实] Hundreds of Yemeni coffeehouses have opened across the United States in roughly nine years, including chains such as Qahwah House and Haraz Coffee House.

[事实] Many stay open late, serve distinctive pastries and pots of coffee, and let customers gather for hours.

[事实] One original goal was to provide Muslim and Arab American communities with a family-friendly “third space” that did not revolve around alcohol.

[推测] Their appeal near colleges and among broader audiences suggests that these cafés are filling some of the social role once strongly associated with Starbucks.

[03:08] **From Bodegas to a New Business Generation**

[事实] Yemeni Americans have a long entrepreneurial history in the United States, especially in corner stores, delis, and bodegas.

[事实] Hakeem opened Yaffa Cafe in Brooklyn in 2019 after growing up around the bodega operated by his father.

[事实] The episode places Yemeni coffeehouses within a wider pattern of immigrant specialization, alongside Vietnamese-owned nail salons, Gujarati-owned motels, and Greek-owned diners.

[推测] The coffeehouse boom represents a generational adaptation of an established immigrant business model rather than a complete break from it.

[05:23] **One Brooklyn Block, Two Generations of Enterprise**

[事实] Hakeem’s Yaffa Cafe is three doors from the Yaffa Deli his father has operated since 1995; another family-run Yemeni bodega sits across the street.

[事实] All three businesses benefit from their locations beside subway entrances and the resulting foot traffic.

[事实] Hakeem sells specialty drinks such as spiced cold brew and cardamom brown-sugar lattes, while his father serves simpler, less expensive coffee at the deli.

[推测] The businesses’ proximity makes the family’s economic evolution visible in a single city block: traditional convenience retail sits alongside a higher-margin specialty café.

[07:31] **The Labor Behind the Bodega Model**

[事实] Hakeem’s father, Tarek, commonly works 12- or 13-hour days and says he rarely sits because he is always serving customers or completing another task.

[事实] Tarek left Yemen during the mid-1990s amid civil war and economic collapse after his father urged him to seek a better future.

[事实] His brother had arrived earlier and gave him an immediate path into the corner-store business.

[事实] Tarek initially spoke no English and learned much of the language, along with the work itself, on the job.

[08:46] **Family Networks as Training and Finance**

[事实] Yemeni relatives helped one another learn how to operate American corner stores, passing practical knowledge among fathers, in-laws, siblings, and cousins.

[事实] Tarek lacked the credit history needed for conventional bank financing when he wanted to open his own store.

[事实] His father-in-law, brother, and cousins supplied informal, interest-free loans consistent with their Muslim customs.

[事实] The Yemeni American Merchants Association estimates that Yemenis operate nearly half of New York City’s bodegas—about 5,000 stores—and also run thousands of small groceries elsewhere in the country.

[10:36] **Why Immigrant Groups Cluster in Particular Industries**

[事实] Economist Bill Kerr studied nearly 40 years of census data to compare industry concentration among 131 immigrant groups.

[事实] His research ranked immigrants from Nepal, Senegal, Zimbabwe, and Yemen among those most likely to concentrate in a particular industry, while immigrants from Cuba, Canada, Germany, and Poland were among the least concentrated.

[事实] The research identified two major correlates of clustering: a relatively small immigrant population and strong social ties within the community.

[事实] Some clusters can begin for contingent reasons, illustrated by actress Tippi Hedren arranging manicure training for Vietnamese refugees in California in 1975.

[13:08] **Social Ties as an Economic Advantage**

[事实] Kerr uses the rate of marriage within an immigrant community as one proxy for social connectedness, alongside shared language and regular social interaction.

[事实] Community gatherings allow entrepreneurs to exchange knowledge about suppliers, products, regulations, and other matters that may not be taught formally.

[事实] Kerr found that immigrants participating in their community’s self-employment cluster generally earned more than comparable people working independently in another field or in wage employment.

[推测] Dense social networks function as a form of business infrastructure, reducing the costs and risks of acquiring knowledge, capital, and trusted partners.

[16:42] **The Costs and Rewards of Specialization**

[事实] Clustered businesses may still involve long hours, thin profit margins, and physically demanding work, as in grocery stores and bodegas.

[事实] Hakeem developed his love of coffee at his father’s deli but belongs to a generation more familiar with specialty coffee, flavored syrups, and non-dairy milk.

[推测] Higher earnings within an ethnic business cluster do not eliminate difficult working conditions; they instead help explain why people may rationally accept those conditions.

[18:49] **What the Second Generation Does Differently**

[事实] Research cited in the episode indicates that second-generation immigrants generally leave their parents’ businesses for less punishing professions.

[事实] Some instead build on the family industry, as seen when children of Gujarati motel owners expand into hotel chains or boutique properties.

[事实] Hakeem worked in his father’s bodega as a teenager and during college but initially pursued freelance photography.

[事实] His difficulty finding a neighborhood café where he could edit photographs inspired him to consider opening one himself.

[20:19] **Turning Yemeni Coffee Heritage into a Business**

[事实] Hakeem and his cousin developed their café idea while learning about Yemen’s centuries-old coffee tradition.

[事实] Dave Eggers’s book *The Monk of Mocha*, about a Yemeni American importing high-end Yemeni coffee, helped convince them that the concept was possible.

[事实] They taught themselves more about coffee, including how to make pour-over coffee, and began searching for a location.

[推测] Yemeni coffee offered Hakeem a way to combine a modern specialty product with cultural identity and a direct connection to his family’s country of origin.

[21:14] **Skepticism, Location, and Inherited Connections**

[事实] Tarek initially doubted that customers would pay more than two dollars for coffee because he did not frequent specialty cafés himself.

[事实] A landlord known through Tarek’s deli offered a nearby former takeout space, giving Hakeem a site with subway access, heavy foot traffic, and proximity to the family store.

[事实] Hakeem drew on the same local community that had watched him grow up and used his father’s business relationships to secure the lease.

[事实] Relatives in Yemen helped identify farms, inspect fields, speak with owners, and source coffee beans.

[推测] Hakeem’s independent venture depended heavily on social capital accumulated by the previous generation.

[23:09] **Importing Coffee from a Country at War**

[事实] The first beans were transported from Yemen in a 50-pound suitcase carried by Hakeem’s father, and the supply sold out within a month.

[事实] Establishing regular imports was difficult because Yemen was at war in 2019, with its main airport and several ports closed.

[事实] The business hired someone to move coffee to Yemen’s northern border and then relied on specialists to carry it into Saudi Arabia.

[事实] Sanctions and a disrupted banking system complicated payments to farmers; a PayPal attempt failed, and the business ultimately used Western Union.

[推测] Hakeem’s sourcing strategy strengthened the café’s cultural authenticity but exposed the business to unusually severe logistical and financial risks.

[24:16] **Profitability, Expansion, and Family Acceptance**

[事实] Hakeem eventually made the coffee business profitable, opened a second shop, and began adding a full kitchen and dinner menu to the original location.

[事实] Tarek, once the idea’s most skeptical observer, now gets a cappuccino from his son’s café every day.

[事实] Hakeem never charges his father for coffee.

[推测] Tarek’s daily cappuccino provides a symbolic resolution: the older generation’s labor and connections enabled a business model it initially struggled to understand.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s strongest feature is its combination of an intimate family story with a broader economic explanation. The neighboring café and deli make abstract ideas such as industry clustering, social capital, and intergenerational mobility concrete and memorable.

It also avoids treating immigrant specialization solely as a response to exclusion. The research presented suggests that community clusters can create measurable financial advantages by circulating capital, skills, contacts, and informal knowledge.

[推测] The episode gives less attention to potential downsides such as competitive saturation, unequal obligations within family networks, or whether rapid chain expansion could weaken the community character that made Yemeni coffeehouses distinctive.

[推测] It is especially valuable for listeners interested in immigrant entrepreneurship, labor economics, family businesses, specialty coffee, and the ways second-generation entrepreneurs reshape inherited opportunities.
