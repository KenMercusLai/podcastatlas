# Suetonius and The Lives of the Caesars

## 概览

This episode introduces Suetonius’s *Lives of the Caesars* through Tom’s new Penguin Classics translation, opening with Suetonius’s dramatic account of Nero’s death on June 9, 68.

The discussion presents the *Lives* as both a record of Rome’s shift from republic to autocracy and a source of many enduring images of Roman imperial power: assassination, sexual scandal, dynastic rivalry, public performance, omens, and grotesque personal detail.

A central theme is that Suetonius makes the Caesars feel unusually vivid. The hosts argue that his mix of archival research, court proximity, moral judgment, gossip, and fascination with bodily detail has shaped not only modern views of Rome, but also later drama from *I, Claudius* to *The Sopranos* and *Game of Thrones*.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Nero’s Death and the New Translation**

[事实] The episode opens with Suetonius’s account of Nero preparing for death, learning the Senate had declared him a public enemy, and finally killing himself with help from his secretary Epaphroditus.

[事实] The passage is identified as describing Nero’s death on June 9, 68.

[事实] The hosts explain that the passage comes from Tom’s new Penguin Classics translation of Suetonius’s *Lives of the Caesars*.

[02:54] **The Range of the Twelve Caesars**

[事实] Suetonius’s biographies run from Julius Caesar through Augustus, Tiberius, Caligula, Claudius, Nero, the four emperors of 69, Vespasian, Titus, and Domitian.

[事实] Nero’s death marks the end of Augustus’s family line, followed by civil war and the rapid succession of Galba, Otho, Vitellius, and Vespasian.

[事实] The hosts frame the collection as the story of Rome’s movement from a republican system to a far more autocratic imperial order.

[05:21] **Why Suetonius Feels So Vivid**

[事实] The hosts say many famous stories about Roman emperors, especially stories of sex and violence, come largely from Suetonius.

[事实] Suetonius is described as interested not only in deaths and scandals, but also in tastes, habits, food, drink, marriage, divorce, jokes, exercise, and bodily behavior.

[推测] The appeal of the biographies lies partly in their refusal to separate political power from everyday human detail.

[08:12] **Sex, Scandal, and Suetonius’s Reputation**

[事实] The episode stresses that Suetonius is especially notorious for his treatment of the Caesars’ sex lives.

[事实] The hosts mention later readers and writers, including Claudian, Einhard, Gilles de Rais, the Marquis de Sade, and Victorian schoolboys, as part of Suetonius’s long and ambivalent reception.

[事实] Tom says his translation does not replace explicit passages with asterisks.

[推测] The discussion presents Suetonius as both a source for political instruction and a text capable of shocking or titillating readers.

[12:26] **From Robert Graves to Modern Television**

[事实] Robert Graves translated the previous Penguin Classics edition and used Suetonius as raw material for *I, Claudius* and *Claudius the God*.

[事实] The hosts connect *I, Claudius* to the BBC television series of the 1970s.

[事实] They also discuss claimed links from Suetonius and *I, Claudius* to later dramas such as *Dynasty*, *The Sopranos*, and *Game of Thrones*.

[推测] The episode suggests that Suetonius helped create a durable template for stories about families, power, murder, sex, and succession.

[16:22] **Augustus as Rome’s Great Actor**

[事实] Dominic highlights Suetonius’s deathbed story in which Augustus asks whether he has played his part in life’s comedy well.

[事实] Tom says Augustus is central to the collection and serves as the model of how to be a Caesar.

[事实] Augustus is described as moving through different names and roles: Gaius Octavius, Julius Caesar, and Augustus.

[推测] The hosts treat Augustus’s political genius as inseparable from performance, persona, and timing.

[18:41] **Violence, Peace, and Divinity**

[事实] Suetonius presents Augustus as capable of extreme violence while avenging Julius Caesar.

[事实] Tom says Suetonius ultimately admires Augustus because he gives the Roman people peace and comes to function as their father.

[事实] The name Augustus is explained as meaning something more than human and close to divine.

[事实] Suetonius includes omens and portents around Augustus and other Caesars, including stories about Julius Caesar crossing the Rubicon and Caesar’s ghost.

[20:51] **Politics as Show Business**

[事实] The hosts compare Suetonius’s interest in political performance to modern politicians and public display.

[事实] Roman emperors are described as expected to appear before the people, stage spectacles, and expose themselves to public approval or disapproval.

[事实] Suetonius’s interest in games, gladiatorial combats, and beast hunts is linked to the emperor’s need to put on a successful show.

[推测] The episode implies that popularity in imperial Rome was tested theatrically as well as institutionally.

[22:10] **After Nero and the Problem of Succession**

[事实] The hosts describe later emperors as struggling to fill Augustus’s shoes.

[事实] Galba is presented as lacking a link to Augustus’s divine family and relying instead on military backing.

[事实] Vespasian founds a new dynasty and is succeeded by Titus and Domitian.

[事实] Tom says the later biographies after Nero feel slighter and less awesome than those of the Julio-Claudian emperors.

[24:55] **Domitian and the End of the Collection**

[事实] Domitian is said to demand the title “master” and to be assassinated in a palace struggle.

[事实] Suetonius ends Domitian’s life by suggesting that happier and more moderate times followed his death.

[事实] The hosts say Suetonius writes from an age of measured and moderate emperors.

[推测] The ending casts the first imperial age as terrifying, theatrical, and morally charged, but also as something already receding into history.

[27:56] **Suetonius as Scholar and Court Insider**

[事实] The episode quotes Suetonius recalling a small bust of Augustus marked “Thurinus” that he gave to Emperor Hadrian.

[事实] Tom uses this anecdote to show Suetonius’s method: he gathers evidence and argues with earlier claims.

[事实] The anecdote also shows Suetonius’s closeness to imperial power, since he could give an object to Hadrian.

[推测] Suetonius’s authority in the episode rests on a combination of scholarly habits and insider access.

[30:01] **Suetonius’s Background**

[事实] Suetonius gives scattered personal references: his grandfather saw Caligula’s bridge of boats, and his father was in Otho’s camp during the civil war.

[事实] He also says he witnessed an old man inspected in court during Domitian’s enforcement of a tax on Judeans.

[事实] An inscription found in 1952 at Hippo Regius is presented as suggesting that Suetonius’s family may have had North African origins.

[事实] Suetonius was connected with Pliny the Younger’s literary circle.

[32:40] **Career, Britain, and Dismissal**

[事实] Pliny the Younger seems to have obtained a post in Britain for Suetonius, which Suetonius turned down.

[事实] A Vindolanda letter from someone called Tranquilus is mentioned as possibly connected to Suetonius, though this is not confirmed.

[事实] Suetonius worked in the imperial archives and libraries and later became Hadrian’s senior secretary handling correspondence.

[事实] He and Septicius Clarus were dismissed after a mysterious scandal involving Hadrian’s wife Sabina.

[36:41] **Sex as Moral Evidence**

[事实] Tom says Suetonius had an unusually broad, almost anthropological interest in sex and many other subjects.

[事实] Suetonius also wrote on courtesans, children’s games, insults, dress, and public spectacles.

[事实] The hosts say Romans did not separate public and private life in the modern way.

[事实] A craving for privacy could be interpreted by Romans as evidence that someone was hiding shameful behavior.

[40:03] **Tiberius, Caligula, and Gossip**

[事实] The hosts say later episodes will explore how much Suetonius’s sexual allegations about Tiberius and Caligula can be trusted.

[事实] Tiberius’s retreat to Capri is presented as inviting Roman suspicion because privacy itself seemed morally suspicious.

[事实] Caligula is presented as the opposite extreme: an emperor said to have publicly paraded shocking behavior.

[推测] The episode treats gossip as historically useful even when its literal truth is doubtful, because it reveals Roman assumptions about power and morality.

[42:29] **Roman Sexual Categories and Power**

[事实] Tom says Romans did not think in modern categories such as heterosexual and homosexual.

[事实] Suetonius may record whether an emperor preferred women or men, but Tom says he treats this as incidental rather than central to identity.

[事实] The Roman moral issue was not gender preference, but whether a powerful man dominated others or allowed himself to be dominated.

[事实] The hosts describe Roman sexual hierarchy as deeply alien and unsettling from a modern perspective.

[45:18] **Shame, Masculinity, and Julius Caesar**

[事实] Suetonius says two men accused after a failed coup against Domitian were spared because they were viewed as sexually debased and therefore politically insignificant.

[事实] Julius Caesar was followed throughout his life by allegations about his youthful relationship with the king of Bithynia.

[事实] The hosts say Caesar’s many adulteries mattered less to Roman critics than the suggestion that he had once taken a submissive role.

[推测] The episode uses Caesar’s reputation to show how durable and damaging sexual shame could be in Roman elite culture.

[47:38] **The Alien and the Intimate**

[事实] Tom says Suetonius’s biographies combine the very alien with the very intimate.

[事实] Examples include grotesque stories about Nero and Domitian alongside details about Augustus’s teeth, Tiberius’s hair, Otho’s appearance, and Vespasian’s expression.

[事实] The episode closes by introducing upcoming episodes on Tiberius, Caligula, and Claudius.

[推测] The lasting power of Suetonius comes from making imperial Rome feel both disturbingly strange and physically close.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] This episode works best as an entry point into Suetonius rather than as a full biography-by-biography treatment. Its strength is the way it connects literary style, imperial politics, sexual morality, and later popular culture.

[事实] The hosts repeatedly emphasize that Suetonius is not just a collector of scandal: he is presented as a scholar, a moral writer, and someone with access to the machinery of imperial power.

[推测] The main limitation is that many of the most sensational claims are introduced as problems to be explored later, especially in relation to Tiberius and Caligula, so this episode deliberately leaves some questions unresolved.

[推测] It is especially suited to listeners interested in Roman emperors, political performance, ancient biography, and the long afterlife of Roman scandal in modern storytelling.
