# Empire: What Is It and Can It Ever Be Good?

## Overview

This episode of *The Rest is History* asks what empire is, where it comes from, why it has been such a persistent form of political organization, and whether it can ever be judged as beneficial rather than simply oppressive.

The discussion moves from the Roman meaning of *imperium* to early Mesopotamian, Egyptian, Persian, Greek, Roman, Arab, Ottoman, Chinese, Russian, British and American examples. A central conclusion is that empires are not only systems of violence and domination; they also create order, identity, infrastructure, culture, moral frameworks and sometimes the very standards by which they are later condemned.

The hosts repeatedly stress the paradoxes of empire: Western anti-imperialism itself draws on biblical, Greek and Roman traditions; decolonization often spreads European assumptions about nation-states; and modern powers such as China, Russia and the United States can still be interpreted as imperial even when they do not use the word.

## Segment Summary

[00:10] **Defining Empire Through Rome**

[事实] The episode begins by asking what empire is, what it is for, where it comes from, and whether it can ever be morally defensible.
[事实] Tom explains that the word “empire” comes from Latin *imperium*, meaning both command and the right to command.
[事实] Under Augustus, *imperium* came to imply not only authority but also the physical territory under Roman control.
[事实] The hosts identify two key features of empire: a dominant center or metropole, and peripheral regions subject to its power.

[03:01] **Early Civilizations and Imperiogenesis**

[事实] Dominic asks whether the earliest civilizations, including Egypt, the Indus Valley and Mesopotamia, often began as empires rather than nation-states.
[事实] Tom distinguishes Egypt, which he says resembled a unified kingdom with shared language, gods and ruler, from Mesopotamia, where rival city-states required walls and militarism.
[事实] Sargon of Akkad is presented as an early figure in the tradition of conquest that later leads to Assyria, Babylon and Persia.
[事实] Assyria is described as a brutal empire built on terror, deportation and spectacular violence.

[06:37] **Persia and the Moral Justification of Empire**

[事实] Tom argues that Assyria’s reliance on terror was insufficient for long-term imperial stability.
[事实] The Persian Empire is presented as a major innovation because it identified imperial rule with truth, light and moral virtue.
[事实] Dominic notes that Persia also offered roads, weights and measures, currency, infrastructure and order.
[推测] The discussion suggests that successful empires often survive by persuading subjects that imperial rule is preferable to disorder.

[09:02] **The Bible, Cyrus and Empire as Deliverance**

[事实] Tom says the Near East had been ruled by empires for much of its history, and that the Old Testament preserves the experience of small peoples being crushed by Egypt, Assyria and Babylon.
[事实] Cyrus the Great is described as a major exception because he allowed deported peoples to return home.
[事实] The people who became the Jews are said to have remembered the Persians favorably because they enabled the rebuilding of Jerusalem and the Temple.
[事实] This example complicates the idea that all empires were experienced only as oppressive.

[10:38] **Greek Culture, Rome and Imperial Identity**

[事实] Dominic raises Alexander’s conquest of Persia and asks whether many Persian subjects may have seen Macedonian “liberation” as a nightmare.
[事实] Tom says Greek rule had shallower roots than Persian rule, but Greek culture became influential because of its glamour and sophistication.
[事实] Rome is described as brutally militaristic, yet able to sell the identity of *Romanitas* so effectively that many conquered peoples came to identify as Roman.
[事实] By the third century, all male adults across the Roman Empire received citizenship.

[13:17] **Empire Versus the Alternatives**

[事实] The hosts ask what most people lived in if they did not live in an empire.
[事实] Tom says that for much of Eurasian history, living outside empire often meant living as a nomad or “barbarian” on the fringes of great powers.
[事实] China is discussed as an example where empire could be seen as the natural state of things by both subjects and invaders.
[事实] Western Europe is presented as unusual because the Western Roman Empire fragmented and was never durably reconstituted.

[15:26] **Nation-States as a Modern Assumption**

[事实] Dominic argues that modern people often assume the nation-state is normal, but that this was not an available model for most of history.
[事实] He says people after Rome’s fall did not imagine modern nations such as Spain or Belgium, but often experienced the collapse as a loss of order, trade, status and law.
[事实] Tom counters that some lower-status people may have experienced the end of Roman rule as liberation from oppressive taxation.
[推测] The exchange frames empire as a recurring tradeoff between order and coercion.

[17:47] **Biblical and Classical Anti-Imperial Traditions**

[事实] Tom says Western attitudes to empire inherit two major traditions: the Bible and the classical world.
[事实] In the Bible, empires are often portrayed negatively, with Pharaoh, Assyria, Babylon and Rome appearing as oppressive powers.
[事实] The Book of Revelation is described as one of the most influential works of anti-imperial propaganda.
[事实] Tom also says Greek memories of resisting Persia helped create a tradition of liberty against empire.

[19:22] **Rome as Both Model and Warning**

[事实] Dominic notes that Britain and other European empires modeled themselves partly on Rome.
[事实] Tom agrees but says the classical inheritance is ambivalent because Greeks celebrated resistance to Persia while later practicing imperialism themselves.
[事实] The Athenians and Spartans are described as having pursued empire after defeating the Persians.
[事实] Roman writers such as Tacitus also portrayed empire as morally corrupting.

[23:54] **Is Empire Always Bad?**

[事实] After the break, Tom answers that empire is not always bad because empires often create the standards by which they are judged.
[事实] He gives the Arab Empire as an example, arguing that later Muslim subjects could interpret conquest as part of God’s will.
[事实] Dominic argues that dismissing long-lasting empires such as the Arab or Ottoman Empire as simply evil is too simplistic.
[事实] The Byzantine Romans are described as people conquered by Rome who later identified Roman identity with God’s purpose.

[26:21] **Empire as a Historical Norm**

[事实] Dominic cites John Darwin’s *After Tamerlane* to argue that empire has often been the natural unit of human organization.
[事实] He says powerful and wealthy states have frequently expressed influence through empire, including Venice, Portugal and Belgium.
[事实] Tom partly disagrees, arguing that Darwin’s focus is largely Eurasian and that Western Europe developed differently after Rome’s collapse.
[事实] Western Europe is described as having a hardwired assumption of competing states rather than durable imperial unity.

[27:57] **Europe, the EU and the Paradox of Anti-Imperialism**

[事实] Dominic notes that some European federalists describe the European Union as needing to become an empire in a world of empires.
[事实] Tom says impulses toward European unity and fragmentation have both been constant.
[事实] He argues that the global nation-state system reflects a European assumption spread by European colonialism.
[事实] Tom claims that Western anti-imperialism is itself deeply Western, rooted in biblical and classical traditions.

[29:59] **Are We Really Post-Imperial?**

[事实] Dominic argues that the future may not belong to nation-states, since China, the United States and Russia can all be seen as empires.
[事实] He calls the idea of a post-imperial age a fantasy.
[推测] This point extends the episode’s argument from ancient and early modern history into current geopolitics.

[30:24] **Changing Views of the British Empire**

[事实] A listener asks how perceptions of the British Empire changed from good to bad in British culture.
[事实] Dominic says people in former colonies may not think much about the British Empire in everyday life, and that the voices heard are often unusually politicized or historically interested.
[事实] He argues that British people were neither unusually proud nor unusually self-critical in recent polling.
[事实] He says many people historically cared more about status, power and prestige than the practical details of empire.

[32:29] **British Anti-Imperialism as an Imperial Export**

[事实] Tom says anxiety about empire ran through the period of British imperial expansion and rule.
[事实] He discusses C. L. R. James’s *Beyond the Boundary* as an anti-imperial Marxist work that is still saturated in British literature and cricket.
[事实] He argues that Britain exported anti-imperialism as well as imperialism.
[事实] Frantz Fanon and Ho Chi Minh are briefly cited as anti-colonial figures shaped by European intellectual or imperial contexts.

[34:13] **Britain, Rome and Boudicca**

[事实] A listener asks whether modern views of ancient empires distort how people judge imperial legacies.
[事实] Tom says British attitudes to Rome are paradoxical because Britons could identify both with the conquered Britons and with the conquering Romans.
[事实] Boudicca is discussed as both a rebel against imperial power and a symbol later associated with British identity.
[事实] Tom notes that an early representation of Britannia showed her as a woman being raped by Emperor Claudius.

[36:00] **Do Empires Try to Become Nations?**

[事实] A listener asks whether long-lasting empires aspire to become single nations or federal states.
[事实] Dominic says probably not at first, though Russia had periods of Russification in places such as the Baltic states and Ukraine.
[事实] The Caucasus is used as an example of how geography and local identity can limit assimilation.
[事实] China and the Uyghurs are discussed as an example of a modern empire struggling with groups that do not see themselves as Han Chinese.

[38:07] **When Does an Empire Stop Being Called an Empire?**

[事实] A listener asks why areas created by imperial conquest, such as parts of Russia, the western United States and Northern Ireland, are later treated as ordinary state territory.
[事实] Dominic says the whole United States can be interpreted as an imperial project.
[事实] He cites Native American reservations, Puerto Rico, the Philippines, territory taken from Mexico, overseas bases and global influence as imperial features.
[事实] He says American policymakers study earlier imperial models such as Rome while Americans often resist calling their country an empire.

[39:49] **Digital and Corporate Empires**

[事实] A listener asks whether today’s empires may exist digitally through companies such as Google, Amazon, Microsoft and Facebook.
[事实] The hosts compare this with commercial empires such as the British East India Company and the Dutch East India Company.
[事实] They note that the East India Company’s possessions were eventually taken over by the British state.
[推测] The hosts leave open whether modern technology companies might eventually be absorbed into or constrained by state power.

[41:38] **Were People Better Off Inside Empires?**

[事实] A listener asks whether people inside empires were better off than those outside.
[事实] Dominic says the answer depends on the empire, contrasting the Belgian Congo with Rome and Persia.
[事实] He argues that in some empires, talented or fortunate people could gain opportunities for advancement, travel and prosperity.
[事实] Tom says great empires often reached natural limits where further conquest was not worth the cost.

[43:21] **The Cycle of Imperial Softness and Barbarian Toughness**

[事实] Tom says peoples outside empires often wanted access to imperial wealth.
[事实] He describes a recurring idea found in Herodotus, Tacitus and Ibn Khaldun: empire softens people, while outsiders retain martial virtues.
[事实] The Greeks, Romans and Arabs are said to have seen themselves as hard, martial peoples ruling over softer conquered populations.
[事实] Dominic connects this trope to modern American anxieties about decadence and loss of founding virtues.

[45:13] **Do Empires Have a 200-Year Lifespan?**

[事实] A listener asks whether all empires have around 200 years in the sun.
[事实] Dominic rejects this, saying China, Byzantium and the Ottoman Empire show that empires can last far longer.
[事实] He argues there is no fixed biological law of imperial decline, though empires may reach limits of expansion.
[事实] He raises the possibility that technology could change the scale and durability of future empires.

[47:22] **Technology, China and the Future of Empire**

[事实] Tom says he does not know whether technology will create new ways of structuring societies and hierarchies.
[事实] He notes that China is using technology to entrench rule over peripheral regions.
[事实] Dominic suggests the European Union might become an empire in all but name in the next century.
[事实] The hosts conclude that the age of empires may not be behind us.

[事实] The hosts say they have only scratched the surface of the subject and answered a small share of listener questions.

## Podcast Commentary/Summary

This episode’s value lies in refusing a simple moral binary. It does not deny conquest, coercion or violence, but it shows that empire has also been a producer of order, identity, culture, infrastructure and political imagination.

A major strength is the breadth of comparison. The discussion moves across Assyria, Persia, Greece, Rome, Islam, Byzantium, Britain, China, Russia, America and the European Union, using each example to test whether empire is an exception, a norm, a moral failure or a recurring form of human organization.

Its limitation is also its breadth: many claims are introduced quickly, and major cases such as the British Empire, China, the Arab Empire and corporate digital power receive only compressed treatment. [推测] Listeners looking for detailed case studies or a systematic moral framework may find the episode suggestive rather than conclusive.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners interested in big-picture historical argument, political ideas and the long history behind modern debates over colonialism, decolonization and global power.
