# Encore: Hallelujah — How Genius Emerges Through Iteration

## Overview

Malcolm Gladwell explores why some works of genius arrive fully formed while others emerge only after years of revision, failure, collaboration, and chance. His starting point is Elvis Costello’s “Deportees Club,” an initially unsuccessful song that Costello later transformed into the haunting acoustic “Deportee.”

Drawing on economist David Galenson’s theory, the episode contrasts “conceptual innovators,” who execute clear ideas rapidly, with “experimental innovators,” who discover their ideas through repeated attempts. Picasso represents the first type; Cézanne, Leonard Cohen, and Costello represent the second.

The history of “Hallelujah” provides the central case study. Cohen labored over the song for years, but its eventual fame depended on further reinterpretations by John Cale and Jeff Buckley, as well as a fragile chain of coincidences. The episode concludes that apparent failures may contain unrealized genius—and that returning to unfinished work can be more valuable than simply leaving it behind.

## Segment Summary

[02:00] **A Mixtape Discovery and a Song Transformed**

[Fact] Gladwell recalls exchanging carefully curated mix CDs with his friend Bruce, who introduced him to an acoustic rerecording of Elvis Costello’s “Deportees Club.”

[Fact] Gladwell had disliked the original version, yet found the same song magical after Costello changed its arrangement and presentation.

[Inference] The anecdote establishes the episode’s central question: how can a failed work become extraordinary without ceasing to be fundamentally the same creation?

[03:25] **“Deportees Club” as a Case Study in Creativity**

[Fact] Costello released “Deportees Club” on his 1984 album *Goodbye Cruel World*, which Gladwell bought when he was 20.

[Fact] Gladwell describes the original recording as awful and explains that he is treating the song as a symbol of how creativity works.

[Fact] He notes a psychological theory that music heard around ages 19 and 20 leaves an especially deep imprint.

[07:23] **The Hitmakers Behind *Goodbye Cruel World***

[Fact] Producers Clive Langer and Alan Winstanley had worked with prominent British artists and helped create major New Wave hits.

[Fact] Costello’s record label recruited them to broaden his commercial appeal after their earlier collaboration with him on *Punch the Clock*.

[Fact] Langer says Costello preferred music that sounded simpler and more authentic, while his own background encouraged elaborate pop production.

[Inference] Their contrasting artistic instincts created a tension between Costello’s desire for emotional directness and the producers’ pursuit of commercial polish.

[11:48] **A Troubled Album and a Creative Standoff**

[Fact] When work began on *Goodbye Cruel World*, Costello told Langer that it might be his final album.

[Fact] Langer recalls that Costello began taking control of the sessions, producing a standoff over who was directing the record.

[Fact] The album was recorded while Costello was under considerable personal and professional strain.

[Fact] On the 1995 reissue, Costello called it the band’s worst album.

[14:20] **From “Deportees Club” to “Deportee”**

[Fact] For the 1995 reissue, Costello recorded a solo acoustic version, changed the melody and some lyrics, and shortened the title to “Deportee.”

[Fact] The new version appeared only on that reissue and reached Gladwell through Bruce’s birthday mix.

[Fact] Langer had never heard the revised version before Gladwell played it for him during their interview.

[Fact] After listening, Langer said it sounded as though Costello had finally found the song.

[17:15] **Genius That Had Not Become Genius Yet**

[Fact] Gladwell argues that the mediocre original contained the beginnings of something exceptional, even though neither Costello nor his producers recognized it at the time.

[Fact] He identifies time and iteration as the forces that allowed the song’s potential to emerge.

[Inference] A disappointing first version may be evidence of an unfinished process rather than proof that the underlying idea lacks value.

[18:13] **Conceptual and Experimental Innovators**

[Fact] Economist David Galenson distinguishes between conceptual innovators and experimental innovators.

[Fact] Conceptual innovators work from clear, articulable ideas, plan carefully, and often produce major work early; Picasso is Gladwell’s example.

[Fact] Experimental innovators proceed through trial and error, repeated drafts, and persistent dissatisfaction; Cézanne represents this path.

[Fact] Experimental creators may take much longer to discover what they are trying to express.

[19:51] **Cézanne’s Art of Endless Revision**

[Fact] At New York’s Metropolitan Museum, Cézanne expert John Elderfield shows Gladwell several related portraits of the artist’s wife.

[Fact] The later portrait is less conventionally finished than some earlier versions, contradicting the assumption that every iteration moves toward a predetermined endpoint.

[Fact] Cézanne repeatedly revisited subjects, often left paintings unsigned, and required his dealer Ambroise Vollard to sit for approximately 100 sessions.

[Inference] For Cézanne, repetition was not merely refinement; it was the method through which the destination itself was discovered.

[23:08] **Two Creative Paths Across Different Fields**

[Fact] Gladwell classifies Herman Melville and Orson Welles as conceptual innovators whose landmark works arrived early.

[Fact] He contrasts them with Mark Twain and Alfred Hitchcock, whose major achievements followed extended experimentation and repeated engagement with the same problems or genres.

[Fact] Gladwell argues that Galenson’s distinction is especially powerful when applied to music.

[24:12] **“Hallelujah” as Experimental Innovation**

[Fact] Leonard Cohen wrote “Hallelujah,” which was later covered by artists including John Cale, Jeff Buckley, Rufus Wainwright, U2, Bob Dylan, and Bon Jovi.

[Fact] The song became common in films, television, live performances, and popular culture.

[Fact] Gladwell presents its history as a textbook example of Cézanne-like creativity rather than instant, Picasso-like invention.

[27:01] **Leonard Cohen’s Years of Labor**

[Fact] Music writer Alan Light says Cohen worked line by line and word by word, discarding large amounts of material.

[Fact] Accounts suggest Cohen created roughly 50 to 80 verses or variations while pursuing the song’s elusive central idea.

[Fact] Cohen reportedly spent about five years writing “Hallelujah,” while Bob Dylan said he wrote “I and I” in 15 minutes.

[Fact] Cohen recorded the song in 1984 for *Various Positions*.

[29:08] **The Rejected Original Version**

[Fact] CBS executive Walter Yetnikoff refused to release *Various Positions*, and the album eventually appeared through the independent Passport Records.

[Fact] The release attracted little attention, and Gladwell describes Cohen’s original “Hallelujah” as not yet fully realized.

[Fact] Cohen continued revising it in concert, slowing it down, lengthening it, and replacing several verses.

[Inference] Institutional rejection did not end the song because Cohen treated the recording as one stage in an ongoing experiment.

[30:48] **John Cale Cracks the Code**

[Fact] John Cale heard Cohen perform a revised “Hallelujah” in New York and asked him for the lyrics.

[Fact] Cohen sent Cale 15 pages, from which Cale selected and combined verses from different versions.

[Fact] Cale altered words, changed the thematic balance, and recorded his interpretation for the 1991 tribute album *I’m Your Fan*.

[Fact] Alan Light credits Cale with finding the form that unlocked the song’s later potential.

[32:39] **Jeff Buckley Builds on Cale’s Version**

[Fact] Jeff Buckley discovered Cale’s recording while house-sitting for his friend Janine in Brooklyn.

[Fact] Buckley performed his own interpretation at Sin-é in New York and later recorded it for his 1994 album *Grace*.

[Fact] Buckley’s famous recording is therefore a reworking of Cale’s adaptation of Cohen’s song, with piano replaced by guitar and transformed by Buckley’s voice.

[Fact] Gladwell says many later renditions effectively descend from this Cohen–Cale–Buckley chain.

[34:32] **Tragedy and the Song’s Delayed Recognition**

[Fact] *Grace* initially peaked at roughly number 160 on the charts and did not make Buckley or “Hallelujah” an immediate commercial success.

[Fact] Buckley disappeared and died while swimming in a Mississippi River channel in 1997.

[Fact] His death brought greater attention to his work, after which covers and soundtrack placements helped “Hallelujah” gather momentum.

[Fact] The song achieved broad recognition roughly 15 years after Cohen first recorded it.

[36:13] **A Fragile Chain of Coincidence**

[Fact] “Hallelujah” survived CBS’s rejection because an independent label released Cohen’s album.

[Fact] Its evolution depended on Cohen continuing to revise it, Cale hearing it live, Buckley encountering Cale’s obscure recording, and later attention following Buckley’s death.

[Inference] Gladwell’s reconstruction shows that experimental masterpieces can depend on unusually vulnerable networks of persistence, collaboration, access, and chance.

[37:21] **Why Experimental Work Is Easy to Lose**

[Fact] Gladwell contrasts “Hallelujah” with Paul Simon’s “Bridge over Troubled Water,” which Simon said arrived quickly and seemed complete.

[Fact] A fully realized conceptual creation can be evaluated immediately, whereas an experimental creation may initially look like a failure.

[Fact] Cézanne attempted to destroy some paintings and abandoned others because he believed he had not achieved his aims.

[Inference] Cultural institutions are better equipped to recognize polished, self-evident works than creations whose value emerges only through later iterations.

[39:19] **The 1984 Conflict Behind Costello’s Failure**

[Fact] “Deportees Club” and “Hallelujah” both first appeared in 1984, when highly polished pop production was culturally dominant.

[Fact] Costello was writing dark, bitter material while his marriage was failing and he faced financial difficulties.

[Fact] Langer and Winstanley were expected to produce carefully constructed hits, but the entire album was completed in only three or four weeks.

[Fact] Gladwell characterizes the result as “perfectionism in a hurry,” combining incompatible aims under severe time pressure.

[41:38] **Returning to Failure**

[Fact] Costello did not simply leave *Goodbye Cruel World* behind; he continued drafting, rewriting lyrics, changing tempos and time signatures, and reusing parts of earlier work.

[Fact] Gladwell places Costello alongside Cézanne as an experimental innovator who resists treating a work as permanently finished.

[Inference] The final beauty of “Deportee” exists because Costello regarded failure as material for further creation rather than as a closed verdict.

## Podcast Review/Summary

The episode’s greatest strength is the way it connects one obscure Costello recording, Cézanne’s painting process, and the long evolution of “Hallelujah” into a coherent argument about creativity. Concrete production histories make Galenson’s abstract distinction between conceptual and experimental innovation easy to understand.

Its most valuable insight is that iteration can change not only the quality of a work but also reveal what the work was supposed to be. The episode offers a persuasive defense of obsessive revision, dissatisfaction, and the willingness to revisit projects that others—or even their creators—have dismissed.

[Inference] The two-category framework may simplify artists whose methods vary from project to project, while the role assigned to coincidence makes the argument more illustrative than predictive. Even so, the episode is especially worthwhile for artists, writers, musicians, producers, and anyone discouraged by an imperfect first attempt.
