# Envisioning Deep Time

## 概览

This episode revisits a Long Now archive talk by artist and experimental philosopher Jonathan Keats. The host frames Keats’s work as philosophy practiced in public: instead of using thought experiments only as arguments, he builds situations that invite people into open-ended questions.

Keats traces a path from conceptual art to experimental philosophy, then moves through projects that stretch ordinary ideas of time: relativistic time-management products, epigenetic “cloning,” 100-year surveillance cameras, 1,000-year cameras, and finally a proposed 5,000-year calendar kept by a bristlecone pine.

The central conclusion is not that these projects are practical in a conventional sense, but that they function as “mental prostheses” for thinking beyond human-scale time. Keats argues that long-duration artworks can help people imagine accountability to future generations.

## 分段落总结

[00:00] **Archive Introduction**

[事实] Host Denise Hearn introduces the episode as an archival Long Now talk by Jonathan Keats, titled “Envisioning Deep Time.”

[事实] She describes Keats as an artist and experimental philosopher whose work turns thought experiments into public situations, objects, and experiments.

[事实] The introduction notes that Keats’s 5,000-year bristlecone pine calendar proposal later became a real collaboration with Long Now in eastern Nevada.

[推测] The framing presents the archive talk as historically important because listeners can hear an idea at the moment it first entered public discussion.

[02:17] **From Conceptual Art to Thinking as Art**

[事实] Keats begins by recalling a 2000 project in which he sat in a San Francisco gallery and thought for 24 hours.

[事实] He used a factory time clock and time cards to mark individual thoughts, treating the thoughts themselves as the artwork.

[事实] People could buy a thought by paying according to their own income for the amount of time Keats spent thinking it, but they received only the punched time card as a receipt.

[推测] The project exposed the difficulty of making thought into an object or commodity without reducing it to a representation.

[05:13] **Recovering a Naive Philosophy**

[事实] Keats says he originally imagined philosophy as open-ended thinking and conversation, but academic philosophy felt too narrow and uninteresting to people outside the field.

[事实] He came to see his gallery project as a way of thinking about thought itself.

[事实] He was drawn to thought experiments but dissatisfied with how they are often used as argumentative devices with predetermined points.

[推测] His later work grows from the desire to make philosophy more public, playful, and genuinely experimental.

[08:00] **Experimental Philosophy as Public Experiment**

[事实] Keats says he began calling himself an experimental philosopher and suggests he may be the only one he knows of.

[事实] For him, a thought experiment should be carried out in the world and allowed to produce unknown conclusions.

[推测] The “experiment” matters less as a scientific test than as a structure for generating conversation, interpretation, and unexpected questions.

[08:38] **Relativistic Time Management**

[事实] Keats critiques conventional time management as managing people rather than time.

[事实] He proposes using relativity and time dilation as the basis for literal time management, beginning with lead “time ingots” marketed through Space Time Industries.

[事实] He extends the idea into a “time warp undershirt,” neutron-star architecture, and cities whose districts spin at different rates to alter experienced time.

[推测] These intentionally absurd designs invite people to reconsider how societies organize time, labor, lifespan, agriculture, and production.

[15:46] **Epigenetic Time Travel and Identity**

[事实] Keats discusses a project about time travel through epigenetic “cloning,” using environmental and chemical influences to make someone more like a historical figure.

[事实] He uses Napoleon as an example, citing diet, lead, arsenic, and mercury as influences tied to Napoleon’s life and era.

[事实] He says the project drew attention, including from Nature, and led to conversations about cloning, epigenetics, identity, and history.

[推测] The project treats identity as less fixed than genetic lineage suggests, opening a speculative way to think about history as mutable rather than sealed.

[20:18] **The 100-Year Camera**

[事实] Keats says his future-facing work began in Berlin with a pinhole camera designed for a 100-year exposure.

[事实] The camera uses ordinary black paper, which slowly bleaches as light enters through a pinhole.

[事实] He explains that a century-long exposure would compress changes in a city into one image, with temporary structures appearing like ghosts against longer-lasting ones.

[事实] In Berlin, Keats and Team Titanic built 100 cameras that people could hide in the city, with the intention that future generations return them for exhibition.

[推测] The hidden cameras create a feeling of accountability because present-day people may imagine being watched by those not yet born.

[26:04] **Bringing Century Cameras to San Francisco**

[事实] Keats says Berlin is not the only city undergoing rapid transformation and connects the project to San Francisco.

[事实] In collaboration with Long Now, the audience is invited to build and hide 100-year cameras after the talk.

[事实] Keats says there is expected to be an exhibition at the Interval in 100 years, though he will not be present to speak about it.

[推测] The participatory workshop turns deep time from an abstract topic into a concrete handoff between generations.

[27:01] **The 1,000-Year Camera**

[事实] Keats then describes a more ambitious 1,000-year camera, noting that a tin-can construction is unlikely to survive that long.

[事实] He draws on archaeology and art conservation, using a copper body, a 24-karat gold pinhole plate, and oil paint made with rose madder pigment.

[事实] One camera was installed at the Arizona State University Art Museum on a 1,000-year loan, with the resulting photograph assigned to the museum in the year 3015.

[事实] A second camera was planned for Amherst College’s Mead Art Museum, aimed at the Holyoke Mountain Range rather than an urban scene.

[推测] By pairing urban and more natural environments, Keats frames the cameras as tools for comparing human-built and ecological change over deep time.

[30:53] **Deep Time as a Cognitive Challenge**

[事实] Keats argues that 1,000-year cameras are no longer about a direct generational handoff but about looking into deep time.

[事实] He says humans are not biologically equipped to think across thousands of years, even though our technologies can now affect climate and environment at that scale.

[事实] He describes long-duration cameras as “mental prostheses” that help people imagine the future looking back on the present.

[推测] The cameras are valuable before they produce images because the imagined image can already influence present choices.

[33:32] **A UNESCO-Scale Vision**

[事实] Keats proposes that UNESCO could distribute inexpensive 100-year cameras widely, potentially making them a birthright for every child.

[事实] He imagines many cameras going out into the world and returning after a century, creating daily exhibitions of global change.

[事实] He also imagines 1,000-year cameras as monumental sentinels built into cities.

[推测] The proposal links mass participation with institutional continuity, suggesting that deep-time imagination needs both intimate and civic forms.

[34:55] **The 5,000-Year Bristlecone Calendar**

[事实] Keats introduces a new project publicly for the first time: a calendar that keeps time according to life on Earth rather than abstract regularity.

[事实] He proposes using a bristlecone pine, whose rings reflect climate and growth conditions, as the basis for a 5,000-year calendar.

[事实] The concept involves placing stones around the tree at projected growth intervals, so the tree would eventually overturn them as it grows.

[事实] Keats says the calendar would fall out of sync with the Gregorian calendar, raising the question of which way of keeping time should be considered “right.”

[推测] The project challenges the authority of standardized time by making ecological change visible as a competing calendar.

[37:45] **Long Now’s Nevada Site**

[事实] Keats says the Long Now Foundation owns more than 800 acres at Mount Washington in eastern Nevada.

[事实] He says the site contains the world’s largest bristlecone pine forest in private hands.

[事实] He has already begun discussing with Long Now how the 5,000-year calendar might be implemented there.

[推测] The site gives the project a practical location and aligns it with Long Now’s mission of long-term thinking.

[38:51] **Naming the Calendar and Related Books**

[事实] In the Q&A, the calendar is named “Centuries of the Bristlecone.”

[事实] Keats says many details remain to be worked out over the next 5,000 years.

[事实] He also mentions two Oxford University Press books: one on the co-evolution of science, language, and technology, and another arguing that art forgeries are the great art of our time.

[推测] The exchange reinforces Keats’s mix of seriousness and humor: the projects are conceptually ambitious while remaining openly provisional.

[40:26] **Accessibility and Hidden Images**

[事实] A question asks whether people could see the camera images as they evolve inside the chamber.

[事实] Keats answers that the cameras are designed to require no future technology or post-processing; once opened, the image is directly visible.

[事实] He says the designs are open source and encourages others to try different approaches.

[事实] He also says his own approach keeps the cameras inaccessible for the full exposure because imagination is part of the psychological effect.

[推测] For Keats, withholding the image is not a limitation but part of how the work trains people to imagine deep time.

[43:03] **Experimental Philosophy as a Practice**

[事实] Keats says the term experimental philosophy comes from older traditions related to natural philosophy.

[事实] He describes himself as a dilettante and says his business cards say “untitled.”

[事实] He encourages others to follow curiosity and create open-ended public conversations through what they do.

[推测] He treats amateurism and porous disciplinary boundaries as strengths rather than weaknesses.

[44:16] **Could Sound Be Recorded Over a Century?**

[事实] A question asks whether media other than images, such as sound, could be captured over very long timescales.

[事实] Keats says sound interests him, especially through possible combinations of clockwork and magnetism, but he finds photography more compelling because sound is already temporal.

[事实] He suggests the challenge would be to layer sound over time without producing only noise or a very long recording.

[推测] The response shows his method in action: he does not close the question, but uses it to invite further experimentation.

[46:52] **Institutions and Experimental Work**

[事实] Keats is asked what society should do to make experimental philosophy viable.

[事实] He says people can do it on their own, but also warns that institutionalization can ossify exploratory work.

[事实] He praises institutions such as Long Now and ZERO1 for providing space, venues, and support without overly controlling ideas.

[事实] He criticizes top-down “sci-art” models where artists are used mainly to illustrate scientific institutions’ existing messages.

[推测] Keats’s preferred institution is one that curates people and conditions for inquiry rather than prescribing outcomes.

[49:22] **How to Build a Century Camera**

[事实] Keats explains that the century camera is simple: a tin can, paper glued inside, a pinhole, glue to seal it, and tape as a shutter.

[事实] Participants are told to hide the camera, start the exposure, remember where it is, and pass the location to a child or future generation.

[事实] The San Francisco version does not use the Berlin 10-euro deposit, though the host mentions an offer to return the ticket price in 100 years.

[推测] The instructions make the project intentionally accessible, turning an audience into collaborators in a century-long artwork.

[52:03] **Closing Gesture**

[事实] Keats gives the host a century camera built from an old Brownie camera.

[事实] The episode closes with Long Now acknowledgments and credits for the speaker, guest host, producer, team, members, and music.

[推测] Ending with a physical camera underscores the talk’s main theme: speculative ideas become meaningful when they are placed into the world.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode is valuable because it makes long-term thinking feel concrete. Keats does not merely argue that people should think in centuries or millennia; he offers objects and rituals that let that timescale become imaginable.

The strongest parts are the progression from humorous conceptual projects to genuinely resonant deep-time proposals. The 100-year and 1,000-year cameras, followed by the bristlecone calendar, build a clear arc from provocation to public philosophy to ecological timekeeping.

[推测] The main limitation is that many projects remain speculative or symbolic, so listeners looking for conventional feasibility, engineering detail, or policy application may find parts of the talk evasive. That said, the symbolic force is also the point.

[推测] This episode is especially suited for listeners interested in long-term thinking, art-science collaborations, philosophy outside academia, environmental imagination, and the question of how present actions might be made accountable to future people.
