# What Caused the First World War?

## 概览

This episode of *The Rest Is History* asks the classic exam question: what caused the First World War? Dominic Sandbrook begins with the familiar chain from the Sarajevo assassination to Austria-Hungary’s war on Serbia, Russia’s mobilisation, Germany’s intervention, France’s involvement, and Britain’s entry after Germany violated Belgian neutrality.

The discussion then turns counterfactual and revisionist. A major thread is whether Britain should have stayed out, whether Germany might have won quickly without British intervention, and whether the later consequences of the war make intervention look disastrous in hindsight.

Rather than giving one single cause, the episode presents the war as a mix of alliance obligations, fear, imperial calculations, status anxiety, military timetables, fatalism, and contingency. The hosts repeatedly stress that explanations of the war often reflect the historian’s own assumptions.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **The Exam Question**

[事实] The episode frames itself around the question asked for decades in history exams: what were the causes of the First World War?
[事实] Dominic Sandbrook is introduced as having contrarian or heretical views on the subject.
[推测] The exam-style framing lets the hosts move between basic narrative, historiography, and counterfactual argument.

[01:18] **The Diplomatic Chain Reaction**

[事实] Dominic summarises Europe before 1914 as divided between the Entente of Russia, France, and Britain’s looser attachment, and the Central Powers of Germany and Austria-Hungary.
[事实] Franz Ferdinand’s assassination in Sarajevo by Gavrilo Princip led Austria-Hungary to seek punitive war against Serbia.
[事实] Russia mobilised as Serbia’s protector, Germany entered against Russia and France, and Britain joined after Germany went through neutral Belgium.
[推测] The opening narrative presents the war as an escalating chain rather than a single isolated decision.

[03:18] **Should Britain Have Stayed Out?**

[事实] The hosts discuss the argument, associated with Niall Ferguson, that Britain should have avoided the war.
[事实] Dominic says Britain could easily have stayed out because many Liberals were reluctant to intervene even at the start of August 1914.
[事实] British leaders feared that a German victory would leave Germany master of Europe, in control of Channel ports, and threatening Britain’s empire.
[推测] In hindsight, Dominic argues that non-intervention is hard to see as worse than the actual consequences: a second world war, Nazism, the Holocaust, Soviet communism, and the Cold War.

[05:59] **Honour, France, and German Victory**

[事实] The hosts note that Britain’s navy had effectively agreed to patrol the Channel while France had no naval presence there.
[事实] Dominic says Britain staying out would have looked like betraying France, and that the French ambassador questioned whether “honour” would vanish from the English dictionary.
[推测] Dominic considers it plausible that Germany would have defeated France in 1914 without British involvement and then turned east against Russia.

[07:55] **Big Causes or Accidents?**

[事实] Tom asks whether the war was caused by broad forces such as capitalism, imperialism, and industrial civilisation, or by contingencies such as Franz Ferdinand’s car taking a wrong turn.
[事实] Christopher Clark’s *The Sleepwalkers* is cited as an example of the vast literature on the war’s origins.
[推测] The hosts treat the question as a Rorschach test: historians tend to find causes that reflect their own interests and assumptions.

[09:20] **Austria, Serbia, and the Normality of War**

[事实] Dominic says the immediate reason was Austria-Hungary’s belief that it could no longer tolerate Serbia undermining its southern border.
[事实] He says Serbian involvement or cover-up around the assassination was murky, but Austria’s response was not unfathomable in historical terms.
[推测] Dominic argues that the war seems mysterious largely because of its catastrophic death toll, not because wars themselves are historically unusual.

[10:47] **Peaceful Europe and the Edwardian Illusion**

[事实] Tom notes that Europe’s great powers had avoided a Napoleonic-scale conflict for about a century before 1914.
[事实] The hosts contrast the image of Edwardian leisure and refinement with the later rise of communism, fascism, and the destruction of older European orders.
[推测] The episode suggests that the shock of 1914 comes partly from the contrast between a remembered world of stability and the disasters that followed.

[11:58] **Earlier Crises and the Shift Against Germany**

[事实] Dominic points to earlier flashpoints, including Austria’s annexation of Bosnia in 1908 and Franco-German hostility after 1870-71.
[事实] Britain had once been more pro-Prussian and anti-French, but attitudes shifted as Germany became more economically and militarily successful.
[事实] Invasion-scare fiction such as William Le Queux’s *The Invasion of 1910* helped reflect and amplify British fear of Germany.
[推测] Germany became Britain’s enemy less because of ancient hostility than because it looked like the rising power most capable of threatening British status.

[14:09] **Empire, India, and the Russian Alliance**

[事实] Dominic says Britain’s alignment with Russia can be explained partly by fear of Russia as a threat to India.
[事实] He argues that British policymakers saw India as essential to imperial status and therefore preferred having Russia on their side.
[推测] The Entente was not simply an alliance of natural friends; it was also an imperial risk-management strategy.

[15:23] **The Heretical German Alliance Counterfactual**

[事实] Dominic jokes but also argues seriously that Britain might have sided with Germany against France and Russia.
[事实] He notes that figures such as Joseph Chamberlain and Lord Haldane had shown pro-German tendencies or explored German alignment.
[事实] Dominic describes Russia as repressive, Belgium as running a brutal colony in the Congo, and Serbia as close to a terrorist state.
[推测] His counterfactual relies on treating Germany not as a proto-Nazi power but as a dynamic, partly democratic, modern state.

[17:49] **The Naval Race**

[事实] Tom challenges the German-alliance idea by pointing to Germany’s naval build-up as a direct threat to Britain.
[事实] Dominic agrees that British fear of the naval race was not irrational because Britain depended on trade, imports, and the Royal Navy.
[事实] Britain’s army was described as small compared with other European armies.
[推测] The naval race made Germany’s rise feel existential to Britain in a way that economic rivalry alone may not have done.

[20:36] **Ireland and the Forgotten Domestic Crisis**

[事实] Dan Jackson’s point is introduced: Britain was consumed by the Irish Home Rule crisis in August 1914.
[事实] Asquith reportedly wrote that Franz Ferdinand’s shooting meant they would not have to talk about Ireland for a while.
[事实] Dominic says Britain was near civil conflict over Home Rule, with gun-running and a near-mutiny in the army.
[推测] Without the European war, Ireland might have become the central crisis of 1914 for Britain.

[23:10] **Was War Easier Than Avoiding War?**

[事实] A question from *Blackadder Goes Forth* is discussed: whether the war happened because it was too much effort not to have one.
[事实] Dominic rejects that phrasing but says each major actor saw war as the least bad option.
[事实] He says Austria, Russia, Germany, and France feared loss of prestige, power, or security if they avoided war.
[推测] The decision-making climate was less simple enthusiasm for war than a shared belief that delay could make the future worse.

[24:16] **Fatalism and Preventive War**

[事实] Dominic says German generals expected war with Russia and often thought it was better to fight sooner because Russia was industrialising and building railways.
[事实] Austria also believed it would be weaker later if it failed to confront Serbia immediately.
[推测] The war was shaped by preventive-war logic: leaders feared that waiting would shift the balance against them.

[25:05] **Alliances and Russia’s Break Point**

[事实] Tom compares the alliances to mountaineers linked by ropes, where one fall drags others down.
[事实] Dominic says Germany’s entry followed Austria’s war, but he identifies Russia’s intervention as the key avoidable moment.
[事实] He argues Russia could have allowed an Austro-Serbian local war but chose not to because of prestige, status, and ambitions around Constantinople.
[推测] In Dominic’s view, Russia’s mobilisation transformed a Balkan war into a wider European crisis.

[26:06] **Ottoman Decline and Mobilisation Timetables**

[事实] Tom raises two explanations: the Ottoman Empire’s decline and A.J.P. Taylor’s argument about railway timetables.
[事实] Dominic says the Ottoman collapse and the Balkan Wars of 1912-13 created competition over territories such as Albania and Macedonia.
[事实] He says Russia had to mobilise early because of its size, and the Tsar understood that mobilisation would be difficult to stop.
[推测] Military machinery and diplomatic negotiation were moving at the same time, making de-escalation harder.

[27:44] **The Willy-Nicky Telegrams and Cold War Memory**

[事实] The Kaiser and Tsar exchanged the “Willy-Nicky” telegrams while their generals were setting military machinery in motion.
[事实] The hosts compare this memory of escalation to Cold War caution during moments such as the Cuban Missile Crisis.
[推测] The First World War became a warning example for later leaders about how quickly crises can cross the brink.

[28:49] **Why It Became a World War**

[事实] The hosts discuss whether Europe stopped being the centre of the world and whether Australians, New Zealanders, Pacific Islanders, Indians, and other colonial subjects should have fought.
[事实] Dominic says they had to be involved because they were part of empire.
[事实] He notes that earlier conflicts such as the Seven Years War and Napoleonic Wars had also drawn colonies into global conflict.
[推测] The war was global not only because of alliances but because European empires extended European wars across the world.

[30:52] **Empire’s Violence and the War**

[事实] Tom raises the argument that European powers had honed mass violence in colonies and that the First World War was a form of imperial violence returning to Europe.
[事实] He cites H.G. Wells’s *The War of the Worlds* and its comparison between Martian destruction and British violence against Tasmanians.
[事实] Dominic is sceptical, saying European powers had already shown their ability to kill each other in the Napoleonic, Crimean, and Franco-Prussian wars.
[推测] The episode treats the imperial “blowback” argument as suggestive but not sufficient as a main cause.

[32:31] **Blackadder, Poets, and Public Memory**

[事实] The hosts discuss whether *Blackadder*, war poets, *Oh, What a Lovely War*, and the “lions led by donkeys” tradition have distorted views of the First World War.
[事实] Dominic says the dominant image of the war as mud, blood, and misery comes heavily from cultural memory.
[事实] He tells a story of veterans attending *Oh, What a Lovely War* and responding by singing along and sharing anecdotes rather than simply showing trauma.
[推测] The episode argues that cultural memory captures part of the war’s horror but can flatten its variety and complexity.

[35:22] **Was It Necessary?**

[事实] Dominic rejects the revisionist claim that the First World War was a necessary war.
[事实] He also says the war was not only the Western Front trenches, noting Italy, the Middle East, and the air war as other dimensions.
[推测] His position combines anti-necessity with anti-simplification: the war was avoidable, but not reducible to one familiar cultural image.

[36:11] **German Victory and War Aims**

[事实] The hosts return to the counterfactual that a quick German victory might have resembled 1870-71 and been better for Britain than the real outcome.
[事实] Dominic says German war aims became more punitive after the war began, including domination over the Low Countries and a German-led customs union.
[事实] He says Germany’s plans for Britain were unclear, though Britain likely would have lost colonies.
[推测] The hosts treat all wartime plans as unstable because the actual outcomes of war often differ from what governments imagined at the start.

[37:47] **The Marxist Explanation**

[事实] Tom raises Lenin’s view that the First World War represented the end-stage crisis of capitalism and imperialism.
[事实] Dominic rejects the idea that the war needs to be explained by the search for profits, markets, or a necessary logic of imperialism.
[推测] The episode places more weight on fear, prestige, and security calculations than on Marxist economic determinism.

[38:30] **Fear as the Central Cause**

[事实] Dominic says wars generally start because people are afraid and see war as the least bad option.
[事实] He argues leaders fear becoming poorer, weaker, or vulnerable to attack if they do not act first.
[推测] Fear is presented as the episode’s closest thing to a unifying explanation for 1914.

[39:13] **Enthusiasm, Horror, and Foreknowledge**

[事实] The hosts acknowledge that some people, including Italian futurists and figures such as Rupert Brooke, were excited by ideas of violence and renewal.
[事实] They also cite Edward Grey’s image of the lights going out over Europe as evidence that policymakers understood the coming calamity.
[事实] Dominic says German military leaders such as Moltke knew a European war would be exhausting, costly, and transformative.
[推测] The mood before war mixed jingoism, fatalism, and dread rather than simple optimism.

[40:26] **Leaders Knew, Yet Chose War**

[事实] Dominic says it is a myth that all leaders believed the war would be over by Christmas, though some ordinary soldiers did.
[事实] He challenges the *Blackadder* image that leaders were fools while ordinary soldiers understood everything clearly.
[事实] He says many ordinary recruits had little idea where they were going or whom they were fighting, while policymakers were often intelligent and well-informed.
[推测] The frightening conclusion is that the war was not caused merely by ignorance; informed leaders chose escalation anyway.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s value lies in how clearly it separates the immediate chain of events from deeper causes and later interpretations. It is especially strong on Britain’s choices, imperial anxieties, the naval race, and the difference between hindsight and what leaders could know in 1914.

A major highlight is the willingness to test uncomfortable counterfactuals, especially whether Britain should have stayed out or even aligned with Germany. The discussion does not present those ideas as settled truth, but uses them to expose how contingent the actual path to war was.

Its limitation is that the episode remains heavily Anglo-centric, which the hosts openly acknowledge. The perspectives of Serbia, Austria-Hungary, Russia, France, Germany, and colonial troops appear mainly through British strategic or historiographical questions.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners who want a lively historiographical overview rather than a detailed military or diplomatic chronology. It would be useful for students, general history listeners, and anyone interested in how historians argue about causation.
