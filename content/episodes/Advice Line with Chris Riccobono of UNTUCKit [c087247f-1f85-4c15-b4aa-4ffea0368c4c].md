# Advice Line with Chris Riccobono of UNTUCKit

## 概览

Guy Raz is joined by UNTUCKit co-founder Chris Riccobono for an advice-focused episode about how early and growing companies can find customers, sharpen positioning, and choose the right marketing channels.

Chris updates listeners on UNTUCKit’s current challenges, including post-COVID debt, high interest rates, tariffs, and a new move into wholesale. He also explains how his sportswear brand Greatness Wins is using wholesale in golf shops as both a revenue channel and a form of brand exposure.

The episode then works through three founder calls: AeroShorts, a college-founded hybrid shorts brand; Snug Safety, a daily check-in app for seniors living alone; and Hockey Ninja, a hockey visor and face-shield company. Across the calls, Chris repeatedly emphasizes focus, cash, clear differentiation, testing, and finding channels that fit the actual customer rather than defaulting to paid social.

## 分段落总结

[00:37] **Advice Line Setup**

[事实] Guy Raz introduces the show as a place where founders call in with business challenges and receive advice from a returning founder guest.

[事实] The guest is Chris Riccobono, co-founder of UNTUCKit.

[事实] Guy recaps Chris’s earlier story: he started without a fashion background, built a shorter button-down shirt meant to be worn untucked, and scaled UNTUCKit rapidly with co-founder Aaron Sanandres.

[02:08] **UNTUCKit’s Current Challenges**

[事实] Chris says UNTUCKit is still dealing with difficult conditions, including COVID-related debt, high interest rates, and tariffs.

[事实] He says tariffs and the removal of the 3-2-1 de minimis rule created a roughly $9 million cash impact that the company had not anticipated.

[事实] UNTUCKit is entering wholesale for the first time, adding department stores alongside e-commerce and its own stores.

[03:16] **Greatness Wins and Wholesale as Marketing**

[事实] Chris says Greatness Wins, his separate sportswear brand, has pivoted toward wholesale earlier than expected.

[事实] The brand is focusing on green-grass golf shops, where golf apparel companies can generate significant revenue.

[事实] Chris frames golf-shop placement as both revenue and “free marketing,” because customers may discover the shirt in a pro shop and later buy other products online.

[推测] For Chris, wholesale is not only a distribution decision but also a response to rising paid-social acquisition costs.

[04:37] **Building an Apparel Brand in 2026**

[事实] Chris says paid social was much easier in 2011, when fewer direct-to-consumer brands were competing for customers.

[事实] UNTUCKit succeeded by using a mix of non-obvious channels, including airline magazines, radio, Howard Stern, newspapers, TV, and paid social.

[事实] He says brands now also need to think about AI discovery, because consumers may ask tools like ChatGPT for product recommendations instead of using traditional SEO.

[推测] The broader lesson is that founders should not assume the currently fashionable marketing channel is the only viable one.

[06:37] **AeroShorts: Affordable Hybrid Shorts**

[事实] Adrian Alvarez calls from Sacramento and introduces AeroShorts, an athleisure and lifestyle brand selling hybrid shorts.

[事实] The shorts are designed for multiple uses, including the beach, pool, gym, hiking, volleyball, surfing, and golf.

[事实] Adrian started the business as a high school senior and is now a college sophomore.

[事实] He found manufacturing partners after attending a sourcing show in Las Vegas.

[08:40] **AeroShorts’ Traction and Goal**

[事实] AeroShorts reached $100,000 in sales in its first year and is on track for about $400,000 in its second year.

[事实] The business sells direct-to-consumer online and keeps its shorts priced around $35.

[事实] Adrian says he has appealed especially to volleyball players but wants AeroShorts to become a mainstream brand that can compete with larger athletic brands.

[09:35] **Define the Tribe Before Expanding**

[事实] Guy asks Adrian to identify his core “tribe” before trying to become mainstream.

[事实] Adrian says his core audience is younger people, especially college and high school students.

[事实] Adrian says about 60% of customers return to buy again.

[事实] Chris says athletic apparel is highly competitive, but Adrian’s youth may be an advantage because younger consumers shop differently through platforms like TikTok.

[13:33] **Focus, Capital, and Differentiation for AeroShorts**

[事实] Chris says Adrian will likely need to raise money if he wants to build a large brand.

[事实] Chris suggests friends-and-family funding, using a number like $150,000 as an example.

[事实] Chris recommends considering a temporary focus on volleyball because there appears to be less competition there than in “shorts for everyone.”

[事实] Chris says Adrian needs to sharpen the brand message and differentiate beyond price.

[15:17] **Where AeroShorts Should Spend**

[事实] Chris says an e-commerce apparel brand ultimately needs a good product and marketing.

[事实] He recommends allocating raised money toward product and marketing, while recognizing that paid social is much harder than it used to be.

[事实] Chris suggests testing lower-cost channels that reach volleyball players and creating TikTok content that tells Adrian’s story.

[推测] Chris’s advice implies that Adrian should use early traction to raise money before growth slows and fundraising becomes harder.

[19:28] **Snug Safety: Daily Check-Ins for Seniors**

[事实] Preet Anand introduces Snug Safety as a free daily check-in service for seniors who live alone.

[事实] Users choose a daily check-in time; if they do not check in, Snug sends reminders and then alerts emergency contacts.

[事实] On the paid plan, Snug can call the user, call emergency contacts, and arrange an emergency-services visit if no one confirms the user is safe.

[20:36] **Snug’s Origin and Revenue**

[事实] Preet says Snug began in 2017 after a customer named Jan described needing help if she could not respond after something like a seizure.

[事实] Preet says more than 17 million seniors live alone in the country.

[事实] Snug has around $400,000 in annual revenue, is growing at 70% or more, and charges $20 per month for its subscription.

[22:26] **Snug’s Awareness Problem**

[事实] Preet says Snug’s main challenge is growing awareness on a limited budget.

[事实] He says roughly 70% of growth comes from word of mouth, with additional growth from Google.

[事实] Snug has also seen some success sponsoring senior YouTubers.

[事实] Preet says the company could deploy about $50,000 in marketing budget this year and possibly more.

[23:14] **Radio and Long-Form Trust Channels**

[事实] Chris recommends radio reads on platforms like Sirius or Spotify because they can target demographics and explain the story in 30 seconds.

[事实] He also suggests testing political-commentary radio hosts because their audiences may be highly loyal.

[事实] Chris says Snug is not a hard sell once the right person understands it.

[事实] He advises testing channels separately so Snug can measure what works and learn whether the message needs adjustment.

[25:38] **Earned Media and Trusted Intermediaries**

[事实] Guy says Snug solves an emotional, trust-based problem.

[事实] Guy suggests that trust-based products are distributed through trusted people and intermediaries.

[事实] He recommends local TV, local newspapers, and community publications as potentially useful earned-media channels.

[事实] Preet says earned media has worked for Snug, including an NPR mention that continued to lift year-over-year performance.

[27:39] **Snug’s Buyer and Partner Channels**

[事实] Chris says Snug should test whether the true customer is the senior or the adult child.

[事实] He says that if adult children are the better target, radio choices might shift toward sports radio or other mainstream programming.

[事实] Chris also suggests geriatric doctors, churches, and similar trusted community settings as possible referral channels, though he notes reaching them may require a sales force.

[推测] The advice points toward a hybrid growth strategy: consumer marketing for awareness plus trusted offline partners for credibility.

[28:50] **Positioning Snug Against Life Alert**

[事实] Preet asks whether Snug should position itself against Life Alert or as an alternative.

[事实] Guy says Snug may be complementary rather than purely competitive because Life Alert is used in emergencies, while Snug provides daily reassurance.

[事实] Chris notes that Life Alert requires the user to be able to press a button, while Snug can help in cases where someone cannot respond.

[事实] Preet gives an example of Snug helping after a low-blood-sugar incident.

[31:35] **Hockey Ninja: Protective Hockey Visors**

[事实] Derek calls from Arlington, Virginia and introduces Hockey Ninja as a hockey equipment company specializing in visors and full-face titanium hybrids.

[事实] Hockey Ninja serves beer-league players, high school players, and youth players.

[事实] Derek says full-face shields are currently the core part of the business.

[32:34] **Hockey Ninja’s Product Differentiation**

[事实] Derek says the product combines a titanium lower cage with a replaceable high-end plastic visor.

[事实] He says Hockey Ninja uses Macrolon, a Lexan plastic from Bayer MaterialScience in Germany.

[事实] Derek says the material is known for impact resistance and optical clarity.

[事实] He says Hockey Ninja tests visors at 120 miles per hour, while other testing bodies test at 65 to 75 miles per hour.

[35:08] **Side Hustle Strategy Question**

[事实] Derek says Hockey Ninja is still a side hustle and that he still loves his full-time job.

[事实] Hockey Ninja averages around $250,000 in sales and has reached as high as $400,000.

[事实] Derek asks whether he should invest his limited time in social media or pursue NHL equipment managers, teams, and possibly player involvement after receiving NHL clearance on several products.

[36:57] **Professional Validation for Hockey Ninja**

[事实] Chris asks whether professional players have worn the product.

[事实] Derek says no NHL players are currently wearing it, but the company recently received clearance and plans to approach equipment managers.

[事实] Chris says equipment products need to be seen on players because safety-related purchases depend heavily on credibility.

[事实] Derek says Hockey Ninja has budget for a player arrangement and previously considered leasing a Range Rover as payment for a two-year deal.

[38:13] **Athlete Partnerships and Marketing Budget**

[事实] Chris says if Hockey Ninja brings on a player, it still needs marketing budget to use that player in ads.

[事实] Chris says UNTUCKit has run ads with athletes including Garrett Cole, Jacob Trouba, and Brock Bowers.

[事实] Guy recommends using a human endorser over generic social media, but suggests avoiding expensive NHL stars and considering lesser-known NHL players who still get playing time.

[事实] Derek says Hockey Ninja is the main visor sponsor for a professional three-on-three league where former NHL players and other high-level players will wear the product.

[40:13] **Equity as a Shortcut to Credibility**

[事实] Chris says if Hockey Ninja’s product is truly better than what players currently wear, Derek could consider giving a major player equity in the company.

[事实] Chris gives Jack Hughes as an example of the kind of major player who could create credibility if he genuinely liked and used the product.

[事实] Chris says another route is signing several third- or fourth-line NHL players for relatively low cost.

[事实] Guy and Chris also mention possible future expansion into other protective optics categories such as baseball visors or ski goggles.

[42:24] **Chris’s Closing Advice to Founders**

[事实] Guy asks Chris what advice he wishes he had known when starting UNTUCKit.

[事实] Chris says entrepreneurship is “really, really hard” and that founders cannot let problems crush them emotionally.

[事实] He says advice can be difficult to implement after a call because founders still have to figure out the practical next steps.

[事实] Chris says he wishes he had enjoyed the journey more and believes the callers are already ahead of most people by getting their businesses this far.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] This episode is especially useful for early-stage consumer founders who are trying to decide where to focus limited capital and attention. The strongest through-line is that a product’s channel should match the customer’s trust behavior, not simply follow the dominant marketing trend.

[事实] Chris repeatedly pushes founders toward concrete tests: define the core audience, raise enough money to learn something, invest in product and marketing, measure specific channels, and use credible people or institutions when the purchase depends on trust or safety.

[推测] The episode’s limitation is that the advice remains high-level in places; callers receive strong strategic direction, but not detailed execution plans for fundraising, media buying, partnerships, or sales operations.

[推测] The best audience is founders of apparel, consumer products, and trust-based services who already have some traction and need to choose a sharper growth path.
