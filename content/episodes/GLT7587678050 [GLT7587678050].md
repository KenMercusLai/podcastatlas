# Communism, Marxism, and the Dream of Earthly Paradise

## 概览

This episode discusses communism as both a historical political force and a still-potent cultural idea. The hosts begin from Marx and Engels’ *Communist Manifesto*, then widen the frame to earlier Christian and utopian traditions that imagined shared property, equality, and justice.

A central argument is that Marxism can be read as a kind of political religion: it has prophets, sacred texts, heresies, visions of redemption, and a promised classless future. The hosts repeatedly compare Marxist ideas with Christian debates about whether paradise can be built on earth.

The discussion then moves through Lenin, Stalin, Soviet totalitarianism, China, the collapse of Soviet confidence, and listener questions. The broad conclusion is skeptical: communism’s ideals of justice and liberation retain emotional power, but attempts to turn them into state systems are presented as tending toward violence, repression, and anti-pluralism.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Communism’s Continuing Presence**

[事实] The episode opens with the first lines of *The Communist Manifesto*, written in 1848 by Karl Marx and Friedrich Engels.

[事实] Dominic Sandbrook frames communism as a political theory that dominated much debate in the late 19th and much of the 20th century.

[事实] The hosts note that the number of communist states has fallen, while China, Cuba, Vietnam, and Laos are still described as technically communist.

[事实] Tom Holland argues that communism remains powerful because its ideal of human liberation and equal justice has obvious appeal.

[03:30] **Marxism Beyond Marx**

[事实] Tom argues that Antonio Gramsci and Frantz Fanon remain highly influential communist or Marxist thinkers in contemporary culture.

[事实] Gramsci is described as emphasizing culture as a route to social justice, while Fanon is described as applying Marxist ideas to colonized societies and the global South.

[推测] The hosts treat Marxism not only as an economic doctrine but also as a continuing cultural vocabulary for talking about power, injustice, and imperialism.

[04:08] **Christian and Utopian Roots**

[事实] Dominic mentions Thomas More’s *Utopia* and the Diggers of the English Civil War as pre-Marx examples of communist-like ideas.

[事实] Tom identifies the early church in Acts as an archetype of communist society, because believers are described as selling possessions and giving according to need.

[事实] Tom links this to Marx’s later formula about giving according to ability and receiving according to need.

[事实] The hosts contrast Pelagius, associated here with the possibility of moral self-redemption and earthly transformation, with Augustine, who says fallen humanity cannot realize heaven on earth.

[06:37] **Radical Religion and Earthly Paradise**

[事实] Tom says the radical fringes of the Reformation attempted to put egalitarian Christian teaching into practice.

[事实] He cites the Hussite rebellion and Tabor as an early attempt to realize a communist society in European history.

[事实] He also names the Anabaptists in Munster and the Diggers as movements connected to this tradition.

[推测] The discussion presents communism as inheriting a much older religious dream of a New Jerusalem built in this world.

[07:58] **Marxism as Political Religion**

[事实] Dominic argues that Marxism is obviously a kind of political religion, pointing to later icons, banners, sacred texts, and prophets.

[事实] Tom says Marx’s writing uses Gothic imagery such as spectres, vampires, bloodsuckers, and parasites.

[事实] Tom argues that Marx treats oppression of the poor by the rich as wrong, but presents his position as scientific rather than metaphysical.

[事实] Dominic says 19th-century ideas often used scientific language because the age was obsessed with science, progress, Darwinism, technology, and railways.

[11:49] **Science, Theology, and Identity**

[事实] Dominic invokes Walter Benjamin’s comparison of historical materialism to an automaton hiding theology beneath machinery.

[事实] He says Marxism can function as a religious identity rather than a carefully reasoned political position.

[事实] He cites David Aronovich’s account of post-war British Marxist circles as a closed social world with its own institutions and worldview.

[事实] Tom adds that Gramsci admired the Catholic Church and compares conflicts between Catholicism and communism to a battle between sibling closed systems.

[14:21] **Marx, Engels, and Industrial Britain**

[事实] Tom says Marx’s philosophy grew from the specific circumstances of mid-19th-century Europe, the 1848 revolutions, and Marx’s exile to Britain.

[事实] Engels is described as running factories in Manchester while also observing the suffering caused by the Industrial Revolution.

[事实] Tom notes that Marx and Engels write with awe about the achievements, restlessness, global reach, and power of the bourgeoisie.

[事实] Dominic says Marx expected revolution to happen in advanced industrial societies such as Britain or Germany, not in agrarian Russia.

[16:35] **Lenin and the Russian Problem**

[事实] Dominic explains that Russia was mostly agrarian, with major industrial centers such as St Petersburg and Moscow, making it an unexpected place for Marxist revolution.

[事实] Lenin is presented as the figure who adapts Marxism by arguing for a revolutionary vanguard party that seizes power and pushes history forward.

[事实] Dominic argues that violence and dictatorship appear from the outset when Marxism is put into practice.

[推测] The Leninist move turns a theory of historical inevitability into a justification for disciplined elite control.

[19:07] **Stalin, Violence, and Marxist Logic**

[事实] Dominic says Stalin hangs over every discussion of communism because he murdered millions of people.

[事实] He summarizes Stephen Kotkin’s argument that Stalin was not simply mad or aberrant but a committed Marxist acting through Marxist ideas.

[事实] Tom says Stalinism develops logically out of Leninism, which itself adapts Marx’s theory to the Russian Revolution.

[事实] Dominic argues that communism has an inherent confiscatory element, because property must be taken from some people and they will resist.

[23:19] **Is Communism Inherently Totalitarian?**

[事实] A listener asks whether communism is inherently totalitarian.

[事实] Tom suggests Stalinism is economically based because remaking the economy requires seizing every lever of control.

[事实] Dominic argues that communism probably is inherently totalitarian because it is not pluralist and cannot easily tolerate dissent.

[事实] Stalin’s rapid industrialization and collectivization are discussed as policies driven by Marxist assumptions about history, industry, and private ownership.

[25:58] **Communism and Nationalism**

[事实] Tom notes that Stalin turned away from Marx’s universalism through the idea of socialism in one country.

[事实] The hosts discuss how communism can fuse with nationalism and cite Ukraine and North Korea as examples of strange or monstrous evolutions.

[事实] Dominic says Soviet leaders after the 1950s became more defensive and conservative, focused on holding power rather than creating a New Jerusalem.

[推测] The conversation implies that communist parties can become ruling elites whose survival interests outweigh revolutionary ideals.

[28:26] **China and Belief**

[事实] The hosts ask whether China is still communist beyond formal party identity.

[事实] Tom says China is formally communist, while Dominic questions whether Chinese Communist Party leaders believe or live according to communist doctrine.

[事实] Dominic compares this to medieval Christian rulers who believed they were Christian while not fully living according to Christian teachings.

[推测] The analogy suggests that official ideology can become a legitimating language even when everyday practice diverges from it.

[29:56] **Has Communism Been Tried Properly?**

[事实] A listener asks whether communism’s failure is baked in, since “proper” attempts seem to end in economic failure and tyranny.

[事实] Tom says Marx’s ideal of justice and liberation still preserves its power if separated from ironclad economic laws and party ideology.

[事实] Tom argues that the classless society never arrives, just as the New Jerusalem cannot be built on earth.

[事实] Dominic replies that regimes calling themselves communist repeatedly produced repression and violence, suggesting the problem may be built into the project.

[34:12] **Western Abundance and Soviet Disillusion**

[事实] Dominic recounts Boris Yeltsin visiting an American supermarket near Houston and being shocked by ordinary consumer abundance.

[事实] The hosts also mention Soviet figures such as Alexander Yakovlev and Oleg Gordievsky being struck by life in Western countries.

[事实] Tom says the spectacle of Western wealth has also been reconfigured since the collapse of Soviet communism, because Marx’s anger at bourgeois wealth rested on claims about exploitation.

[事实] Dominic challenges exploitation-based explanations by pointing to rising life expectancy and living standards in Asia and Africa, while acknowledging environmental costs.

[38:36] **Would Marxism Have Emerged Without Marx?**

[事实] A listener asks whether communist ideology was inevitable in Europe even if Marx had not lived.

[事实] Dominic says some form of Marxism or communism would likely have emerged because Marx built on thinkers such as Saint-Simon, Fourier, Robert Owen, Proudhon, and Bakunin.

[事实] He adds that Marx had enormous intellectual ability and was a strong propagandist, with *The Communist Manifesto* described as a great read.

[事实] Tom suggests Marx’s distinctive contribution may have been a sharper hostility to religion than some other communist thinkers had.

[40:15] **Mensheviks, Bolsheviks, and Revolution**

[事实] A listener asks whether the Mensheviks could have launched the Russian Revolution instead of the Bolsheviks.

[事实] The hosts say this was unlikely, because the Bolsheviks were more willing to be brutal and more extreme.

[事实] Dominic notes that the Bolsheviks gained support in 1917 because they offered peace while rivals remained committed to the war.

[事实] The hosts compare this with Stalin defeating Trotsky by appearing to offer stability against Trotsky’s permanent revolution.

[42:13] **Could Non-Communist Russia Have Defeated Nazi Germany?**

[事实] A listener asks whether a non-communist Russia could have resisted and defeated Nazi Germany.

[事实] Dominic discusses Sean McMeekin’s argument for viewing the Second World War through Stalin and Russia, while rejecting McMeekin’s claim that the Allies should have fought Stalin as well as Hitler.

[事实] Dominic says communist control may have helped the Soviet state discipline millions and accept immense sacrifice.

[事实] Tom and Dominic also note that harsh treatment of soldiers and deep violence predated Stalinism in Russian history.

[45:24] **Technology, Science Fiction, and Future Communism**

[事实] A listener asks whether technological abundance might make a communist system inevitable in 100 or 1000 years.

[事实] Dominic says there is a lot of communism in science fiction and discusses a group he calls the Positists, who linked communism with technological modernity and advanced extraterrestrial civilizations.

[事实] Dominic rejects the idea that communism will become inevitable through technology, saying he finds it implausible and against human nature.

[推测] The closing exchange returns to the episode’s main skepticism: abundance alone may not remove conflict, ambition, or disagreement.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s main value is its wide historical range. It does not treat communism only as a 20th-century state ideology, but connects it to early Christianity, medieval and Reformation radicalism, 19th-century science, Marxism-Leninism, Stalinism, and contemporary cultural politics.

Its strongest thread is the argument that communism functions like a political religion: a system of meaning, redemption, heresy, sacrifice, and promised transformation. The hosts make this argument through historical comparisons rather than through a narrow ideological debate.

[推测] The limitation is that the conversation is openly interpretive and skeptical, especially about whether communism can ever avoid repression when turned into state power. Listeners looking for a sympathetic account of communist theory may find the treatment partial, though the hosts do acknowledge the enduring appeal of justice, liberation, and equality.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in intellectual history, political religion, Marxism’s Christian echoes, and the relationship between utopian ideals and coercive power.
