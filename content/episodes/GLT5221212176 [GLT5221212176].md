# The Rest Is History: Top Ten Eunuchs

## 概览

This episode uses a “top ten eunuchs” format to examine how eunuchs appear across ancient, medieval, early modern, and modern history. The discussion repeatedly returns to the same tension: castration was brutal and often imposed through slavery, punishment, religious extremism, or family ambition, yet in some courts and professions it could also open routes to unusual power.

The hosts move from Alexander the Great’s Persian court to Nero’s Rome, Byzantine Constantinople, Ottoman Algiers, 18th-century opera, medieval Paris, Russian sectarian religion, and Ming China. Their examples show eunuchs as lovers, courtiers, treasurers, generals, singers, religious figures, and imperial servants.

A central conclusion is that eunuchs were historically common because rulers valued people who could not found dynasties or impregnate women, while some societies also associated eunuchs with beauty, loyalty, administration, or spiritual purity. The episode ends by stressing how unusual the modern world is in largely rejecting eunuch-making as acceptable.

## 分段落总结

[00:25] **The Eunuch Trade Across Medieval Europe**

[事实] The episode opens with a 10th-century example in which Slavic captives taken by German warriors were transported to Verdun, castrated by Jewish surgeons, and then sold into Islamic courts in Spain.

[事实] The hosts frame the subject as both grim and historically important, noting the suffering behind the jokes.

[推测] The opening example establishes the episode’s broader argument that eunuchs sat at the intersection of war, slavery, religion, medicine, and court politics.

[02:25] **Bagoas and Alexander the Great**

[事实] Bagoas is introduced as a Persian eunuch associated with Darius and later Alexander the Great after Alexander’s conquest of Persia.

[事实] The hosts explain that ancient empires used eunuchs at court because they could not have children and therefore were less likely to found rival dynasties.

[事实] Roman sources portray Bagoas as cunning and manipulative, while later romanticized portrayals cast him as loyal and loving toward Alexander.

[推测] The conflicting depictions of Bagoas reveal how eunuchs were often used as screens for other people’s anxieties about loyalty, sexuality, foreignness, and power.

[06:41] **Sporus, Nero, and Imperial Cruelty**

[事实] Sporus is described as a free boy whom Nero had castrated because he resembled Nero’s dead wife, Poppaea Sabina.

[事实] Sporus was dressed and treated as Poppaea, accompanied Nero, and was present around the time of Nero’s death.

[事实] After Nero, Sporus passed through the hands of other imperial figures before killing himself when Vitellius planned to use him in a violent arena spectacle.

[推测] Sporus’s story is presented less as a tale of career opportunity than as an extreme example of imperial power turning grief and desire into violence.

[11:19] **Why Courts Wanted Eunuchs**

[事实] The hosts list several reasons eunuchs were valued: they could guard harems, could not father children, could be seen as more loyal, and could preserve youthful beauty if castrated before puberty.

[事实] In Roman culture, eunuchs could also be viewed as sinister, foreign, or “oriental,” especially by more conservative figures.

[推测] This contradiction explains why eunuchs could be both trusted insiders and targets of suspicion in the same political cultures.

[13:40] **Boston Corbett and Religious Self-Castration**

[事实] Boston Corbett is identified as the man who shot and killed John Wilkes Booth, Abraham Lincoln’s assassin.

[事实] The hosts say Corbett worked as a hatter, likely suffered mercury poisoning, became intensely religious, and castrated himself with scissors after an encounter with prostitutes.

[事实] He later served in the Union Army but repeatedly clashed with authority, claiming divine instruction over military orders.

[推测] Corbett’s case shows castration as an act driven by religious obsession and mental instability rather than court service or economic strategy.

[17:27] **Origen and Christian Ambiguity**

[事实] The hosts discuss Origen, a major early Christian theologian from Alexandria, who was said by Eusebius to have castrated himself.

[事实] The scriptural basis cited is Matthew 19:12, which refers to people making themselves eunuchs for the kingdom of heaven.

[事实] The hosts express serious doubts about whether Origen actually did it, citing hostile church politics and Origen’s own later warning against literal self-castration.

[推测] Origen’s example illustrates how accusations or legends of castration could become part of theological reputation and polemic.

[21:05] **Samson Rowley, Hassan Aga, and Ottoman Algiers**

[事实] Samson Rowley was a 16th-century Norfolk merchant’s son captured from the ship Swallow, castrated, converted to Islam, and became known as Hassan Aga.

[事实] He rose to become treasurer of Algiers, an Ottoman-linked North African power.

[事实] The hosts connect Rowley to wider Elizabethan-Ottoman relations and to the large-scale movement of enslaved Europeans into the Islamic world.

[推测] Rowley’s career suggests that violent displacement could sometimes be followed by unexpected advancement inside imperial systems.

[27:16] **Narses and the Last Roman Triumph**

[事实] Narses was an Armenian eunuch in the Byzantine Empire who rose through the treasury at Constantinople and served Justinian.

[事实] During the Nika riots, he helped split the Blues from the Greens, enabling Justinian’s forces to crush the uprising.

[事实] Narses later became a successful general in Italy, defeated Gothic and Frankish enemies, and celebrated what the hosts call Rome’s last triumph.

[事实] He administered Italy effectively for years into old age.

[推测] Narses is presented as one of the strongest examples of eunuch status creating trust and access that made extraordinary political and military authority possible.

[32:27] **Farinelli and the Economics of Castrati**

[事实] Farinelli is introduced as the most famous castrato singer, born in 1705 in southern Italy.

[事实] The hosts say thousands of boys were castrated annually in 18th-century Italy, often by poor families hoping their sons would become lucrative singers.

[事实] Farinelli became a major European star, performed in Italy, Vienna, London, Versailles, and Madrid, and earned enormous sums.

[事实] Castrati continued into the recording era, with the last castrato singer retiring in 1913 and the papacy banning the practice in 1903.

[推测] Farinelli’s story exposes the cold economic logic behind some forms of castration: bodily harm was justified by families and institutions as an investment.

[36:39] **Peter Abelard and Heloise**

[事实] Peter Abelard is described as a brilliant medieval theologian, rhetorician, and celebrity teacher in Paris.

[事实] He began an affair with Heloise, the niece of Fulbert, and she became pregnant with a son named Astrolabe.

[事实] After a secret marriage and family anger, Abelard was attacked in his room and castrated.

[事实] He withdrew to monastic life, later returned to intellectual prominence, was accused of heresy, and remained linked to Heloise through their letters and burial legend.

[推测] Abelard differs from many other examples because castration did not create his public identity; it damaged and reshaped an already famous life.

[41:34] **Selivanov and the Skoptsy**

[事实] Kondrati Ivanovich Selivanov was an 18th-century Russian peasant and sectarian leader who persuaded large numbers of followers to undergo castration.

[事实] He claimed to be both the Messiah and the former Tsar Peter III.

[事实] His followers, called Skoptsy, believed sexual organs were signs of original sin and practiced forms of male castration and female breast removal.

[事实] The hosts say the sect may have reached around 100,000 members by the early 20th century and was later suppressed by the Soviet Union.

[推测] The Skoptsy show the most extreme religious version of the episode’s theme: castration as a path to spiritual purification and communal identity.

[45:23] **Zheng He and Chinese Imperial Eunuchs**

[事实] The hosts describe China as one of the great historical centers of eunuch power, with eunuchs present in bureaucracy for thousands of years.

[事实] Zheng He was a Muslim from Yunnan, captured and castrated by the Ming, then rose rapidly at the imperial court.

[事实] He led seven major voyages across the Indian Ocean as far as Africa, bringing back animals including ostriches, zebras, and a giraffe.

[事实] He fought pirates, forced the king of Ceylon to submit, died during one of the voyages, and was buried at sea.

[推测] Zheng He becomes the episode’s largest-scale example of how eunuch status could place someone at the center of imperial ambition and global exploration.

[48:22] **The End of Eunuchs and Modern Attitudes**

[事实] The hosts say the last imperial eunuch died in 1996.

[事实] They classify the episode’s examples into categories including religious figures, people whose eunuch status enabled advancement, and people caught in violent love or punishment stories.

[事实] They conclude that eunuchs were common across much of “civilized” history, while the modern world is unusual in largely not having them.

[推测] Their final discussion suggests that eunuch-making is now broadly treated as beyond acceptable limits, even though modern attitudes toward gender and surgery are more fluid in other respects.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its range: it avoids treating eunuchs as a single historical type and instead shows how different societies used, feared, exploited, romanticized, and empowered them.

[推测] Its strongest moments are the comparisons across cultures, especially between court eunuchs like Narses and Zheng He, religious cases like Origen and Selivanov, and tragic figures like Sporus and Abelard.

[推测] The limitation is tonal: the hosts acknowledge the suffering involved, but the comic style may feel uncomfortable given how many examples involve slavery, child mutilation, coercion, or sexual violence.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in political history, court culture, religious extremism, and the strange social roles created by power, rather than listeners looking for a narrow institutional history of eunuchs.
