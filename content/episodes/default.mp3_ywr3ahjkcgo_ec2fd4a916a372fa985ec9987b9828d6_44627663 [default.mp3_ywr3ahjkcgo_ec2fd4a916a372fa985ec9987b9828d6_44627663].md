# Planet Money Goes to Auction: The Business of a Book Deal

## 概览

This episode opens a Planet Money series on how the show’s own book came into being, using the project as a way to examine the hidden economics of publishing. The central question is how an idea becomes a marketable book, and what happens when editorial ambition has to pass through agents, proposals, publishers, auctions, and financial risk models.

The story follows executive producer Alex Goldmark, literary agents Laura Nolan and Jane von Mehren, writer Alex Maiassi, and Norton editor Tom Mayer. Their path shows that nonfiction publishing is not just about good ideas; it is also about audience, platform, comparable titles, advances, sales projections, and competitive bidding.

The key conclusion is that book publishing mixes art and commerce in a highly structured marketplace. Planet Money’s brand and existing audience made the book attractive, but the final deal still came down to auction strategy, publisher fit, and a choice between more upfront money from a Big Five publisher and Norton's educational distribution pitch.

## 分段落总结

[00:26] **The Hidden Economy Behind Books**

[事实] The host frames books as one of the most influential technologies, shaping which ideas reach mass audiences.

[事实] The episode uses the making of the Planet Money book to investigate the business machinery behind publishing.

[推测] The show positions the book industry as an example of how markets influence culture, not just consumer products.

[03:35] **Why Planet Money Considered a Book**

[事实] Executive producer Alex Goldmark says the book idea first came through literary agents retained by NPR to explore whether NPR properties could become books.

[事实] Planet Money had a history of participatory projects, including a T-shirt, a barrel of oil, a comic book, a record, and a board game.

[事实] Alex was initially lukewarm because the book idea started as a business opportunity rather than a reporting curiosity.

[05:44] **The Role of Literary Agents**

[事实] Laura Nolan and Jane von Mehren explain that literary agents help authors sell book ideas to publishers and usually earn about a 15% commission.

[事实] They had already worked on NPR-related books and saw potential in Planet Money because its audience had bought comic books and attended a live musical.

[推测] The agents viewed Planet Money less as a single book idea and more as a proven audience platform.

[07:47] **Finding a Broad Book Concept**

[事实] The agents argued that a Planet Money book could reach people in airports, libraries, schools, and other places where podcasts do not naturally travel.

[事实] Alex initially suggested narrower ideas, including pamphlets explaining specific industries.

[事实] The final concept became a field guide to the global economy, using reported stories about work, dating, investing, saving, and daily life.

[09:46] **Turning the Idea Into a Proposal**

[事实] The team needed a book proposal to show publishers what the book would be like and why it would be a good business bet.

[事实] Alex Maiassi contacted Planet Money around the same time and said he wanted to write a Planet Money book.

[事实] Over about a year, the two Alexes, Jane, and Laura developed sample writing, a chapter outline, and a business case.

[推测] The proposal functioned as both a creative blueprint and a sales document.

[11:32] **Seeding the Publishing Market**

[事实] Before sending the proposal out, Jane and Laura talked up the Planet Money book at industry lunches, drinks, and meetings.

[事实] They describe publishing as a small, connected world where information travels heavily through relationships and word of mouth.

[事实] The episode explains that publishing has consolidated into the Big Five, with many smaller imprints inside large corporate groups.

[13:39] **Consolidation and Risk**

[事实] The episode says consolidation has made profitability more important and pushed publishers toward less risky books.

[事实] Publishing has increasingly favored authors with platforms and built-in audiences, similar to Hollywood’s reliance on familiar intellectual property.

[事实] Planet Money’s proposal cited prior sales of nearly 17,000 comic books and more than 20,000 T-shirts.

[推测] Planet Money looked appealing because it reduced uncertainty for publishers compared with an unknown author.

[15:32] **How Publishers Think About Books**

[事实] Norton editor Tom Mayer says publishers buy book rights based on both what they like and what they think can be profitable.

[事实] He compares a publisher’s annual list to a basket of stocks, where some books succeed, some do okay, and some fail.

[事实] He says publishing is a power law business where 20% of books make 80% of the money.

[推测] The episode presents book acquisition as a portfolio decision even when editors resist thinking too mechanically.

[17:45] **Gatekeeping and Editorial Selectivity**

[事实] Tom says he receives roughly 500 proposals from reputable agents each year, gets excited about 30 or 40, and buys 10 or 12.

[事实] He estimates he signs up about 2% of the agent-submitted proposals he receives.

[事实] Editors generally do not read unsolicited proposals that do not come through vetted agents.

[推测] Access to trusted agents is portrayed as a major filter in whether a book idea reaches publishers.

[19:24] **Norton’s Interest and Valuation Problem**

[事实] Tom did not read the Planet Money proposal immediately, but after a reminder he felt it was a strong fit for Norton.

[事实] Norton had experience publishing books that explain business, finance, and economics to general readers.

[事实] Tom’s colleagues supported pursuing the book but questioned how many copies it would actually sell and whether Planet Money promotion would convert into sales.

[推测] The hardest internal question was not whether the book had appeal, but how much that appeal was worth.

[21:36] **Publisher Speed Dating**

[事实] Jane and Laura received requests from 23 publishers for meetings, which they describe as an unusually high number.

[事实] The team held 23 half-hour Zoom meetings over four days with prospective publishers.

[事实] Norton pitched a strategy involving college courseware and economics syllabi, while another publisher suggested a glossy, graphics-heavy version.

[推测] The meetings created competitive pressure and helped prepare the ground for an auction.

[24:59] **Designing the Book Auction**

[事实] The auction was conducted by email rather than as a live auction.

[事实] Jane and Laura strategically revealed that 23 publishers had shown interest to increase competitive urgency.

[事实] Tom describes several publishing auction formats, including round robin, one-round best bid, and two-round best bid.

[事实] Jane and Laura chose a two-round best bid structure, sometimes likened to a wedding cake because bidders are narrowed by tiers.

[29:09] **Modeling the Bid**

[事实] Tom and his team used sales projections and comparable titles to estimate demand.

[事实] The bid had to account for manufacturing, marketing, and especially the advance against royalties.

[事实] A larger advance means the publisher must sell more copies before the book becomes profitable.

[事实] Norton had to be careful because it is employee owned and was competing against larger conglomerates with deeper pockets.

[32:05] **The First Round of Bidding**

[事实] The bidding window opened Tuesday, March 28, 2023, and closed the next morning at 11 a.m.

[事实] Jane and Laura received 16 bids, which the episode describes as high for a book auction.

[事实] The offers included not only advances but also rights packages involving translation and international markets.

[事实] About 10 editors advanced to the second round after the agents compared the packages.

[35:06] **Winner’s Curse and the Second Round**

[事实] Norton’s first-round bid was the highest and became the new floor for the next round.

[事实] Tom worried about the winner’s curse, the idea that auction winners may overpay when competition is intense and valuation is difficult.

[事实] In the second round, bids remained close because publishers improved their offers by roughly similar amounts.

[推测] The auction forced publishers to balance enthusiasm with fear of overcommitting.

[37:26] **The Beauty Contest**

[事实] When financial offers were close, the auction entered what Tom calls the “beauty contest,” where chemistry, vision, and strategy matter.

[事实] The final choice came down to Norton and one Big Five publisher.

[事实] The Big Five publisher offered more upfront money, a strong publicity push, and distribution into airports and independent bookstores.

[事实] Norton offered slightly less money but proposed full color and a strategy to bring the book into educational courseware and textbooks.

[39:02] **Choosing Norton**

[事实] Alex Goldmark found Norton’s educational pitch compelling because Planet Money has a mission to help people understand the economy.

[事实] Alex had to choose between more upfront money from the Big Five publisher and Norton’s potentially longer-term educational strategy.

[事实] After several days of deliberation, Alex chose Norton.

[推测] The choice suggests mission fit outweighed the highest immediate advance.

[40:04] **The Deal and What Came Next**

[事实] Tom celebrated when Jane and Laura told him Norton had won the book.

[事实] The exact winning bid was not disclosed, but Alex Goldmark says the deal was seven figures, and the episode says it was more than $1 million and less than $2 million.

[事实] After the deal, Jane and Laura received the first installment of their commission.

[事实] The agents then handed the project over to the editor, shifting the work from selling the book to making the book.

[43:33] **From Book Deal to Bookmaking**

[事实] The episode ends by noting that winning the deal was only the start, because the team then had to figure out how to actually write the book.

[事实] The next episode is framed as an exploration of how to make a book after the contract is signed.

[事实] The episode also promotes preorders, a poster incentive, live events, and the book website.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] This episode is valuable because it turns Planet Money’s own book deal into a concrete case study of publishing economics. Instead of explaining advances, auctions, and publisher consolidation abstractly, it follows a real proposal through agents, editors, meetings, bids, and final selection.

[推测] The strongest part is the access to both sides of the market: the agents trying to create demand and the editor trying to price risk. That makes the episode useful for listeners interested in books, media businesses, creative labor, or how cultural products become financial bets.

[推测] A limitation is that some key numbers remain undisclosed, especially the exact advance and detailed bid comparisons. The episode gives a range for the final deal, but listeners looking for a full financial breakdown will not get complete transparency.

[推测] The episode is best suited for listeners who enjoy behind-the-scenes business stories and for writers or media workers who want to understand how nonfiction book deals are packaged, pitched, auctioned, and sold.
