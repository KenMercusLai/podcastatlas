# A Very Terminator Christmas

## Overview

This episode reconstructs the improbable making of the 1992 television remake of *Christmas in Connecticut*, directed by Arnold Schwarzenegger immediately after *Terminator 2*. What began as a modest update of a 1945 screwball comedy became a star-driven experiment mixing romance, action, and Schwarzenegger’s outsized comic instincts.

Through interviews with the producer, writers, executives, and crew, the episode traces the project from Schwarzenegger’s surprise involvement through chaotic production and unusually lavish premieres. The resulting film was critically mixed and technically awkward, yet its collaborators remember making it with genuine affection.

The larger conclusion is that creative value cannot always be measured by prestige or polish. Schwarzenegger’s friendship with producer Stan Brooks endured, later contributing indirectly to California’s film-production tax-credit campaign.

## Segment Summary

[02:17] **Two Very Different Christmas Traditions**

[Fact] Malcolm Gladwell contrasts his family’s minimalist Christmas celebrations with colleague Ben Naddaff-Hafrey’s elaborate family traditions.

[Fact] After learning that Gladwell had never seen *It’s a Wonderful Life*, Ben introduces an even stranger Christmas story: the making of an obscure 1990s television movie.

[03:54] **The Forgotten Schwarzenegger Christmas Movie**

[Fact] Ben identifies the movie as a remake of *Christmas in Connecticut*, directed by Arnold Schwarzenegger.

[Fact] The original 1945 comedy follows a celebrated homemaking columnist who cannot cook, does not live on a Connecticut farm, and must sustain her deception while hosting a returning war hero.

[Fact] Ben interviewed roughly a dozen people to determine why Schwarzenegger chose this unlikely project.

[06:54] **A Modest Remake Takes Shape**

[Fact] Producer Stan Brooks obtained permission from TNT to remake a title from the MGM library during the early cable-television boom.

[Fact] He reconceived the heroine as a Martha Stewart-like television personality whose domestic empire is fraudulent.

[Fact] Janet Brownell wrote an initial script close in spirit to the original, and Dyan Cannon was cast in the lead.

[09:08] **Schwarzenegger Makes an Unexpected Offer**

[Fact] Agent Lou Pitt called Brooks and proposed Schwarzenegger as director shortly after the actor completed *Terminator 2*.

[Fact] Schwarzenegger wanted a low-budget, family-friendly project where failure would carry limited risk.

[Fact] TNT approved the arrangement and offered him $100,000 to direct.

[12:03] **The Script Becomes “Schwarzeneggerized”**

[Fact] Schwarzenegger asked for more humor and danger, including a new action sequence in which the male lead rescues a child.

[Fact] *Commando* writer Jeph Loeb and his writing partner were hired to revise the dialogue and pursue Schwarzenegger’s vision.

[Fact] Schwarzenegger also sought advice from directors including Ivan Reitman and Steven Spielberg.

[Fact] Brownell felt the project had turned into something very different, though the later writers said its basic structure remained hers.

[18:22] **An Unlikely Cast and a High-Risk Production**

[Fact] Dyan Cannon was joined by Tony Curtis and Kris Kristofferson, a combination that TNT executive Nick Lombardo considered exceptionally strange.

[Fact] The production had a $3 million budget, a rapidly revised script, and a first-time feature director coming directly from a blockbuster action film.

[Fact] Despite Schwarzenegger’s fame, the small television-movie budget made the experiment financially significant for TNT.

[22:06] **Filming Begins with an Unconvincing Blizzard**

[Fact] Production began in Los Angeles about two months before Christmas and filmed largely in story order to simplify continuity for the inexperienced director.

[Fact] The opening rescue sequence was shot on a soundstage but failed to achieve the epic quality Schwarzenegger wanted.

[Fact] A crew member attributed the scene’s awkward staging to inexperience, limited set space, and poor blocking.

[24:33] **Controlled Chaos Becomes Uncontrolled**

[Fact] Most filming took place at a South Pasadena house standing in for the heroine’s Connecticut home.

[Fact] Dyan Cannon objected that Schwarzenegger’s enormous trailer was bigger than hers, highlighting the unusual balance of power between star and director.

[Fact] The episode argues that screwball comedy demands precisely controlled chaos, while this production had a large-personality cast, little time, a small budget, and a hastily rewritten script.

[Fact] Kristofferson’s character combined an unexplained academic past with a performance described as stiff and oddly sexualized.

[27:25] **The Baby-Bath Scene Goes Wrong**

[Fact] The original film uses a baby-bathing scene to expose the heroine’s fraud and establish the male lead’s competence and romantic appeal.

[Fact] In the remake, the actors cover the baby in shampoo without properly rinsing it, even though the script treats the bath as a success.

[Fact] Interviewees called the sequence clumsy and identified the choice of take as a directing failure.

[Fact] Other visible flaws included wheels beneath a sleigh and chaotic staging around a falling Christmas tree.

[31:01] **A Flawed Shoot Remembered Fondly**

[Fact] Despite the disorder, most interviewees described the production as a happy memory and appreciated its sheer improbability.

[Fact] Schwarzenegger worked energetically on the small film while *Terminator 2* was earning extraordinary theatrical revenue.

[Fact] His recurring insult “forehead” entered both the movie and the crew’s wrap-party sweatshirts.

[Fact] The shoot finished after 20 days.

[32:43] **Why the Movie’s Oddness Still Works**

[Fact] Ben describes the film as improvisatory, self-referential, sweet, and joyful despite its technical weaknesses.

[Fact] Crew members valued the opportunity to work with Schwarzenegger and veteran actor Tony Curtis.

[Inference] The film’s enduring appeal comes less from disciplined execution than from the visible enthusiasm of the people making it.

[36:27] **The Accidental Origin of “Cooking for Two”**

[Fact] Years later, Ben arranged for author Mitch Albom to perform the movie’s closing song during a live interview with Gladwell.

[Fact] Albom had been producer Stan Brooks’s college roommate and wrote the food-themed song on roughly two days’ notice because the production could not afford its preferred track.

[Fact] Albom’s wife recorded the version selected for the movie.

[41:12] **A Television Movie Gets a Blockbuster Premiere**

[Fact] The production received an unusually elaborate Los Angeles screening with red carpets, press, packed theaters, and prominent filmmakers in attendance.

[Fact] Brownell cried during the screening because she thought the finished movie was bad and her name remained attached to it.

[Fact] TNT promoted the film heavily and premiered it in April 1992, shortly before Easter rather than Christmas.

[Fact] Schwarzenegger later arranged another high-profile screening in Washington attended by political figures.

[44:19] **Mixed Reviews, Lasting Affection**

[Fact] The movie received mixed reviews and did not establish Schwarzenegger as a director.

[Fact] Ben says he does not consider it a good film but loves its weirdness, commitment, and joyful quality.

[Fact] He frames the story behind the movie as one of friendship between Schwarzenegger and Brooks, who remained close after production.

[45:35] **From Christmas Movie to California Policy**

[Fact] After becoming governor of California, Schwarzenegger appointed Brooks to the California Film Commission.

[Fact] Brooks helped lobby for tax credits intended to counter film production leaving California, including by making a documentary about affected families.

[Fact] California’s first such credits passed in 2009 and were subsequently renewed.

[47:05] **The Gift of Making Things**

[Fact] Brooks told Schwarzenegger that he preferred producing several modest movies each year to waiting years between prestigious features.

[Fact] Ben connects that philosophy to his own pleasure in constructing this unusually detailed account of an obscure remake.

[Fact] The episode closes with a passage from *A Christmas Carol* about accepting others’ laughter and finding satisfaction in one’s own joyful heart.

[Inference] Schwarzenegger’s experiment matters not because it produced a classic, but because the collaborators found friendship and fulfillment in making it.

## Podcast Commentary/Summary

The episode’s greatest strength is its transformation of an obscure production anecdote into a layered story about ambition, friendship, and creative pleasure. Firsthand recollections from writers, executives, and crew members give the account both credibility and comic energy.

Its close examination of failed scenes is especially effective because the criticism remains affectionate. The contrast between Schwarzenegger’s blockbuster stature and the remake’s visibly limited resources makes the production history more memorable than the movie itself.

The episode is less useful as a detailed analysis of the original *Christmas in Connecticut* or Schwarzenegger’s complete directing career. Its argument also depends heavily on fond retrospective memories, which may soften conflicts surrounding the production.

It will appeal most to listeners interested in Hollywood history, cult films, creative failure, and stories about projects whose human legacy exceeds their artistic reputation.
