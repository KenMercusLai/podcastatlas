# Toast: How Aman Narang Turned a Slow Dinner Check Into a Restaurant Platform

## 概览

This episode traces how Aman Narang and his co-founders moved from a small consumer payment app idea to building Toast, a broader operating platform for restaurants. The original frustration was simple: diners wanted to pay and leave faster, while restaurants wanted to turn tables more efficiently.

The first app did not gain traction, but it revealed a deeper problem: restaurant point-of-sale systems were clunky, fragmented, expensive, hard to update, and central to nearly every part of restaurant operations. Toast’s pivot was to replace or unify much of that system with a cloud-based, restaurant-specific platform.

The conversation follows Toast through early product failures, investor skepticism, painful customer support issues, a CEO transition, COVID-era crisis, public listing, and Aman’s return as CEO. A recurring theme is that the company’s advantage came from staying close to restaurant operators and building for the messy details of busy restaurants.

## 分段落总结

[00:50] **The Core Opportunity**

[事实] The host introduces Toast as a point-of-sale service born from the frustration of waiting for a dinner check.

[事实] The episode frames restaurants as a large but difficult market: thin margins, mission-critical systems, and low willingness to switch software.

[事实] Toast is described as supporting nearly one in five restaurants in America and generating more than $2 billion in annual revenue.

[推测] The episode positions Toast’s success as less about one payment feature and more about discovering a much larger infrastructure problem inside restaurants.

[03:54] **Endeca and the Co-Founder Foundation**

[事实] Aman joined Endeca in 2006 after earning a master’s degree in computer science from MIT.

[事实] Endeca’s core business was e-commerce search, and Aman worked on an internal business intelligence effort that felt like a startup inside the company.

[事实] Aman met Steve Fredette at Endeca, and Steve Fredette and John Graham later became Toast co-founders.

[推测] The difficult early work at Endeca gave the founders shared experience with product-market fit struggles before they started Toast.

[05:48] **The Mobile Shift Before Toast**

[事实] When the iPhone emerged, Aman and Steve saw mobile as a major technology shift and explored building mobile commerce apps.

[事实] They built mobile apps for brands on the side before Steve Papa encouraged them to build Endeca’s mobile business internally.

[事实] The mobile business became a meaningful part of Endeca’s revenue before Oracle acquired Endeca around the end of 2011.

[推测] This period shaped the founders’ belief that new platforms, such as mobile and Android, could open opportunities in old markets.

[07:43] **Leaving After the Oracle Acquisition**

[事实] After Oracle acquired Endeca, Steve Fredette quit first, then John Graham quit, while Aman initially hesitated because the team had not settled on a specific idea.

[事实] The founders explored ideas in local commerce and were aware of companies such as LevelUp, Groupon, and Square.

[事实] Aman says the acquisition gave them enough money to avoid worrying about salary for a couple of years.

[推测] The acquisition reduced personal financial pressure and made it easier for the founders to take startup risk.

[09:40] **The Dinner Check Frustration**

[事实] The first concrete idea came from Firebrand Saints, a bar near their workplace in Kendall Square, where customers could wait more than ten minutes for the check.

[事实] The founders asked whether they could build an app that let diners pay from their phones.

[事实] They convinced Gary Stark, the restaurant owner, to let them try the idea.

[推测] The initial concept appealed because it solved a visible consumer annoyance while also promising restaurants faster table turnover.

[12:02] **The First App**

[事实] The first app took a couple of months to build.

[事实] The app identified the table, pulled check data from the restaurant’s point-of-sale system, and let users pay with a card on file while adjusting the tip.

[事实] Steve Fredette and John Graham initially wrote the code, while Aman says he contributed more after the pivot to point of sale.

[推测] The app’s dependence on existing POS data made integration the hidden hard part of what seemed like a simple payment experience.

[13:29] **Why the First Product Failed**

[事实] The team launched the app at Firebrand Saints with friends and family, and about 50 people may have paid with it during the launch event.

[事实] Aman says the app was buggy and built on unreliable infrastructure that was not designed for cloud connectivity.

[事实] After the launch event, the team could not generate meaningful ongoing consumer usage.

[推测] The product failed because it required both consumer behavior change and deep restaurant-system integration before the experience was reliable enough.

[14:30] **Discovering the Real POS Problem**

[事实] At the time, many restaurants used on-premise POS systems with local servers and hardware inside the restaurant.

[事实] These systems handled many workflows, including table service, counter service, bar tabs, drive-throughs, kitchen routing, employee clock-ins, schedules, and restaurant-grade hardware.

[事实] Aman says the team underestimated how much existing POS systems did and how mission-critical they were.

[推测] The founders’ early naivete helped them enter the market, but the real opportunity only became clear after they confronted operational complexity.

[16:56] **The Pivot From Payment App to Platform**

[事实] Restaurant owners were not strongly persuaded by the faster-checkout value proposition.

[事实] The app was integrated with one POS system, and scaling it would have required integrations with many different providers.

[事实] By early 2013, after several months of trying, the team realized restaurant owners disliked their point-of-sale systems and were more willing to discuss the broader technology platform.

[推测] The failed app became a customer discovery tool that redirected Toast toward a much larger and more defensible market.

[20:25] **The Finale Desserts Insight**

[事实] Aman recalls speaking with Chris Kane of Finale Desserts, who showed how many disconnected systems were needed to run his restaurants.

[事实] The restaurant used separate systems for POS, payments, loans, accounting, inventory, online ordering, gift cards, payroll, and scheduling.

[事实] Online orders were still coming into the restaurant through a fax machine in 2013.

[事实] Because other vendors did not want to integrate with Toast, the team decided to build as much of the system as possible themselves.

[推测] This conversation helped define Toast as an integrated restaurant operating system rather than a narrow POS replacement.

[22:58] **Choosing a Restaurant-Specific Strategy**

[事实] Toast deliberately chose to focus on restaurants instead of building a horizontal system for many types of small businesses.

[事实] The team targeted busier restaurants that were typically served by systems such as Micros, NCR, and PosiTouch.

[事实] The goal was to build a purpose-built platform for restaurants from day one.

[推测] Specialization became Toast’s wedge against larger incumbents and broader small-business platforms.

[23:43] **Early Fundraising and Skepticism**

[事实] Aman quit in early 2013, around the time Toast pivoted from the app to point of sale.

[事实] Steve Papa invested about $500,000 to help the company get started.

[事实] Institutional investors were skeptical because restaurants had low margins, were not seen as tech-forward, and already had large incumbent vendors.

[推测] Toast’s fundraising challenge reflected a mismatch between investor assumptions about restaurants and the founders’ direct customer learnings.

[26:29] **First Customers and Basement Operations**

[事实] In 2013, Toast focused on talking to restaurant owners and finding early customers.

[事实] The team worked from Aman’s unfinished basement to save money, eventually fitting around ten people there.

[事实] Toast’s first customer went live in July 2013.

[推测] The basement phase forced the team to stay close to implementation details and customer pain rather than operate at a distance.

[28:27] **The First Live Install Breaks**

[事实] The first restaurant customer was Dwell Time in Cambridgeport.

[事实] Within about 20 minutes of taking the first few orders, the system went down.

[事实] The team manually wrote orders on paper, took them to the kitchen, and wrote down credit card numbers.

[事实] Aman says the experience taught them that restaurant systems are mission-critical and cannot break.

[推测] This failure likely sharpened Toast’s understanding that reliability and support mattered as much as product features.

[30:01] **Convincing Restaurants to Switch**

[事实] Aman says he pitched hundreds of restaurant owners in the first couple of years, and very few said yes.

[事实] New restaurants were easier targets because they already had to set up a system.

[事实] Toast’s pitch included low or no upfront cost, a SaaS fee, and features that staff and owners liked.

[事实] The team coded, installed, trained, and gathered feedback inside restaurants.

[推测] Toast’s early sales motion depended on high-touch service because the product category required trust and operational change.

[31:39] **Expanding Beyond Boston**

[事实] Toast initially stayed in Boston because restaurant sales, service, and installation had to happen in person.

[事实] Around early 2014, Steve Papa helped connect Toast with Gordon Food Service, a food distributor based in Grand Rapids, Michigan.

[事实] The GFS partnership helped Toast launch outside Boston, including Miami and Chicago.

[推测] Distribution partnerships gave Toast a way to scale beyond its local network before it had a mature national sales organization.

[33:27] **Competition and Execution Limits**

[事实] Aman says customer feedback that Toast was the best cloud solution reinforced his belief that the company was onto something.

[事实] He says he prefers customer obsession over competitor obsession.

[事实] After reaching around 100 customers and gaining interest through the GFS partnership, Toast realized it was not ready to scale.

[事实] Early support used a Google Voice number that rang Aman’s phone.

[推测] At that stage, Toast’s biggest constraint was not demand or competitors but operational readiness.

[36:56] **Chris Comparato and Scaling Discipline**

[事实] By early 2015, Toast had grown to a few hundred customers but was struggling with hardware returns, outages, downtime, and unhappy support experiences.

[事实] Aman says he had been too focused on revenue growth and not enough on building systems to scale.

[事实] Toast brought in Chris Comparato, whom the founders knew from Endeca, as CEO.

[事实] Aman says his day-to-day work did not change much because the team was focused on survival rather than titles.

[推测] Chris’s arrival marked a shift from founder-driven hustle toward more structured customer experience and operations.

[39:51] **The COVID Crisis**

[事实] Between 2015 and 2020, Toast had been growing more than 100% annually.

[事实] In early April 2020, Aman says revenue was down more than 90% because restaurants were closed and not processing payments.

[事实] Toast cut about 55% of its team.

[事实] Customers told Toast they needed better tools for off-premise sales, which led to QR-code menu, ordering, and payment capabilities.

[推测] COVID forced Toast to return to a startup-like level of focus while serving a much larger base of employees and customers.

[42:29] **Going Public and Aman Becoming CEO**

[事实] Toast went public in 2021.

[事实] Aman became CEO in 2024 after Chris Comparato’s long tenure.

[事实] Aman says he became comfortable stepping into the role because of his history, context, and passion for the business.

[事实] He also wanted key people around him committed to the next phase of the company.

[推测] Aman’s return as CEO reflects continuity rather than a clean break from the previous leadership era.

[43:56] **Growth, AI, and the Next 80 Percent**

[事实] Aman says Toast has more than 20% of small-business restaurants and an even larger share of sales volume because Toast restaurants are busier than average.

[事实] Toast launched a “Built for Busy” marketing campaign.

[事实] Aman says Toast IQ Grow is part of the company’s AI platform and that customers who switched to it saw sales rise 8%.

[事实] He describes opportunities to use Toast’s data to improve decisions around marketing, purchasing, suppliers, pricing, and staffing.

[推测] Toast’s next growth argument is that its data can make independent restaurants operate more like sophisticated chains.

[46:20] **Moat, Expansion, and Culture**

[事实] Aman says Toast must preserve the hunger, scrappiness, and customer centricity that helped it succeed.

[事实] Toast has launched in Canada, the UK, Dublin, Ireland, and has a team in Australia.

[事实] The company is also building products for retail categories such as grocery stores, convenience stores with gas stations, liquor stores, and hardware stores.

[推测] Toast is trying to protect its position by expanding both geographically and into adjacent local-business categories.

[48:20] **Work, Luck, and Timing**

[事实] Aman credits much of Toast’s success to the skills, values, and work of the team, including Steve, John, Chris, and other leaders.

[事实] He also says luck mattered, including raising capital before COVID, launching at the right time for Android hardware, and benefiting from payments opening up in 2012 and 2013.

[事实] He says Chris joining at the right time helped Toast scale.

[推测] The episode’s final lesson is that execution mattered most when combined with favorable platform shifts and timely strategic breaks.

[49:51] **Full-Circle Closing**

[事实] Firebrand Saints, the restaurant where the original payment-app idea began, closed in 2017.

[事实] It was replaced by Shybird, a rotisserie chicken restaurant.

[事实] When the show contacted Shybird, the restaurant said it used Toast.

[推测] The closing detail reinforces the episode’s narrative arc: the idea that began in one restaurant eventually became part of the infrastructure used by its successor.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode is valuable because it shows how a failed narrow product can uncover a much bigger business. The strongest part is the detailed account of how Toast moved from consumer convenience to operational infrastructure by listening to restaurant owners.

The conversation is also useful for founders building in complex, low-margin industries. It highlights that customer pain alone is not enough; reliability, support, distribution, switching costs, and timing can determine whether the product can actually scale.

[推测] The episode is less focused on financial mechanics and competitive strategy than on founder learning and company-building moments. Listeners looking for deep analysis of Toast’s economics, margins, or public-market performance may find those areas only lightly covered.

[推测] It is especially suited for entrepreneurs, product builders, restaurant-tech operators, and anyone interested in how vertical SaaS companies discover and expand their wedge.
