# The Alabama Murders, Part 1: The True Church

## Overview

Psychologist Kate Porterfield introduces the story of Kenny, a death-row prisoner who survived a botched execution and initially spoke to her not about the ordeal, but about the love he received while saying goodbye to his family. His experience opens a seven-part investigation into a decades-long chain of crime, punishment, and compounded suffering.

The first episode moves to the Shoals region of northwestern Alabama and examines the close-knit Church of Christ community in which preacher Charles Sennett lived. The church offered intellectual seriousness, simplicity, and belonging, but its emphasis on rules, certainty, and public discipline could also generate fear and overwhelming shame.

Sennett’s outward success concealed affairs, mental illness, financial trouble, an abusive marriage, and a growing personal crisis. The episode argues that this concealed contradiction became the starting point of a “failure cascade” driven by failures of character, justice, and compassion.

## Segment-by-segment summary

[01:04] **A Death-Row Story Begins with Love**

[Fact] Malcolm Gladwell met psychologist Kate Porterfield, whose work brought her into contact with Kenny, a man on death row.

[Fact] When Porterfield visited Kenny two months after a failed execution attempt, he spent hours describing his loving goodbyes and the support of his family before discussing the execution itself.

[Fact] Gladwell frames the series around two questions: why the case took so many disturbing turns, and why a system intended to answer suffering so often creates more suffering.

[02:50] **The Botched Execution and a Longer Chain of Violence**

[Fact] Kenny’s failed execution was punishment for a crime committed more than 30 years earlier.

[Fact] Gladwell says the underlying events had already claimed three lives and that his account draws on Porterfield’s story as well as other participants’ versions.

[Fact] The episode is the opening installment of a seven-part series titled *The Alabama Murders*.

[04:10] **The Shoals: An Insular Alabama Setting**

[Fact] The story takes place in the Shoals of northwestern Alabama, encompassing Sheffield, Tuscumbia, Muscle Shoals, and Florence along the Tennessee River.

[Fact] Florence has no interstate or major airport, and preacher Grant Asbell describes it as a place people must intentionally visit.

[Fact] Asbell recalls that allegations against preacher Charles Sennett were profoundly disruptive in this small religious community; another minister publicly defended Sennett as a faithful Christian brother.

[07:54] **The Church of Christ’s Distinctive World**

[Fact] The Shoals is described as a spiritual center of the Church of Christ, with many congregations near Florence.

[Fact] The denomination’s services are marked by a cappella singing, recurring devotional language, simple architecture, and the absence of organs or religious artwork.

[Fact] The Church of Christ is portrayed as a small, closely connected world in which personal and institutional relationships frequently overlap.

[10:56] **Rules, Certainty, and the “True Church”**

[Fact] Theologian Lee Camp explains that many members historically understood the Church of Christ not as one denomination among others, but as the restored New Testament church and therefore the “true church.”

[Fact] Gladwell characterizes the tradition as intellectually serious and centered on close biblical reading, rules, certainty, simplicity, and clarity.

[Fact] Camp says the church gave him love and shaped his life, while also causing harm through fear, exclusion, and the threat of being “disfellowshipped.”

[Fact] Practices described in the episode included strict restrictions on divorce, women’s participation in services, instrumental music, dancing, mixed-gender swimming, and clothing considered immodest.

[15:35] **Charles Sennett’s Rise and Collapse**

[Fact] Charles Sennett was the son of a Church of Christ preacher, married Elizabeth Darlene in 1962, and had two sons with her.

[Fact] After earning a doctorate in divinity, he became a successful preacher in Jasper, Alabama, where the congregation reportedly tripled in size.

[Fact] Rumors of an affair led church elders to fire him, after which he suffered a nervous breakdown, became suicidal, and spent weeks in a psychiatric hospital.

[Fact] Medical records described him as depressed, confused, agitated, lacking insight, and exercising poor judgment.

[18:16] **A New Church and Another Secret Relationship**

[Fact] Sennett eventually became minister of the small, working-class West Side Church of Christ in Sheffield, a step down from his earlier position.

[Fact] Former congregants remembered him as charismatic, generous, and quick to help sick or elderly people, although one also described him as having a “split personality.”

[Fact] Rumors circulated that Sennett was involved with a congregant named Doris; a book of poems in his office contained her photograph, and someone reportedly saw him give her a Valentine.

[Fact] The episode states that Sennett was in love with a woman who was not his wife.

[21:59] **Marriage, Debt, and a Closing Trap**

[Fact] In the winter of 1988, Elizabeth wanted a divorce, and Sennett told her, “I won’t lose another church.”

[Fact] He was $150,000 in debt after failed business ventures, his behavior became erratic, and he continued preaching while concealing the full state of his life.

[Fact] The episode describes him as volatile, abusive toward his wife, financially unstable, and engaged in an affair with a congregant.

[23:19] **How Dysfunction Survived Behind a Public Mask**

[Fact] Gladwell asks how someone so apparently unsuited to ministry could remain a preacher in a community intensely concerned with personal conduct.

[Fact] Minister Rodney Plunkett says Sennett worked enormously hard to keep his conduct hidden outside a tight circle of biological family.

[Fact] Lee Camp suggests that overwhelming shame, combined with the absence of a constructive understanding of grace, can have terrifying psychological consequences.

[Inference] The church’s culture of certainty and discipline may have encouraged Sennett to conceal his failures rather than admit them and seek help.

[26:12] **Grace, Forgiveness, and the Fear of Damnation**

[Fact] Gladwell defines Christian grace as unearned divine favor extended regardless of sin.

[Fact] According to the episode, many Church of Christ members later concluded that their church—especially 40 or 50 years earlier—had not adequately understood grace.

[Fact] Grant Asbell recounts that his uncle lost a church role after speaking too much about grace and forgiveness.

[Fact] Asbell describes growing up with the fear that an unrepented violation immediately before death might result in eternal damnation.

[Inference] Within that framework, Sennett may have seen no acceptable path toward divorce and a new relationship without losing both his community and, in his understanding, his salvation.

[30:04] **From One Failure to Catastrophe**

[Fact] The episode cites a joke that it was easier to receive forgiveness in the Church of Christ for murder than for divorce.

[Fact] Gladwell explains a “failure cascade” through the proverb “for want of a nail” and the 2003 Northeastern blackout, in which a small initial problem triggered progressively larger failures.

[Fact] He presents *The Alabama Murders* as a human failure cascade caused by failures of character, justice, and compassion rather than by mechanical defects.

[Inference] This framing positions Sennett’s hidden transgressions and the community’s response to them as the first links in a chain that will eventually encompass murder and capital punishment.

## Podcast Review/Summary

The episode’s main strength is its refusal to treat the coming crime as an isolated act. It patiently reconstructs the geography, theology, social bonds, and moral pressures surrounding Charles Sennett before describing the larger catastrophe.

Its use of Church of Christ insiders adds nuance: the tradition is presented as a source of love, learning, discipline, and belonging, while its historical rigidity and limited conception of grace are also subjected to serious criticism. The contrast between Sennett’s generous public persona and concealed private dysfunction creates a compelling foundation for the series.

[Inference] Because this is an introductory episode, it leaves the central murder and the legal case largely unresolved and relies at times on memories and acknowledged hearsay. It is best suited to listeners interested in true crime that emphasizes religious culture, institutional dynamics, psychology, and the cumulative causes of injustice rather than immediate procedural detail.
