# General Gordon of Khartoum: Slavery, Empire, and Martyrdom

## 概览

This episode continues the story of General Gordon, moving from his earlier fame as “Chinese Gordon” into his Sudan years and the final crisis at Khartoum. The hosts frame Sudan as a place where Egyptian rule, British financial influence, the East African slave trade, and evangelical anti-slavery politics all collided.

The discussion presents Gordon as sincere, brave, and passionately anti-slavery, but also erratic, religiously intense, and politically impractical. His Christian moral certainty made him a powerful Victorian symbol, yet also made him easy for governments, newspapers, military figures, and imperial projects to use.

The final arc follows the confused decision to send him back to Sudan in 1884, the siege of Khartoum, the delayed British relief expedition, Gordon’s death, and the creation of his martyr legend. The hosts use the story to examine wider contradictions in British imperialism: humanitarian purpose, strategic self-interest, press pressure, religious conflict, and later revenge.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **McGonagall’s Poem and Gordon’s Reputation**

[事实] The episode opens with verses from William McGonagall’s poem on General Gordon’s death at Khartoum, emphasizing Gordon’s piety, modesty, anti-slavery actions, and heroic image.

[事实] The hosts recap that the previous episode covered Gordon’s career up to his first journey to Sudan, including his fame as “Chinese Gordon” and his years in Gravesend working with boys.

[推测] The poem sets up the central theme of the episode: Gordon as a Victorian saintly hero whose legend is both moving and historically complicated.

[01:56] **Sudan, Egypt, Britain, and the Slave Trade**

[事实] The hosts describe Sudan as under Egyptian control, while Egypt itself is formally Ottoman but heavily influenced by Britain because of debt.

[事实] Sudan’s economy is said to depend heavily on slavery, and the East African slave trade remains strong while slavery is being abolished elsewhere.

[事实] The Khedive of Egypt presents anti-slavery as a goal, but the hosts argue he also wants to reassert control over Sudan.

[推测] Anti-slavery becomes both a genuine moral cause and a convenient justification for imperial intervention.

[04:05] **Christian Abolitionism and Moral Conflict with Islam**

[事实] Gordon is described as passionately opposed to slavery, with his anti-slavery commitment rooted in evangelical Christian conviction.

[事实] The hosts argue that Britain’s anti-slavery campaign was morally sincere but also became a tool that gave imperial intervention moral sanction.

[事实] They contrast Gordon’s inward evangelical understanding of God with Islamic legal traditions that had long treated slavery as legitimate.

[推测] The episode frames this as not only a political clash but a clash of moral and religious worldviews.

[06:24] **Gordon’s First Sudan Mission**

[事实] Gordon is first sent to Equatoria, roughly present-day South Sudan and northern Uganda, where Sudanese slave hunters prey on African populations.

[事实] By 1877, he becomes governor-general of the whole Sudan, a vast territory the hosts compare in scale to Western Europe.

[事实] Conditions are described as extreme, with disease, heat, insects, poor support, hostile inhabitants, and savage slave traders.

[事实] Gordon also opposes the ivory trade because it drives slavery and threatens elephants, leading the hosts to call him a conservationist as well as an anti-slavery figure.

[09:19] **Failure, Isolation, and Resignation**

[事实] Gordon’s efforts against slavery are portrayed as ultimately unsuccessful because stopping local routes does not end the market for slaves.

[事实] He reads the Bible for hours each day while working in lonely and harsh conditions.

[事实] He resigns around late 1879 or early 1880, saying he lacks the support he needs.

[推测] His failure in Sudan intensifies both his public celebrity and his sense of religious isolation.

[10:28] **Restless Appointments and Questions About Character**

[事实] Leopold II asks Gordon to serve in the Congo, while Gordon also briefly becomes private secretary to Lord Ripon, Viceroy of India.

[事实] Gordon hates the India role and resigns after being disturbed by the need to tell a polite lie about whether the viceroy would read a book of poetry.

[事实] The hosts discuss later claims that Gordon was drunk or sexually suspect, noting that many of these stories came from Evelyn Baring, who disliked him.

[事实] They conclude that Gordon probably was not generally a drunk, though he may have drunk brandy under stress.

[14:03] **Religious Eccentricity and Holy Geography**

[事实] Gordon becomes convinced in the Seychelles that Coco de Mer trees are linked to the Garden of Eden and the tree of knowledge.

[事实] He tells senior colonial and military figures these theories, leaving them bewildered.

[事实] In Jerusalem, he identifies a site outside the old city as Golgotha, now associated with the Garden Tomb tradition rather than the Church of the Holy Sepulchre.

[推测] The hosts treat these ideas as signs that Gordon’s solitude and religious intensity were pushing him toward eccentricity.

[16:48] **Leopold, the Congo, and W. T. Stead**

[事实] In 1884, Gordon is considering Leopold II’s offer to go to the Congo, where Leopold wants to use Gordon’s anti-slavery reputation to legitimize his project.

[事实] The hosts say Leopold’s real aim is to front a brutal private estate with a Christian and humanitarian image.

[事实] Gordon receives a telegram from journalist W. T. Stead, editor of the Pall Mall Gazette, asking to interview him.

[推测] Gordon may already be suspicious of Leopold’s intentions before the Sudan crisis interrupts the Congo plan.

[18:37] **Stead’s Spiritualism and the Sudan Interview**

[事实] The hosts digress into Stead’s spiritualist beliefs and a story involving his daughter Estelle, a ghost, and later communication after Stead’s death on the Titanic.

[事实] When Stead interviews Gordon, Gordon wants to discuss the Congo, but Stead redirects him to the crisis in Sudan.

[事实] Stead tells Gordon that the major news is the Sudan, where chaos and anti-slavery disruption have helped produce a great Islamist uprising.

[推测] The spiritualism digression reinforces the episode’s portrait of late Victorian Britain as a world where religion, empire, celebrity, and unusual beliefs overlapped.

[21:28] **The Mahdi and Apocalyptic Revolt**

[事实] Mohammed Ahmad claims to be the Mahdi, a figure associated with apocalyptic expectation and the coming transformation of the world.

[事实] The hosts compare the Mahdist uprising with the Taiping Rebellion, the Ghost Dance, and modern movements such as Al-Qaeda, the Taliban, and Islamic State.

[事实] Gordon does not view the Mahdi primarily as a religious figure, instead linking the uprising to slave hunters and local grievances.

[推测] The hosts suggest Gordon misunderstands the depth of the Mahdi’s religious appeal because he is fixated on slavery.

[25:29] **Gladstone, Liberal Ambivalence, and Press Pressure**

[事实] Britain has a Liberal government under Gladstone, whose party is strongly anti-slavery but also wary of intervention and military cost.

[事实] Stead publishes an interview titled “Chinese Gordon on the Sudan” and an editorial pushing “Chinese Gordon for the Sudan.”

[事实] Newspapers and street sellers amplify the call to send Gordon, turning him into the obvious public solution.

[事实] Garnet Wolseley, a senior army figure and Gordon’s friend, is described as wanting a Sudan expedition partly to enhance his own military position.

[29:44] **An Unclear Mission Becomes a Fatal One**

[事实] British ministers appear to think Gordon is being sent on a fact-finding mission, while Gordon seems to think he must evacuate Khartoum.

[事实] The hosts say another idea develops: that Gordon might go to Sudan and govern it himself.

[事实] The Queen supports sending Gordon, and he is quickly dispatched toward Sudan.

[事实] In Cairo, Evelyn Baring and the Khedive tell Gordon to go as governor-general, changing the meaning of the mission.

[推测] The hosts present this ambiguity as the point where the final disaster becomes likely.

[35:30] **Arrival at Khartoum**

[事实] Gordon travels south with Colonel Stewart and reaches Berber, where he learns the Mahdi’s forces have cut off the route from the Red Sea.

[事实] Gordon arrives in Khartoum on 18 February 1884.

[事实] He burns debtors’ books, destroys instruments of punishment, and performs public acts that present him as a reforming liberator.

[事实] He begins evacuating women and children, but also settles into the palace and sends telegrams filled with religious reflection.

[38:06] **No Allies, No Retreat**

[事实] Gordon asks for Zubair, a local warlord and slave trader, as the man he needs to help govern Sudan.

[事实] British anti-slavery campaigners and Liberal political concerns prevent Gordon from getting Zubair.

[事实] The Mahdi’s forces continue closing in, while Gordon refuses to leave Khartoum if it means abandoning others.

[事实] The Mahdi sends letters urging Gordon to surrender and embrace Islam, but Gordon refuses.

[推测] The anti-slavery cause that helps make Gordon famous also prevents him from using the ally he thinks might save the situation.

[41:22] **Siege and the Delayed Relief Force**

[事实] Gladstone delays sending a relief force, but eventually agrees, giving Wolseley the expedition he wanted.

[事实] Wolseley’s force only reaches Cairo in September 1884, after Gordon has already been in Khartoum for months.

[事实] The Mahdi’s army surrounds and bombards Khartoum, while Gordon organizes defenses, chains across rivers, and local military discipline.

[事实] As conditions worsen, Khartoum’s inhabitants are reduced to eating dogs, and the British relief column advances slowly because of supply and water needs.

[44:31] **The Fall of Khartoum and Gordon’s Death**

[事实] On 26 January 1885, Mahdist forces break into Khartoum after identifying a weakness in the defenses.

[事实] Thousands are massacred, and Gordon is killed and beheaded, though the exact circumstances of his death remain uncertain.

[事实] The Mahdi had wanted Gordon captured alive, but Gordon dies during the fall of the city.

[事实] British steamers arrive outside Khartoum two days later and do not land.

[推测] The timing of the relief force’s arrival intensifies Gordon’s later image as a betrayed martyr.

[46:58] **Public Shock and Blame**

[事实] Wolseley is recalled and sends a furious telegram to Queen Victoria blaming Gladstone.

[事实] Gladstone’s nickname “GOM” is inverted by critics into “MOG,” meaning “Murderer of Gordon.”

[事实] The hosts compare the reaction in Britain and beyond to a Princess Diana-style public emotional explosion.

[事实] Gordon is mourned as a Christian Western hero who was abandoned and betrayed at Khartoum.

[47:37] **Omdurman, Revenge, and Imperial Memory**

[事实] In 1898, the British return and destroy the Mahdist army at Omdurman, where Churchill is present and later writes about the campaign.

[事实] The hosts say the campaign is not really about avenging Gordon but about stopping the French.

[事实] Kitchener despoils the Mahdi’s tomb after the battle, and the hosts discuss stories of skulls and trophies connected to later officers.

[事实] The episode links this violence to Hilaire Belloc’s line about the Maxim gun and imperial military superiority.

[49:21] **Gordon as Saint, Symbol, and Problem**

[事实] Wolseley describes Gordon as an embodiment of manly and Christian virtues.

[事实] The hosts say Gordon became a saint-like figure across Britain and the English-speaking world.

[事实] Lytton Strachey later reacts against this heroic image, ending his account with bitter irony about Omdurman, slaughter, empire, and Evelyn Baring’s reward.

[事实] The hosts argue that Gordon’s legacy is more complicated than simple imperial jingoism because of his anti-slavery, conservationist, and humanitarian commitments.

[推测] They suggest Gordon’s modern heirs may be found among liberal interventionists and campaigners against slavery, looting, and wildlife destruction as much as among imperial nostalgists.

[53:15] **Closing Poem**

[事实] The episode ends with more verses from McGonagall’s poem, again presenting Gordon as pious, charitable, brave, betrayed, and finally rewarded in heaven.

[事实] The poem describes Gordon’s death in highly heroic and religious terms, matching the Victorian martyr image discussed throughout the episode.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its refusal to reduce Gordon either to a saint or to a simple imperial villain. It shows how a real anti-slavery conviction could coexist with imperial violence, strategic confusion, and religious certainty.

[推测] Its strongest sections are those linking Gordon’s personal psychology to wider systems: evangelical abolitionism, British press politics, Gladstonian hesitation, Egyptian rule, Islamic revolt, and later imperial revenge. The story works because the individual drama and the geopolitical context constantly illuminate each other.

[推测] A limitation is that the transcript gives much more space to British politicians, journalists, generals, and later British memory than to Sudanese perspectives inside the Mahdist movement or Khartoum itself. Listeners looking for a Sudan-centered account may find the framing heavily British.

[推测] The episode is especially suited to listeners interested in Victorian empire, religious politics, abolitionism, military failure, and the long roots of modern debates over humanitarian intervention.
