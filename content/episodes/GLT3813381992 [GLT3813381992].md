# The Roads to Thermopylae

## 概览

This episode sets up the Battle of Thermopylae by widening the frame from a simple “Greeks versus Persians” story to a contest shaped by empire, memory, propaganda, local rivalries, and Herodotus’ Greek source tradition.

The central argument is that Persia was not a shadowy eastern backdrop but the dominant imperial power of the age, while Greece was fragmented, peripheral, and politically unstable. The hosts repeatedly stress that modern assumptions about “the West” inheriting only from Greece miss how much the West also inherits from Persian ideas of moralized empire and global order.

Rather than reaching the battle itself, the episode traces the road to Thermopylae: the Ionian revolt, the burning of Sardis, the fall of Miletus, Marathon, Themistocles’ naval policy, Xerxes’ vast preparations, Greek divisions, Delphic prophecy, and the joint land-sea defensive plan at Thermopylae and Artemisium.

## 分段落总结

[01:20] **Herodotus and the Anniversary Frame**

[事实] The episode opens with Herodotus’ account of the Spartan dead at Thermopylae, including the famous inscription about obedience to Spartan law.

[事实] The hosts frame Thermopylae and Salamis as two major engagements of the Greco-Persian Wars in 480 BC.

[事实] Tom Holland says the episode is tied to the 2,500th anniversary of Salamis and to the way Herodotus’ history made the Greek confrontation with Persia foundational to historical writing.

[03:28] **Reframing Persia and Greece**

[事实] Holland says that as a child he identified strongly with Athens and Sparta, but later came to think that the modern West owes as much to Persia as to Greece.

[事实] Persia is described as the greatest power that had ever existed up to that point, ruling from the Aegean to Central Asia and the Indus, and commanding older civilizations such as Egypt and Babylon.

[推测] The episode’s interpretive aim is to unsettle the familiar Hellenocentric version of the Persian Wars by taking Persian power and ideology seriously.

[08:50] **Herodotus, Perspective, and Custom**

[事实] The hosts discuss how maps and narratives often center Greece, even though Persia would have been the central power for much of the Eurasian world.

[事实] Holland uses Herodotus’ story of Darius questioning Greeks and Indians about funeral customs to illustrate the idea that “custom is king.”

[事实] Herodotus is presented as a Greek writer who could nevertheless imagine the world through Persian eyes, partly because he came from Halicarnassus, a city under Persian rule.

[12:43] **The Problem of Sources**

[事实] The hosts note that the events of Thermopylae, Salamis, and the Persian Wars are known through Greek sources, not Persian narrative accounts.

[事实] Holland says there is not a single Persian mention of these events in surviving Persian material.

[推测] This source imbalance means Xerxes and the Persian campaign are inevitably filtered through Greek literary, political, and moral assumptions.

[13:09] **Persian Victory Narrative**

[事实] Holland says the Persian defeat was real, but the Persian king could probably present the campaign as successful because there were no modern media or public documentation systems.

[事实] A later Greek writer, Dio, is cited as possibly preserving an echo of Persian propaganda: Xerxes defeated the Spartans at Thermopylae, killed Leonidas, captured and burned Athens, imposed tribute, and returned to Asia.

[推测] From a Persian imperial viewpoint, the campaign could be framed less as humiliation and more as punishment and restoration of order.

[14:56] **Persian Imperial Ideology**

[事实] Holland says the Persians were the first people to moralize empire, dividing the world into truth and lie, light and darkness.

[事实] Persian kingship is described as impersonal and ideological: the king of kings embodies the essence of kingship rather than appearing as a gossipy individual figure.

[推测] This helps explain why Persian royal records did not produce the kind of narrative history that Greek and later Roman writers did.

[16:47] **Why Persia Attacked Greece**

[事实] The trigger is traced to instability along imperial borders and especially to the Ionian revolt against Persian rule in the 490s BC.

[事实] The Ionian Greek cities on the coast of Asia Minor sought help first from Sparta and then from Athens.

[事实] Sparta declined to cross the sea, while Athens, newly energized by democracy after expelling Hippias, agreed to help.

[19:44] **Ionian Revolt and Athenian Radicalism**

[事实] The hosts describe Ionian rebellion as a mix of resentment over tribute, anti-tyrant politics, elite ambitions, and broader instability.

[事实] Athens’ democratic system is described as rooted in the demos’ power and in a supernatural sense of community tied to Attica, Athena, and the soil.

[推测] Athenian support for the Ionians reflected both political sympathy and the confidence of a newly revolutionary state.

[21:38] **Sardis, Miletus, and Shame**

[事实] Athenian forces joined an attack on Sardis, captured the city but not its acropolis, and accidentally burned temples.

[事实] The Persians suppressed the Ionian revolt, destroyed Miletus, deported inhabitants, castrated boys, and sold girls into slavery.

[事实] A play in Athens about the fall of Miletus was banned and its playwright fined, which Holland links to Athenian shame, guilt, and fear.

[24:23] **Marathon as Punitive War**

[事实] Holland compares Persia’s response to Athens with a superpower punishing a “terrorist state,” saying Athens had burned a Persian city and had to be punished.

[事实] Darius sent an amphibious expedition that first captured Eretria and then landed at Marathon.

[事实] The Athenians marched out with hoplites, while Sparta delayed because of a festival; only Plataea came to Athens’ aid.

[25:39] **The Battle of Marathon**

[事实] At Marathon, the Athenians attacked when they believed Persian cavalry was being loaded onto ships.

[事实] Holland says no Greek army had previously defeated the Persians in pitched battle, making the Athenian charge especially courageous.

[事实] The Athenians weakened their center, allowed the Persian center to advance, then enveloped it and won; afterward they rushed back to defend Athens from a possible Persian naval move.

[事实] The Athenians lost 190 men, while the Persians lost thousands, and Marathon became the greatest achievement in Athenian memory.

[29:48] **Themistocles Sees the Next Threat**

[事实] After Marathon, most Athenians assumed the Persian danger had passed, but Themistocles understood that a larger storm was coming.

[事实] Themistocles is described as a child of the democratic revolution and a prototype democratic politician who appealed to poorer citizens.

[事实] He argued that Athens should develop naval power and improve its harbor by shifting attention from Phaleron to the better-protected Piraeus.

[32:29] **Silver, Ships, and Ostracism**

[事实] In 483 BC, a major silver deposit was discovered at Laurion, creating a large financial windfall for Athens.

[事实] Aristides argued that the money should be distributed among Athenians, while Themistocles argued it should be invested in a fleet.

[事实] The conflict was settled through ostracism: Aristides was exiled, Themistocles’ policy passed, and Athens built the fleet that would later matter against Persia.

[推测] Themistocles publicly sold the fleet as useful against Aegina while privately seeing Persia as the true strategic threat.

[35:21] **Xerxes and the Moral Mission**

[事实] Darius died after Marathon and was succeeded by Xerxes, son of Atossa and grandson of Cyrus through his mother.

[事实] Xerxes is described as physically imposing, well trained, capable, and interested in gardens.

[事实] Holland says a Persian war party pushed for invasion, and Xerxes understood the campaign as a duty to empire, people, and cosmic order.

[事实] Athens and Sparta had both killed Persian ambassadors who demanded earth and water, the symbols of submission, making them targets for punishment.

[38:02] **The Scale and Logistics of Invasion**

[事实] Because Xerxes was king of kings, Holland says he had to invade with a force appropriate to imperial status, including contingents from across the empire.

[事实] Herodotus’ figures are described as exaggerated; Holland estimates the force at roughly 250,000 to 300,000, still enormous.

[事实] The Persians built pontoon bridges over the Hellespont and dug canals near Mount Athos so the fleet could avoid a dangerous route.

[事实] A storm destroyed the first bridges, and the symbolic punishment of the sea was seen by Greeks as an offense against Poseidon.

[40:58] **Greek Fear and Disunity**

[事实] Xerxes allowed captured Greek spies to inspect the full scale of his forces before sending them back, spreading fear among the Greeks.

[事实] Athens and Sparta had to resist, but other Greek states did not necessarily see resistance as their best option.

[事实] Argos stayed neutral after demanding command, while Thebes had strong reasons to side with Persia because of its hostility toward Athens and Plataea.

[推测] The episode presents the war less as unified Greece against Persia and more as a conflict shaped by local grudges and Persian divide-and-rule strategy.

[44:10] **Herodotus’ Xerxes and the March West**

[事实] The Persians relied on Greek contingents, including Ionian ships, and on Greek mercenaries.

[事实] Xerxes marched from Sardis to Troy and sacrificed to Athena before crossing toward Europe.

[事实] Herodotus describes Xerxes looking over his forces and weeping because none would be alive in a hundred years; Holland says this is likely a Greek projection.

[事实] Holland nevertheless argues Xerxes must have been impressive because he controlled and held together a vast army.

[46:18] **Delphi, Prophecy, and the Wooden Wall**

[事实] As the Persian army and fleet advanced, the Greeks consulted Apollo at Delphi.

[事实] The Spartans received a prophecy that Sparta could survive only if one of its kings died.

[事实] The Athenians first received a devastating oracle, then a second one saying that only the “wooden wall” would stand and mentioning “divine Salamis.”

[事实] Themistocles interpreted the wooden wall as the Athenian fleet rather than the Acropolis’ fortifications.

[48:48] **Evacuation and Salamis Strategy**

[事实] Themistocles argued that Athens should abandon Attica, send women and children to Troezen, move old men and divine statues to Salamis, and trust the fleet.

[事实] Salamis is described as strategically useful because its narrow straits could reduce the Persian fleet’s numerical advantage.

[推测] Themistocles’ reading of the oracle turned religious ambiguity into a naval strategy.

[50:03] **Thermopylae and Artemisium**

[事实] The Greeks recognized they could not hold the line near Mount Olympus, but identified Thermopylae as a narrow road between sea and cliffs.

[事实] The plan required holding both land and sea: Thermopylae on land and Artemisium near Euboea at sea.

[事实] Because the Olympic festival was underway, the Spartans sent Leonidas with 300 men, all of whom had already had children.

[事实] Leonidas gathered about 5,000 men on the march, including anti-Persian Thebans, while the Greek fleet sailed to Artemisium.

[事实] The episode ends with Leonidas ready on land, Themistocles effectively ready at sea, and the first Persian ships sighted near Skiathos.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s strongest value is its refusal to treat Thermopylae as an isolated heroic set piece. By foregrounding Persia, Herodotus, Marathon, Athenian naval policy, and Greek disunity, it makes the coming battle feel historically grounded rather than merely legendary.

[推测] The discussion is especially useful for listeners interested in how historical memory is constructed: the hosts keep returning to who wrote the sources, whose perspective survives, and how Persian self-presentation may have differed from Greek narratives.

[推测] Its main limitation is that the title expectation is deferred: the actual Battle of Thermopylae does not begin here. This is a setup episode, best suited to listeners who enjoy strategic context, historiography, and political background before battlefield narrative.
