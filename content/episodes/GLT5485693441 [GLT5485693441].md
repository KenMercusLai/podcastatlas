# The Fall of the Bastille: Violence, Myth, and Revolution

## 概览

This episode examines the fall of the Bastille in July 1789 not simply as a moment of liberation, but as a violent and unstable turning point in the French Revolution. The hosts frame the episode through Simon Schama’s argument that violence was present from the revolution’s beginning, while also acknowledging the competing view that 1789 opened a new political vision of liberty and universal rights.

The discussion traces how hunger, soaring bread prices, financial crisis, distrust of royal troops, and the dismissal of Jacques Necker pushed Paris into panic. Camille Desmoulins’ call to arms at the Palais Royal becomes the spark for street mobilization, while soldiers’ unreliability and defections leave the city effectively outside royal control.

The storming of the Bastille is presented as both a practical hunt for gunpowder and a symbolic event later transformed by revolutionary mythmaking. The actual prison held only seven inmates, yet the revolutionary imagination recast it as a Gothic fortress of tyranny.

The episode ends by showing that the king briefly sought accommodation with the National Assembly and Paris, while violence continued in the lynchings of Foulon and Berthier de Sauvigny. The central conclusion is that the Bastille’s fall created both a language of national liberation and a precedent for political bloodshed.

## 分段落总结

[00:55] **A Violent Opening to Bastille Memory**

[事实] The episode opens with Élysée Lustelot’s description of Berthier de Sauvigny’s mutilation after the fall of the Bastille.
[事实] Dominic says Simon Schama used this kind of violence to argue that horrific bloodshed was central to the French Revolution from the beginning.
[事实] The hosts contrast celebratory readings of 14 July as liberation with darker readings that see it as a preview of the Terror.
[推测] The opening deliberately challenges the familiar national-holiday image of the Bastille by foregrounding lynching and political vengeance.

[05:04] **Crisis Before July 1789**

[事实] The hosts recap that France faced bad harvests, hunger, financial crisis, government debt from foreign wars, and a political struggle between monarchy and the National Assembly.
[事实] Around 20,000 troops, many Swiss or German-speaking, were moving toward Paris, creating fear among Parisians.
[事实] Bread prices rose to about 15 sous by 14 July, compared with a more normal level of about 8 sous.
[事实] Bertrand Barère blamed food shortages on “enemies of the people” and called for them to be discovered, intimidated, and punished.
[推测] The episode presents scapegoating as an early pattern that would later become central to revolutionary politics.

[07:39] **Necker’s Dismissal Sparks Panic**

[事实] Jacques Necker, the popular Swiss Protestant finance minister, was dismissed by Louis XVI on Saturday 11 July.
[事实] Necker left Versailles for Brussels and wrote that he would still honor two million pounds of personal security for grain shipments to France.
[事实] The king’s advisers expected a quieter reaction because the National Assembly would not meet on Sunday.
[事实] Instead, large Sunday crowds in Paris gathered at the Palais Royal, where news of Necker’s dismissal spread quickly.
[推测] Necker’s firing was interpreted not as a routine ministerial change, but as evidence that the court was moving against reform and the people.

[09:45] **Camille Desmoulins and the Call to Arms**

[事实] Camille Desmoulins, a 26-year-old friend of Maximilien Robespierre, stood on a table at the Café Foy and urged the crowd “to arms.”
[事实] Desmoulins encouraged people to wear green cockades, calling green the color of hope.
[事实] Crowds left the Palais Royal, disrupted theatres, invaded a wax museum, and paraded wax heads of Necker and the Duke of Orléans.
[推测] The wax heads foreshadow the real severed heads that later become part of the revolutionary crowd’s political theatre.

[13:36] **Street Fighting and Military Breakdown**

[事实] Crowds clashed with German-speaking Royal Allemand cavalry around the Place Vendôme and the Tuileries gardens.
[事实] Rumors spread that German and Swiss troops were massacring the people.
[事实] The Gardes Françaises arrived and sided with the crowd against the cavalry.
[事实] Baron de Besenval ordered royal troops to withdraw from Paris because he could not trust his soldiers to hold the line.
[推测] The loss of military control is presented as the moment when royal authority in Paris effectively collapsed.

[16:00] **Destruction, Commune, and National Guard**

[事实] Crowds attacked gun shops and armorers, armed themselves, and demolished parts of the customs wall around Paris.
[事实] The wall, associated with tax farming and Lavoisier, symbolized internal economic barriers and fiscal oppression.
[事实] City officials and electoral representatives gathered at the Hôtel de Ville and formed a commune to manage Paris.
[事实] A militia called the Garde Bourgeoise was created and later became the National Guard.
[事实] The green cockade was replaced by red and blue, the colors of Paris.
[推测] Middle-class revolutionaries were already trying to channel street violence into institutional order.

[21:00] **Food, Weapons, and the Road to the Bastille**

[事实] Rioters sacked the Convent of Saint-Lazare and found food including wine, vinegar, cheeses, and other stores.
[事实] The discovery strengthened claims that privileged groups had been hoarding food.
[事实] Crowds searched for weapons, ransacked a royal storehouse, and later broke into Les Invalides.
[事实] On 14 July, around 80,000 people went to Les Invalides and seized about 5,000 muskets and around 20 cannons.
[事实] They still lacked gunpowder, which led attention to the Bastille.

[25:28] **The Bastille as Reality and Myth**

[事实] The hosts describe the Bastille as less imposing than later revolutionary imagery suggested, though it had thick walls and eight round towers.
[事实] It was built during the Hundred Years’ War and later used as a prison for prisoners of state.
[事实] Lettres de cachet allowed imprisonment without normal legal process, making the Bastille a symbol of arbitrary royal power.
[事实] Prisoners included alleged conspirators, seditious writers, and aristocratic delinquents sent there at family request.
[事实] Conditions could be relatively comfortable for some prisoners, with books, food, alcohol, games, and private possessions.
[推测] The Bastille’s symbolic power mattered more than its actual prison conditions.

[30:24] **A Prison Already Becoming Political Theatre**

[事实] Writers such as Voltaire and Linguet helped create the image of the Bastille as a Gothic living tomb of tyranny.
[事实] By the 1780s, the government itself had considered demolishing and redeveloping the Bastille because of its bad reputation.
[事实] The Marquis de Sade had recently shouted revolutionary claims from the Bastille window before being moved to Charenton.
[事实] Governor Bernard René de Launay had 250 barrels of gunpowder, 82 old soldiers, 32 Swiss guards, limited food, and no water supply.
[推测] De Launay appears in the episode less as a villain than as an ordinary official trapped by a collapsing political situation.

[32:49] **Negotiations at the Gate**

[事实] The crowd outside the Bastille later became celebrated as the vainqueurs, with 954 officially recognized participants.
[事实] Most were artisans from the Saint-Antoine neighborhood, and many had not been born in Paris.
[事实] Delegates from the Hôtel de Ville entered the Bastille while de Launay was having a late breakfast or brunch.
[事实] A later delegate demanded the Bastille’s guns, cannons, gunpowder, and takeover by the new militia.
[事实] De Launay refused without instructions from Versailles.
[推测] At this stage, the immediate issue was control of weapons and gunpowder, not only the Bastille’s symbolic meaning.

[36:03] **The Storming of the Bastille**

[事实] While officials debated, the crowd outside lost patience and entered the outer courtyard.
[事实] Some attackers climbed onto a perfume shop and cut the ropes of a drawbridge, which fell and killed at least one person.
[事实] Miscommunication between soldiers and crowd helped trigger shooting by the Swiss guards.
[事实] The Gardes Françaises arrived with cannons, including the Siamese cannon mentioned earlier.
[事实] De Launay threatened to blow up the fortress and gunpowder, but his own men resisted that plan.
[事实] By about five o’clock, de Launay surrendered and white flags flew above the Bastille.

[39:20] **Seven Prisoners and a New Mythology**

[事实] The attackers found only seven prisoners: four forgers, one aristocratic libertine, and two men described as lunatics.
[事实] One prisoner, known as Major White, was carried through the city but was later returned to confinement elsewhere.
[事实] Revolutionary artists and propagandists produced images of torture chambers, iron maidens, men in iron masks, and exaggerated battlements.
[事实] Pierre-François Palloy gained the contract to demolish the Bastille and developed tours, relics, and souvenirs.
[推测] The episode treats the Bastille’s afterlife as an early example of revolutionary branding and myth production.

[42:59] **Lynching the Defenders**

[事实] Some Swiss guards initially avoided harm by removing their uniforms.
[事实] An invalid soldier named Becquart, who had helped prevent de Launay from blowing up the gunpowder, had his hand cut off and was later hanged after being misidentified.
[事实] De Launay was dragged through Paris, beaten, stabbed to death, shot after death, and decapitated by a pastry cook named Desnot.
[事实] Jacques de Flessel, suspected of misleading the crowd about weapons, was also killed and decapitated.
[事实] The crowd paraded the heads of de Launay and Flessel on pikes.
[推测] The hosts use these killings to show how quickly suspicion, rumor, and symbolic punishment overtook legal process.

[46:27] **Versailles Reacts**

[事实] Rain helped calm the disorder in Paris that evening.
[事实] The Vicomte de Noailles brought news of the Paris violence to the National Assembly at Versailles.
[事实] The Assembly reacted with anxiety and shock rather than simple celebration.
[事实] Louis XVI’s diary entry “rien” referred to his hunting journal, not necessarily ignorance of the Bastille’s significance.
[事实] On 15 July, Louis addressed the National Assembly without guards and promised to withdraw troops.
[推测] The king’s gesture temporarily suggested compromise, but it also showed that the monarchy was now reacting to events rather than controlling them.

[48:44] **Bailly, Lafayette, and the Tricolour**

[事实] Jean-Sylvain Bailly and the Marquis de Lafayette led a delegation from the National Assembly to Paris.
[事实] Bailly became mayor of Paris, and Lafayette took command of the new National Guard.
[事实] Lafayette proposed adding Bourbon white to the red and blue of Paris, creating the red, white, and blue cockade associated with the French tricolour.
[事实] On 16 July, some royal relatives and nobles began leaving France, becoming early émigrés.
[事实] On 17 July, Louis XVI went to Paris in a plain carriage and accepted the tricolour cockade.
[事实] Crowds chanted “long live the nation” rather than “long live the king.”
[推测] The visit marked a visible shift from France as the king’s realm toward the nation as the central political actor.

[54:21] **Universal Ideals and Continuing Violence**

[事实] Some contemporaries saw the king’s reconciliation with an armed people as an event unique in world history.
[事实] Bookseller Nicolas Ruault wrote that morals, laws, customs, government, and people themselves would now change.
[事实] Dominic argues that the violence unleashed by the Bastille could not easily be contained.
[事实] A week later, Joseph Foulon de Doué was hanged and decapitated after rumors that he had told the poor to eat straw.
[事实] Berthier de Sauvigny was forced to confront Foulon’s severed head, then killed, mutilated, and paraded.
[事实] The episode closes by linking Schama’s argument about violence to the coming discussion of revolutionary ideals and the Declaration of the Rights of Man and of the Citizen.
[推测] The conclusion sets up the French Revolution as a collision between universalist political aspiration and escalating coercive violence.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its refusal to flatten the Bastille into a single heroic image. It shows why the event could become a national symbol of freedom while also making clear that rumor, hunger, fear, and revenge shaped what actually happened.

[推测] Its strongest feature is the close chronological narration: Necker’s dismissal, Desmoulins’ speech, the search for arms, the failed negotiations, the storming, and the killings are connected into one escalating chain. The hosts also make effective use of vivid details, especially the wax heads, the seven prisoners, and the later souvenir trade around the Bastille.

[推测] The limitation is that the episode leans strongly into the Schama-style interpretation of revolutionary violence, even though it acknowledges more progressive readings. Listeners looking for a fuller defense of the revolutionary tradition may find that side less developed here.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in the French Revolution as lived crisis rather than as abstract legend: political violence, crowd behavior, royal weakness, revolutionary symbolism, and the making of historical myth.
