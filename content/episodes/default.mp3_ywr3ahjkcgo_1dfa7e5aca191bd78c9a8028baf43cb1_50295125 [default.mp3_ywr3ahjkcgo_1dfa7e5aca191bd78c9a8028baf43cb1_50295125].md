# Why We Misread What Others Think of Us

## 概览

Many people routinely underestimate how much others like them and overestimate how harshly they are being judged. Psychologist Erica Boothby calls these systematic distortions “social illusions,” with the “liking gap” describing the tendency to believe that a conversation partner enjoyed an interaction less than they actually did.

These illusions arise partly because our own insecurities, thoughts, and intentions are highly accessible to us, while other people’s inner experiences remain hidden. We therefore project self-critical thoughts onto others, focus too narrowly on our perceived mistakes, and miss evidence that people are often more charitable and interested than we assume.

The episode also presents practical responses: recognize the bias, seek an outside perspective, ask people directly, approach conversations with curiosity, and voice positive thoughts instead of keeping them private. These habits can reduce needless anxiety and prevent imagined rejection from undermining real relationships.

## 分段落总结

[00:00] **Social Illusions and Misreading Other People**

[事实] People spend substantial time wondering whether others notice their flaws, mock them, or find them boring.

[事实] The episode argues that these imagined judgments are often dramatically inaccurate.

[事实] Social illusions can distort relationships both at home and in the workplace, making social life appear more negative than reality warrants.

[02:31] **The Experience That Inspired the Research**

[事实] Boothby described meeting a potential collaborator and leaving convinced that she had performed poorly and would not be invited to collaborate.

[事实] Her partner, who had overheard the exchange, believed the two had connected well and that Boothby had made a strong impression.

[事实] This discrepancy between the participant’s internal experience and an observer’s assessment prompted research into how people evaluate their own conversations.

[04:24] **Discovering the Liking Gap**

[事实] A field study at personal-development workshops found that people considered their conversation partners more interesting than they believed those partners considered them.

[事实] Boothby calls this pattern the “liking gap”: people commonly enjoy an initial conversation and like the other person while underestimating how much they are liked in return.

[事实] A longitudinal study of college roommates found that this gap persisted for several months and disappeared only near the end of the academic year.

[事实] Boothby describes the liking gap as one of several social traps that make people excessively pessimistic about their relationships.

[08:24] **High-Stakes Encounters and Self-Criticism**

[事实] People often share their true feelings about an interaction with third parties rather than with the person involved, allowing mistaken impressions to persist.

[事实] In high-stakes situations such as dating, people become especially attentive to what they said badly, failed to say, or wish they could change.

[事实] Boothby recalled worrying that graduate-school peers would judge her for watching *The Bachelor*, only to learn that one peer belonged to a group that watched it every week.

[事实] Familiarity reduces uncertainty but does not eliminate social illusions, because even close companions lack complete access to one another’s thoughts.

[13:40] **Why Conversations End at the Wrong Time**

[事实] Research discussed in the episode found that conversations rarely end when either participant wants them to end; they may be shorter or longer than both people prefer.

[事实] Participants rely on indirect signals instead of clearly stating what they want, and they often conceal the very information needed for accurate interpretation.

[事实] Fear of rejection makes people guarded, while attention to their own negative thoughts can obscure more positive explanations.

[事实] Boothby suggests that revealing feelings somewhat earlier could reduce uncertainty in developing relationships.

[17:27] **Projecting Insecurity onto Other People**

[事实] As an exchange student in Italy, Boothby struggled to participate socially because she initially spoke little Italian.

[事实] She later realized that she had projected her own harsh self-evaluation onto people who were not thinking about her as critically as she imagined.

[事实] Boothby says conversation partners are generally charitable and do not expect every remark to be perfectly delivered.

[事实] Her experience illustrates how people may interpret their own awkwardness as evidence of negative external judgment.

[19:52] **The Illusion of Transparency**

[事实] The “illusion of transparency” is the belief that internal feelings and thoughts are more visible to others than they really are.

[事实] Boothby teaches the same course twice in succession and feels that her lower energy makes the second session worse, yet both classes give her virtually identical ratings.

[事实] The second class cannot compare its lecture with the first, while Boothby evaluates herself using information and comparisons unavailable to her students.

[事实] The same asymmetry appears in conversations: speakers notice every flaw in their delivery, whereas listeners encounter only the version presented to them.

[21:40] **The Spotlight on Our Perceived Flaws**

[事实] In one experiment, participants wearing a Pablo Escobar shirt overestimated how much another person noticed or thought about the shirt.

[事实] Wearers focused intensely on the embarrassing garment, while observers attended to many other features of the situation.

[事实] People commonly evaluate themselves at a microscopic level but form broader impressions of others, such as whether someone seems friendly, trustworthy, or safe.

[事实] The episode argues that imperfect or embarrassing moments can make people relatable because observers are often more forgiving than expected.

[27:18] **The Invisibility Illusion**

[事实] Cafeteria surveys found that people believed they watched others more than others watched them, as though they alone possessed an invisibility cloak.

[事实] Two lawyers discussing sensitive information in a public restaurant failed to recognize that a nearby reporter could hear their conversation.

[事实] Waiting-room studies similarly showed that each participant felt like the observer rather than the observed.

[事实] “Gaze deflection” contributes to this illusion: people disguise their attention and tend to watch someone when that person is looking elsewhere.

[事实] People may underestimate observation when no direct interaction occurs, yet overestimate scrutiny once self-consciousness is activated during an interaction.

[32:54] **Underestimating How Much Others Think About Us**

[事实] People replay advice, stories, arguments, and other conversations in their minds but systematically underestimate how long they remain in someone else’s thoughts.

[事实] After conflicts, both partners may believe they are thinking about the disagreement more than the other person is.

[事实] This belief can reduce confidence that the other party wants reconciliation.

[事实] The distortion reflects an information imbalance: our own thoughts are immediately accessible, while other people’s thoughts are hidden unless communicated.

[35:38] **How Age and Personality Shape the Liking Gap**

[事实] A study involving children aged four to eleven found no liking gap among four-year-olds, but the effect appeared around age five and increased through age eleven.

[事实] This emergence coincides with children becoming more concerned about reputation and the impressions they make.

[事实] Boothby hypothesizes that the liking gap may be especially large among teenagers and may decline with age, but she explicitly states that the necessary lifespan data were not yet available.

[事实] Early findings indicate that shy people display a larger liking gap; Boothby expects social anxiety may have a similar association.

[事实] Self-critical reflection may help people improve future interactions, but it also has costs and therefore requires balance.

[40:28] **Recognizing the Bias and Seeking Outside Views**

[事实] Awareness of the liking gap can be helpful, and Boothby reports that clinical psychologists have used the concept to reassure patients that their feelings are common.

[事实] After a promising coffee meeting, Boothby and her new acquaintance each privately told a mutual friend that they feared the other was uninterested.

[事实] The mutual friend corrected their mistaken beliefs and encouraged them to contact each other.

[事实] Friends, relatives, therapists, and other outside observers can provide perspectives less distorted by the self-critical thoughts of the people directly involved.

[43:17] **Asking Directly Instead of Guessing**

[事实] The episode uses a scene from *Ted Lasso* to show how incomplete information can lead someone to imagine a threatening explanation for another person’s behavior.

[事实] Simply asking can reveal what someone is actually thinking and replace speculation with direct information.

[事实] People may avoid questions because they do not want to pry, because they respect another person’s privacy, or because they fear the answer.

[事实] Direct communication can dismantle illusions, although the episode acknowledges legitimate reasons for approaching private subjects carefully.

[44:50] **Replacing Self-Monitoring with Curiosity**

[事实] Research on social anxiety suggests entering a conversation with the goal of learning as much as possible about the other person.

[事实] This goal shifts attention away from one’s own mistakes and toward the conversation partner.

[事实] Boothby recommends cultivating genuine curiosity, asking questions, and following up on the answers.

[事实] Asking questions is described as a “win-win”: it reduces self-critical thinking while also making the other person feel attended to and increasing liking.

[46:49] **Compliments as Evidence Against Imagined Rejection**

[事实] Boothby recalled expecting an expert surfer she admired to criticize her, only for him to praise her positioning and natural style.

[事实] The compliment mattered because it showed that he had noticed her positively and had chosen to express that judgment.

[事实] Boothby subsequently began voicing more compliments instead of keeping favorable observations to herself.

[事实] Compliments can counter the liking gap by giving people direct evidence that others notice and value them more than they expect.

[事实] Positive thoughts about other people may be common even though expressing them aloud is relatively rare.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s main strength is its clear connection between psychological research and familiar experiences: replaying awkward remarks, fearing rejection after a good conversation, or assuming that others notice a small flaw. The studies and personal stories make the liking gap easy to recognize without reducing the discussion to abstract theory.

Its practical advice is modest but useful. Seeking another perspective, asking directly, staying curious, and offering sincere compliments all address the information gaps that allow social illusions to persist. The episode is especially relevant to people who feel shy, socially anxious, overly self-conscious, or uncertain about new relationships.

A limitation is that several broader questions remain unresolved. Boothby explicitly lacks data on the liking gap across adolescence and later adulthood, and some proposed relationships involving age, social anxiety, or narcissism are hypotheses rather than established findings.

[推测] The broader value of the episode lies in reframing social confidence: it does not require assuming that every interaction went perfectly, but recognizing that other people are usually evaluating us less microscopically—and often more generously—than we evaluate ourselves.
