# Pushkin Goes to the Olympics

## 概览

Malcolm Gladwell introduces a selection of Olympic stories from several Pushkin podcasts, connecting his childhood fascination with the Games to questions about equality, technology, athletic performance, and historical mythmaking.

The first story examines the exclusion of women from distance running and Kathrine Switzer’s dramatic participation in the 1967 Boston Marathon. The second explores how MRI scans and artificial intelligence can map individual muscles, helping elite athletes diagnose imbalances and refine rehabilitation.

The final excerpt revisits Jesse Owens and German long jumper Lutz Long at the 1936 Berlin Olympics. It first presents their celebrated friendship as proof that sport can transcend racism and war, then ends by warning that this emotionally powerful account may have been “too good to be true.”

## 分段落总结

[00:45] **Gladwell’s lifelong fascination with the Olympics**

[事实] Malcolm Gladwell recalls watching the 1976 Montreal Olympics on a rented black-and-white television because his family did not own one.

[事实] He remembers athletes and events including Nadia Comăneci, John Walker, Lasse Virén, Alberto Juantorena, Cornelia Ender, Don Quarrie, and the women’s 4×100-meter freestyle relay.

[事实] The episode brings together previews from *Cautionary Tales*, *What’s Your Problem?*, and *Revisionist History*, alongside references to other Pushkin Olympic programming.

[02:47] **The myths used to exclude women from distance running**

[事实] The marathon’s origin story presents the event as so punishing that its first legendary runner supposedly collapsed and died after delivering news from Marathon to Athens.

[事实] After the women’s 800-meter race at the 1928 Olympics, newspapers falsely portrayed the competitors as collapsing or requiring medical attention.

[事实] Nine women competed, nobody dropped out or needed a doctor, and the first six finishers all surpassed the previous world record.

[事实] Despite the race’s achievements, the controversy was used to remove the women’s 800 meters from the Olympics for three decades.

[推测] The episode treats the supposedly medical objections to women’s running as institutional prejudice reinforced by sensational journalism.

[06:02] **Kathrine Switzer prepares for the Boston Marathon**

[事实] Kathrine Switzer began running after her father encouraged her to become someone others would cheer for rather than a cheerleader.

[事实] At Syracuse University, which had no women’s sports teams, she trained with the men’s cross-country team and became determined to run the Boston Marathon.

[事实] Volunteer coach Arnie Briggs initially doubted that a woman could complete the race but promised to take her if she first covered the distance in training.

[事实] Switzer completed a 31-mile training run, then entered under her customary signature, “K. V. Switzer,” after confirming that the rules did not explicitly prohibit women.

[08:32] **The 1967 race and an official’s attack**

[事实] Switzer started the Boston Marathon on April 19, 1967, wearing race number 261 and running with Briggs and her boyfriend, Tom Miller.

[事实] Other runners reacted with surprise but generally welcomed and encouraged her.

[事实] Around the four-mile mark, a race official pursued Switzer, grabbed her, and attempted to tear off her number while shouting at her to leave the race.

[事实] Miller knocked the official aside, allowing Switzer and her companions to continue as press photographers documented the confrontation.

[事实] Switzer was neither the first woman to run a marathon nor the first woman to run Boston; Bobbi Gibb was already about a mile ahead of her in that race.

[13:00] **Using MRI and AI to understand elite athletes**

[事实] Biomedical engineering professor Sylvia Blemker co-founded Springbok Analytics, which combines MRI scans with artificial intelligence to produce detailed muscle analysis.

[事实] Its clients include Olympic athletes, Major League Baseball, and professional basketball and soccer teams.

[事实] Blemker says some elite athletes have muscles so highly developed that they exceed the company’s rating scale, while other muscles can be unusually small.

[事实] Fast athletes such as sprinters and running backs often have heavily developed thighs and hips but relatively small calf muscles, reducing mass near the end of the leg.

[15:23] **Diagnosing an injured soccer player’s imbalance**

[事实] Springbok worked with members of the U.S. women’s national soccer team and their support staff to help keep athletes healthy.

[事实] One player had a known side-to-side weakness following an injury, but conventional testing could not identify which individual muscles caused it.

[事实] A roughly ten-minute MRI scan was processed into a three-dimensional “digital twin” displaying the athlete’s muscles, bones, balance, development, and muscle health.

[事实] The analysis found imbalances not only in the quadriceps but also in a calf muscle and deep hip muscles, illustrating how an injury can affect connected regions of the body.

[19:34] **Turning muscle data into targeted training**

[事实] Coaches can design fine-grained exercises for individual muscles identified as weak by the scans.

[事实] Electromyography-based biofeedback shows athletes which muscles they are activating, allowing them to consciously recruit a specific muscle more effectively.

[事实] Teams can repeat scans after three or six months to determine whether training is producing the intended changes and adjust the program accordingly.

[事实] Blemker reports that the featured soccer player was healthy, preparing for competition, and expected to appear at the Olympics.

[22:50] **Jesse Owens and the stakes of the Berlin Games**

[事实] The *Revisionist History* series asks why the United States competed in the 1936 Berlin Olympics despite already knowing what Hitler and the Nazis represented.

[事实] Jesse Owens, the son of an Alabama sharecropper, had set three world records within one hour in 1935 despite having a bad back.

[事实] Owens won four gold medals in Berlin: the 100 meters, 200 meters, long jump, and 4×100-meter relay.

[事实] During long-jump qualifying, he failed on his first two attempts and faced elimination with only one jump remaining.

[推测] The episode emphasizes how narrowly Owens’s enduring public legend may have depended on one successful qualifying attempt.

[27:02] **Milan Tiff explains the long-jump approach**

[事实] Former triple jumper Milan Tiff explains that a jumper benefits from a preliminary rhythm—a “giddy-up” or “jiggle”—before beginning the approach.

[事实] Tiff says Owens used a standing start because of his sprinting background, making it easier for him to lose track of his steps.

[事实] Tiff had known Owens personally and heard his stories about Berlin.

[事实] According to the account presented, Owens was distracted by Hitler’s presence, racial pressure, and the ability of German champion Lutz Long.

[32:25] **Lutz Long’s celebrated intervention**

[事实] Owens’s 1978 autobiography says Long approached him before his final qualifying attempt, recognized his distress, and advised him to begin his takeoff six inches behind the foul board.

[事实] Owens followed the advice, qualified for the final, and later set an Olympic record to win the event.

[事实] Long, who won silver, publicly embraced Owens in front of Hitler.

[事实] Owens’s account says the two athletes met repeatedly during the Games, remained friends afterward, and exchanged letters while Long served in the German army.

[36:46] **A moving friendship—and an unresolved historical challenge**

[事实] The story includes a purported final wartime letter in which Long asks Owens to find his son after the war and explain how human relationships can transcend conflict.

[事实] The Owens–Long friendship became central to books, films, documentaries, reference works, and the monetary value attached to Long’s Olympic silver medal.

[事实] The story has been used to argue that American participation in the Berlin Games enabled an Olympic friendship that crossed racial, national, and wartime divisions.

[事实] The excerpt closes by calling the story “too good to be true” but does not include the subsequent evidence or argument explaining that claim.

[推测] By ending at this reversal, the preview invites listeners to question how inspirational Olympic legends are constructed and whether their emotional value can survive historical scrutiny.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s chief strength is its range. It connects institutional sexism, an athlete’s physical courage, advanced biomedical technology, and the political mythology of the Olympics without reducing the Games to either pure inspiration or pure propaganda.

The Switzer excerpt is especially effective because it contrasts a famous act of resistance with two less familiar corrections: the 1928 women’s race was misrepresented, and Bobbi Gibb was ahead of Switzer in Boston. The muscle-imaging discussion then provides a concrete, accessible account of how technological innovation can influence training and rehabilitation.

The Jesse Owens section demonstrates how compelling narratives acquire cultural authority through repetition. Its cliffhanger is also the excerpt’s main limitation: listeners hear the celebrated account in detail but not the evidence that apparently complicates or undermines it.

This compilation is well suited to listeners interested in Olympic history, gender and sport, sports science, or the relationship between public memory and historical fact.
