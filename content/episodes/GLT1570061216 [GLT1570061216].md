# Babylon Before Nebuchadnezzar: The Deep Roots of a Sacred City

## 概览

This episode revisits Babylon before the famous age of Nebuchadnezzar, asking what Babylon was, when it emerged, who lived there, and how a relative latecomer became the cultural center of Mesopotamia.

The discussion frames Babylon as both historically late and mythologically central: a city founded long after the first Mesopotamian urban civilizations, yet later imagined by Babylonians as the sacred heart of time, space, and divine order.

The episode follows Babylon from Amorite origins through Hammurabi, Kassite rule, repeated foreign sackings, the rise of Marduk, and devastating conflict with Assyria. A recurring theme is how conquerors kept taking the statue of Marduk, showing that Babylon’s religious prestige mattered even to its enemies.

## 分段落总结

[01:25] **Returning to Early Babylon**

[事实] The hosts say they previously covered Babylon in the age of Nebuchadnezzar and now want to do justice to the city’s much older background.
[事实] Listener questions ask what Babylon was, when it was founded, who lived there, who ruled it, and how it became the cultural epicenter of ancient Mesopotamia.
[推测] The episode is structured as a prequel, explaining the deep foundations behind the later Babylon familiar from biblical and classical traditions.

[02:27] **Babylon as a Latecomer in Deep Mesopotamian Time**

[事实] Babylon is described as founded shortly after 2000 BC, in modern-day Iraq, on the Euphrates.
[事实] Mesopotamia is explained as the land between the Euphrates and Tigris, where rivers gave birth to urban civilization.
[事实] The first great Mesopotamian cities are said to date to around 4000 BC, roughly two thousand years before Babylon.
[推测] The hosts use this chronology to unsettle the modern assumption that Babylon belongs to the very beginning of civilization.

[04:08] **Marduk, Esegila, and Babylon’s Sacred Myth**

[事实] By the sixth century BC, Marduk was enshrined as king of the gods, and the Esegila was understood as his home.
[事实] Babylonian myth presented the Esegila as the first building and Babylon as the first city, built within a primordial creation story.
[事实] In that story, Marduk kills a sea serpent, makes heaven and earth, and creates humans to do work for the gods.
[推测] Babylon’s later religious imagination made the city not just politically important but cosmically central.

[06:00] **Sargon of Akkad as Imperial Model**

[事实] The Babylonians looked back to Sargon of Akkad, remembered as a great conqueror and ruler of the first great empire.
[事实] Akkad is described as an imperial city based on tribute and plunder, full of foreigners and exotic animals.
[事实] The exact location of Akkad is uncertain; the consensus mentioned is that it was probably on the Tigris, though possibly on the Euphrates or even near Babylon.
[推测] Sargon served as a legitimizing precedent for later Babylonian kings who wanted to imagine themselves in an imperial tradition.

[07:31] **Babylonian Antiquarianism and Written Sources**

[事实] Nabonidus, the last native king before Persian conquest, is described as intensely interested in antiquities.
[事实] His daughter is linked to a museum-like collection at Ur, with objects reaching back to early Mesopotamian history and apparent explanatory captions.
[事实] The hosts say nineteenth-century archaeology uncovered cuneiform tablets, and that Babylonian and Assyrian writings now supplement Greek and biblical sources.
[推测] The episode presents Babylonian culture as unusually self-conscious about history, memory, and the authority of the past.

[10:57] **Sumu-Abum, the Amorites, and Early Kingship**

[事实] Sumu-Abum is described as an Amorite and as the first known king of Babylon.
[事实] The Amorites are described as Semitic speakers, probably from Syria, operating on the fringes of urban society.
[事实] Babylonian kingship is presented as a divine order in which the king protects subjects, gives justice, and ensures correct rituals.
[推测] Early Babylonian monarchy is portrayed as both political rule and religious mediation between humans and gods.

[14:27] **Mud Brick Cities and Cyclical Time**

[事实] The hosts say little or no trace remains of the earliest Babylon because Mesopotamian buildings were made largely from baked mud bricks rather than stone.
[事实] Great structures such as ziggurats could decay, collapse, and be rebuilt over earlier layers.
[事实] The Esegila is described as genuinely ancient, destroyed and rebuilt, and later remembered as the oldest building in Babylon and even the world.
[推测] The material instability of mud-brick cities reinforced a sense of destruction, rebuilding, and cyclical time.

[16:18] **Astronomy, Astrology, and Babylon’s Self-Understanding**

[事实] Babylon is described as famous for sorcerers, astronomers, and astrologers.
[事实] The hosts say Babylonians believed the past and future were written in the heavens, and that events in Babylon reflected heavenly realities.
[事实] Babylon was understood by its inhabitants as sacred and central, not merely an ordinary city.
[推测] This cosmic worldview helped Babylon’s prestige survive political defeat and foreign domination.

[17:29] **Hammurabi as Conqueror and Lawgiver**

[事实] Hammurabi is described as a great conqueror who built an empire across much of southern Mesopotamia and into Syria.
[事实] He is also remembered as a great lawgiver, though the hosts stress that whether the “code” was a law code in the modern sense is debated.
[事实] The text associated with Hammurabi includes royal boasting, marriage rules, inheritance matters, and claims about justice under the gods.
[推测] Hammurabi’s reputation rests on both military achievement and the survival of a monumental written source.

[20:54] **Hammurabi’s Greatness and the Collapse of His Empire**

[事实] Hammurabi saw himself as a titanic figure and heir to Sargon.
[事实] Mesopotamian society is described as believing strongly in great kings.
[事实] Hammurabi’s empire largely crumbled after his death, as conquered city-states sought independence.
[推测] His empire appears in the episode as a personal creation more than a durable institutional system.

[22:57] **The Hittites and the Theft of Marduk’s Statue**

[事实] The Hittites from Anatolia attacked Babylon and took the statue of Marduk.
[事实] Marduk is described as the patron and symbol of Babylon, with his statue treated as the god’s real presence in the Esegila.
[事实] The hosts distinguish between outsiders who may have taken the statue as a humiliating trophy and culturally related peoples who feared they were literally taking the god.
[推测] The repeated seizure of Marduk’s statue shows how religious symbolism became a central weapon in Mesopotamian power struggles.

[27:37] **The Kassites and Babylonian Continuity**

[事实] After the Hittite attack, Babylon was taken over by the Kassites, whose language is described as obscure and unrelated to nearby languages.
[事实] The Kassites ruled Babylon for about 400 years.
[事实] They became highly Babylonian and patronized the traditions that tied Babylon to its past.
[事实] During this period, Babylon was one of the great Near Eastern powers linked with Egypt, the Hittites, and Mitanni.
[推测] The Kassites are presented as an example of conquerors being absorbed by the prestige of the culture they ruled.

[30:13] **From City-State World to Babylonia**

[事实] The hosts say that under the Kassites, independent city-states began to be absorbed into a broader entity called Babylonia.
[事实] Babylon is described as a great capital, though the surviving evidence gives little concrete sense of its appearance.
[事实] Clay tablets include drawings, but these are described as symbolic and difficult to use as realistic city plans.
[推测] Babylon’s political importance grew alongside its cultural and religious authority over the wider region.

[31:35] **Assyria as Rival and Admirer**

[事实] The Assyrians are located in northern Iraq, around areas such as Mosul and Kurdistan.
[事实] They are described as one of the most terrifying ancient empires, associated with beheadings, impalings, destruction, and forced population transfers.
[事实] The Assyrians respected Babylon as a cultural center while also attacking and pulverizing it.
[事实] After one attack, they stole the statue of Marduk but returned it because they feared sacrilege.
[推测] Assyria’s relationship with Babylon combined reverence, rivalry, insecurity, and violence.

[35:08] **The Elamite Sack and the Loss of Hammurabi’s Code**

[事实] In 1176 BC, Babylon was sacked by the Elamites from Iran.
[事实] The Elamites took the statue of Marduk and also took Hammurabi’s famous law code to Susa.
[事实] The law code was later found by French archaeologists and taken to the Louvre.
[事实] The hosts compare the seizure of Hammurabi’s code to taking an ancient prestige object such as Magna Carta.
[推测] The taking of old Babylonian artifacts shows that Babylon’s past had already become politically valuable.

[37:48] **Nebuchadnezzar I and the Return of Marduk**

[事实] Around fifty years after the Elamite sack, the first Nebuchadnezzar attacked Elam to recover Marduk.
[事实] His first campaign was hit by plague, but a later summer campaign surprised the Elamites and brought Marduk back.
[事实] The hosts say this return marks the point from which Marduk is enshrined as “top dog.”
[事实] Babylonian royal boasting is contrasted with Assyrian boasting: Babylonians emphasized buildings, while Assyrians emphasized violent conquest.
[推测] Recovering Marduk helped turn Babylon’s local god into a broader divine authority.

[40:19] **Revolt Against Assyria and Sennacherib’s Destruction**

[事实] Babylon later revolted against Assyria and allied with Elam.
[事实] The Elamite king was unable to assist, and Babylon faced the Assyrians alone.
[事实] Babylon held out for fifteen months before falling.
[事实] Sennacherib claimed to have destroyed Babylon’s buildings, temples, walls, and foundations, even flooding the city so it could not be recognized.
[推测] The episode uses Sennacherib’s destruction to explain why earlier Babylon is so archaeologically difficult to reconstruct.

[42:57] **Marduk in Nineveh and the Rebuilding of Babylon**

[事实] Sennacherib took Marduk’s statue to Assyria and claimed that Nineveh was now Babylon.
[事实] His son Esarhaddon, who had a Babylonian wife, restored Marduk’s statue and sponsored the rebuilding of Babylon.
[事实] Babylon’s lake was drained, and work began again on the central ziggurat, linked by the hosts to the model for the Tower of Babel.
[推测] Assyrian rulers tried to appropriate Babylon’s sacred legacy but also feared the consequences of violating it.

[45:17] **Brotherly War and Ashurbanipal’s Brutality**

[事实] Esarhaddon’s sons included Shamash-shuma-ukin, king of Babylon, and Ashurbanipal, the more powerful Assyrian king.
[事实] In 652 BC the brothers went to war, and Babylon was captured.
[事实] Shamash-shuma-ukin’s palace burned, and he supposedly threw himself into the flames.
[事实] Ashurbanipal is described as both an intellectual king with a library and a ruler who boasted of extreme violence against enemies.
[推测] The episode ends by emphasizing the paradox of Assyrian civilization: literary sophistication alongside spectacular brutality.

[46:49] **Link to Nebuchadnezzar’s Babylon**

[事实] The hosts close by pointing listeners back to their earlier episode on Nebuchadnezzar.
[事实] They say that after Ashurbanipal, Assyria imploded, and a revived Babylonian Empire helped dismember it.
[事实] Nebuchadnezzar’s later Babylon is associated with the Ishtar Gate, the sack of Jerusalem, the Tower of Babel tradition, and the Hanging Gardens question.
[推测] This final link frames the present episode as the long prehistory of the Babylon most listeners already imagine.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it turns Babylon from a familiar biblical or classical symbol into a much longer historical process: a late-founded city that gradually acquired cosmic, political, and cultural authority.

[推测] Its strongest through-line is the statue of Marduk. By following how different conquerors stole, feared, returned, or appropriated it, the hosts make religious prestige concrete and show why Babylon mattered even when militarily weak.

[推测] The limitation is that the discussion is necessarily broad and often acknowledges gaps in the evidence, especially around the physical form of early Babylon and the interpretation of ancient texts such as Hammurabi’s code.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners interested in ancient history, Mesopotamian religion, biblical background, empire, and the way cities build prestige through memory as much as through military power.
