# The Hundred Years’ War: England’s Triumph Unravels

## 概览

This episode closes the first phase of the Hundred Years’ War. It begins with England apparently triumphant after Poitiers: King John II of France is captive in London, English loot is pouring in, and Edward III seems close to securing a vast settlement in France.

The discussion then shows why that victory was fragile. France falls into civil disorder, Paris experiences revolutionary turmoil, the Jacquerie erupts, and free companies ravage the countryside. Edward III’s attempt to turn military success into outright conquest fails, leading instead to the Treaty of Brétigny.

After the break, the episode follows the reversal. The Black Prince’s rule in Aquitaine becomes financially and politically unstable, Charles V rebuilds French power, and Bertrand du Guesclin helps shift the war away from open battle toward attrition. By the end, England’s gains have largely collapsed, while both kingdoms are left scarred and unstable.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Sir Nigel and the Afterglow of Poitiers**

[事实] The episode opens with a reading from Arthur Conan Doyle’s *Sir Nigel*, depicting Nigel being recognised and knighted after capturing King John II at Poitiers.

[事实] The hosts frame *Sir Nigel* as a formative historical romance for Tom Holland and use it to return to the drama of the Battle of Poitiers.

[推测] The literary opening sets up the episode’s central contrast between chivalric glory and the harsher political consequences that follow victory.

[02:34] **England Appears to Have Won**

[事实] After Poitiers, England holds both King John II of France and King David II of Scotland.

[事实] Loot from France floods into England, and a chronicler claims French spoils became a marker of status.

[事实] King John spends lavishly while captive in London, buying items including an organ, whale meat, and ermine-trimmed hats for his jester.

[推测] England’s position looks overwhelming, but the hosts signal that battlefield success does not yet amount to lasting victory.

[04:27] **The Windsor Settlement and France’s Collapse**

[事实] In 1358, John signs a ransom treaty at Windsor involving a huge ransom and major territorial concessions to Edward III.

[事实] Edward is to hold an expanded Aquitaine, Calais, Pontieu, and parts of Normandy as sovereign possessions, while renouncing claims to Normandy and the French crown.

[事实] In France, authority passes to the Dauphin Charles, who is physically unimpressive but intelligent and resilient.

[推测] The settlement depends on a French state capable of raising money and enforcing order, which is precisely what France lacks.

[06:10] **Charles the Bad and Parisian Disorder**

[事实] Charles the Bad escapes prison in November 1357, returns to Paris as a hero, and pressures the Dauphin over confiscated lands and executed allies.

[事实] He empties the prisons of Paris, rides to Normandy to raise an army, and effectively declares civil war.

[事实] Paris sees revolutionary conditions after Poitiers, with the bourgeois seizing power and murdering two of the Dauphin’s associates in front of him.

[推测] The monarchy’s military humiliation undermines not just the king but the wider authority of the French nobility.

[08:33] **The Jacquerie and the Great Company**

[事实] A peasant uprising known as the Jacquerie breaks out north of Paris and spreads in pockets through places including Amiens and Normandy.

[事实] Charles the Bad suppresses the Jacquerie, gaining noble support, but then loses that support by trying to rule Paris through the third estate.

[事实] Free companies of battle-hardened soldiers, many formerly connected to English campaigns, ravage France and even threaten Avignon until the pope buys them off.

[推测] France’s crisis becomes less like a conventional war between states and more like a general breakdown of military and social order.

[12:13] **Edward III’s Final Invasion and the Limits of Conquest**

[事实] By spring 1359, John is willing to sign away even more, including half of France, Brittany, and Normandy, but the Dauphin and Estates General reject the terms.

[事实] Edward III invades France for the last time, marches to Reims, and attempts to put the city under siege, apparently seeking coronation there.

[事实] Edward fails to take Reims and later raids up to Paris, humiliating the Dauphin but failing to capture the city.

[推测] The campaign shows that even a devastated France cannot simply be conquered by English armies.

[17:18] **Lancaster’s Warning and the Treaty of Brétigny**

[事实] The Duke of Lancaster urges Edward III to negotiate, warning that England could lose in one day what it had gained in twenty years.

[事实] In May 1360, Edward and the Dauphin negotiate at Brétigny, producing terms similar to the earlier Windsor agreement.

[事实] Edward receives a vastly expanded sovereign Aquitaine and gives up his claim to the French throne.

[推测] Brétigny is a strong result for England, but it also reveals that Edward has accepted limits on what his victories can deliver.

[21:34] **The Black Prince’s Aquitaine**

[事实] After Brétigny, the Black Prince becomes Duke of Aquitaine and presides over a court described as extraordinarily rich and chivalric.

[事实] The new duchy remains unstable because its lands have been ravaged and free companies remain difficult to control.

[事实] The hosts connect the war to changes in England, including the growing autonomy of Parliament and the political importance of the longbowmen-producing classes.

[推测] English military success helps strengthen groups outside the aristocracy, creating consequences at home as well as in France.

[26:04] **Taxation, Castile, and the Black Prince’s Decline**

[事实] The Black Prince taxes his English lands and his Aquitanian subjects heavily to fund his court and policies.

[事实] His subjects begin looking toward the French king as someone to whom they might appeal, even though the Treaty of Brétigny forbids it.

[事实] In 1366, the Black Prince campaigns in Castile, defeats a French army, but returns ill and heavily indebted after being double-crossed by his Spanish ally.

[推测] The Castilian adventure enhances the Black Prince’s military reputation while worsening the financial pressures that destabilise his rule.

[28:00] **Charles V Rebuilds France**

[事实] John II dies in April 1364 after returning voluntarily to England because he cannot fulfil the treaty terms.

[事实] The Dauphin becomes Charles V and pursues the recovery of lands ceded by his father.

[事实] Charles V restores order, increases revenues, wins back local loyalties, and appoints Bertrand du Guesclin as constable based on ability rather than pedigree.

[事实] Du Guesclin avoids open battle with the English and favours a war of attrition.

[推测] Charles V succeeds because he refuses to fight the kind of war that had allowed England to win at Crécy and Poitiers.

[31:32] **The English Position Collapses**

[事实] In 1368, people in Aquitaine appeal to Charles V against the Black Prince’s taxation.

[事实] Charles summons the Black Prince, who refuses on the grounds that he is sovereign and not answerable to the French king.

[事实] Charles sends troops against English possessions in Aquitaine, and Edward III responds by tearing up the treaty and reclaiming the French throne.

[事实] This renewed phase of war goes disastrously for England, with English positions disintegrating.

[推测] The legal ambiguity of Aquitaine becomes the opening Charles needs to undo Brétigny.

[32:44] **Deaths, Defeats, and Lost Glory**

[事实] Sir John Chandos dies after slipping on his surcoat during fighting on a riverbank and being stabbed through the face.

[事实] The Black Prince sacks Limoges after it throws off loyalty to him; Froissart presents the suffering there as especially terrible.

[事实] The Black Prince returns to England ill, gives up Aquitaine, later contracts dysentery, and dies in 1376.

[事实] Edward III declines into senility, comes under the influence of Alice Perrers, and dies in 1377 after losing his French lands.

[推测] The episode presents the end of Edward III and the Black Prince as a grim collapse of the heroic image built in earlier episodes.

[38:01] **Survivors and the End of the First Phase**

[事实] Charles the Bad is outmanoeuvred by Charles V, loses his French holdings, retreats to Navarre, and dies in 1387 after being burned in brandy-soaked linen.

[事实] John of Gaunt survives and leads the last great chevauchée from Calais to Bordeaux in 1373, but most of his troops are lost by the time he arrives.

[事实] Charles V dies in 1380 having recovered most English conquests except Calais and defeated Charles the Bad.

[事实] Both England and France remain scarred by war, heavy taxation, returning soldiers, and the accession of young kings: Richard II and Charles VI.

[推测] The first stage ends not with a clean victor, but with exhaustion, instability, and unresolved dynastic conflict.

[41:25] **Burgundy, Agincourt, and the Next Stage**

[事实] Richard II is overshadowed by his uncle John of Gaunt, while Charles VI is overshadowed by his uncle Philip the Bold, Duke of Burgundy.

[事实] Philip the Bold is identified as the founder of the Burgundian ducal line discussed in another episode.

[事实] The hosts point forward to the second phase of the Hundred Years’ War, including Henry V, Agincourt, and Joan of Arc.

[推测] The episode frames the Hundred Years’ War as a series of distinct phases rather than one continuous conflict.

[44:09] **Return to Sir Nigel**

[事实] The episode ends with another reading from Conan Doyle’s *Sir Nigel*, reflecting on memory, honour, England, and the dead.

[事实] Dominic says the series has introduced him and listeners to the appeal of *Sir Nigel*.

[推测] Ending with Conan Doyle returns the episode to the romance of medieval chivalry while leaving listeners aware of the violence and instability beneath that romance.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s main value lies in showing how a spectacular military advantage can fail to become durable political control. It moves from Poitiers and ransom diplomacy to social breakdown, taxation, attrition, and the rebuilding of French power under Charles V.

A major strength is the way the hosts connect battlefield events to wider consequences: Parisian revolution, peasant revolt, mercenary violence, English parliamentary development, and the long-term instability of both kingdoms. The tone mixes serious historical explanation with jokes and modern analogies, which makes a complex sequence easier to follow.

[推测] The episode is especially suitable for listeners who already heard the earlier parts of the Hundred Years’ War series, because it assumes familiarity with figures such as Edward III, the Black Prince, John II, Charles the Bad, and the battles of Sluys, Crécy, and Poitiers.

[推测] Its limitation is that the military collapse after 1368 is summarised through key figures rather than narrated campaign by campaign, so listeners wanting a detailed operational account of the reconquest may need a more specialised treatment.
