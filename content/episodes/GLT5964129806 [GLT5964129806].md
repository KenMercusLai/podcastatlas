# Beef and Liberty: Roast Beef and English Identity

## 概览

This episode uses roast beef as a way into English national identity: not merely a dish, but a symbol tied to liberty, plainness, Protestant patriotism, anti-French feeling, and the figure of John Bull.

The hosts trace the association from Tudor and early modern England, where beef was abundant and widely remarked on by foreign travellers, through the 18th century, when roast beef became politically charged against French sauces, opera, luxury, Whig cosmopolitanism, and metropolitan manners.

The second half shows how actors, writers, clubs, songs, and artists turned beef into public patriotic theatre. Fielding, Hogarth, Garrick, the Beefsteak Clubs, and John Bull all help explain how “beef and liberty” became a cultural shorthand before fading after Waterloo and surviving mostly as memory, branding, and caricature.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Roast Beef as English Myth**

[事实] The episode opens with Joseph Addison writing in *The Tatler* in 1710 about roast beef, King Arthur, Agincourt, the Black Prince, Beefeaters, and Queen Elizabeth’s maids of honour.

[事实] Dominic frames the episode’s theme as “beef and liberty,” linking roast beef to English distinctiveness, liberty, John Bull, and national self-image.

[事实] The hosts note that roast beef now competes with fish and chips and chicken tikka masala as an emblematic English dish.

[推测] The opening establishes roast beef as a symbolic lens for discussing England rather than as a purely culinary topic.

[03:25] **Tudor England and the Beef-Eating Reputation**

[事实] Shakespeare’s *Henry V* is cited as already linking English fighters with beef, mastiffs, rough strength, and animal courage.

[事实] The hosts say Tudor English men and women ate more meat than many of their European neighbours.

[事实] The French label “les rosbifs” is mentioned as evidence that the beef-eating image was both English self-image and foreign perception.

[推测] The early modern stereotype connected diet with assumptions about English violence, robustness, and lack of refinement.

[04:52] **Land, Prices, and Travellers’ Accounts**

[事实] Ben Rogers’s book *Beef and Liberty* is identified as a major source for the episode.

[事实] England’s green lowlands, dairy farming, and productive farmland are presented as reasons beef was relatively available.

[事实] The hosts say beef became cheaper relative to other products over time, helped by enclosures and the agrarian revolution.

[事实] Travellers from Venice, Italy, and France remarked on the large quantity of meat eaten in London and on the Sunday roast custom.

[10:28] **Beef Across Class and Yeoman Identity**

[事实] A Swiss traveller in 1726 is quoted as saying roast beef was eaten at both a king’s table and a tradesman’s.

[事实] Beef is described as relatively universal in England, not just an aristocratic luxury.

[事实] The Yeomen of the Guard are discussed as “Beefeaters,” and the hosts say they were still given large daily beef rations into the 19th century.

[推测] Beef could become a national symbol because it seemed to cross class lines while still suggesting abundance and strength.

[11:47] **Roasting, Gravy, and Plain English Taste**

[事实] The hosts contrast English roasting with continental frying, braising, and stewing.

[事实] Roast beef was cooked on a spit, first by hand, then sometimes with dogs on treadmills, and later with mechanical meat jacks.

[事实] English roast beef was served with its own juices as gravy, not with elaborate stocks or sauces.

[事实] English mustard and horseradish are described as stronger than continental versions, often sharpened with vinegar.

[推测] The cooking method reinforces the episode’s larger contrast between English plainness and French refinement.

[14:48] **Puddings and Heavy Old English Eating**

[事实] In the 18th century, puddings were eaten alongside the main course rather than only after it.

[事实] The hosts explain that older puddings were closer to sausages or haggis, and that sweet and savoury were not sharply separated.

[事实] Christmas pudding is described as a surviving remnant of this older food world.

[事实] Roast beef and pudding are paired with ale, porter, or port rather than French wine.

[推测] The hosts use the heaviness of the diet humorously, but also to show why beef became linked with robustness and masculinity.

[17:25] **French Cuisine as the Opposite of English Plainness**

[事实] French cooking is described as courtly, top-down, ordered, sophisticated, and associated with nouvelle cuisine.

[事实] The French are credited with separating sweet and savoury courses, valuing vegetables, making soups rather than broths, and using stocks and rich sauces.

[事实] English writers treated ragus and fricassees as suspicious, fashionable French intrusions.

[推测] Culinary style becomes a political metaphor: French sauces suggest hierarchy, luxury, and artificiality, while roast beef suggests liberty and directness.

[20:00] **French Fashion, Whigs, and Moral Panic**

[事实] Robert Campbell’s 1747 book *The London Tradesman* is cited for attacking Frenchified food and praising Elizabethan plain cookery.

[事实] The hosts connect changing 18th-century manners with china, glassware, cotton, tea, coffee, travel, and Europeanisation.

[事实] French food is associated with Whig grandees, Robert Walpole, the Pelham family, cosmopolitanism, luxury, and corruption.

[事实] London is described through rural Tory suspicion as a place of courtiers, placemen, fops, pastry cooks, and hairdressers draining the country.

[推测] Food operates here as a proxy for anxieties about class, party politics, urban life, and foreign influence.

[24:27] **Hannah Glass and Patriotic Cookery**

[事实] Hannah Glass’s *The Art of Cookery Made Plain and Easy* was published in 1747.

[事实] The hosts place the book shortly after the 1745 Jacobite rising and amid conflict with France.

[事实] Glass is described as warning against French sauces, French cooks, expense, richness, and unpatriotic imitation.

[推测] Her cookbook turns domestic cooking into a defence of national character.

[27:27] **Actors, Opera, and Cultural Resistance**

[事实] After the break, Dominic says actors and artists were surprisingly important champions of patriotic cookery.

[事实] The arrival of opera in 1710 is presented as a major cause of theatrical anxiety about foreign influence.

[事实] *The Spectator*, *The Tatler*, and Jonathan Swift are described as critics of opera and advocates of more “manly” entertainments.

[事实] Foreign cookery, music, and fashion are grouped together as part of the same cultural threat.

[推测] Theatre mattered because it could spread patriotic food symbolism to large audiences through performance.

[30:46] **Fielding and “The Roast Beef of Old England”**

[事实] Henry Fielding wrote an attack on Walpole called *The Grub Street Opera*.

[事实] A cook character in Fielding’s work sings against French politeness, ragus, and continental dainties while praising old English roast beef.

[事实] The play was suppressed, but Fielding reused the song, and Richard Leveridge gave it a catchier tune.

[事实] By the mid-18th century, audiences sang the song before and after other plays.

[事实] The tune later endured at formal dinners of the Royal Navy, Royal Artillery, United States Marine Corps, and Canadian Armed Forces.

[推测] The song helped turn roast beef into a patriotic and military ritual.

[33:48] **Beefsteak Clubs and “Beef and Liberty”**

[事实] Beefsteak Clubs began appearing from about 1705, with the first associated with Drury Lane Theatre.

[事实] The Sublime Society of Beefsteaks was founded in 1735.

[事实] Members mentioned include William Hogarth, John Wilkes, Dr Johnson, and the future George IV.

[事实] Members wore special clothing and badges, including buttons bearing “Beef and Liberty,” and ate beef with mustard, horseradish, and baked potatoes.

[事实] The Beefsteak Club lasted until 1867.

[推测] These clubs made beef patriotism social, performative, and clubbable while still claiming to stand for plain English values.

[35:31] **Hogarth, Calais, and Anti-French Satire**

[事实] Hogarth’s painting *The Gate of Calais* is discussed, later known through prints as *The Roast Beef of Old England*.

[事实] Hogarth had been arrested as a suspected spy in Calais while sketching.

[事实] The painting shows a large side of beef going to an English tavern while ragged French soldiers and a fat Catholic friar look on.

[事实] Theodosius Forrest wrote a cantata based on the painting for performance at the Haymarket Theatre.

[推测] Hogarth’s image made the contrast between English abundance and French deprivation visually memorable.

[38:00] **Frogs, Invasion Fears, and Self-Caricature**

[事实] Hogarth’s later image *The Invasion* shows French troops preparing to embark for England while roasting frogs.

[事实] David Garrick wrote a caption contrasting English beef and beer with French soup and roasted frogs.

[事实] By the 1760s, Dominic says the beef theme was becoming a caricature people lived up to.

[事实] Boswell’s 1762 journal is cited: he ate a large beef steak alone and then watched cockfighting for five hours to perform the stereotype of a “true-born old Englishman.”

[推测] The roast-beef myth worked both sincerely and comically, which helped it endure.

[40:37] **John Bull and the Political Body of England**

[事实] In 1760s prints, Englishmen were represented as butchers accompanied by bulldogs or mastiffs.

[事实] John Bull was created in 1712 by John Arbuthnot and peaked from the 1760s through the French Revolution.

[事实] John Bull is described as fat, stout, waistcoated, beer-drinking, pudding- or beef-eating, bluff, sceptical, and anti-intellectual.

[事实] During the French Revolution, John Bull was contrasted with skinny, enthusiastic, rationalist, atheistical Jacobins.

[事实] Edmund Burke’s image of silent cattle under the British oak is quoted as a way of contrasting a quiet majority with noisy critics.

[推测] John Bull condensed roast-beef politics into a human national character.

[42:16] **Decline After Waterloo**

[事实] Dominic says the beef-and-liberty theme felt old-fashioned after the French Revolutionary and Napoleonic Wars.

[事实] The Industrial Revolution made Britain more urban, weakening the rural squire as the soul of the nation.

[事实] Victorian rural squires are described as backward figures being superseded by railways, industrialists, and businessmen.

[事实] After Waterloo, the French invasion threat faded, and anti-French culinary panic lost urgency.

[推测] Roast beef stopped being a live political argument and became more of a nostalgic symbol.

[44:07] **Modern Echoes and Food as History**

[事实] Hawksmoor steak restaurants are mentioned as using “Beef and Liberty” as a logo.

[事实] The hosts discuss whether Texan steak culture carries a similar association between meat, patriotism, masculinity, and red-blooded identity.

[事实] Ian Botham is described as a modern John Bull-like figure: “beefy,” patriotic, badly behaved, and associated with defeating foreigners at cricket.

[事实] The hosts consider Australian barbecue culture, but Tom doubts a direct line of descent and links it more to hunting.

[事实] They argue that food, culture, and customs are windows into how a society thinks.

[47:08] **Mad Cow Disease and the Enduring Beef Memory**

[事实] Tom says mad cow disease was emblematic because beef still carried a folk memory as an emblem of England.

[事实] The idea of cows being turned into cannibals and going mad is described as a potent and depressing metaphor.

[事实] Ben Rogers says the reaction to mad cow disease inspired him to write *Beef and Liberty*.

[事实] The episode ends with the anthem of the Sublime Society of Beefsteaks praising British beef, freedom, and liberty.

[推测] The ending links 18th-century culinary patriotism to modern unease about English identity and decline.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its ability to make a narrow subject feel historically large. Roast beef becomes a route into agriculture, class, theatre, party politics, anti-French sentiment, military ritual, satire, and national myth.

[推测] Its strongest sections are the links between food and politics: the contrast between roast beef and French sauces, the rise of the Beefsteak Clubs, and Hogarth’s visual satire all show how everyday taste can become ideological.

[推测] The episode is less focused on ordinary diners outside England and gives more space to literary, artistic, and elite club culture than to household practice across regions or classes. That limitation is partly balanced by the travellers’ accounts and discussion of yeomen.

[推测] It will suit listeners interested in cultural history, English identity, food history, 18th-century politics, and the comic afterlife of national stereotypes.
