# La Marseillaise and the Roman Style of the French Revolution

## 概览

This episode uses *La Marseillaise* as a way into the atmosphere of France in 1792: war, paranoia, republican expectation, fear of tyranny, and the need for new symbols as monarchy and old religious authority began to lose legitimacy.

The hosts trace how the song was written in Strasbourg after France declared war on Austria, then spread through soldiers and volunteers from Marseille until it became emotionally tied to the radical phase of the Revolution. They emphasize that its power came not only from the tune and lyrics, but from who sang it: armed citizen-volunteers presenting themselves as defenders of the nation.

The second half broadens into the Revolution’s fascination with ancient Rome. Roman republican imagery, Jacques-Louis David’s art, the bonnet rouge, the fasces, sans-culotte dress, and elite adoption of working-class styles all appear as part of a new revolutionary language of identity, virtue, liberty, and political belonging.

## 分段落总结

[00:12] **Opening With La Marseillaise**

[事实] The episode opens with producer Theo Young-Smith singing *La Marseillaise*.
[事实] The hosts note that the song became the official anthem of the French Republic in 1795, was disliked by Napoleon, banned under Louis XVIII and Charles X, and later endured as France’s national anthem.
[事实] They describe the lyrics as intensely martial, full of blood, tyranny, soldiers, citizens, and a call to arms.
[推测] The opening frames the anthem as both thrilling and unsettling: a song of liberation that also carries revolutionary violence.

[04:52] **The Song’s Origins in Strasbourg**

[事实] *La Marseillaise* was written on 25 April 1792, five days after France declared war on Austria.
[事实] The setting was a public dinner in Strasbourg hosted by Philippe Dietrich, the mayor of Strasbourg.
[事实] Dietrich is described as a Protestant, scientist, Freemason, former aristocrat, ironmaster, and industrialist, embodying contradictions within the Revolution.
[事实] Dietrich wanted a new marching song suitable for an army of patriotic citizens rather than songs tied to feudalism and superstition.

[07:22] **Rouget de Lisle and the First Performance**

[事实] Captain Rouget de Lisle, an engineer, Freemason, and part-time composer, wrote the song overnight and called it “Song of the Army of the Rhine.”
[事实] Dietrich liked the song, his wife played it at the piano, and Madame Dietrich arranged it for military band.
[事实] The hosts describe the song as having an almost apocalyptic, millennial drama.
[推测] Its emotional force came from matching the revolutionary mood of war, purification, judgment, and patriotic mobilization.

[09:17] **A Song That Goes Viral**

[事实] The hosts say the tune may have had musical antecedents, including Viotti, Grison, and Mozart’s Piano Concerto No. 25.
[事实] Soldiers carried the song from Strasbourg into other parts of France.
[事实] In Montpellier, François Mireur heard it from a soldier, learned it, and later sang it at a Jacobin meeting in Marseille.
[事实] The song was printed and distributed after being enthusiastically received by the Jacobins.

[11:44] **From Marseille to Paris**

[事实] Mireur joined a group of about 500 volunteers recruited in the south of France to travel from Marseille to Paris as Fédérés.
[事实] They left on 2 July, armed and dragging artillery, singing the song as they marched north.
[事实] Because the original title no longer suited southern volunteers, it was renamed “the war song of the armies on the borders.”
[事实] By the time they reached Paris, the song had gained a powerful reputation and electrified an already anxious city.

[13:35] **Republican Anthem and Political Contest**

[事实] The hosts argue that revolutionary France needed new songs because older political anthems were tied to monarchy.
[事实] The song later became associated with the Marseillais volunteers, hence *La Marseillaise*.
[事实] Some Girondins parodied the song as tensions with Jacobins intensified, turning its blood imagery into a charge of anarchy and violence.
[事实] The hosts note that anxieties about the violent lyrics have persisted, including later proposals to rewrite them.

[15:04] **Citizen-Soldiers and Roman Resonance**

[事实] The hosts argue that the song’s appeal came partly from being sung by citizen-volunteers rather than professional soldiers.
[事实] The lyrics contain no kings, crowns, or monarchy, making them implicitly republican.
[事实] On 14 July, the Marseillais had a place of honor at the Fête de la Fédération on the Champ de Mars.
[事实] The Champ de Mars is linked to Rome’s Campus Martius, where citizens assembled for war.
[推测] The song gave a new and radical revolutionary movement an antique, Roman aura of civic virtue.

[17:46] **The Revolution’s Roman Imagination**

[事实] After the break, the hosts discuss the paradox that revolutionaries wanted to begin the world anew while also imagining themselves as ancient Roman republicans.
[事实] They distinguish the admired Rome from Christian Rome and imperial Rome: the focus was the early Republic after the expulsion of kings.
[事实] They connect this Roman model to American revolutionary imagery and to figures such as Brutus, Horatius, Cicero, and the anti-tyrannical tradition.
[事实] Timothy Tackett is cited for the claim that Cicero was quoted far more often than Rousseau in revolutionary speeches and newspapers.

[22:05] **Jacques-Louis David and Revolutionary Visual Culture**

[事实] Jacques-Louis David is presented as central to giving the Revolution a Roman visual language.
[事实] His paintings emphasized austere early republican virtue, patriotism, sacrifice, and devotion to country.
[事实] The hosts say David’s pre-revolutionary paintings appeared, after 1789, to have anticipated revolutionary ideals.
[事实] David helped stage public revolutionary ceremonies, including festivals on the Champ de Mars and the transfer of Voltaire’s remains to the Panthéon.

[26:36] **Anti-Clericalism and Replacing Old Symbols**

[事实] The hosts discuss growing hostility toward priests who refused the oath of allegiance to the Revolution.
[事实] They mention legislation after the Tuileries that forbade even oath-swearing priests from wearing clerical clothing in public.
[事实] Christianity is described as increasingly associated with reaction, superstition, throne, and altar.
[推测] Roman symbolism became useful because it offered a pre-Christian civic language with which revolutionaries could replace older monarchical and religious symbols.

[28:46] **Bonnet Rouge, Fasces, and Revolutionary Objects**

[事实] People could buy brooches stamped with Brutus’s head and pins shaped like fasces.
[事实] The fasces are explained as Roman symbols carried by lictors before elected magistrates.
[事实] The bonnet rouge, or red cap, derived from the Roman pileus, a cap given to freed slaves, and was also linked with the Phrygian cap.
[事实] By 1790, revolutionary journals were proposing the bonnet rouge as a symbol of liberty.

[31:26] **The Red Cap as Political Identity**

[事实] In the Cordeliers Club, speakers had to wear a bonnet rouge when addressing delegates.
[事实] Robespierre opposed a 1792 motion that all Jacobins should wear the bonnet rouge all the time, preferring the cockade.
[事实] When people broke into the Tuileries, they made Louis XVI wear the bonnet rouge before drinking a toast to the Republic.
[推测] The bonnet rouge functioned as a visible test of revolutionary belonging, more intense and socially charged than the cockade.

[34:08] **Sans-Culottes and Revolutionary Dress**

[事实] A contemporary observer in 1792 linked the bonnet rouge to sans-culotte enthusiasm for Greek and Roman symbols of freedom.
[事实] Sans-culottes are described as urban working-class radicals, especially artisans, with both class-based and ideological identity.
[事实] Their dress included loose trousers, wooden clogs, and a short jacket called the carmagnole.
[事实] The carmagnole is linked to Marseille through Italians from near Turin who had settled there.
[推测] The Marseillais looked like ideal bearers of the new revolutionary spirit because their regional, working-class, and martial image fused together.

[36:30] **Elites Dressing Like the People**

[事实] The hosts say elites also began adopting working-class revolutionary styles.
[事实] The Girondin ministry appointed in March 1792 was known as the sans-culotte ministry.
[事实] Philippe Égalité, formerly the Duke of Orléans, is described wearing short jacket, pantaloons, round hat, loose neck handkerchief, short unpowdered hair, and shoes tied with strings.
[事实] Madame Tussaud’s description of Philippe Égalité is used as evidence for elite revolutionary cross-dressing.

[39:07] **Titus Hair and the Fusion of Rome and the Street**

[事实] The short unpowdered hairstyle was called the Titus style, after a role in Voltaire’s play *Brutus*, restaged in May 1791.
[事实] The hosts argue that this hairstyle was both working-class Parisian and Roman republican in meaning.
[事实] The Marseillais entered Paris through self-consciously working-class areas near where the Bastille had stood, wearing carmagnoles and singing a song of civic and martial virtue.
[事实] Royalists were frightened by their arrival; one Royal Guard officer described them as 500 fanatics, mostly drunk, followed by the dregs of the people.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s value lies in connecting a familiar national anthem to the political culture that produced it. Rather than treating *La Marseillaise* simply as patriotic music, the hosts show how it condensed war, anti-tyranny, civic identity, republicanism, and revolutionary violence into one portable cultural object.

A major strength is the movement from song to symbolism: Strasbourg, Marseille, Paris, Roman republicanism, David’s visual culture, the bonnet rouge, and sans-culotte clothing all become part of one argument about how revolutions need new rituals and signs.

The limitation is that the episode deliberately stops before major political events it had intended to cover, especially Louis XVI’s trial and execution. [推测] Listeners looking for a chronological account of those events may need the next season for the narrative payoff.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in political culture, symbols, nationalism, revolutionary aesthetics, and the way ideas move through music, clothing, public ritual, and historical memory.
