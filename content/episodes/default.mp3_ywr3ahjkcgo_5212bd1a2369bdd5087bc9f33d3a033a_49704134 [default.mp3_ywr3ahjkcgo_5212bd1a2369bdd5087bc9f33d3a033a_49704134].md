# Why Nonviolent Resistance Works

## Overview

Violence is often portrayed as the surest way to achieve radical change. Political scientist Erica Chenoweth once shared that assumption, influenced by military history, popular culture, and research into terrorism. A challenge from fellow scholar Maria Stephan led her to test it against more than a century of revolutionary campaigns.

Their research produced a striking result: nonviolent campaigns succeeded about twice as often as armed campaigns. Their advantage did not come from moral persuasion alone, but from their ability to attract broad participation, weaken an opponent’s supporting institutions, innovate tactically, and maintain discipline under repression.

Case studies from Serbia, Sudan, and Spain illustrate both sides of the finding. Creative nonviolent tactics can broaden participation and place authorities in difficult dilemmas, while outbreaks of protester violence can alienate potential allies, justify repression, and strengthen solidarity within security forces.

## Segmented Summary

[00:00] **Questioning the presumed effectiveness of violence**

[Fact] George Washington’s claim that military preparedness preserves peace introduces the widespread belief that force is an effective instrument of political change.

[Fact] War films, police dramas, books, and national histories repeatedly depict violence as unpleasant but necessary and effective.

[Fact] The episode asks whether radical change truly flows from the barrel of a gun or whether nonviolent resistance works better.

[02:49] **Erica Chenoweth’s path from military history to political research**

[Fact] Chenoweth became interested in war as a child through accounts of the siege of Sarajevo and World War I, including stories of heroism and horrific battlefield conditions.

[Fact] After the September 11 attacks, she pursued graduate research on terrorism and examined competing claims about whether terrorist violence achieves its stated goals.

[Fact] She initially believed violence could be politically effective when groups possessed sufficient capacity.

[Fact] She argues that American culture frequently memorializes war while giving comparatively little attention to questioning its utility.

[08:31] **A skeptical encounter with nonviolent resistance**

[Fact] At a 2006 workshop, Chenoweth encountered the claim that protests, strikes, boycotts, and other forms of noncooperation could rival or outperform armed insurgency.

[Fact] She responded with examples of successful violent revolutions and failed nonviolent movements, including Tiananmen Square and the Syrian uprising.

[Fact] Maria Stephan challenged her to move beyond selected anecdotes and compare violent and nonviolent campaigns systematically.

[12:27] **Designing a demanding historical test**

[Fact] Chenoweth and Stephan assembled comparable campaigns from countries around the world, focusing on attempts to overthrow governments or achieve national independence.

[Fact] They imposed a strict definition of success: a campaign had to attain its stated objective within one year of peak mobilization and play a decisive role in that outcome.

[Fact] Independence campaigns had to achieve both legal and practical independence rather than limited autonomy.

[Fact] Chenoweth expected armed movements to outperform nonviolent ones when the historical evidence was assembled.

[17:02] **Serbia: humor, dilemmas, and broader participation**

[Fact] Serbia’s Otpor movement challenged Slobodan Milošević after a fraudulent election, using parallel vote verification as well as mass demonstrations.

[Fact] In its “Dinar for Change” action, activists invited passersby to strike a barrel bearing Milošević’s image, leaving police with the dilemma of tolerating the spectacle, arresting participants, or absurdly arresting the barrel.

[Fact] Otpor used humor to undermine the image of autocratic invincibility and employed “dilemma actions” that made almost any official response politically costly.

[Fact] Activists also recruited grandparents to demonstrations, expanding the movement’s diversity while exploiting a social taboo against police publicly beating elderly people.

[Fact] Milošević ultimately left power, police ceased defending him, and protesters occupied parliament, where they reportedly found ballots pre-marked for him.

[23:42] **Sudan: withdrawing the cooperation that sustains power**

[Fact] Protests against Omar al-Bashir expanded in 2018 after increases in fuel and food prices, with the Sudanese Professionals Association organizing strikes, stay-at-home actions, demonstrations, marches, and sit-ins.

[Fact] After Bashir’s removal, protesters continued demanding civilian representation and democratic transition from the new military council.

[Fact] Security forces killed, tortured, and sexually assaulted more than 100 people during the Khartoum massacre, after which organizers called for a general strike and mass noncooperation.

[Fact] The strike disrupted ordinary life without exposing millions of people to direct confrontation with security forces, pressuring the military council to negotiate with civilian representatives.

[Fact] Chenoweth counts the campaign as successful under the study’s criteria because it removed Bashir and established a transitional government, while acknowledging that later turmoil and renewed military control complicated the outcome.

[27:07] **The dependence of rulers on pillars of support**

[Fact] Even highly repressive rulers depend on workers, civil servants, businesses, political elites, media, police, and security forces to remain in power.

[Fact] Nonviolent movements become effective when they extend resistance into these constituencies and persuade large numbers of people to withdraw cooperation.

[Fact] When those supporting relationships fracture, authoritarian systems can collapse rapidly.

[29:17] **Spain: how violence can shrink a movement’s coalition**

[Fact] Spain’s largely peaceful 15M movement initially enjoyed support from about 65 percent of the public.

[Fact] Surveys conducted before and after an episode of protester violence found that average support for the movement fell by roughly 12 percent.

[Fact] Existing supporters largely remained loyal, but politically adjacent, neutral, and potentially sympathetic people became substantially less supportive.

[Fact] Violence is particularly risky for small movements that need to expand, because it can alienate the third parties required to build a winning coalition.

[Fact] Regimes may deliberately provoke nonviolent protesters into violence because it helps justify repression, demands for law and order, and expanded state authority.

[35:53] **The central finding: nonviolent campaigns succeed more often**

[Fact] Across the campaigns studied, nonviolent resistance was about twice as likely to succeed as violent resistance.

[Fact] Roughly half of the nonviolent campaigns succeeded, compared with about one quarter of armed campaigns.

[Fact] The researchers do not claim that nonviolent resistance always works or that armed resistance never succeeds.

[Fact] Success rates for nonviolent campaigns increased during the latter half of the twentieth century and into the early twenty-first century.

[38:36] **Four mechanisms behind successful nonviolent resistance**

[Fact] Successful movements tend to be large and diverse, and nonviolent methods generally permit broader participation than armed struggle.

[Fact] A broad coalition creates personal connections to an opponent’s pillars of support, making defections among officials, business leaders, civil servants, media, police, and security forces more likely.

[Fact] Tactical innovation allows movements to shift from easily suppressed demonstrations toward strikes, stay-at-home actions, and other forms of noncooperation.

[Fact] The strategic objective is not necessarily to change a dictator’s mind, but to remove the social and institutional support on which the dictator depends.

[Fact] Organizational resilience and discipline help movements continue recruiting and adapting under repression without fragmenting or turning impulsively to violence.

[41:45] **The 3.5 percent rule—and its limits**

[Fact] In an analysis of 323 revolutionary or “maximalist” campaigns, none appeared to fail after actively mobilizing approximately 3.5 percent of the population.

[Fact] Although 3.5 percent is a small proportion, it represents millions or tens of millions of people in large countries.

[Fact] The measure counts active participants rather than all supporters or sympathizers.

[Fact] Chenoweth treats the threshold as a rule of thumb, not an iron law, because movements reaching it may already enjoy support from a much larger share of society.

[44:06] **Tactical gains versus strategic consequences**

[Fact] Violent incidents can generate immediate benefits such as media attention or short-term protection from an attack.

[Fact] Their longer-term effects may include broader repression, increased surveillance and infiltration, harm to participants’ families, and alienation of potential supporters.

[Fact] Violence can unify security forces and harden an opponent rather than encourage defections.

[Fact] Research discussed in the episode associates terrorism with political polarization and greater public support for expanded government powers to restore order.

[47:19] **Recovering the overlooked history of nonviolent power**

[Fact] Nonviolence is often understood solely as a moral position and therefore dismissed as noble but strategically naive.

[Fact] Chenoweth describes it instead as a practical strategy that has helped shape modern nations and remains broadly available.

[Fact] One interpretation of the American Revolution emphasizes the decade before armed conflict, when colonists used economic noncooperation and built alternative judicial and political institutions.

[Fact] Under that interpretation, colonists had already created substantial independence before war began, while British military action represented an attempt to recover lost control.

[Inference] Cultural memory may exaggerate the political importance of battlefield victories because dramatic violence is easier to memorialize than the slower withdrawal of cooperation that makes institutional change possible.

## Podcast Review/Summary

The episode’s strongest contribution is its separation of morality from strategic effectiveness. It does not argue merely that nonviolence is ethically preferable; it presents comparative evidence that disciplined civil resistance often produces better political outcomes than armed struggle.

The case studies make the underlying mechanisms concrete. Humor in Serbia, mass noncooperation in Sudan, and declining support after violence in Spain show how movements compete for participation, legitimacy, and defections from an opponent’s coalition.

Its principal limitation is that classifying campaigns and defining success require judgment. Sudan also demonstrates that removing a ruler or creating a transitional government does not guarantee durable democracy, while the 3.5 percent threshold can be misleading if treated as a universal formula.

[Inference] This episode is especially useful for listeners interested in political psychology, social movements, authoritarian power, and the practical design of collective action. Its broader lesson is that durable power rests less on coercion alone than on networks of cooperation—and those networks can be withdrawn.
