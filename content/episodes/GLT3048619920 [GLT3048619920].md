# Yeltsin’s Russia and the Making of Putin

## 概览

This episode examines Russia in the 1990s as the crisis-ridden setting from which Vladimir Putin emerged. The hosts frame post-Soviet Russia through a Weimar Republic comparison: a proud country that lost an empire, lacked deep democratic traditions, endured economic collapse, and became receptive to strongman politics.

The discussion follows Boris Yeltsin’s presidency through shock therapy, privatization, oligarchic wealth, institutional collapse, conflict with parliament, public humiliation, and war in Chechnya. The key conclusion is that Putin’s appeal cannot be understood without understanding the trauma, poverty, disorder, and national humiliation many Russians associated with the Yeltsin years.

The episode then connects domestic collapse to geopolitics: NATO expansion, Russian resentment, the Baltic states’ desire for protection, and Chechnya’s wars. It ends with Putin’s appointment as prime minister in 1999, the second Chechen war, and Yeltsin’s resignation on December 31, 1999.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Weimar Russia and the 1990s Seedbed**

[事实] The episode opens with Robert Harris’s 1998 novel *Archangel*, which compares Russia under Yeltsin to the Weimar Republic.

[事实] The hosts say the comparison rests on lost empire, resentment, weak democratic tradition, border disputes involving ethnic nationals, anti-Semitism, economic crash, and the search for a Hitler-like figure.

[事实] They argue that Russia’s 1990s were marked by wars, terrorism, political chaos, and economic meltdown.

[推测] The hosts treat the 1990s as the crucial background for understanding why Putin later appeared attractive to many Russians.

[04:20] **Shock Therapy and Economic Collapse**

[事实] Yeltsin entered power without a settled economic plan, while shortages in Russian shops were already severe.

[事实] Yegor Gaidar, aged 35, became a key figure in advocating rapid “shock therapy” toward a market economy.

[事实] In January 1992, price liberalization caused everyday goods such as bread or milk to rise sharply, with the hosts also describing prices in one year as rising by 2500%.

[事实] The hosts say this was catastrophic for pensioners and people on fixed incomes.

[推测] The reformers believed rapid liberalization was the only way to force supply and demand to function after Soviet controls.

[08:16] **Institutional Collapse and Social Dislocation**

[事实] The hosts describe Moscow in the early 1990s as suddenly full of beggars, including Afghan war veterans who had lost limbs.

[事实] They compare the collapse of Soviet-era factories and institutions to the dissolution of monasteries in England, because workplaces had also provided social services and structure.

[事实] Rutskoi criticized the young reformers as “young boys in pink shorts and yellow boots,” reflecting resentment toward westernized economists.

[推测] The episode links this resentment to later ideas of “authentic” Russian masculinity that Putin would exploit.

[10:20] **Privatization and the Rise of Oligarchs**

[事实] Yeltsin’s team wanted to privatize state industries quickly and create a class of capitalists.

[事实] Citizens received vouchers worth 10,000 rubles, while managers and workers could buy up to 25% of factories.

[事实] Managers, organized crime figures, and entrepreneurial actors bought up vouchers from people desperate for cash.

[事实] Later, Anatoly Chubais arranged loans-for-shares deals to support Yeltsin’s 1996 re-election campaign.

[事实] The hosts cite Yukos and Sibneft as examples of major companies sold for fractions of their stated value to figures including Mikhail Khodorkovsky, Boris Berezovsky, and Roman Abramovich.

[13:34] **Poverty, Lawlessness, and Yeltsin’s Political Weakness**

[事实] By 1993-94, the hosts say authority had largely broken down, with bribery, gunmen in markets, and beggars on the streets.

[事实] Male life expectancy collapsed from almost 70 to almost 60.

[事实] The hosts cite estimates that by the end of the 1990s, 70-80% of Russians lived below the poverty line.

[事实] They describe Yeltsin’s 1996 re-election as crooked and rigged, with oligarchs effectively buying his victory against communist candidate Gennady Zyuganov.

[推测] The scale of poverty and corruption made Yeltsin vulnerable to both communist opposition and nationalist backlash.

[15:28] **The 1993 Constitutional Crisis**

[事实] Yeltsin clashed with the Russian parliament, which included communists and nationalists and opposed his reforms.

[事实] In September 1993, Yeltsin dissolved parliament unconstitutionally, and parliament responded by impeaching him.

[事实] Armed men occupied the parliament building while Yeltsin’s troops surrounded it.

[事实] Yeltsin accused parliament of being filled with bandits, mercenaries, communist revanchists, and fascist leaders.

[事实] The army stormed the parliament, and tanks fired on the parliament building.

[21:09] **Putin in St. Petersburg and the New Presidential System**

[事实] After defeating parliament, Yeltsin pushed through a constitution giving the president much greater executive power.

[事实] Anatoly Sobchak, mayor of St. Petersburg and Putin’s former law professor, helped write the new constitution.

[事实] Putin worked under Sobchak as vice mayor, formally handling foreign investment but functioning as a fixer and go-between with business and organized crime.

[事实] The hosts say Putin retained strong security-service links, though they acknowledge uncertainty over his exact FSB status.

[推测] Putin’s later usefulness came partly from being able to connect the Yeltsin political world with security-service interests.

[23:33] **NATO’s Origins and Post-Cold War Expansion**

[事实] The hosts explain NATO as a Cold War mutual defense alliance founded in 1948-49.

[事实] They describe it as a defensive treaty in which an attack on one member is treated as an attack on all.

[事实] NATO also bound the United States into European defense.

[事实] After the Cold War, NATO expanded despite earlier verbal American statements during German reunification talks that there were no plans or expectations to expand.

[事实] The hosts say expansion was driven partly by Eastern European countries wanting protection.

[推测] NATO expansion deepened Russian feelings of humiliation because former Warsaw Pact states joined the alliance Russia had long viewed as its enemy.

[27:29] **The Baltic States and the Security Dilemma**

[事实] The hosts say Poland, Hungary, and other Central European states were part of the first NATO expansion wave.

[事实] They describe the Baltic states as formerly independent countries annexed by Stalin and never fully reconciled to Soviet rule.

[事实] The Baltic states wanted NATO protection, while Russia saw their potential membership as provocative.

[事实] The hosts note that some Baltic states had large Russian-speaking populations, making the issue more inflammatory.

[推测] The episode presents NATO expansion as understandable from Eastern Europe’s perspective and threatening from Russia’s perspective.

[29:32] **The First Chechen War**

[事实] Chechnya is described as a majority-Muslim region in the Caucasus whose people were deported under Stalin and later allowed back by Khrushchev.

[事实] Chechnya declared independence in 1990 and did not sign the same agreement to remain in the Russian Federation that most other federal subjects accepted.

[事实] At the end of 1994, Yeltsin sent troops into Grozny, the Chechen capital.

[事实] The hosts describe the attack on Grozny as a disaster, with Russian conscripts killed and Russian army morale in poor condition.

[事实] They say the first Chechen war involved horrific atrocities on both sides, caused around 120,000 deaths, and ended in another stalemate.

[31:12] **Bombings, the Second Chechen War, and Putin’s Succession**

[事实] In 1998, the ruble collapsed and Russia faced another financial crisis.

[事实] In August 1999, Chechen Islamic militants launched an incursion into Dagestan, giving Russia a pretext for a second Chechen war.

[事实] In September 1999, apartment bombings across Russia killed about 330-350 people.

[事实] The hosts say there have long been claims that the bombings may have been a false flag operation, including a story that FSB men were caught planting bombs.

[事实] Yeltsin appointed Vladimir Putin prime minister in August or September 1999, after Putin had run the FSB and made himself useful to the Yeltsin regime.

[事实] On December 31, 1999, Yeltsin resigned and said Putin would succeed him before a spring election.

[推测] The hosts portray Putin’s succession as a deal in which Putin protected Yeltsin, Yeltsin’s family, and those who had benefited under him.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it explains Putin’s rise through the lived experience of the 1990s rather than treating it as a sudden authoritarian accident. Its strongest sections connect economic collapse, oligarchic enrichment, state weakness, humiliation, and war into one political story.

[推测] The discussion is especially useful for listeners trying to understand why order, strength, and restored national pride could become persuasive themes in post-Yeltsin Russia.

[推测] A limitation is that the episode moves quickly across complex issues such as privatization, NATO expansion, Chechnya, and the apartment bombings, so some claims are presented in compressed form rather than deeply evidenced within the transcript itself.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners who want a narrative overview of how the Yeltsin years prepared the ground for Putin, rather than a detailed economic or legal history of post-Soviet Russia.
