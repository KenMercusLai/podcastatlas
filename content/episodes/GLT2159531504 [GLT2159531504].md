# Treason in Modern Britain: People, Power and Plot

## 概览

This episode of *The Rest is History* is recorded at the National Archives exhibition *Treason: People, Power and Plot*, with Neil Johnston guiding the hosts through documents that show how treason law changed from a medieval crime against the monarch into a broader, more unstable language of state power, sovereignty, rebellion and legitimacy.

The discussion begins with the trial of Charles I, where Parliament effectively reversed the old understanding of treason by claiming that the king had levied war against the people. From there, it moves through the Restoration, the punishment of the regicides, the Popish Plot, the American Revolution, Irish republicanism, and twentieth-century treason cases.

A recurring conclusion is that treason is never only a legal category. It is repeatedly described as political theatre, a tool governments adapt to crisis, and a charge whose meaning depends on who claims sovereignty and who wins power.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **The National Archives Exhibition**

[事实] The episode is recorded at the National Archives for the exhibition *Treason: People, Power and Plot*.
[事实] Neil Johnston explains that the exhibition brings together records from the 1352 Treason Act through to the modern day.
[推测] The exhibition frames treason as a long-running legal and political problem rather than a fixed historical category.

[02:13] **Charles I and the Reversal of Treason**

[事实] The hosts examine records of parliamentary proceedings from late 1649 or 1650 concerning the trial of Charles I.
[事实] Neil says the court empowered itself from the rump of the House of Commons to put the king on trial for treason.
[事实] Charles I refused to recognise the court and would not enter a plea.
[推测] The trial exposed a basic conflict between inherited royal sovereignty and Parliament’s claim to represent the people.

[03:39] **Sovereignty Moves from Monarch to People**

[事实] Under the older Treason Act, treason meant acts such as plotting against or imagining the death of the king.
[事实] The Commons claimed that Charles had levied war against the people and that sovereignty lay with the Commons rather than the monarch.
[事实] Neil describes treason trials as generally theatrical, with verdicts often close to a foregone conclusion in the early modern period.
[推测] The trial of Charles I anticipates a modern idea of treason as an offence against the state or nation, not only against the monarch’s person.

[07:42] **How the Trial Record Was Made**

[事实] The record shown in the exhibition was written after the events, based on contemporary notes taken by clerks.
[事实] The House of Commons instructed the clerks in February 1649 to compile the account, and the volume took about a year to emerge.
[事实] Neil says the volumes are heavily disputed but became very important.
[推测] The survival of these records mattered because they later became evidence in a changed political world.

[08:16] **The Restoration and the Declaration of Breda**

[事实] After Oliver Cromwell died in September 1658, Richard Cromwell lacked the authority to manage conflicts among army, Puritan and parliamentary factions.
[事实] General George Monk’s march on London in early 1660 helped end the republic.
[事实] Charles Stuart issued the Declaration of Breda through Sir John Grenville on 1 May 1660.
[事实] Charles promised not to refight the civil wars, left land disputes to Parliament, and offered liberty of conscience within limits.

[11:02] **Pardon, Oblivion and the Regicides**

[事实] Charles II proposed an act of general pardon, indemnity and oblivion.
[事实] The pardon excluded the 59 men associated with passing sentence on Charles I.
[事实] They were not legally defined as “regicides” but as traitors under the old logic of compassing and imagining the king’s death.
[推测] The Restoration settlement depended on forgetting much of the civil war while making a limited example of those most directly tied to the king’s execution.

[12:13] **Punishing the Regicides**

[事实] A proclamation in June 1660 ordered regicides to hand themselves in.
[事实] Nineteen trials took place in October before a special court under the authority of King’s Bench.
[事实] Thomas Harrison was among those executed, while the bodies of Cromwell, Henry Ireton and John Bradshaw were dug up and posthumously punished.
[事实] Two regicides escaped to America.

[13:23] **Public Reaction to Execution**

[事实] Neil says reactions to the executions were mixed: some thought the regicides deserved punishment, while others were revolted.
[事实] Contemporary accounts described the executions at Charing Cross as extremely bloody and disturbing.
[事实] The authorities had to be wary of large crowds gathering to witness executions.
[推测] The spectacle of treason punishment began to carry political risks for the restored monarchy.

[14:48] **The Decline of Spectacular Treason Punishment**

[事实] The hosts ask when hanging, drawing and quartering male traitors and burning female traitors became unacceptable.
[事实] Neil says that, as far as he is aware, this became increasingly unpalatable in the eighteenth century.
[推测] Enlightenment sensibilities and emerging political pluralism made spectacular violence less usable as a routine political tool.

[15:04] **Charles II and Limited Retribution**

[事实] Neil argues that Charles II was more politically astute, less dogmatic and more flexible than Charles I.
[事实] Charles II accepted a limited form of retribution rather than returning with an army.
[事实] By the end of Charles II’s reign, proto-political parties such as Whigs and Tories were emerging.
[推测] The Restoration helped move Britain toward a political culture where opposition was not automatically treated as treason.

[16:57] **The Popish Plot**

[事实] Neil describes the Popish Plot as a remarkable episode built on Titus Oates’s lies about Jesuits and Catholics.
[事实] Oates had failed in several religious and institutional settings before returning to London with some knowledge of Catholic and Jesuit networks.
[事实] The crisis occurred while James, Duke of York, was known to be Catholic and could succeed Charles II.
[推测] Oates’s allegations gained force because they exploited existing fears about Catholic power and succession.

[20:15] **Oates Before the Privy Council**

[事实] Oates brought his allegations before the Privy Council in September and October 1678.
[事实] Charles II initially dismissed the claims as nonsense, but ministers and councillors pursued them.
[事实] Oates alleged that Jesuits and Louis XIV were part of a plan to overthrow the Protestant succession and place James on the throne.
[事实] Neil says the core of the allegation was nonsense.

[22:01] **Coincidence and Escalation**

[事实] Edward Coleman’s papers contained language about facilitating the overthrow of the Protestant Crown, which seemed to support part of Oates’s claims.
[事实] The justice who notarised the initial allegations was found dead in an unexplained murder.
[事实] These events helped Oates become seen as a saviour of Protestant England.
[推测] A few apparently confirming events allowed a false conspiracy story to expand into national politics.

[23:51] **Trials, Propaganda and Political Parties**

[事实] The Popish Plot moved from private and political arenas into Parliament and the courts.
[事实] Oates was championed by powerful figures, including the Earl of Shaftesbury.
[事实] Catholics and Jesuits were charged, condemned and executed despite not being guilty of treason.
[事实] The arguments around excluding James from the throne are linked to the emergence of Whigs and Tories.

[25:10] **Aftermath of the Popish Plot**

[事实] Oates’s story eventually fell apart in the legal arena, and he later perjured himself.
[事实] Neil says the episode emboldened Charles II and consolidated James’s position.
[事实] James II was later forced into exile, but he was not called a traitor.
[推测] The Popish Plot damaged trust in treason accusations while keeping anti-Catholic fears politically powerful.

[26:49] **The American Declaration as Treason**

[事实] After the break, the hosts discuss the Declaration of Independence as a treasonous document from the British perspective.
[事实] Neil says the exhibition uses it to show movement away from treason against the monarch personally and toward treason against the state.
[事实] The colonists’ early grievances concerned taxation, representation, justice and self-determination.
[推测] The American Revolution turned British treason law into an Atlantic argument about who had legitimate authority.

[29:03] **Treason in the American Founding**

[事实] The 1352 Treason Act applied in British North America through colonial charters.
[事实] Once independence was declared, those loyal to the king could be treated as traitors by the new American side.
[事实] The United States incorporated treason into its founding constitutional framework, redefining it as levying war against the republic.
[推测] American treason law preserved an English legal inheritance while redirecting allegiance from monarch to republic.

[31:10] **Ireland, Reform and the United Irishmen**

[事实] The Treason Act applied to Ireland from 1495.
[事实] Neil says late eighteenth-century Irish movements were influenced by American independence and the French Revolution.
[事实] The Society of United Irishmen was founded in October 1791 by figures including Theobald Wolfe Tone.
[事实] The initial goals involved parliamentary reform and Catholic representation rather than immediate republicanism.

[33:07] **Thomas Hardy and Sedition Versus Treason**

[事实] Thomas Hardy of the London Corresponding Society was tried for treason.
[事实] Neil says Hardy and his associates were careful not to commit treasonous acts, though they committed seditious acts.
[事实] The prosecution argued that radical parliamentary reform would indirectly lead to the king’s death.
[事实] Hardy was acquitted because the treason case fell apart in court.

[35:04] **Trial Papers and the Rule of Law**

[事实] The exhibition includes trial papers and evidence gathered against Hardy.
[事实] The Treasury Solicitor underlined passages in Thomas Paine’s *Rights of Man* considered treasonous or seditious.
[事实] Neil says the case shows the vague emergence of a modern conception of the rule of law.
[推测] The Hardy trial marks a point where political radicalism became harder to prosecute successfully as treason.

[36:08] **The 1798 Irish Rebellion and Wolfe Tone**

[事实] Lord Edward Fitzgerald was posthumously attainted after the 1798 rebellion.
[事实] Wolfe Tone was accused of treason but went into exile when the Dublin government could not make the charges stick.
[事实] Tone sought support first in the United States and then in France, where he became an officer in the French army and received French citizenship.
[事实] The United Irishmen became a secret organisation seeking French help to overthrow British rule in Ireland and create a self-determining republic.

[38:34] **Republicanism and the Limits of Prosecution**

[事实] The hosts ask whether advocating a republic is inherently treasonous under a monarchy.
[事实] Neil says that under the wording of the act it could be, but prosecution became much harder.
[事实] Republican ideas, loyalism, attempted risings and conspiracies all remained part of nineteenth-century British politics.
[推测] As republican belief became more widespread, treason law became too broad and nebulous to apply consistently.

[40:26] **William Joyce and Twentieth-Century Treason**

[事实] William Joyce, known as Lord Haw-Haw, was the last person executed for treason in Britain.
[事实] Joyce was born in Brooklyn and had falsified documents to obtain a British passport.
[事实] The court treated the passport as creating allegiance to the Crown.
[事实] Joyce broadcast to Britain from Nazi Germany during the Second World War.

[41:33] **Bills of Attainder and Modern Discomfort**

[事实] The hosts discuss bills of attainder and whether they had become effectively unusable.
[事实] Neil says Churchill considered how Nazi leaders might be dealt with during the Second World War.
[事实] The idea of attainting Hitler was put to Churchill, but he dismissed it.
[推测] By the twentieth century, older mechanisms of political punishment could still be imagined legally but were difficult to defend publicly.

[42:30] **The Easter Rising and the 1916 Proclamation**

[事实] The final major document is connected to the Easter Rising of 1916.
[事实] The collection includes the bottom half of the Proclamation read by Patrick Pearse outside the General Post Office in Dublin.
[事实] Because type had been confiscated, the document was printed in two halves.
[事实] British forces later found the printing press in Liberty Hall and produced more copies.

[44:00] **Courts-Martial After the Rising**

[事实] Martial law was imposed in Ireland after the Easter Rising.
[事实] Rebel leaders were tried by court-martial and executed in secret.
[事实] The court-martial papers gathered evidence and intelligence reports to prove guilt.
[事实] Neil says the rebels saw themselves as fighting a war for independence rather than committing treason.

[45:19] **The Monarch’s Person and Late Treason Cases**

[事实] The hosts mention a 1981 incident in which a man fired six blank bullets at the Queen during Trooping the Colour.
[事实] He was charged with treason because he directly targeted the monarch.
[事实] Neil says that if charged under the 1352 legislation, this fits the older focus on harm to the monarch’s person.
[推测] Modern treason law remains clearest when it resembles its medieval core: direct attack on the sovereign.

[46:11] **Can Treason Law Be Reframed?**

[事实] Neil declines to say whether treason law can be adequately redeemed or changed.
[事实] He says governments have repeatedly updated the terms on which treason law is useful to them.
[事实] A 1661 law redefined treason to include seeking to harm Charles II’s person.
[推测] The episode presents treason law as historically adaptable but legally unstable.

[47:16] **Final Judgments and Exhibition Details**

[事实] Asked whom he would condemn, Neil chooses Oliver Cromwell.
[事实] Asked whom he would save, Neil chooses Willie Pearse, saying he was junior and had the wrong surname.
[事实] The exhibition opened on 5 November 2022, was free to attend, required no booking, and was said to run until April.
[事实] Neil co-wrote the accompanying book, *A History of Treason*.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s strongest value is its use of archival objects to make legal history concrete. Instead of treating treason as an abstract statute, the discussion moves through trial books, acts, notebooks, proclamations and court-martial papers to show how documents preserve moments when political legitimacy was being contested.

A major strength is the through-line from Charles I to modern cases: treason shifts from loyalty to the monarch’s body, to loyalty to Parliament, state, nation, republic or empire. The hosts also keep returning to the practical problem that a treason charge depends not only on law but on power, public opinion and prosecutorial risk.

[推测] The episode is especially useful for listeners interested in British constitutional history, early modern politics, the American Revolution, Irish nationalism, or the relationship between law and state authority. Its limitation is that several enormous subjects, especially Ireland and America, are necessarily compressed into short discussions.

[推测] As a podcast episode, it works best as a guided historical tour rather than a full legal history of treason. Its lasting point is that treason is most revealing when the meaning of loyalty itself is under pressure.
