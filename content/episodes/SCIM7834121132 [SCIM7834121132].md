# Dr. Nick Epley on Social Connection, Misjudging Others, and Everyday Conversations

## 概览

This episode centers on Dr. Nick Epley’s research into social connection: how people infer what others think, why those inferences are often wrong, and how small everyday interactions can improve well-being. The discussion moves from theory of mind, eye gaze, voice, and stereotypes into concrete questions about strangers, loneliness, social anxiety, family, adoption, and modeling social behavior.

A major conclusion is that people often underestimate how positively others will respond when approached with genuine interest. Epley argues that many barriers to connection are mistaken beliefs, not fixed social realities, and that testing those beliefs through real interaction can reduce anxiety and create better moments.

The conversation also expands beyond casual socializing. Epley connects his research to his own family life, including adoption, raising a daughter with Down syndrome, parenting children whose paths differ from parental expectations, and learning to treat connection as a daily habit rather than a rare deep event.

## 分段落总结

[00:58] **Episode frame and guest introduction**

[事实] Andrew Huberman introduces Dr. Nick Epley as a behavioral scientist at the University of Chicago who studies social connection.
[事实] The episode focuses not only on close relationships, but also on everyday interactions with people one does not know well.
[事实] Huberman says the conversation will cover assumptions about others, online and offline social perception, and Epley’s family experiences.

[02:50] **Anthropomorphism and mind reading**

[事实] Epley explains anthropomorphism as an attempt to infer what is happening inside another agent, whether that agent is a person, animal, deity, planet, or oneself.
[事实] He describes this kind of mind reading as useful for interpreting current behavior and predicting what someone may do next.
[事实] He says people do not always infer correctly, but that making mental-state inferences is generally better than not doing so.

[05:36] **How people infer minds: self, stereotypes, and behavior**

[事实] Epley says people use themselves as a guide when they know little about others, producing egocentric assumptions.
[事实] He says stereotypes can contain some accuracy but tend to exaggerate differences between groups.
[事实] He describes behavior as powerful but tricky evidence, because people often infer a simple intention that corresponds too directly to what they observe.
[事实] He gives hitting someone as an example: without context it may look aggressive, but with self-defense context it is interpreted differently.

[09:04] **Eye gaze and social intelligence**

[事实] Huberman emphasizes the importance of vision and eye gaze for understanding intent and attention.
[事实] Epley agrees that eyes provide valid information and says humans are extremely sensitive to cues from gaze.
[事实] He cites research comparing toddlers, chimpanzees, and orangutans: they performed similarly on physical reasoning tasks, but human toddlers excelled on social reasoning tasks.
[事实] He says gaze helps humans infer what another person is thinking about and anticipate action.

[17:20] **Voice as evidence of an active mind**

[事实] Epley says voice carries information about emotion, enthusiasm, deliberation, sarcasm, and intentionality.
[事实] He contrasts voice with text, saying typed communication often loses cues that make jokes or sarcasm clear.
[事实] In research on political disagreement, hearing a person’s voice reduced the tendency to view the other side as less thoughtful, rational, or intelligent.
[推测] The discussion implies that voice humanizes people because it makes the process of thinking more perceptible.

[22:26] **Words, tone, and job pitches**

[事实] Epley rejects the pop-psychology claim that most communication is nonverbal, saying words still matter.
[事实] In studies of MBA elevator pitches, listeners judged candidates as more intelligent and hireable when they heard the pitch rather than only reading it.
[事实] The MBA students themselves expected written and spoken pitches to be judged similarly.
[推测] Epley’s point is not that writing is weak, but that ordinary writing often fails to convey the liveliness of a mind.

[25:03] **AI, voice, and anthropomorphism**

[事实] Huberman asks whether richer AI voices or avatars could improve how people understand one another.
[事实] Epley says AI is increasingly likely to be used for social purposes, including preparing for difficult conversations.
[事实] He warns that if people connect with AI instead of other humans, that could create problems.
[推测] Epley predicts that humanlike voice in AI will increase trust because people will anthropomorphize it more readily.

[27:04] **Public figures, politics, and ambiguity**

[事实] Huberman asks whether increased video and audio exposure to public figures makes people better at assessing them.
[事实] Epley says he does not know, and emphasizes that people are ambiguous stimuli.
[事实] He describes my-side bias: people with different prior beliefs can interpret the same statement or behavior in very different ways.
[推测] More information alone may not improve judgment if listeners begin from strongly different interpretive frames.

[30:20] **Isolation, texting, and social contact**

[事实] Epley says going from no contact to some contact is a large improvement for well-being.
[事实] He cites Gallup well-being data discussed by Daniel Kahneman and Angus Deaton, saying a day spent entirely alone is associated with substantially worse well-being.
[事实] He says texting can help maintain an existing relationship, but is not as good as talking for building a relationship over time.
[推测] The episode treats digital contact as useful but limited, not as equivalent to isolation.

[34:15] **Why humans are built for connection**

[事实] Epley links human sociality to brain organization, including the social brain hypothesis and the size of the neocortex relative to social complexity.
[事实] He says isolation would have been dangerous for much of human history, and that loneliness pushes people to reconnect.
[事实] He says loneliness is associated with cortisol spikes and can compromise cardiovascular and immune function.
[事实] He describes connection, including hugs and meaningful conversations, as rewarding signals that the body is built to seek.

[37:20] **Selfhood, solitude, and the looking-glass self**

[事实] Huberman reflects that extended isolation can distort thinking and raise existential questions about whether one’s inner life has any external impact.
[事实] Epley says the closest research concerns solitary confinement, which is harmful to mental health and sense of self.
[事实] He references the sociological idea that people understand themselves through other people.
[推测] The discussion frames social interaction not only as pleasant, but as part of how identity and self-worth are maintained.

[44:17] **Social media and responsiveness**

[事实] Huberman suggests that provocative online comments may give isolated people a sense that their words affect others.
[事实] Epley shifts the point to conversation, saying back-and-forth responsiveness lets people detect that others are paying attention.
[事实] He describes nods, vocal acknowledgments, and synchrony as part of why conversation feels good.
[推测] The episode implies that online platforms may become healthier if they reward constructive responsiveness rather than outrage.

[48:18] **Cooperation as the function of social connection**

[事实] Epley says social connection helps people coordinate and cooperate.
[事实] He argues that groups capable of cooperation outperform groups where people cannot coordinate.
[事实] He emphasizes cooperation with non-kin as a defining feature of human social life.
[推测] In this framing, connection is not merely emotional; it supports survival, institutions, and collective action.

[50:36] **Adoption, family roles, and love beyond kin**

[事实] Huberman and Epley discuss adopted family members and reject the idea that adopted children are not truly family.
[事实] Epley says he and his wife have three adopted children.
[事实] He describes adoption as a shift from hypothetical to real, after which the role of parent becomes central.
[事实] He says people often care beyond what narrow self-interest or genetic kinship would predict.

[57:24] **Strangers, familiarity, and culture**

[事实] Huberman describes a South American social setting where people mostly interacted with friends they already knew rather than strangers.
[事实] Epley says there has always been some anxiety about strangers because they involve uncertainty.
[事实] He says familiar people and familiar objects are easier to trust because their risks are better known.
[推测] The conversation suggests that reluctance toward strangers may come from uncertainty rather than hostility.

[60:00] **Manners, politeness, and missed openings**

[事实] Huberman argues that manners can create low-level social openings, such as asking how someone’s day is going.
[事实] Epley says manners can be thought of as habits and sensibilities about decency.
[事实] He notes that people sometimes avoid engaging others because they think interrupting would be impolite.
[事实] He says norms differ across places, citing the UK and Japan as contexts where not intruding can be seen as polite.

[64:08] **Underestimating others’ interest**

[事实] Epley says he often opens conversations with strangers.
[事实] His research finds that people underestimate how interested others are in engaging with them.
[事实] He says two silent people may each infer that the other does not want to talk, even when both might be willing.
[事实] He frames social connection as an invitation rather than a demand.

[65:28] **Good moments and everyday well-being**

[事实] Epley describes a 23-minute Uber ride in which he had a deep conversation with an Iranian driver who shared painful life experiences.
[事实] He says the goal was not a lasting relationship, but making that moment better.
[事实] He compares well-being to something that needs repeated replenishment through good moments.
[事实] He says his research changed his life by making him notice opportunities to take interest in other people and offer compliments.

[70:31] **Brief exchanges and connection to humanity**

[事实] Huberman reflects on brief social moments such as a fist bump or recognizing a shared music interest.
[事实] Epley says interactions with one stranger may change one’s feeling about a broader group or about humanity.
[事实] He gives examples of complimenting a woman’s red glasses and talking with a young man named Gustavo on a train.
[推测] These examples support the idea that small interactions can affect mood and social trust beyond the immediate exchange.

[78:40] **Extroversion, introversion, and well-being**

[事实] Epley says extroversion is strongly correlated with happiness and positive affect.
[事实] He describes studies where people asked to act more extroverted reported more positive feelings.
[事实] He says introverts and extroverts alike tend to feel better when spending time with others than when alone.
[推测] He treats social engagement like exercise: people may differ in habits, but many would benefit from doing a little more.

[82:27] **Social anxiety and real exposure**

[事实] Huberman asks about people with social anxiety.
[事实] Epley says social anxiety is treatable and discusses exposure therapy with reference to Stefan Hofmann.
[事实] He says real exposure, not simulated or pretend exposure, is what helps when the fear is misplaced.
[事实] He argues that exposure works by changing beliefs about how others respond, not merely by numbing anxiety.

[86:52] **Rejection therapy and asking for help**

[事实] Epley describes Jia Jiang’s project of making unusual requests for 100 days to face rejection.
[事实] Jiang expected repeated rejection, but was accepted more often than rejected in the posted requests Epley’s lab reviewed.
[事实] Examples included asking for Olympic-ring-shaped donuts, speaking to a plane, planting a rose, and using a backyard.
[事实] Epley connects this to the underestimation of compliance effect: people underestimate how often others will agree to help.
[推测] The story illustrates that fear of rejection often prevents both the requester and the helper from having a positive experience.

[95:00] **Boundaries, risk, and being “sticky”**

[事实] Huberman raises concern that some people misread casual friendliness as deeper closeness.
[事实] Epley acknowledges that people need sensitivity to risks and to how others respond.
[事实] He says women may reasonably be more concerned about men misinterpreting friendliness because of physical risk.
[事实] He recommends starting small and safe, such as greeting a familiar person whose name one does not know.
[推测] The guidance is to practice connection while respecting cues and boundaries, not to pursue interaction at any cost.

[103:00] **Phones, earbuds, and ambiguous cues**

[事实] Huberman says phones and earbuds make it harder to know whether someone is available for conversation.
[事实] Epley says such cues are ambiguous: a person may use earbuds because nobody is talking to them.
[事实] He describes saying hello to a man named Tibo, who removed his earbud and became a friend over time.
[事实] Epley says beliefs about others should be treated as bets that may be wrong.
[推测] The episode encourages low-pressure testing of ambiguous social cues rather than assuming rejection in advance.

[107:20] **Shared venues and social readiness**

[事实] Huberman mentions churches, religious gatherings, festivals, TED, and sauna gatherings as places where people may be more open to interaction.
[事实] He suggests people may be seeking settings where interaction with strangers is socially expected.

[事实] Epley says Down syndrome is not what defines Lindsay in daily life; she has her own personality, interests, friends, and preferences.
[事实] He says families may underestimate their strength, available support, and the love they may feel for a child with Down syndrome.
[事实] He is careful not to tell all families what to do and frames his comments through his own family’s story.
[推测] The discussion challenges assumptions that a diagnosis should determine how people imagine a child’s life or a family’s future.

[事实] Huberman asks how bystanders should respond when a child or adult with atypical behavior is disruptive in public.
[事实] Epley compares it to responding to stuttering: wait patiently, do not make the issue the center of attention, and continue respectfully.
[事实] He says that when one knows the person or caregiver better, one can ask directly how to help.
[推测] The practical advice is to choose patience and respect over pity, avoidance, or performative concern.

[129:16] **Outdoors, hunting, and conservation as connection**

[事实] Epley says he grew up in rural Iowa, hunting and fishing with his father.
[事实] He describes outdoor activities as social, often involving family, friends, and community.
[事实] He says he does conservation work, including invasive species removal and planting trees on land enrolled in the Conservation Reserve Program.
[推测] For Epley, outdoor life is not an escape from sociality but another setting where care, skill, and relationship are practiced.

[131:16] **The Oregon elk hunt and unexpected cooperation**

[事实] Epley describes going elk hunting in northeast Oregon with his oldest son Ben.
[事实] They encountered a group of hunters, and Ben initially wanted to move on.
[事实] Epley chose to talk with them, and the group shared local knowledge, invited them to dinner, and stayed in contact afterward.
[事实] Epley says the experience became better because they connected rather than competing or avoiding one another.
[推测] The story repeats the episode’s core pattern: uncertainty about strangers can hide opportunities for generosity and belonging.

[216:50] **Ethical hunting and stereotypes**

[事实] Huberman notes that people often hold negative stereotypes about hunters.
[事实] Epley says most hunters he has met care about the outdoors and do not treat killing an animal as the main point.
[事实] He says he uses a crossbow now because it helps him take animals more accurately, ethically, and quickly.
[推测] The exchange connects social perception to stereotypes: groups may be more complex and conscientious than outsiders assume.

[218:50] **Modeling social habits across generations**

[事实] Huberman asks how older generations can model good social interaction for younger people.
[事实] Epley says people should pay attention to habits because small repeated behaviors are what others observe.
[事实] He describes a “hello walk” at work, where he deliberately keeps his head up, smiles, and greets people on the way to his office.
[事实] He says social research becomes useful when translated into routine habits.
[推测] The final lesson is that social connection is taught less by abstract advice than by repeated visible behavior.

[事实] Huberman says he applies “classroom rules” to social media by avoiding exchanges he would not allow in a classroom.
[事实] He says he blocks hostile exchanges to maintain education and respect rather than to avoid criticism.
[事实] Epley agrees that small routine norms shape what people experience as appropriate.
[推测] The conversation frames online community standards as another form of modeled social behavior.

[推测] The episode’s main value is practical: it turns social psychology into everyday behaviors such as saying hello, offering a sincere compliment, asking for help, and treating silence or earbuds as ambiguous rather than final rejection.

[推测] A limitation is that some claims about broader social trends, such as changing manners, church attendance, or post-pandemic gathering behavior, are discussed conversationally rather than deeply evidenced in the transcript.
