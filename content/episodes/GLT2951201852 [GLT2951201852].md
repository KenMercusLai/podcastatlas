# Cortes, Malinche, and the Road to Tenochtitlan

## 概览

This episode follows Hernan Cortes as he turns inland from the Mexican coast toward Tenochtitlan, the capital of the Mexica, and asks what he thought he was doing when he marched toward a vast, wealthy, militarized world he barely understood.

A central theme is interpretation: Spanish accounts are treated as political, literary, and self-justifying texts, while Malinche’s role as translator and possible strategist is repeatedly emphasized. The hosts stress that misunderstandings, opportunism, and deliberate spin shaped how later history remembered the conquest.

The episode’s major turning points are Cortes’s encounter with Tlaxcala, the alliance or employment relationship that followed, the massacre at Cholula, and the Spaniards’ first sight of the Valley of Mexico. It ends with Cortes and his followers inside Tenochtitlan, hosted in a royal palace but unsure whether they are guests, prisoners, or something stranger.

## 分段落总结

[01:41] **Cortes Turns Inland**

[事实] The episode opens with a passage from Dominic’s children’s history book about Cortes urging his men to march forward and “conquer or die.”

[事实] The hosts discuss Cortes’s decision to move inland toward Tenochtitlan after scouting the coast of Mexico.

[事实] Malinche is presented as a crucial interpreter and adviser, though the hosts say it is impossible to know whether she was manipulating Cortes, was a victim, or had some other role.

[推测] The framing gives Malinche more agency than older conquest narratives often allowed.

[03:55] **Unreliable Spanish Sources**

[事实] Bernal Diaz is described as the best Spanish source for the events, but his memoir may contain invented or highly shaped material.

[事实] The hosts argue that Spanish accounts of the conquest were political because the conquistadors needed to protect themselves from prosecution.

[事实] They also say the Spanish framed their actions through chivalric romances and classical models such as Caesar and Alexander.

[推测] The episode treats the conquest narrative not simply as a record of events, but as a story later actors crafted to justify themselves.

[05:40] **Cortes’s Motives**

[事实] The hosts say they do not know what Cortes expected to achieve by marching on a huge, rich, armed city.

[事实] Cortes is contrasted with Alexander: Alexander had already conquered the Persian Empire, while Cortes barely understood the language, customs, religion, food, flora, or fauna around him.

[事实] His motives are described as a mixture of courage, lunacy, greed, and confidence in Christ.

[事实] His followers were several hundred armed Spaniards, many of them tradesmen rather than professional soldiers, accompanied by porters, enslaved Tainos, and Totonacs.

[07:46] **The Mountain March and Zautla**

[事实] Cortes’s party moved inland from the coast into colder, wetter mountain terrain, where many Taino porters died or were said to have died.

[事实] The hosts note that Spanish descriptions of the mountain journey resemble classical and Alexander-style literary formulas.

[事实] At Zautla, the local chief Olintetl hosted the Spaniards, and Cortes saw skulls displayed in the square.

[事实] Olintetl is said to have told Cortes that all men were vassals of Montezuma because Montezuma ruled the world.

[推测] The exchange highlights how both Europeans and Mesoamericans were encountering worldviews they could not yet fully interpret.

[10:23] **Entering Tlaxcala**

[事实] Cortes crossed into Tlaxcala, a Nahua confederation of four cities that shared language, religion, and customs with the Mexica but did not pay tribute to them.

[事实] Tlaxcala was surrounded by the lands of the Aztec Triple Alliance and hated the Mexica.

[事实] The Tlaxcalans and Mexica fought regular “flower wars,” described as a mix of war, ritual, sport, and performance.

[事实] The hosts say the Mexica tolerated Tlaxcala partly because destroying it would have been too great a challenge.

[12:15] **Tlaxcalan Attacks and Spanish Violence**

[事实] For about three weeks, Tlaxcalans attacked Cortes’s party, while the Spaniards became stuck on a hill and ran short of food.

[事实] Montezuma’s envoys continued to bring gifts and warnings that the Spaniards should avoid Tlaxcala.

[事实] Cortes and Pedro de Alvarado launched night attacks on villages, tortured priests, and mutilated women and children as warnings.

[事实] Spanish cannon fire devastated densely packed Tlaxcalan attackers in one battle where only one Spaniard died.

[推测] The hosts interpret Cortes’s violence as a form of political signaling to Tlaxcala and the Mexica.

[14:28] **The Tlaxcalan Deal**

[事实] In mid-September, the Tlaxcalans stopped attacking and invited Cortes into their city.

[事实] They began addressing Cortes as “Malinche,” and Malinche was central to the negotiations.

[事实] Cortes and his men were welcomed in Tlaxcala with crowds, hospitality, and brides, and they stayed for two or three weeks.

[事实] The hosts argue that the old view sees Cortes recruiting Tlaxcalans, while newer conquest historiography sees the Tlaxcalans recruiting the Spaniards as mercenaries.

[推测] The power in the alliance may have lain more with Tlaxcala than with the Spaniards, because Tlaxcala had the troops, food, and local knowledge.

[17:05] **Cholula and Massacre**

[事实] After Tlaxcala, the Spaniards went south to Cholula, a beautiful pilgrimage center with a great pyramid and a major temple of Quetzalcoatl.

[事实] Cholula had recently shifted from alliance with Tlaxcala toward Tenochtitlan.

[事实] Malinche reportedly warned Cortes that the Cholulans were planning to attack, based on information from the Tlaxcalans.

[事实] Cortes and his Tlaxcalan allies massacred the Cholulans, sacked and burned the city, enslaved people, and committed rape and pillage.

[推测] The massacre served both Tlaxcalan interests against Cholula and Cortes’s need to present himself as a terrifying player in regional politics.

[20:33] **First Sight of the Valley of Mexico**

[事实] After Cholula, the Spaniards crossed toward the Valley of Mexico through the volcanoes known as the white woman and the smoking man.

[事实] From the heights they saw fields, farms, villages, a huge lake, causeways, floating gardens, canoes, and the island capital of Tenochtitlan.

[事实] The hosts say the wider metropolitan area may have contained at least a million people, while estimates for Tenochtitlan itself vary widely.

[事实] Spanish observers said Seville looked like a village compared with what they saw.

[推测] The visual scale of Mexica urban civilization shocked the Spaniards because it exceeded their expectations of the Americas.

[24:11] **Astonishment and Loss**

[事实] Bernal Diaz described the lake cities, pyramids, and towers as so extraordinary that some Spaniards wondered whether they were dreaming.

[事实] The hosts believe Diaz’s tone of wonder is probably sincere.

[事实] The Spaniards compared what they saw to Venice, Constantinople, and Rome.

[事实] Diaz later wrote that the beautiful places he saw had been torn down and destroyed.

[推测] The hosts read Diaz’s description as carrying regret, because the Spaniards who admired the city were also among those who destroyed it.

[27:37] **Approaching Tenochtitlan**

[事实] As the Spaniards advanced, they were repeatedly met by emissaries, including Cacama, king of Texcoco and Montezuma’s nephew.

[事实] The messages remained mixed: the visitors were welcomed, but also told that Montezuma was ill or too busy and that they should not come further.

[事实] Crowds stared at the Spaniards’ horses, dogs, white faces, and red beards, while the Spaniards could not tell whether the crowds were curious or hostile.

[事实] On November 8, 1519, Cortes’s party crossed the causeway from Iztapalapan toward Tenochtitlan.

[推测] The hosts treat this date as a symbolic pivot in world history because of what it foreshadows about imperialism and colonialism.

[30:22] **Formal Entry and Moctezuma’s Arrival**

[事实] The Spanish entered in ranks, with Cortes and senior captains at the front, followed by infantry, horsemen, crossbowmen, Tlaxcalans, porters, and enslaved people.

[事实] Mexica nobles performed lengthy formal gestures that the Spaniards did not understand, while Malinche translated for Cortes.

[事实] The party was met by Jaguar Knights and then by Moctezuma, who arrived on a litter under a jeweled canopy.

[事实] Moctezuma wore a large green feathered headdress and was carried by high-ranking figures including his brother Cuitlahuac and his nephew Cacama.

[32:31] **Rethinking Moctezuma**

[事实] The hosts reject the traditional portrayal of Moctezuma as a weak ruler who simply believed the Spaniards were gods and wanted to surrender.

[事实] Moctezuma had ruled for about 17 years and is described as formidable, effective, and politically skilled.

[事实] He expanded frontiers, crushed rebellions, sent officials to gather tribute, and ruled a relatively united Mexica empire.

[事实] The hosts contrast this with the divided Inca world the Spanish later encountered in Peru.

[推测] Moctezuma’s caution may have reflected a ruler trying to assess an unprecedented threat while preserving his people and authority.

[37:15] **The Meeting of Cortes and Moctezuma**

[事实] Cortes tried to embrace Moctezuma, but Moctezuma’s brother and nephew stopped him.

[事实] Cortes and Moctezuma exchanged necklaces.

[事实] What they said to each other is described as highly controversial among historians.

[事实] Moctezuma appears to have welcomed Cortes with formal hospitality, saying the city was his home and that he could rest there.

[推测] Phrases later read as surrender may have been courtly formulas of welcome rather than literal political submission.

[39:52] **Did Moctezuma Surrender?**

[事实] Traditional sources such as Cortes’s letters and Bernal Diaz present Moctezuma as surrendering his kingdom and accepting the Spanish king as overlord.

[事实] The hosts and modern historians discussed in the episode find this implausible.

[事实] Cortes needed the Spanish king to believe this version because otherwise he looked like a man acting against orders in a failed private adventure.

[事实] Matthew Restall’s work is cited as reshaping how the hosts understand the meeting between Cortes and Moctezuma.

[推测] The surrender story may have grown from legal self-defense, mistranslation, selective memory, and Spanish exploitation of Nahua hospitality language.

[44:11] **What Nobody Knows**

[事实] The hosts emphasize that no one knows exactly how Malinche translated, whether she spun the encounter, or how Moctezuma’s words were understood in the moment.

[事实] Moctezuma may have publicly framed the Spaniards as long-lost kin to show his people that he understood the situation.

[事实] The hosts say Moctezuma clearly had not surrendered his kingdom.

[事实] They also identify a major gap in the narrative: where the Tlaxcalans stayed and how Moctezuma handled the presence of his enemies inside the city.

[推测] Spanish sources downplay the Tlaxcalans because acknowledging their scale would make the conquest look less like a heroic Spanish achievement.

[46:09] **Guests, Prisoners, or Exhibits**

[事实] The episode ends with the Spaniards lodged in a royal palace in the heart of Tenochtitlan.

[事实] They were hundreds of miles from the coast and thousands of miles from Spain, surrounded by people whose language most of them did not speak.

[事实] The hosts say the central question is whether they were guests, prisoners, or something else.

[事实] They preview Matthew Restall’s theory that the Spaniards may have been treated as creatures destined for a zoo-like status.

[推测] The cliffhanger turns the familiar conquest story into a problem of mutual uncertainty rather than simple Spanish mastery.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s strongest value is historiographical: it does not simply retell Cortes’s march, but asks how conquest stories were constructed, who benefited from them, and which Indigenous actors have been minimized.

[推测] Its best moments come from reframing power. Tlaxcala, Malinche, and Moctezuma are treated not as background figures in a Spanish adventure, but as political actors operating inside their own urgent calculations.

[推测] The main limitation is that many of the most important questions remain unresolved because the sources are partial, self-interested, and filtered through translation. The hosts acknowledge this directly rather than filling every gap.

[推测] This episode is especially suited to listeners interested in the Spanish conquest, Aztec history, historical memory, and the way historians read unreliable sources.
