# Diocletian and the Remaking of Rome

## 概览

This episode traces the rise of Diocletian, born Diocles in Dalmatia near modern Split, from a very humble background to Roman emperor. The hosts frame his ascent through the crisis of the third century, when civil wars, frontier pressure, economic breakdown, plague and tax collapse nearly destroyed the Roman imperial order.

The central argument is that Diocletian ended the old Augustan settlement and rebuilt the empire on new foundations: a militarized bureaucracy, a divided imperial system, stronger frontiers, heavier taxation and a more ceremonial, distant model of rulership. He is presented as both a cultural traditionalist and an institutional revolutionary.

The episode also emphasizes the darker side of his rule, especially the Great Persecution of Christians beginning in 303. It ends with Diocletian’s unprecedented abdication, retirement to his palace in Dalmatia, the eventual failure of the tetrarchy, and the lasting importance of his reforms for the later Roman and Byzantine worlds.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **Croatia-ish and the Subject of Diocletian**

[事实] The episode opens by connecting Croatia to Roman history through Diocletian, who was born in the early 240s AD at Salona, near modern Split.

[事实] The hosts note that Diocletian’s Palace remains central to the city of Split and is one of the most extraordinary surviving Roman structures.

[事实] Diocletian was born as Diocles and came from an obscure background; his father was a scribe who may have been a freedman or possibly a slave.

[推测] The episode uses Diocletian’s Croatian or Dalmatian origin as a way to discuss a wider transformation of the Roman Empire rather than modern Croatian national history.

[02:29] **The Third-Century Crisis**

[事实] Diocletian’s rise is explained through the chaos of the third century, when the Roman Empire faced roughly 50 years of instability.

[事实] The Augustan settlement had balanced autocratic imperial power with the continuing prestige of the Senate, but this system broke down under military, fiscal and political pressure.

[事实] Rome faced stronger enemies on several fronts, especially the Sassanian Empire in the East, which replaced the Parthians and became a major rival power.

[事实] Civil wars, frontier invasions, collapsing tax bases, famine and plague weakened the empire and made the army the key surviving institution of order.

[06:46] **The Rise of Illyrian Soldier-Emperors**

[事实] As military pressures increased, professional officers replaced senators in command roles, and many of these officers came from Illyricum in the Balkans.

[事实] Diocletian belonged to this Illyrian military world and rose through the Roman army, eventually commanding cavalry on the Danube.

[事实] Earlier Illyrian emperors such as Claudius, Aurelian and Probus had already shown that Balkan military men could become highly effective rulers.

[推测] The hosts suggest that Rome’s crisis created conditions in which hard, practical military ability mattered more than aristocratic education or senatorial pedigree.

[12:38] **Carus, Numerian and Diocletian’s Seizure of Power**

[事实] The emperor Carus died in 283 during a successful eastern campaign, with the story being that he was struck by lightning.

[事实] Carus’s son Numerian became co-emperor but died while being carried back west in a litter, with soldiers noticing a smell before discovering his corpse.

[事实] At a military council, Diocles accused the Praetorian prefect Aper of murdering Numerian, killed him publicly, and was proclaimed emperor as Diocletian in late November 284.

[推测] Because the sources are limited and Diocletian benefited from the events, the episode leaves open the possibility that the official story may not be the whole truth.

[16:16] **Defeating Carinus and Ending the Augustan Order**

[事实] Diocletian still had to face Numerian’s brother Carinus, who ruled in the West from Rome.

[事实] Carinus was portrayed very negatively by Diocletian’s propagandists, though the hosts say he may actually have been effective.

[事实] Carinus met Diocletian near modern Belgrade and was assassinated just as he seemed close to victory, leaving Diocletian sole emperor.

[事实] Diocletian did not go to Rome to have his power ratified by the Senate, signaling the Senate’s declining importance.

[18:46] **Stabilizing the Empire and Choosing Maximian**

[事实] After becoming emperor, Diocletian faced instability on the Danube, rebellion and tax protests in Gaul, and the breakaway rule of Carausius in Britain.

[事实] Diocletian campaigned successfully in the Balkans and appointed Maximian, another Illyrian soldier, first as Caesar and later as Augustus.

[事实] Maximian crushed the Bagaudae in Gaul and pushed enemies back beyond the Rhine, though he could not immediately retake Britain because he lacked control of the Channel.

[推测] Diocletian’s appointment of Maximian shows his belief that one ruler could no longer govern the entire Roman Empire effectively.

[23:31] **Two Augusti and the Logic of Shared Rule**

[事实] In 286, Diocletian promoted Maximian to Augustus, creating two senior emperors.

[事实] The hosts describe this as both a radical innovation and a return to older Roman habits of divided authority, such as the republican consulship.

[事实] Diocletian took the title Jovius, associating himself with Jupiter, while Maximian took Herculius, associating himself with Hercules.

[推测] The system made emperorship more openly sacred and hierarchical than under Augustus, despite Diocletian presenting himself as a traditionalist.

[26:05] **The Tetrarchy**

[事实] In 292, Diocletian and Maximian adopted two deputies as Caesars, creating a system of four rulers known as the tetrarchy.

[事实] Constantius became Caesar in the West, while Galerius became Caesar in the East; the empire was divided geographically while all four remained emperors.

[事实] Constantius was given responsibility for Gaul and Britain, Maximian for Italy and Africa, Galerius for Greece and the Balkans, and Diocletian kept the richest eastern provinces.

[事实] The hosts argue that Diocletian chose trusted and capable men who were strong enough to govern but not imaginative enough to rival him.

[29:58] **Military Success and the Treaty of Nisibis**

[事实] Constantius retook Britain, Diocletian suppressed a rebellion in Egypt, and Diocletian and Galerius won a major war against Persia.

[事实] The Romans captured the Persian royal family, forcing the Persian king to accept humiliating terms.

[事实] The Treaty of Nisibis in 299 greatly strengthened Rome’s eastern frontier.

[推测] The hosts treat the eastern settlement as perhaps Diocletian’s most important contribution to imperial stability because it gave him space to reform the state.

[31:36] **Administrative and Military Reforms**

[事实] Diocletian increased Roman forces, improved fortifications and reorganized the empire’s administration.

[事实] He divided provinces into smaller units and created larger administrative groupings called dioceses, with officials called vicars.

[事实] Italy lost its special privileges and began to be taxed like other regions.

[事实] The hosts describe the empire under Diocletian as a military-fiscal system that existed to raise money, pay troops and defend itself.

[推测] These reforms helped create the administrative foundations later inherited by the Byzantine Empire.

[34:40] **Imperial Ceremony and Centralized Order**

[事实] Diocletian spent much of his time in Nicomedia and moved across the Balkans and the East rather than ruling from Rome.

[事实] He introduced proskynesis, requiring people entering the emperor’s presence to bow or prostrate themselves.

[事实] This ceremonial style contrasted sharply with Augustus’s earlier pose of being merely a senator among senators.

[推测] The hosts suggest that Diocletian’s court style partly borrowed from Persian models, while his bureaucracy reflected the structural needs of governing a large pre-industrial empire.

[37:05] **Inflation and the Edict on Maximum Prices**

[事实] Diocletian tried to stop inflation by stabilizing the currency and issuing new gold and silver coinage.

[事实] In 301, he introduced the Edict on Maximum Prices, setting legal maximum prices for many goods.

[事实] The hosts say the price edict did not work, though it remains valuable to historians because it records prices and wages.

[事实] Diocletian’s attempt to strengthen gold and silver coinage had more lasting success, especially through the later gold solidus.

[38:30] **The Great Persecution of Christians**

[事实] After stabilizing the eastern frontier, Diocletian turned to religious order and asked why the gods had allowed Rome to suffer.

[事实] In Antioch, the Christian deacon Romanus tried to stop sacrifices to the gods and was punished and executed.

[事实] Diocletian and Galerius discussed how to handle Christians; Galerius pushed for harsher action, and the oracle of Apollo at Didyma supported persecution.

[事实] In February 303, Diocletian had a church in Nicomedia destroyed and issued an edict against Christians requiring scriptures to be handed over and churches to be pulled down.

[事实] The hosts identify this persecution as the worst remembered by the church, especially in the East.

[42:32] **Rome, Triumph and Abdication**

[事实] In November 303, Diocletian visited Rome for the first and last time to celebrate the twentieth anniversary of his rule and a triumph.

[事实] The hosts say this was the last pagan triumph celebrated in Rome.

[事实] Diocletian disliked Rome and left by the end of the year; he then became seriously ill.

[事实] In 305, he abdicated on the same hill near Nicomedia where he had been proclaimed emperor, becoming the first Roman emperor to voluntarily give up power.

[44:51] **Succession Problems and Constantine**

[事实] When Diocletian abdicated, Maximian was also required to retire, though he did not want to.

[事实] Galerius and Constantius became Augusti, while Maximinus and Severus became Caesars.

[事实] Constantine, son of Constantius, was present at Diocletian’s abdication but was not made Caesar.

[事实] Constantine later joined Constantius in Britain, and after Constantius died in York, Constantine claimed power directly.

[推测] The episode presents this succession arrangement as inherently unstable because it left powerful imperial sons without clear roles.

[46:29] **Retirement, Split and Diocletian’s Legacy**

[事实] Diocletian retired to Dalmatia near Salona, where he had built a vast palace-like fortress.

[事实] He famously grew cabbages there and refused Maximian’s suggestion that they return to power.

[事实] Diocletian died in 312, by which time the tetrarchic system had already broken down into civil war.

[事实] The hosts argue that Diocletian was not a failure because his reforms allowed the Roman Empire to survive its near-collapse and remain powerful in the fourth century.

[事实] The palace he built became the basis of the city of Split, whose name is linked in the episode to the Latin palatium.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode is valuable because it makes Diocletian’s reign intelligible as both an emergency response and a long-term transformation. The hosts show how military crisis, fiscal strain and frontier pressure pushed Rome away from the older Augustan model toward a more centralized, bureaucratic and militarized empire.

Its main strength is the balance between narrative and institutional history: murders, mysterious deaths and imperial rivalries keep the story moving, while the larger argument explains why Diocletian mattered beyond his own reign. The discussion of the tetrarchy, taxation, bureaucracy, ceremonial rulership and persecution gives the episode a broad historical range.

A limitation is that some major issues are necessarily compressed, especially the wider third-century crisis, Constantine’s later rise and the full consequences of Christian persecution. [推测] Listeners already familiar with Roman history may want more detail on sources and chronology, while general listeners will likely find the episode accessible and lively.

[推测] This episode is especially suitable for listeners interested in late Roman history, imperial collapse and recovery, the origins of Byzantine administration, or the historical background behind Split and Diocletian’s Palace.
