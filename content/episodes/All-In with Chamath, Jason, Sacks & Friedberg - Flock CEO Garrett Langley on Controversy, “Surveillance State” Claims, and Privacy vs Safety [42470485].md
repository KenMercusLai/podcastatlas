# Flock CEO Garrett Langley on Privacy, Safety, and Surveillance-State Concerns

## 概览

This episode centers on Flock Safety and the tradeoff between public safety and privacy. Jason Calacanis frames Flock as a company whose success has made it both effective and controversial: its license plate reader cameras are used across thousands of cities, but critics worry about surveillance, police abuse, and mission creep.

Garrett Langley argues that Flock is designed as a narrower, privacy-conscious system compared with some competitors: it does not use facial recognition, does not capture video for license plate readers, and does not look inside cars. He also says Flock has reduced its default data retention period from 30 days to seven days, while leaving final decisions to elected local governments and state laws.

A major theme is accountability. Langley acknowledges that simply having audit logs was not enough, especially for large agencies, and says Flock has introduced mandatory audit assistance to detect suspicious searches by police users. The discussion then expands to AI, drones, transparency portals, local democratic control, and the risk that public fear or misinformation could lead communities to remove tools that may help solve serious crimes.

## 分段落总结

[00:00] **The Privacy-Safety Debate**

[事实] Jason introduces the episode as an All-In interview about balancing safety and privacy, with Flock Safety at the center of the current controversy.

[事实] He says Flock has had strong adoption in communities and has reportedly helped reduce crime, but has also drawn new privacy concerns.

[事实] Jason says the discussion will go directly into controversies, data recording, access, audit logs, and what Flock does not do.

[推测] The episode is structured less as a product interview and more as a public accountability conversation about a powerful civic technology.

[02:17] **What Flock Safety Does**

[事实] Garrett Langley says he started Flock nine years earlier with the mission of helping people live in safer neighborhoods.

[事实] Flock’s original product was a license plate reading camera that takes a still image of a vehicle, reads the plate, and can now detect details like roof racks, bumper stickers, dents, and damage.

[事实] Langley says Flock helped solve north of one million crimes, find more than 10,000 missing people, and was used in over 6,000 cities across the country.

[事实] He cites a recent Atlanta case where a stolen car with a two-year-old inside was found within an hour with help from Atlanta police and Flock cameras.

[04:25] **Local Approval and Data Retention**

[事实] Langley says Flock contracts usually go through city councils, and that Flock attended close to 10,000 city council meetings in the prior year.

[事实] He says Flock has a team whose job is to explain what the company does and does not do at these public meetings.

[事实] Langley describes data as “a liability, not an asset,” and says retention length affects product effectiveness.

[事实] Flock’s default retention period was historically 30 days and has recently been reduced to seven days.

[推测] Langley is trying to position local democratic approval and shorter defaults as the company’s answer to centralized surveillance concerns.

[08:10] **Seven Days vs Thirty Days**

[事实] Langley says Flock believes around 90% of crimes can be solved with seven days of data retention.

[事实] He says the remaining cases can include serious incidents like homicides discovered days later or sexual assault victims who report after more than a week.

[事实] Langley says Flock believes a majority of the remaining cases can be addressed with 30 days of data.

[事实] He says state laws vary, citing New Jersey as having a five-year requirement and Washington as having a maximum retention limit.

[推测] The retention debate is presented as a tradeoff between minimizing routine data storage and preserving evidence for severe or delayed-reporting crimes.

[11:09] **What Flock Says It Does Not Do**

[事实] Jason contrasts Flock with private security systems that may use facial recognition, video recording, or cameras looking onto public roads.

[事实] Langley says Flock does not do facial recognition, does not capture video for its license plate cameras, does not look inside cars, and does not allow people searches through its license plate camera system.

[事实] Langley says some of Flock’s competitors do the things he says Flock avoids.

[推测] Flock’s core defense is that critics may be attributing broader surveillance capabilities to Flock that belong to other systems or competitors.

[13:03] **Backlash, Vandalism, and Communication Failures**

[事实] Jason says some people have ripped down cameras and argues that “activists” is a better description than calling them terrorists.

[事实] Langley admits he has made mistakes in communication over the past year and over the company’s history.

[事实] Langley says some people are encouraged by TikTok and Instagram to take action against cameras and may not realize they are committing a felony.

[事实] He says communities have the right to remove Flock through city council decisions, though he views that as choosing to be less safe.

[推测] Both speakers treat vandalism as illegitimate but acknowledge that underlying privacy fears can be real.

[16:43] **Trust in Police and Audit Abuse**

[事实] Langley says some critics’ core concern is not Flock itself but a lack of trust in police.

[事实] Jason agrees that public concern can be justified, citing police departments that subverted body cameras and masked ICE agents without visible identifiers.

[事实] Langley says Flock originally relied on audit logs, but large agencies with thousands of daily users made manual review insufficient.

[事实] Flock built an audit assistance tool four months before the interview to detect abnormal use patterns.

[事实] Langley says repeated searches for the same plate without putting it on a hot list can indicate stalking-like abuse.

[20:11] **Catching Bad Police Users**

[事实] Langley says Flock had detected just under a dozen abuses over the prior eight years before the new audit assistance tool.

[事实] He says recent headlines showed Flock catching more bad police users than he had hoped.

[事实] Langley says some Georgia police chiefs publicly fired officers for abusing the system, including one chief who fired nine officers.

[事实] He says audit assistance is now mandatory rather than optional.

[推测] Flock is shifting from passive logging to active enforcement because public legitimacy depends on visible consequences for misuse.

[22:11] **Supervisor Approval and Agency Size**

[事实] Jason suggests a double-key or supervisor approval system where officers must justify searches and receive approval from a senior person.

[事实] Langley says Flock has considered this type of feature, but that the burden differs between large agencies and small departments with only about 20 employees.

[事实] Langley says rules may need to differ for big cities, medium cities, and small towns.

[事实] He says abuse is an industry-wide problem and cites 7,000 reported abuses of California’s state database in the prior year.

[推测] The episode frames one-size-fits-all compliance as impractical, but also implies that larger agencies need stronger controls.

[26:36] **Flock’s Responsibility as a Technology Provider**

[事实] Langley says his view of Flock’s responsibility has evolved.

[事实] He says elected officials rely on police chiefs, and police chiefs rely on Flock to understand the technology, so Flock must set strong defaults.

[事实] Jason compares the situation to Ring and says technology companies can underestimate how much people fear new safety tools.

[事实] Langley says Flock is now moving more slowly with AI features than it might have in the past.

[推测] Langley is acknowledging that Flock cannot simply describe itself as a neutral tool vendor once its product affects civil liberties.

[28:33] **AI, 911, and Predictive Policing Concerns**

[事实] Langley uses AI in 911 systems as an example of high-risk automation, saying hallucination risk is much more serious in emergencies than in booking a flight.

[事实] He says Flock does not handle 911 calls.

[事实] Jason raises a possible AI use case where software identifies abnormal vehicle patterns that might indicate someone casing houses.

[事实] Langley says Flock does not want to define suspicion and wants a human in the loop for public safety decisions.

[事实] He says AI should ideally help officers solve more crimes while reducing the chance of targeting the wrong person.

[32:47] **Cities Leaving Flock**

[事实] Jason says dozens of cities, possibly 60 or 70 out of around 6,000, have stopped using Flock.

[事实] Langley says many of these decisions are based on misinformation, including one case where officials thought Flock used facial recognition after seeing a competitor’s website.

[事实] Langley describes a Washington case where cameras were turned off, a boy was later shot, and a neighboring city’s Flock cameras helped find the car and make an arrest.

[事实] Langley says when he explains the facts to people, many conclude that cities can set limits and guardrails while still becoming safer.

[推测] The discussion presents misinformation as one of Flock’s biggest business and public policy risks.

[35:11] **Austin, Retention Compromise, and Returning Cities**

[事实] Jason says Austin canceled or did not renew Flock in 2025, then later debated the issue again after a shooting case where Manor police used Flock to help identify suspects.

[事实] Jason says the three teens in that case were arrested 28 hours later and were 15, 16, and 17 years old.

[事实] Jason says the ACLU’s proposed retention period is 48 hours, while many police likely want 30 days and communities may settle around seven or 14 days.

[事实] Langley says close to 20 cities had already turned their cameras back on.

[事实] Langley says he is willing to lose customers that do not want accountability requirements.

[38:10] **Vehicle Details and Investigation Use**

[事实] Jason asks about automated license plate readers and whether Flock stores vehicle details such as dents, color, make, and model.

[事实] Langley says witnesses often remember distinctive vehicle features rather than license plates.

[事实] He describes an Atlanta homicide case where a missing back-right hubcap helped detectives start an investigation.

[事实] Langley emphasizes that such vehicle details are the beginning of an investigation, not the end.

[推测] Flock’s value is portrayed as narrowing investigative leads rather than directly proving guilt.

[39:18] **San Francisco and Unequal Safety**

[事实] Langley says Flock is one small part of a larger transformation in San Francisco involving officials, enforcement, police leadership, prosecutors, and community expectations.

[事实] Jason argues that people with private security, gates, guards, and home cameras can underestimate the safety concerns of people without those protections.

[事实] Langley agrees that support for Flock often comes from people who lack other ways to protect themselves.

[事实] Jason says he does not want a national Flock database or government facial recognition, but is personally comfortable with license plate data for two weeks or 30 days.

[推测] The episode links the privacy debate to class and neighborhood inequality, not just abstract civil liberties.

[44:40] **When Critics Become Supporters**

[事实] Langley says an elected official in Illinois who strongly opposed Flock became a supporter after being the victim of political violence.

[事实] He says someone shot at her house and police used a Flock camera to find the person.

[事实] Langley says people do not get to choose when they become victims of violence, but everyone wants justice afterward.

[推测] This anecdote is used to argue that attitudes toward surveillance tools can change when people personally experience serious crime.

[45:55] **PR Damage and Company Morale**

[事实] Jason describes the prior year as a PR disaster for Flock.

[事实] Langley says the biggest damage has been internal morale, especially when employees see online anger over things Flock says it does not do.

[事实] He says the controversy has also brought Flock closer to customers who view the technology as critical to public safety.

[事实] Langley says the business is still growing, raised more money at the beginning of the year, but churn rose from zero to around 1%.

[推测] The company’s public challenge is not only policy but also employee confidence and brand trust.

[49:38] **Drones as Flock’s Fastest-Growing Unit**

[事实] Langley says drones are Flock’s fastest-growing business unit, while license plate readers are just under 50% of forward-looking revenue.

[事实] He says Flock also has live video cameras and software products.

[事实] Flock drones live in HVAC-controlled docks, typically on police or fire department roofs.

[事实] Langley describes a Dallas-Fort Worth area case where a drone responded to a 911 call about a man with a gun and determined the object was a lighter.

[事实] He says the drone flew at 400 feet, around 60 miles per hour, arrived in under a minute, and used 40x optical zoom.

[51:52] **Drone Oversight and Transparency**

[事实] Jason raises concerns about drones being used to stalk someone or look into windows and asks about third-party auditing.

[事实] Langley says law enforcement needs to implement drones better than it implemented older technologies like body cameras.

[事实] He says city managers can hold police chiefs accountable and can see audit logs.

[事实] Langley says many customers have transparency portals showing audit logs, camera counts, drones, and drone flights, with a delay.

[事实] He says public access to drone flight information is possible, but Flock has not had a customer ask for that feature.

[推测] Drone expansion will likely intensify the same privacy-versus-safety debate because drones feel more intrusive than fixed license plate readers.

[54:30] **Regulation and Closing Position**

[事实] Langley says New Hampshire has a three-minute license plate reader retention rule and that Washington’s 21-day rule is the next nearest state-level example he mentions.

[事实] He says there is little state regulation and no federal regulation beyond air safety for drones.

[事实] Jason concludes that communities should decide whether to use Flock, whether to set retention at 48 hours, and what caps are reasonable.

[推测] The closing consensus is that Flock’s legitimacy depends on local control, strong defaults, transparency, and willingness to accept limits.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode is valuable because it forces a CEO in a controversial public safety sector to answer concrete questions about retention periods, abuse detection, facial recognition, AI, drones, and local democratic control. It avoids treating privacy and safety as simple opposites.

[推测] Its strongest moments are the detailed discussions of audit assistance, abnormal search patterns, and how misuse by police can be detected. These sections give listeners a clearer way to evaluate Flock beyond slogans like “surveillance state” or “crime-fighting tool.”

[推测] The main limitation is that the conversation is largely between a sympathetic interviewer and the company’s CEO. Privacy advocates, civil liberties lawyers, or residents from cities that rejected Flock are discussed but not directly represented.

[推测] This episode is most useful for listeners interested in policing technology, civic privacy, AI governance, local government procurement, and the operational tradeoffs behind public safety tools.
