# Puzzle Rush: What Timed Tests Really Measure

## Overview

Malcolm Gladwell and his assistant, Camille Baptista, take the LSAT to investigate a deceptively simple question: why do standardized tests treat answering quickly as evidence of intelligence? Their experience reveals that succeeding on the LSAT requires not only correct reasoning, but also specialized strategies for reading, eliminating answers, and making decisions at an intentionally uncomfortable speed.

A comparison with chess shows that changing a time limit can dramatically reorder who performs best. Classical, blitz, bullet chess, and Puzzle Rush use similar underlying skills, yet reward different combinations of calculation, intuition, and rapid pattern recognition.

Research by law professor William Henderson further challenges the LSAT’s predictive authority. The test predicts performance relatively well on tightly timed law-school exams, but much less effectively on take-home exams and papers. The episode concludes that evaluation systems do not simply discover ability: their chosen time constraints help define which kinds of ability become visible.

## Segment Summary

[00:33] **Taking the LSAT in the Name of Science**

[Fact] Gladwell and Baptista join hundreds of prospective law students at Pace University to take the LSAT, although neither intends to become a lawyer.

[Fact] Gladwell worries that his age and lack of standardized-testing experience will put him at a disadvantage against the 24-year-old Baptista.

[Fact] Their friendly competition provides a practical experiment through which the episode examines time pressure and test performance.

[03:18] **William Henderson’s Question About Speed**

[Fact] Gladwell’s experiment was inspired by William Henderson’s paper, “The LSAT, Law School Exams and Meritocracy: The Surprising and Under-Theorized Role of Test-Taking Speed.”

[Fact] Henderson, formerly a firefighter-paramedic, says the LSAT and law-school exams produced more time pressure than emergency work.

[Fact] His experience led him to question why intellectually demanding legal problems are evaluated under severe time limits.

[05:50] **The Stopwatch Behind Standardized Testing**

[Fact] Standardized tests evaluate both whether a person can answer correctly and how quickly the person can do so.

[Fact] The LSAT contains roughly 125 multiple-choice questions across five sections, with only 35 minutes allotted to each section.

[Fact] The test plays a major role in determining law-school admission, yet unused time from one section cannot be transferred to another.

[Inference] The episode argues that the LSAT embeds an unproven assumption that faster reasoning is inherently better reasoning.

[08:00] **Learning to Read at an Uncomfortable Speed**

[Fact] Test-preparation experts tell Gladwell that completing the LSAT requires working faster than feels comfortable and following the test’s rules rather than one’s normal intellectual habits.

[Fact] Their reading strategy prioritizes identifying the structure of an argument and moving on, even when parts of a passage remain unclear.

[Fact] Gladwell is advised to eliminate obviously bad answers before identifying the correct one, because the clock makes exhaustive consideration impractical.

[Fact] This approach conflicts with his habit of pausing, reflecting, and following interesting digressions.

[12:32] **A Practice Session Under Pressure**

[Fact] During a timed practice passage, Gladwell repeatedly asks how much time remains, feels his heart accelerate, and begins to panic.

[Fact] He spends about seven and a half minutes processing seven questions without yet selecting final answers, leaving little margin within a 35-minute section.

[Fact] After confidently choosing the wrong answer on another problem, he is told that Baptista has the advantage in their competition.

[15:12] **The LSAT Experience**

[Fact] Gladwell completes reading comprehension with time to spare but struggles when he reaches the analytical-reasoning logic games.

[Fact] He believes he needs to slow down to solve them, but the test format does not permit him to do so.

[Fact] Afterward, he describes entering a state of panic and expects Baptista to have earned the higher score.

[Inference] His experience reinforces the episode’s distinction between being unable to solve a problem and being unable to solve it within an imposed deadline.

[18:35] **Chess Without a Clock**

[Fact] Chess grandmaster Hikaru Nakamura tells Gladwell that without time limits, elite human chess games would likely end in draws because players could examine each move too thoroughly.

[Fact] Nakamura says that with 30 minutes per move, he does not think he would lose to any human opponent, including Magnus Carlsen.

[Fact] Time limits are therefore essential to making competitive chess practical and producing decisive results.

[20:49] **Classical Chess Versus Blitz**

[Fact] Classical chess allows extensive calculation, while blitz gives each player only five minutes for the entire game and places greater emphasis on rapid pattern recognition.

[Fact] Nakamura is ranked lower in classical chess than in blitz, where his quick intuition and pattern recognition make him one of the world’s best players.

[Fact] Fabiano Caruana is presented as the opposite type: exceptionally strong at deep calculation with sufficient time, but less dominant when intuition must replace extended analysis.

[Inference] The contrast suggests that rankings reflect both a player’s ability and the particular speed at which the competition requires that ability to operate.

[24:09] **Bullet Chess and Puzzle Rush**

[Fact] Bullet chess allows only one minute per player, and Nakamura says he is better than Carlsen in that format.

[Fact] Puzzle Rush gives players five minutes to solve as many chess positions as possible; Nakamura reports holding the highest score of 55.

[Fact] Gladwell argues that Nakamura’s Puzzle Rush achievement could be considered one of his greatest accomplishments if the chess world chose to value that format more highly.

[Inference] What institutions recognize as excellence depends partly on which version of a task they designate as authoritative.

[27:40] **Hares, Tortoises, and Arbitrary Rankings**

[Fact] Gladwell states that the finishing order in a cognitive task changes according to how much time participants receive.

[Fact] He characterizes the LSAT as a system that rewards fast “hares,” while classical chess is more accommodating to methodical “tortoises.”

[Fact] The same participants can therefore receive very different rankings when only the time constraint changes.

[29:46] **Does the LSAT Predict Legal Ability?**

[Fact] The LSAT is justified partly as a predictor of first-year law-school grades.

[Fact] Henderson asks whether this prediction works because law schools often use the same kind of time-pressured examinations as the LSAT.

[Fact] His data show that LSAT scores are relatively strong predictors of in-class exam performance but relatively weak predictors of performance on take-home exams and papers.

[Inference] This evidence indicates that the LSAT may predict success under similar testing conditions more reliably than it predicts legal ability in a broader sense.

[31:13] **Changing the Method of Evaluation**

[Fact] After conducting his research, Henderson gives greater weight to take-home exams and allows four hours for in-class exams rather than two.

[Fact] His exams are open-book, include a word limit, and may disclose one possible question in advance.

[Fact] A student who had previously regarded himself as average earned an A on Henderson’s take-home exam and produced a well-organized analysis.

[Fact] Henderson concluded that changing the assessment method can change students’ ordinal ranking.

[33:30] **The Hidden Cost for Deliberate Thinkers**

[Fact] Gladwell argues that many methodical thinkers are labeled mediocre because schools and professions evaluate them through speed-based systems.

[Fact] He identifies personally with these “tortoises” and describes his mother as someone who works carefully, checks everything repeatedly, and refuses to guess.

[Fact] Such a person might be highly suited to work demanding accuracy while still performing poorly on the LSAT because she would not rush or leave reasoning incomplete.

[Inference] The episode’s broader warning is that institutions may exclude valuable forms of intelligence when they confuse speed with merit.

## Podcast Commentary/Summary

The episode’s strongest feature is its use of chess to make an abstract measurement problem concrete. Classical, blitz, bullet, and Puzzle Rush demonstrate that a single change in allotted time can reward entirely different cognitive strengths without changing the basic domain being tested.

The combination of Gladwell’s LSAT experience and Henderson’s research also exposes a circularity in legal education: a speed-based admissions test appears predictive partly because schools later use similarly speed-based exams. That does not make the LSAT useless, but it narrows what its predictive power can reasonably be said to establish.

[Inference] The episode is especially valuable for educators, admissions professionals, employers, and people whose abilities have been judged through timed testing. Its main limitation is that it presents only the first half of the LSAT investigation and postpones both the final scores and the testing authority’s response until the next episode.
