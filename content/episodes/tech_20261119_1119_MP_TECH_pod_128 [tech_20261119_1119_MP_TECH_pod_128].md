# Beyond Dot-Com: The Business, Politics, and Creativity of New Web Domains

## Overview

ICANN has opened its first application round for new top-level domains since 2012, attracting more than 1,000 applications. The discussion examines why control of extensions such as `.agent` can be commercially valuable and how country-code domains can become unexpected economic assets.

AI-related domains are a major focus. Google, OpenAI, and Meta are competing for `.agent`, reflecting expectations that AI agents may need distinct online identities. Applicants face substantial costs, technical requirements, and potentially expensive auctions.

The episode also explores community and brand-specific domains, the continuing dominance of `.com`, and the expressive possibilities of alternatives such as `.meow`, `.garden`, and `.BTW`. Domains emerge as a niche where business, technology, geopolitics, branding, and playful wordcraft intersect.

## Segmented Summary

[00:20] **ICANN Opens a New Domain Application Round**

[Fact] For the first time in more than a decade, ICANN is processing applications for new top-level domains comparable to `.com` and `.org`.

[Fact] More than 1,000 applications are under consideration.

[Fact] Controlling a sought-after extension can become a lucrative business because its operator may sell registrations to companies and individuals.

[01:00] **Country Codes as Unexpected Economic Assets**

[Fact] Anguilla’s `.ai` country-code domain benefited from surging demand for AI-related websites and email addresses.

[Fact] According to the guest, `.ai` contributed almost half of Anguilla’s national revenue in the previous year, with proceeds supporting infrastructure such as schools and airports.

[Fact] Other country codes repurposed beyond their geographic meaning include Montenegro’s `.me`, Tuvalu’s `.tv`, and Colombia’s `.co`.

[02:12] **Tech Giants Compete for `.agent`**

[Fact] Google, OpenAI, and Meta have applied for `.agent`, the most sought-after extension in the current round.

[Fact] The guest says applicants may want AI agents to possess their own identities as they move around the internet.

[Fact] Because several organizations want `.agent`, the extension will likely be awarded through an auction.

[02:59] **Application Costs and High-Stakes Auctions**

[Fact] This is the first opportunity to create new top-level domains since 2012.

[Fact] Applying costs approximately $250,000, and operating an extension requires technical expertise.

[Fact] Google paid $25 million for `.app` in a previous auction, while the record auction price was $135 million for `.web`.

[Fact] `.web` remains unavailable to the public after becoming tied up in a legal dispute lasting roughly a decade.

[03:47] **Public Registries, Private Domains, and Commercial Risk**

[Fact] Some operators acquire extensions to sell individual domains, as Google does with `.app`.

[Fact] Other extensions serve cities, niche communities, or unconventional identities, including `.Beijing`, `.Québec`, `.pizza`, and `.WTF`.

[Fact] Companies may reserve an extension exclusively for internal use; Google uses `.google` without allowing public registrations.

[Fact] The commercial payoff is uncertain, as demonstrated by the expensive but still unreleased `.web`.

[Fact] Other contested extensions include `.robot` and several additional AI- and technology-related names.

[05:32] **Community Applicants and the `.meow` Proposal**

[Fact] The domain application process largely favors organizations with substantial financial and technical resources, though community applicants may receive priority.

[Fact] Belgium’s nonprofit Dot Meow Foundation applied for `.meow` as a domain for the queer community.

[Fact] The foundation plans to direct proceeds from domain sales toward queer projects and businesses.

[06:20] **Why Brands Want Their Own Extensions**

[Fact] Major companies and brands applied for proprietary extensions in 2012, including Google, Amazon, and Banana Republic.

[Fact] Some companies may obtain these extensions defensively to prevent others from controlling their brand names.

[Fact] About 300 companies applied for brand-specific extensions in the current round.

[Fact] Airbnb could potentially give hosts individual portals such as `Samantha.Airbnb`, although the guest presents this as a possible use rather than a confirmed plan.

[07:18] **The Enduring Dominance of `.com`**

[Fact] The guest describes `.com` as a marker of credibility and business success.

[Fact] Desirable `.com` domains can be expensive because another company may already own them or an investor may be holding them for resale.

[Fact] Alternatives such as `.garden`, `.space`, and `.dentist` allow domain names to better express a project or brand.

[Fact] “Domain hacks” combine the text on both sides of the dot to form a word or phrase.

[Speculation] Alternative extensions may become more common as the supply of desirable `.com` names continues to shrink.

[08:19] **Domains as a World of Money, Politics, and Wordplay**

[Fact] Pete Millspah became interested in domains through registering names for coding projects and buying personal domains as gifts.

[Fact] He describes unfinished projects tied to unused registrations as a common pattern among web developers.

[Fact] His deeper research revealed a field shaped by major financial interests, politics, geopolitics, contested rules, and playful language.

[09:18] **Notable Names in the Current Round**

[Fact] Millspah highlights `.ETC` and `.BTW` as playful extensions that could support creative informational sites.

[Fact] He also mentions cozy or culturally expressive possibilities such as `.jazz`.

[Fact] Taylor Swift’s management company applied for `.TaylorSwift`.

[Speculation] The celebrity-specific application stands out because its eventual purpose is less predictable than the technology-focused applications from companies such as OpenAI, Meta, and Google.

## Podcast Commentary/Summary

The episode provides a concise introduction to the usually invisible system behind web addresses. Its strongest feature is the range of examples, moving from Anguilla’s national finances to AI-industry competition, nonprofit community funding, corporate branding, and personal creativity.

The discussion also makes clear that domain extensions are not merely technical labels. They can function as revenue-generating infrastructure, defensive intellectual property, community identity, or raw material for online expression.

Its main limitation is the short runtime: issues such as ICANN governance, auction rules, legal disputes, operating costs, and potential abuse receive little detailed scrutiny. The economic prospects of new extensions also remain unresolved.

[Speculation] The episode is particularly suitable for listeners interested in internet infrastructure, AI business strategy, digital branding, and the political economy of seemingly ordinary technologies.
