# The Marquis de Sade: Depravity, Revolution, and Modernity

## 概览

This episode of *The Rest Is History* examines the Marquis de Sade as both a notorious figure of sexual depravity and a serious, unsettling figure in intellectual history. The hosts begin with *The 120 Days of Sodom*, warning listeners that the subject is unsuitable for children and stressing how difficult, disgusting, and disturbing Sade’s writing is.

The central argument is that Sade matters beyond scandal. Tom argues that his novels combine obscene violence with a radical anti-Christian, materialist philosophy that anticipates later debates about nature, desire, power, psychoanalysis, and totalitarianism.

The discussion follows Sade’s aristocratic upbringing, repeated scandals, imprisonment under monarchy, revolution, and Napoleon, his prison writing, his role during the French Revolution, and his later reception. The hosts repeatedly contrast his personal exploitation and violent imagination with the strange fact that, as a revolutionary official, he appears to have acted with some restraint.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **Opening Warning and The 120 Days of Sodom**

[事实] The episode opens with a passage from *The 120 Days of Sodom*, but the hosts stop because the text is too filthy to read aloud.
[事实] The hosts describe Sade as a byword for sexual depravity and call the novel revolting, unsettling, boring, and darkly funny.
[事实] They warn that the episode is not suitable for children or family listening.
[推测] The opening is designed to show that Sade cannot be treated as a merely eccentric literary figure.

[02:05] **The Castle of Silling**

[事实] The hosts summarize *The 120 Days of Sodom* as a story about four libertines who retreat to an inaccessible castle.
[事实] The libertines isolate themselves with servants, storytellers, sexually experienced participants, and victims.
[事实] The castle’s bridge is demolished, making it a closed world of planned abuse and domination.
[推测] The castle becomes a model for Sade’s larger interest in power exercised without outside restraint.

[03:27] **Why Sade Matters**

[事实] Tom argues that Sade is fascinating because he stands near major events in French history and pushes Enlightenment ideas to disturbing extremes.
[事实] Sade was imprisoned under the ancien régime, threatened during the Revolution, and later confined under Napoleon.
[事实] Tom claims Sade foreshadows aspects of Darwinism, Nietzsche, Freud, and totalitarian regimes.
[推测] The episode’s main historical claim is that Sade is important because his extremity exposes tensions inside modern ideas about freedom, nature, and morality.

[06:40] **Family Background and Childhood**

[事实] Sade was born in June 1740 into an aristocratic family whose father served as a diplomat.
[事实] His mother had connections to the Condé household, and Sade was initially raised in a highly privileged Parisian setting.
[事实] At age four, he punched the Prince de Condé in the face and was sent away to Provence.
[事实] The hosts link his troubled family background, Gothic castles, and later Jesuit schooling to recurring themes in his fiction.

[10:26] **War, Libertinage, and Sexual Philosophy**

[事实] Sade attended military school and served in seven campaigns during the Seven Years’ War.
[事实] The hosts say aristocratic libertinage was not unusual in the eighteenth century, but Sade’s tastes and behavior went far beyond accepted limits.
[事实] Sade connected sexual desire to philosophy, rejecting Christian morality and presenting impulses as rooted in nature.
[推测] His importance comes partly from turning private vice into a systematic argument against inherited moral limits.

[15:42] **Marriage and First Scandal**

[事实] In 1763 Sade married Renée-Pélagie de Montreuil in a match that exchanged aristocratic status for money.
[事实] Six months later, he was accused by a prostitute, Jeanne Testard, of sexual misconduct and blasphemous anti-Christian acts.
[事实] He was arrested and sent to Vincennes, but his mother-in-law Madame de Montreuil helped secure his release.
[推测] At this stage, his mother-in-law appears to be protecting both family reputation and Sade himself.

[18:34] **Rose Keller and the Lettre de Cachet**

[事实] In 1768, Rose Keller accused Sade of luring her under false pretenses and violently abusing her.
[事实] Sade denied part of the accusation while admitting to whipping her.
[事实] Madame de Montreuil paid Keller off and used a royal lettre de cachet to have Sade imprisoned without a public trial.
[推测] The episode presents the lettre de cachet as both a shield from scandal and a mechanism that could later destroy Sade’s freedom.

[20:21] **Lacoste, Marseilles, and Long Imprisonment**

[事实] After being warned to behave, Sade went to Marseilles, held an orgy, used Spanish fly, and committed illegal sexual acts.
[事实] He then ran off with his sister-in-law Anne-Prospère, further enraging his family.
[事实] In 1774-1775, parents accused him of abducting young girls who had been taken into service at Lacoste.
[事实] In February 1777, after being tricked back to Paris, Sade was arrested and began thirteen years in prison.

[24:20] **Prison Writing and 120 Days**

[事实] Sade spent seven years in Vincennes before being transferred to the Bastille in 1784.
[事实] In prison he wrote extensively, including *The 120 Days of Sodom* in 37 days at the end of 1785.
[事实] The hosts describe the novel as structured to escalate toward extreme violence and murder.
[推测] The regulated structure of horror in the novel is why later readers connected it to modern systems of organized torture and death.

[28:35] **Bastille Crisis and Lost Manuscript**

[事实] On July 2, 1789, Sade shouted from his Bastille cell that prisoners were being slaughtered, helping inflame the public mood.
[事实] He was transferred to Charenton before the Bastille was stormed and lost the hidden manuscript of *The 120 Days of Sodom*.
[事实] He was released in spring 1790, but Renée refused to see him and divorced him two months later.
[推测] The Revolution allowed Sade to recast himself as a victim of despotic imprisonment.

[30:22] **Citizen Sade**

[事实] After release, Sade had money problems, wrote plays and novels, and began a relationship with Marie-Constance.
[事实] He threw himself into revolutionary politics as “Citizen Sade” in the radical Section des Piques.
[事实] He served on committees inspecting orphanages and hospitals and appears to have performed those duties responsibly.
[事实] As president of the section, he did not send his in-laws to trial, despite having reason to resent them.

[32:24] **The Terror and Near Execution**

[事实] During the Terror, Sade was jailed as an immoral and suspect former aristocrat.
[事实] His sons’ links to counter-revolutionary forces counted against him.
[事实] Marie-Constance helped get him moved to a better prison, where the guillotine was placed close enough for him to smell the blood.
[事实] He was sentenced for execution on July 27, 1794, but survived because Robespierre fell the same day.

[34:58] **Justine and the Attack on Virtue**

[事实] After release in October 1794, Sade was poor, worked at a theatre, sold Lacoste in 1796, and continued writing.
[事实] *Justine* is presented as a parody of Samuel Richardson’s *Pamela*, reversing the idea that virtue is rewarded.
[事实] In Sade’s story, Justine’s virtue brings suffering, while her vicious sister Juliet prospers.
[推测] The hosts treat this as especially subversive because revolutionary politics placed so much emphasis on virtue.

[37:23] **Napoleon and Final Confinement**

[事实] After Napoleon came to power, Joseph Fouché’s police raided Sade’s publisher and traced *Justine* back to him.
[事实] Sade was imprisoned again and transferred to Charenton in 1803, where he remained for the rest of his life.
[事实] At Charenton he staged plays and continued behaving scandalously.
[事实] He died on December 1, 1814, after spending decades of his life in confinement.

[38:34] **Influence and Modern Thought**

[事实] The hosts say Sade was internationally notorious even before his death.
[事实] Tom argues that Sade prefigured crude “survival of the fittest” interpretations of Darwin by treating nature as a struggle in which the strong dominate the weak.
[事实] Sade is also presented as a precursor to Nietzsche because he rejected not only Christianity’s God but Christian values.
[事实] He argued that morality varies by culture and that people should trust their own impulses.

[43:08] **Sadism, Psychoanalysis, and French Theory**

[事实] Richard von Krafft-Ebing coined the term “sadism” from Sade’s name.
[事实] Krafft-Ebing treated *The 120 Days of Sodom* as a kind of scientific document rather than only fiction.
[事实] The hosts say Freud and later psychoanalytic thought took seriously the idea that sexual identity is central to personal identity.
[事实] Sade influenced French thinkers including Foucault and attracted interest from some feminist writers, though Andrea Dworkin rejected him strongly.

[45:42] **Progressive Arguments Taken to Extremes**

[事实] In *Philosophy in the Boudoir*, Sade tried to construct morality on nature rather than Christianity.
[事实] The hosts say he argued against the death penalty and in favor of female liberation, abortion rights, and legal equality for gay people.
[事实] They then stress that Sade pushed tolerance of desire toward defenses of rape, incest, murder, and population control.
[推测] The episode’s unsettling claim is that Sade can sound progressive at one moment and openly totalitarian at the next.

[49:00] **Rome, Fascism, and Genocide**

[事实] Sade looked to ancient Rome for examples of societies organized without Christianity.
[事实] He argued that Christianity weakened Rome by discouraging spectacles of torture and death.
[事实] The hosts connect his praise of strength, contempt for charity, and willingness to let the weak die to later fascist and Nazi ideas.
[事实] They also connect his arguments about population and death to communist totalitarianism under Stalin and Mao.

[50:44] **Closing Assessment**

[事实] The hosts say Sade remains shocking because different eras find different parts of him unsettling.
[事实] Tom says he has repeatedly tried and failed to finish *The 120 Days of Sodom*, calling it effectively unreadable.
[事实] The hosts mention that Sade was banned in Britain after the Moors murders and later appeared as a Penguin Classic.
[推测] The conclusion is that Sade is historically important not despite his repulsiveness, but because his repulsiveness forces uncomfortable questions about modernity.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it turns a sensational subject into a serious discussion of intellectual history. It does not simply recount scandals; it uses Sade to explore how ideas about nature, freedom, sexuality, virtue, and power can become dangerous when pushed to extremes.

[事实] The hosts repeatedly avoid reading the most explicit material and warn that the subject is unsuitable for children. [推测] Even so, the episode is best suited to adult listeners interested in French history, the Enlightenment, the Revolution, political violence, and the darker roots of modern thought.

[推测] A limitation is that the discussion is driven mainly by the hosts’ interpretation of Sade’s significance. The victims of Sade’s conduct appear mostly through scandals, police reports, and biographical episodes, so the moral and social cost of his behavior is present but not examined in equal depth.
