# Project Dillard: How College Rankings Perpetuate Privilege

## 概览

Malcolm Gladwell investigates why Dillard University, a historically Black liberal arts college in New Orleans, sits near the bottom of the U.S. News college rankings despite its strong academic culture and notable success in producing Black STEM graduates.

The episode argues that major ranking variables—including peer reputation, graduation rates, institutional wealth, student selectivity, and standardized test scores—frequently reward colleges for enrolling affluent, highly prepared students rather than for developing students with fewer advantages.

A statistical thought experiment shows that Dillard could rise dramatically by acquiring billions of dollars and replacing much of its current student body with wealthier, higher-scoring applicants. Yet doing so would undermine its mission. The episode concludes that the rankings perpetuate privilege while overlooking institutions that help underserved students realize their potential.

## 分段落总结

[02:01] **Introducing Dillard and the Ranking Mystery**

[事实] Dillard University is a small liberal arts college in New Orleans and part of the group of historically Black institutions sometimes called the Black Ivy League.

[事实] U.S. News places Dillard near the bottom of its liberal arts college rankings, prompting Gladwell to investigate what the ranking system means by a “better” college.

[事实] About three-quarters of Dillard students are eligible for Pell Grants, their average family income is slightly above $30,000, one-third are first-generation college students, and virtually all are Black.

[推测] Dillard serves as a case study for whether rankings measure educational quality or primarily reflect the advantages students and institutions already possess.

[06:11] **Rankings as a Perpetuation of Privilege**

[事实] Gladwell argues that the U.S. News standard is heavily biased against colleges serving disadvantaged populations.

[事实] Dillard president Walter Kimbrough describes the rankings as a “perpetuation of privilege.”

[事实] Gladwell enlists statisticians at Reed College to analyze how Dillard performs under the variables used by the U.S. News algorithm.

[08:15] **What Determines Academic Reputation**

[事实] U.S. News asks senior college officials to rate the academic reputation of peer institutions on a scale from one to five.

[事实] Reed College student Lauren Rabe finds that endowment size alone predicts about half of a college’s peer-assessment score.

[事实] Adding tuition, the percentage of white students, Pell Grant enrollment, and other institutional characteristics raises the model’s predictive power to 91.3%.

[事实] Dillard has a peer-assessment score of 2.6, annual tuition of about $19,000, and an endowment of $105 million, while Williams College has over $2 billion more in its endowment.

[推测] The analysis suggests that purported judgments of academic reputation largely reproduce visible indicators of wealth and student demographics.

[11:00] **Graduation Rates and Student Circumstances**

[事实] U.S. News rewards high six-year graduation rates, while Dillard’s graduation rate is approximately 50%.

[事实] Kimbrough recounts meeting a student who did not know where she would live during the Thanksgiving and Christmas holidays.

[事实] He argues that family illness, job loss, housing instability, and limited income can interrupt students’ education for reasons a college cannot control.

[事实] Bowdoin has a graduation rate of approximately 95%, and one-fifth of its students come from families in the top 1% by income.

[推测] Graduation rates can reveal more about the economic security of admitted students than about the educational effectiveness of their colleges.

[15:58] **Why Wealth Dominates the Rankings**

[事实] Numerous ranking variables are tied to money, including faculty resources, spending per student, alumni giving, and graduate indebtedness.

[事实] Robert Morse of U.S. News argues that greater spending gives students a better chance of receiving a richer academic experience.

[事实] Gladwell challenges the assumption that additional spending automatically produces proportionally better educational quality.

[推测] Counting institutional wealth through several correlated categories may amplify its influence across the ranking formula.

[18:35] **The Two-Billion-Dollar Thought Experiment**

[事实] Gladwell imagines Dillard receiving $2 billion and adopting every incentive embedded in the U.S. News system.

[事实] Kimbrough would ordinarily use such funding to expand Dillard and educate more students, but the selectivity metric rewards admitting fewer applicants.

[事实] To improve its ranking, Dillard would need students who are financially secure, likely to graduate, capable of donating later, and strong on conventional admissions measures.

[推测] The ranking system creates a conflict between Dillard’s access-oriented mission and the institutional behavior required to gain prestige.

[20:38] **Luxury Amenities and the “Jenny” Strategy**

[事实] Gladwell proposes attracting affluent students by constructing exceptionally luxurious dormitories.

[事实] A luxury real-estate professional estimates that hotel-suite-level accommodation could cost at least $2 million per room, excluding land.

[事实] A 200-student luxury dormitory could therefore cost roughly $400 million.

[事实] Gladwell imagines these affluent students graduating at high rates, donating to Dillard, and indirectly improving its peer reputation.

[推测] The deliberately absurd scenario illustrates how ranking incentives can redirect educational resources toward amenities designed to recruit wealthy students.

[24:48] **Buying a Higher Rank**

[事实] Reed College’s simulation shows that substituting Williams College’s financial profile for Dillard’s would move Dillard from rank 161 to a tie at 103 without changing classroom fundamentals.

[事实] Adding the test scores associated with highly prepared students would raise Dillard from 103 to 43.

[事实] Adjusting the remaining variables—including student-to-faculty ratios and faculty resources—would move the simulated Dillard to third place.

[事实] Achieving that position would require shrinking enrollment and excluding many of the students Dillard currently exists to educate.

[推测] The experiment demonstrates that a college can transform its ranking without a corresponding transformation in teaching quality or institutional spirit.

[28:17] **The Cost of Losing the Institution’s Soul**

[事实] Gladwell invokes Mark 8:36, asking what is gained by winning the world while losing one’s soul.

[事实] The imagined elite Dillard would have a radically different student body, resource base, and institutional purpose.

[推测] A high ranking obtained by abandoning Dillard’s mission would represent institutional defeat rather than genuine improvement.

[30:39] **Dillard’s Overlooked STEM Success**

[事实] Kimbrough says Dillard ranks among the leading institutions producing Black undergraduate physics graduates and ranks first on a per-capita basis.

[事实] In 2019, Dillard produced 13 Black physics graduates, compared with one at Harvard.

[事实] Over the preceding decade, Dillard graduated 35 Black physics students, while Harvard graduated five.

[事实] Xavier University, another HBCU in New Orleans, sends more African American students to medical school than any other school in the country.

[推测] Outcomes such as Dillard’s physics record capture educational contribution more directly than measures of institutional wealth or admissions selectivity.

[32:29] **Developmental Education Versus “Cream Skimming”**

[事实] UCLA researcher Mitchell Chang explains that many students at selective institutions leave STEM after losing confidence.

[事实] He contrasts institutions that select students already at the top with HBCUs that meet students where they are and develop their potential.

[事实] The STEM pipeline at HBCUs is described as less “leaky” than at many predominantly white institutions.

[推测] Dillard’s effectiveness may come from an academic culture centered on support and development rather than competition among already advantaged students.

[34:24] **A Community That Keeps Students Enrolled**

[事实] Dillard students describe direct access to the president, attentive advisers, and faculty who contact them when they miss class.

[事实] One student says support from faculty and peers helped them remain enrolled after a death in the family.

[事实] HBCUs achieve these outcomes while enrolling more first-generation, lower-income, and less academically prepared students.

[推测] Dillard’s close relationships function as educational infrastructure, even though rankings do not adequately recognize them.

[36:28] **Redefining an Elite College**

[事实] Gladwell describes Dillard as a school that helps students succeed in their chosen subjects, develops their potential, and remains affordable to working families.

[事实] He asks whether those qualities should constitute the definition of an elite institution.

[事实] Kimbrough once challenged a U.S. News representative to justify a system in which excluding poor, Black, part-time, and non-traditional students improves a college’s rank.

[事实] According to Kimbrough, the representative did not directly answer the question.

[38:01] **Collective Complicity in the Ranking Game**

[事实] Gladwell summarizes the ranking system’s implicit rules as “more is better than less, rich is better than poor, white is better than Black.”

[事实] He argues that college leaders, applicants, and parents sustain the system by submitting data, competing for highly ranked institutions, and treating admission as a status symbol.

[事实] Gladwell urges Kimbrough to persuade colleges to withdraw collectively from the U.S. News rankings.

[推测] The episode’s final argument is that rankings retain their authority because the higher-education community continues to participate in and validate them.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s chief strength is its combination of statistical analysis, institutional reporting, student testimony, and satire. The simulations make the ranking system’s incentives concrete: Dillard can climb by becoming richer and more selective even if nothing fundamental changes in its classrooms.

Its most valuable contribution is the distinction between selecting excellent students and helping students become excellent. Dillard’s STEM record and supportive culture offer evidence that educational value cannot be inferred solely from graduation rates, test scores, reputation, or spending.

[推测] The episode is openly argumentative and gives less attention to possible benefits of standardized comparisons or to alternative interpretations of some correlations. Its thought experiment is intentionally exaggerated, so it is more effective as a critique of incentives than as a complete technical audit of the ranking methodology.

[推测] This episode is especially useful for students, parents, educators, and policymakers who want to examine what college rankings measure, whom they advantage, and which forms of institutional achievement they leave invisible.
