# Britain’s Empire of Booze

## 概览

The episode explores how Britain helped shape several drinks now associated with celebration and status: champagne, claret, port, sherry, Marsala and whisky. Recorded at Berry Brothers with drinks writer Henry Jeffreys, the discussion uses his book *Empire of Booze* to argue that Britain’s wealth, shipping networks and consumer tastes had an outsized influence on global alcohol styles.

A central theme is that many drinks thought of as French, Portuguese, Spanish, Italian or Scottish were altered by British demand. Champagne depended partly on strong English glass bottles and later on British taste for dry styles; Bordeaux claret, port and sherry were all shaped by trade routes, politics, branding and what British consumers wanted to drink.

The conversation also stresses that taste is historical. Older wines were often sweeter, stronger, less fresh and more oxidized than modern drinkers expect. The episode closes by extending the argument to whisky, where Scottish blended whisky became a global product partly through English drinking habits, marketing and the crisis in cognac caused by phylloxera.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **A festive episode about drink history**

[事实] The hosts frame the episode around Boxing Day, New Year’s Eve and drinks associated with the festive season.
[事实] They record at Berry Brothers, founded in 1698 and located in St James’s.
[事实] Henry Jeffreys joins them to discuss his book *Empire of Booze*, whose argument is that Britain deeply influenced wine and drink history.
[推测] The setting reinforces the episode’s focus on elite British drinking culture, commerce and long-running wine institutions.

[03:12] **Champagne and the English bottle**

[事实] Jeffreys says sparkling champagne required strong bottles because ordinary glass would break under pressure.
[事实] He credits Sir Kenelm Digby, around 1630, with inventing or probably inventing a strong glass bottle.
[事实] Digby was linked to the Gunpowder Plot through his father, corresponded with major scientists and was a founder member of the Royal Society.
[事实] Strong bottles made it possible to trap carbon dioxide during fermentation and create a champagne-like fizz.
[推测] The episode presents champagne as partly dependent on English scientific and manufacturing innovation, despite its French regional identity.

[05:18] **Fizz before champagne**

[事实] Before controlled sparkling wine, accidental fizz was treated as a fault in wine.
[事实] Jeffreys says British cider makers and early Royal Society figures experimented with making drinks fizzy.
[事实] A brief English fashion for wine-style sparkling ciders arose during conflict with France, but it did not last.
[事实] The technology later crossed the Channel, and French producers developed it more successfully.
[推测] Britain is portrayed as an origin point for the technique, while France turned it into a refined and commercial product.

[06:48] **Dom Pérignon and champagne mythmaking**

[事实] Jeffreys calls the story that Dom Pérignon invented sparkling champagne a myth promoted by Moët & Chandon as marketing.
[事实] He says Dom Pérignon was important in champagne history and worked with grape varieties such as Chardonnay and Pinot Noir.
[事实] According to Jeffreys, Dom Pérignon would not have wanted fizz and would have seen it as a fault.
[推测] The discussion treats champagne history as shaped as much by later branding stories as by technical invention.

[07:42] **British taste and dry champagne**

[事实] Britain was, until recently, the largest export market for champagne.
[事实] Jeffreys says champagne was created for British tastes in important respects.
[事实] Earlier champagne could be sweet, especially for the Russian court, while brut dry champagne was created for the English market.
[推测] The prestige style now associated with champagne owes much to British middle-class dining habits and demand for wine to accompany food.

[08:21] **Britain as wine merchant to the world**

[事实] The hosts discuss Jeffreys’s argument that Britain’s role as a wealthy consumer and trading power shaped global drink styles.
[事实] Jeffreys says a British wine merchant could access more varieties of wine than the king of France.
[事实] Bordeaux could reach London by sea faster than it could reach Paris by road.
[事实] Champagne, sparkling wine, claret and port became paradigms imitated around the world.
[推测] The episode links British influence over drink to the broader reach of the British economy and empire.

[09:33] **Champagne prestige and cost**

[事实] Jeffreys says champagne is more expensive to produce than still wine because the traditional method requires bottling, added sugar, long aging and removing yeast.
[事实] He also says that once champagne houses are established, the extra per-bottle cost is not enough to explain the full price premium.
[事实] Prestige and marketing are presented as major reasons champagne commands high prices.
[推测] Champagne’s value is shown as a mix of production cost, scarcity signals and cultural meaning.

[10:52] **Claret and medieval Bordeaux**

[事实] The discussion moves from champagne to claret, which comes from Bordeaux and has a long connection with England.
[事实] Jeffreys says medieval Bordeaux wine was very different from modern Bordeaux and was closer to a dark rosé.
[事实] This older claret had to be drunk quickly because it could turn to vinegar within months.
[事实] Modern-style claret emerged in the 17th century rather than the Middle Ages.
[推测] Bordeaux’s English connection mattered early, but the recognizable luxury style developed later through trade and consumer targeting.

[12:19] **The making of modern Bordeaux**

[事实] Jeffreys discusses Arnaud de Pontac of Château Haut-Brion as a figure who created a more expensive new style of Bordeaux for London.
[事实] The wine used riper grapes, selected fruit and new barrels, producing a darker wine closer to modern Bordeaux.
[事实] It sold in London for two or three times the price of ordinary wine.
[事实] Marketing and access to rich London customers were central to its success.
[推测] The story presents modern Bordeaux as both a product innovation and a luxury branding exercise.

[13:36] **Château brands and merchant power**

[事实] Jeffreys explains that Bordeaux château names became brands, unlike older Burgundy bottles that often emphasized place names.
[事实] The Médoc was drained by Dutch engineers and later planted speculatively by wealthy lawyers.
[事实] Landowners built elaborate châteaux whose images helped sell the wines.
[事实] Merchants remained powerful in the Bordeaux trade into the 20th century and sometimes adjusted wines with port or brandy for London tastes.
[推测] Bordeaux branding depended on visible estates and labels that could communicate status to distant buyers.

[16:37] **British taste: sweet, strong and sometimes dry**

[事实] The hosts ask whether British drinkers historically liked sweet, strong and overflavored drinks.
[事实] Jeffreys agrees this was generally true and says sherry was often sweetened for British consumers.
[事实] He also notes that connoisseurs existed and that a fashion for dry champagne developed in Britain.
[推测] British taste is presented as broad rather than uniform: mass preference leaned robust, while elite fashion could push in different directions.

[17:14] **Claret, port and political identity**

[事实] Jeffreys says claret had a political dimension because the Stuarts were aligned with France.
[事实] After the Glorious Revolution, claret became harder to obtain and more heavily taxed.
[事实] Hanoverian supporters drank port enthusiastically, while Jacobites and some Scots drank claret as a sign of loyalty to the Stuarts.
[事实] The hosts summarize this as claret being associated with Tories and port with Whigs.
[推测] Drink choices functioned as political signals as well as matters of taste.

[18:54] **The Methuen Treaty and port**

[事实] The hosts discuss the Methuen Treaty of 1703 with Portugal.
[事实] The treaty lowered duties on Portuguese wines compared with French wines and boosted the Portuguese wine trade.
[事实] Portugal is described as Britain’s oldest ally.
[事实] During this section, the hosts and guest are drinking Graham’s 30-year-old port.
[推测] The episode treats port’s rise in Britain as a direct result of diplomacy, war and taxation as much as consumer preference.

[20:03] **British merchants in Portugal**

[事实] Jeffreys says British merchants settled in northern Portugal but often did not mix with the Portuguese.
[事实] The Portuguese phrase “bebado inglês” is given as meaning “English drunk.”
[事实] John Mytton is cited as an extreme port drinker who reportedly drank four to six bottles a day and took 2,000 bottles of port to Cambridge.
[推测] The anecdotes underline port’s association with extravagant British elite drinking.

[21:48] **From rustic Portuguese wine to port**

[事实] Jeffreys says early Portuguese wine sent to England could be rustic and not very good.
[事实] Some wine was transported in animal skins and sometimes strengthened with brandy.
[事实] Port-like wine improved when merchants sourced riper, stronger wines from the Douro Valley.
[事实] Jeffreys notes that Douro table wines can naturally be strong, around 14 or 15 percent alcohol.
[推测] Port’s quality depended on moving from unsuitable coastal-style wines to richer inland wines better suited to export and British taste.

[22:40] **Pombal and regulated port**

[事实] The Marquis of Pombal demarcated the boundaries of the port-growing region in the 18th century.
[事实] The hosts note that France did not demarcate its wine regions in the same way until much later.
[事实] Pombal forbade planting elderberry bushes because elderberries were used to deepen wine color.
[事实] English names such as Sandeman, Taylor and Graham remain strongly associated with port houses.
[推测] Port became an early example of geographic regulation and brand discipline in the wine trade.

[24:39] **Shakespeare, sack and sherry**

[事实] The second half opens with Falstaff praising sack in Shakespeare’s *Henry IV* plays.
[事实] Jeffreys says sack is largely the drink now called sherry, though the term was broader.
[事实] Sack could refer to strong sweet wines from Spain, Jerez or the Canary Islands.
[事实] Francis Drake’s raid on Cádiz is discussed as having brought a large quantity of stolen sherry to London.
[推测] Shakespeare’s repeated references suggest sack was already familiar and fashionable in late Elizabethan and early Stuart England.

[26:24] **When sherry became modern sherry**

[事实] Jeffreys says Shakespearean sack was not yet fortified and was younger than modern sherry.
[事实] Sherry as now recognized developed through long aging and blending in the solera system.
[事实] The solera system mixes younger and older wines through stacked barrels, drawing wine from the oldest layer and topping up above.
[事实] This style took shape in the 18th century after stocks of old wine created a taste for aged sherry.
[推测] Modern sherry emerged from both accident and systematization: unsold stocks revealed a desirable style, then producers made it repeatable.

[28:02] **Sherry merchants and fortification**

[事实] Jeffreys says sherry was shaped not only by British taste but also by American and French tastes.
[事实] Many sherry merchants were Spanish or French, while some English names, such as Osborne, became deeply integrated into Spanish culture.
[事实] Jeffreys questions the simple claim that fortification was mainly for transport, noting that strong well-made wine can travel without fortification.
[事实] He says surplus wine was sometimes distilled into brandy, which was then used to strengthen wine.
[推测] Fortification is presented as partly about consumer taste for stronger drinks, not just preservation.

[30:07] **Robust British flavours and Victorian sherry**

[事实] The hosts connect fortified wines with British taste for robust flavours, comparing them with roast beef, mustard, horseradish and later Indian food.
[事实] Jeffreys says that by the middle of the 19th century, roughly 40 percent of the wine drunk in Britain was sherry.
[事实] Victorian sherry could resemble something like Harvey’s Bristol Cream, though often with only a small amount of very sweet Pedro Ximénez blended in.
[事实] Dickens is mentioned as someone who liked sherry cobblers.
[推测] Sherry’s Victorian popularity suggests that fortified, sweetened and cocktail-friendly wines were central to everyday middle-class hospitality.

[32:31] **Historic drinking levels**

[事实] The hosts discuss William Pitt the Younger as a “three bottle man.”
[事实] Jeffreys says historic bottles were often smaller than modern wine bottles, sometimes closer to imperial pint bottles.
[事实] William Wilberforce is discussed as pledging moderation by limiting himself to six glasses of wine a day.
[事实] Jeffreys says small beer was commonly drunk in London and was not always thought of as meaningfully alcoholic.
[推测] The episode complicates stories of heavy drinking by pointing out differences in bottle size, glass size and everyday alcohol norms.

[34:12] **Marsala, Nelson and a drink that faded**

[事实] Marsala is introduced as a drink that has largely fallen by the wayside.
[事实] Jeffreys says Marsala was originally promoted as a Madeira substitute and was linked to a merchant named John Woodhouse from Liverpool.
[事实] Nelson tried Marsala, liked it and helped make it a drink supplied to Royal Navy officers.
[事实] British merchants such as Woodhouse, Ingham and Whitaker were involved in Marsala’s 19th-century golden age.
[推测] Marsala’s history shows how British endorsement could elevate a regional wine into an imperial or naval drink.

[37:46] **Why Marsala declined**

[事实] Jeffreys says Marsala suffered from a race to the bottom after the wine went out of fashion.
[事实] Unlike sherry and Madeira, Marsala producers did not maintain quality as successfully.
[事实] Producers began making versions mixed with eggs or flavored with almonds or vanilla.
[事实] Marsala became associated with cooking, especially in Italian American dishes.
[推测] Marsala’s decline is presented as a warning that brand dilution can erase a drink’s prestige.

[39:05] **Old wine and tasting the past**

[事实] Jeffreys says Madeira is one of the few wines that can last long enough for modern drinkers to taste very old bottles.
[事实] Berry Brothers is said to sell very old Madeiras dating back to the 19th century.
[事实] Jeffreys says some modern wines can recreate historical styles, including Lustau Old East India Sherry.
[事实] Sherry and especially Madeira could improve after traveling through hot tropical conditions.
[推测] Madeira and certain sherries offer unusually direct sensory links to historical drinking cultures.

[41:00] **How old wine tasted different**

[事实] Jeffreys says wine in 1750 or 1850 would generally have tasted sweeter and less fresh than modern wine.
[事实] Modern fresh fruit flavors in wines such as Sauvignon Blanc and Pinot Grigio depend on temperature-controlled fermentation.
[事实] Older wines, especially sherries and Madeiras, were often more oxidized, nuttier and less fresh.
[推测] Modern palates may underestimate how technologically recent “freshness” is in wine.

[41:50] **Whisky and New Year’s Eve**

[事实] The hosts introduce whisky as a drink associated with New Year’s Eve, especially in Scotland.
[事实] Jeffreys says whisky, like the other drinks discussed, was shaped for English tastes in its modern form.
[事实] Early Scottish whisky often stayed in Scotland, was frequently made illegally and was not aged like modern whisky.
[事实] Jeffreys describes early malt whisky as strongly flavoured and closer to moonshine than modern aged whisky.
[推测] The episode reframes whisky as a Scottish product whose global style was partly built through external market demand.

[44:31] **Blended whisky and the London market**

[事实] Merchants such as John Walker, Shivas Brothers and others bought Highland and Lowland whiskies and blended them.
[事实] Storing whisky in barrels that had held port, Madeira or rum improved the spirit through time and residual wine influence.
[事实] Phylloxera destroyed vines in Cognac, creating a shortage of brandy for English clubs.
[事实] Scottish merchants created lighter blended whiskies for the London market, suited to mixing with soda.
[推测] Scotch whisky’s rise depended on both production innovation and a market opening caused by crisis in a rival drink.

[47:05] **Scotch branding, Irish whisky and American whiskey**

[事实] Jeffreys says Scotch whisky was marketed through “Brand Scotland,” including tartan, Highland imagery, Walter Scott and royal enthusiasm for Scotland.
[事实] Irish whiskey was more popular than Scotch until the late 19th century.
[事实] Irish whiskey was later pushed out by lighter, fruitier blended Scotch whiskies that were better marketed.
[事实] American whiskey is described as different because it uses grains such as corn and rye, and Jeffreys says he likes it in cocktails.
[推测] Marketing and mixability mattered as much as national tradition in determining which whisky styles became dominant.

[49:02] **From British influence to American and global influence**

[事实] The hosts ask whether America or China has replaced Britain as the global arbiter of drinks taste.
[事实] Jeffreys says American influence has already affected cocktails and Bordeaux.
[事实] Robert Parker is mentioned as an American critic whose preference for big wines influenced Bordeaux producers.
[事实] Jeffreys says many countries now make whisky using a Scotch template, including England, Taiwan, Japan and China.
[推测] The older British role in shaping drinks has been partly replaced by broader global markets, though Scotch remains a powerful model.

[51:03] **Final recommendation and closing**

[事实] Asked to recommend one historically evocative drink for the New Year, Jeffreys chooses a nice glass of port.
[事实] The specific drink in the room is Graham’s 30-year-old tawny port.
[事实] Jeffreys suggests it may evoke the 19th century more than the 18th.
[事实] The episode closes by mentioning Jeffreys’s forthcoming book on English wine.
[推测] The closing recommendation brings the episode back to its main claim: festive drinks can be understood as historical artifacts as well as pleasures.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode is valuable because it makes drink history feel concrete: bottles, shipping routes, barrels, taxes, fashions and brand names all become evidence for how tastes were made. Its strongest idea is that Britain did not simply import luxury drinks; it helped define what those drinks became.

The best sections are the ones that overturn familiar assumptions: champagne is not treated as purely French, port becomes a political and diplomatic product, and whisky becomes a story about English demand as well as Scottish production. The guest also keeps technical points accessible without reducing them to trivia.

A limitation is that the discussion is deliberately anecdotal and conversational, so some claims are presented through memorable examples rather than detailed sourcing inside the transcript. [推测] Listeners looking for a systematic economic history of alcohol would need Jeffreys’s book or other sources alongside the episode.

[推测] This episode is especially suited to listeners interested in British history, food and drink culture, wine, whisky, or the way commerce and prestige shape everyday tastes.
