# Jack the Ripper: The Double Event and the Panic in Whitechapel

## 概览

This episode continues the Jack the Ripper series by focusing on the night of September 29-30, 1888, when Elizabeth Stride and Catherine Eddowes were killed within less than an hour of each other. The hosts treat the “double event” as both a criminal investigation and a window into late Victorian fears about poverty, immigration, empire, policing, and urban darkness.

The discussion weighs witness statements, forensic details, police decisions, and letters attributed to “Jack the Ripper.” The hosts repeatedly stress uncertainty: some clues look suggestive, but many are compromised by poor visibility, panic, press sensationalism, hoaxes, and later conspiracy theories.

A major theme is how quickly the murders became part of popular culture. Newspapers, ballads, vigilante patrols, tourists, anti-immigrant rumours, and hoax letters all helped turn the killer into a public figure before anyone knew who he was.

## 分段落总结

[02:00] **Fear and Public Fascination**

[事实] The episode opens with a contemporary newspaper description of Cooney’s lodging house on Flower and Dean Street, where people sang dark comic songs about Jack the Ripper.

[事实] The hosts say that by autumn 1888 the Ripper story had gripped London, Britain, and the wider world.

[推测] The songs are presented as both entertainment and a way for frightened people to manage panic.

[04:55] **Recap of the Murders So Far**

[事实] The hosts recap the deaths of Martha Tabram on August 7, Polly Nichols on August 31, Annie Chapman on September 8, Elizabeth Stride on September 30, and Catherine Eddowes shortly afterward.

[事实] Elizabeth Stride was found in Duckfield’s Yard near a socialist club for Russian and Polish Jewish immigrants.

[事实] Catherine Eddowes was found about 45 minutes later in Mitre Square, inside the City of London police jurisdiction.

[推测] The episode frames the double killing as an escalation that deepened public terror and strained police coordination.

[06:05] **Catherine Eddowes’ Crime Scene**

[事实] City police surgeon Dr Frederick Gordon Brown examined the Mitre Square body and described severe mutilation.

[事实] The hosts say the killer acted very quickly, probably within less than 15 minutes, and escaped unseen.

[事实] The killer removed Eddowes’ womb and left kidney.

[推测] The speed and anatomical targeting are treated as evidence that the killer had some practical knowledge of bodies, though not necessarily medical training.

[08:02] **Elizabeth Stride’s Final Hours**

[事实] Elizabeth Stride was Swedish, born in Gothenburg, and had been seen in a cheap lodging house on Flower and Dean Street before her death.

[事实] The transcript says she may have eaten bread and cheese, may have drunk, had hard sweets in her hand, and wore a red rose on her bodice.

[事实] Her throat was cut, but her body was not mutilated.

[推测] The hosts lean toward the idea that Stride was a Ripper victim whose killer was interrupted before he could mutilate her.

[12:38] **Witnesses Near Duckfield’s Yard**

[事实] PC William Smith reported seeing a man and a woman with a red rose near the murder site.

[事实] Israel Schwartz said he saw a man assault a woman near the gateway and heard the word “Lipski.”

[事实] William Marshall also reported seeing a woman and man talking, with the man described as short, decently dressed, and wearing a peaked cap.

[推测] The repeated references to a peaked or deerstalker-style cap make the witness accounts feel connected, but the hosts do not treat them as conclusive identification.

[15:36] **The “Lipski” Taunt**

[事实] The hosts explain that “Lipski” referred to Israel Lipski, a Polish Jewish man hanged in 1887 for murder in a controversial case.

[事实] Inspector Abberline believed the word was probably an anti-Semitic taunt aimed at Schwartz, not evidence of an accomplice.

[推测] If Abberline’s view is correct, the killer may have been familiar with East End slang and may not have been Jewish.

[19:21] **Identifying Catherine Eddowes**

[事实] Catherine Eddowes was initially difficult to identify because she was poor, used false names on pawnbroker tickets, and was known to her partner John Kelly as Kate Conway.

[事实] She was born in Wolverhampton in 1842, had lived with Thomas Conway for nearly 20 years, had three children, later separated from him, and lived with John Kelly.

[事实] She had been arrested for drunkenness earlier on Saturday and released at 1:00 a.m. on Sunday.

[推测] Her death soon after release underlines how exposed homeless and precarious women were in Whitechapel at night.

[21:42] **Eddowes’ Mutilation and Possible Skill**

[事实] Eddowes’ throat was cut, her body and face were mutilated, and the killer removed her left kidney and part of her uterus.

[事实] Dr Gordon Brown believed the injuries were inflicted at the scene rather than elsewhere.

[事实] Brown thought the removal of the kidney suggested knowledge of anatomy.

[推测] The hosts reject the need to assume the killer was a doctor, suggesting alternatives such as butcher, slaughterman, medical student, or skilled amateur.

[23:54] **Witnesses Near Mitre Square**

[事实] Joseph Lavender and Joseph Levy saw a man and woman near an alley around 1:30 a.m.

[事实] Lavender described the man as about 30, around five foot seven, with a moustache, medium build, rough appearance, and a peaked deerstalker cap.

[推测] The hosts treat the description as suggestive because it overlaps with earlier witness descriptions, while noting poor nighttime visibility.

[24:32] **The Goulston Street Apron and Graffiti**

[事实] PC Alfred Long found a bloodied piece of Catherine Eddowes’ apron in a doorway on Goulston Street.

[事实] Nearby, chalk graffiti read: “The Jews are the men that will not be blamed for nothing,” with “Jews” spelled “Juwes.”

[事实] Sir Charles Warren ordered the graffiti wiped away before dawn because he feared anti-Jewish violence.

[推测] The graffiti may have been unrelated, may have been left by the killer, or may have been intended to misdirect suspicion toward Jews.

[28:16] **Masonic Conspiracy Theories**

[事实] The hosts discuss Stephen Knight’s book *Jack the Ripper: The Final Solution*, which links the murders to royalty and Freemasonry.

[事实] They say Knight treated the spelling “Juwes” as a Masonic clue.

[事实] The hosts reject this theory, saying the supposed Masonic link is unsupported.

[推测] The discussion is used to set up a later episode on Ripper conspiracy theories rather than to endorse them.

[32:17] **Panic After the Double Killing**

[事实] On Sunday, September 30, news of the double killing spread and crowds gathered outside the murder sites.

[事实] The hosts say streets became deserted at night, some women moved westward, some huddled together for protection, and some carried knives.

[事实] Local shopkeepers complained that fear and fog were keeping customers away.

[推测] The murders are presented as a crisis of public confidence in law and order in East London.

[33:47] **Rumours, Empire, and Popular Culture**

[事实] The episode describes theories blaming Americans, cowboys, Native Americans, “foreigners,” and other outsiders.

[事实] The hosts connect some press comparisons to Buffalo Bill’s Wild West show and Victorian imperial imagery.

[事实] Ballads and special newspaper editions turned the murders into commercial entertainment almost immediately.

[推测] The hosts argue that the Ripper story exposed anxiety that “savagery” might exist inside the imperial capital itself.

[39:23] **Police Pressure and Failed Tactics**

[事实] Newspapers called for rewards, but the Home Office disliked rewards because they could encourage hoaxes.

[事实] Sir Charles Warren and Home Secretary Henry Matthews were already in conflict, and Warren later decided to resign.

[事实] Robert Anderson returned from Switzerland and proposed arresting prostitutes or denying them police protection, but the idea was rejected.

[事实] Police conducted house-to-house searches with broad cooperation from East End residents, but found nothing.

[42:02] **Bloodhounds, Disguises, and Vigilantes**

[事实] The police experimented with bloodhounds named Barnaby and Burgo, but the plan collapsed amid practical problems.

[事实] A detective sergeant tried dressing as a woman to lure the killer and was attacked by cab washers.

[事实] The Mile End Vigilance Committee organised patrols using unemployed locals equipped with whistles, sticks, and galoshes.

[推测] The hosts suggest vigilante patrols may have deterred the killer temporarily and may explain why the next murder occurred indoors.

[45:48] **Press Criticism and the Saucy Jack Postcard**

[事实] Newspapers sharply criticised Scotland Yard and the Home Secretary for failing to catch the killer.

[事实] The Central News Agency received a bloodstained postcard dated October 1, referring to a “double event.”

[事实] The hosts say handwriting experts link the postcard to the earlier “Dear Boss” letter.

[推测] The hosts believe both messages were probably hoaxes, likely written by someone educated, possibly a reporter.

[48:45] **The “From Hell” Letter**

[事实] On October 16, George Lusk of the Mile End Vigilance Committee received a parcel containing half a human kidney and a letter beginning “From hell.”

[事实] Doctors agreed the kidney was human, but there was no evidence that it came from Catherine Eddowes.

[事实] The handwriting differed from the “Dear Boss” and postcard messages, and some spelling suggested an Irish voice.

[推测] The hosts find this letter harder to dismiss than the others, but still treat its authenticity as unresolved.

[51:28] **The Investigation Overwhelmed**

[事实] Chief Inspector Donald Swanson said police detained 80 people, followed leads on more than 300 others, and had 994 files open for the Elizabeth Stride murder alone.

[事实] Inspector Abberline later remembered his team having 1,600 files and working exhausting hours.

[事实] By late October, Robert Anderson and Sir Charles Warren reported that the police had no useful clue.

[推测] The flood of letters and false leads helped turn Jack the Ripper into a literary figure as much as a criminal suspect.

[53:15] **Return to the Streets and Mary Jane Kelly**

[事实] As weeks passed without another killing, vigilante patrols weakened and women returned to the streets.

[事实] PC Walter Dew recalled women joking, “I’m the next for Jack.”

[事实] The episode introduces Mary Jane Kelly, who lived with Joe Barnett in Miller’s Court and read newspaper stories about the murders.

[事实] On November 9, Thomas Bowyer looked through Kelly’s broken window after she failed to answer the door and saw something horrifying.

[推测] The cliffhanger positions Mary Jane Kelly’s death as the next and possibly most culturally significant stage of the Ripper story.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s main value is its disciplined handling of uncertainty. It gives vivid details from witness statements, police reports, newspapers, and later interpretations, but repeatedly separates plausible inference from firm evidence.

Its strongest sections connect the crimes to the wider world of 1888: anti-Semitism, immigration panic, imperial stereotypes, tabloid sensationalism, vigilante action, and the emergence of Jack the Ripper as a public myth.

[推测] Its limitation is that, because this is one part of a larger series, several major questions are deferred: the Mary Jane Kelly murder, the full cultural analysis, and the final suspect discussion are all left for later episodes.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested not only in true crime, but in how crime, media, policing, class, and urban fear interacted in late Victorian London.
