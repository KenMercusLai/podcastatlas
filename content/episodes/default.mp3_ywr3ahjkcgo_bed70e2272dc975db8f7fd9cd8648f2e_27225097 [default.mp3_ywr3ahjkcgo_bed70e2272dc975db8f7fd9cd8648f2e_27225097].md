# How Squishy Dumplings Became a Viral Toy Business

## 概览

This Planet Money episode uses the viral rise of squishy dumpling toys to explain how a small toy company turned a simple sensory object into a major hit. The story starts with 13-year-old Juju, whose TikTok-driven collecting shows how unboxing, rare variants, and social sharing helped the toy spread.

The episode’s core conclusion is that the dumpling’s success was not just luck. RMS combined “small-i” innovation, surprise packaging, scarcity, and a flexible supply chain that could respond quickly when TikTok attention exploded.

The discussion also shows how toy economics have changed. Viral demand now depends on social platforms, fast trend detection, limited drops, and production systems that can ramp up without leaving companies stuck with unsold inventory.

## 分段落总结

[01:03] **Juju and the squishy dumpling craze**

[事实] The episode introduces Juliana, or Juju, a 13-year-old collector who has many squishy dumplings and is treated as knowledgeable among her peers.

[事实] Squishy dumplings are rubbery, stretchy, gel-filled toys shaped like dumplings, each packaged in a small plastic steamer basket.

[事实] Juju first became interested after watching TikTok videos where people opened the toys without knowing which color or version they would get.

[推测] Juju’s story is used as a consumer-level example of how the toy’s appeal depends as much on the reveal as on the object itself.

[04:36] **The business question behind the fad**

[事实] Host Erika Barris frames the episode around why squishy dumplings went viral and what business strategies made the success possible.

[事实] The episode asks where trends like this come from, how a company builds a supply chain for viral demand, and who was behind the product.

[推测] The episode treats the toy as a case study in modern consumer demand rather than only as a novelty product.

[06:15] **Squishy toys as a fast-growing category**

[事实] Toy industry analyst Kristin McLean says 2026 has been a strong year for toys and that squishy toys are among the fastest-growing categories.

[事实] She says squishy toy sales have grown 279% since January.

[事实] McLean connects the popularity of squishy, sensory, slimy, and fidget toys to people seeking playfulness, joy, calm, and relief from anxiety.

[推测] The broader sensory-toy trend helped create favorable conditions before squishy dumplings themselves became a breakout product.

[08:06] **RMS and “small-i” innovation**

[事实] The squishy dumplings people are most excited about are made by RMS, which stands for Really Magical Stuff.

[事实] Zach Barber, CEO of RMS in the U.S., says part of the product’s success came from luck and part from business lessons learned over time.

[事实] One lesson is that a hit toy does not always require inventing something entirely new; it can come from tweaking an existing product to match a trend.

[事实] Zach gives examples including a reward chart inspired by the Supernanny trend and a glittery butterfly-shaped popper based on an existing fidget toy.

[12:01] **Turning the dumpling into a mystery toy**

[事实] RMS first found the squishy dumpling concept through the open-market toy ecosystem in Yiwu, China.

[事实] The first version debuted in 2020 and sold about 100,000 units, which the episode describes as not a flop but not a major breakout.

[事实] In 2023, Zach noticed that mystery and surprise were major toy trends, so RMS changed the dumpling into a blind-opening product.

[事实] Juju says the fun comes from opening the package without knowing what version she will get.

[15:00] **The psychology of surprise rewards**

[事实] The episode compares squishy dumplings to trading cards and slot machines because buyers keep trying for a desired rare outcome.

[事实] It names the psychology behind this as variable ratio reinforcement, where uncertainty about when a reward arrives keeps people engaged.

[事实] After adding mystery, Zach says squishy dumpling sales more than doubled in one year.

[推测] The blind-box format made repeat purchasing feel more like a game than a normal retail transaction.

[15:17] **Scarcity, series, and rare drops**

[事实] Zach identifies scarcity as another key lesson from the squishy dumpling’s rise.

[事实] RMS released different series of dumplings, including shark, green apple scented, galaxy, and holographic versions.

[事实] Each series could include rare or special versions, encouraging collectors to keep buying until they completed a set.

[事实] Juju used the possibility that a store might run out to persuade her parents to take her to Five Below.

[18:04] **Scarcity by design**

[事实] Juju’s mother says the repeated purchases turned into a financial discussion because each toy cost around five dollars.

[事实] She also says she became excited by the hunt and once stopped at three or four stores on her way home from work.

[事实] Zach describes the chase as a dopamine hit and says the company uses constant special-release drops to keep interest alive.

[事实] Kristin McLean says Beanie Babies pioneered the model of limited editions, special releases, and timed drops.

[19:35] **TikTok turns the product into mass demand**

[事实] Zach says RMS saw about 500 million views of people reacting to and unboxing squishy dumplings online.

[事实] He says regular people were promoting the product without being asked because they loved it.

[事实] Zach says RMS sold millions of squishy dumplings last year and ten times that, in the tens of millions, during the first half of this year.

[推测] TikTok functioned as unpaid distribution and marketing, but the company still needed operational readiness to benefit from it.

[21:33] **The experience becomes the product**

[事实] Zach says people were hooked not only on the dumpling itself but on the reaction to opening it.

[事实] The episode includes examples of people buying multiple dumplings and customizing or reacting to them for online audiences.

[事实] The host says the unpaid marketing apparatus was luck, but RMS’s preparedness for viral demand was not luck.

[推测] The episode suggests that social-media-native toys are designed around shareable moments, not just physical features.

[22:25] **A nimble supply chain built for trends**

[事实] RMS had struggled to get toys into major U.S. retailers, so Zach focused on stores selling inexpensive impulse toys, including Five Below.

[事实] RMS built its business around trends, short orders, and fast production.

[事实] Zach says RMS works out production schedules by week and can give a factory approval shortly before production begins.

[事实] He says RMS produces close to ship dates instead of manufacturing millions far in advance.

[23:25] **Avoiding inventory risk**

[事实] Zach says many toy companies produce in advance, present prototypes to retailers more than a year before store launch, and hold stock in storage.

[事实] RMS does not warehouse anything in the U.S. and has zero domestic stock.

[事实] Products are delivered from the factory directly to retailers.

[事实] Zach says he would rather refuse an order than warehouse hundreds of thousands of units for a retailer.

[24:54] **How viral toys are changing the industry**

[事实] Zach says major retailers are now calling RMS and adapting to its model because squishy dumplings became successful.

[事实] Kristin McLean says large companies are trying to become more nimble and are hiring people to track what is breaking on TikTok.

[事实] She says older toy brands are worried about smaller companies taking shelf space and about not knowing which platforms customers are using.

[事实] Juju says the squishy dumpling trend seems to be dying down, and she is now more interested in making sourdough bread because of TikTok.

[推测] The ending underlines how quickly viral attention can move from one object or hobby to another.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s value is that it turns a light, funny consumer fad into a clear explanation of business mechanics. It connects individual behavior, platform-driven virality, product design, scarcity, and inventory risk in a way that is easy to follow.

A major strength is the use of Juju and her mother alongside Zach Barber and Kristin McLean. The collector story shows the demand side, while the RMS and toy-industry interviews explain how companies try to profit from that demand.

[推测] The main limitation is that the episode focuses on one company’s account of its strategy and does not deeply examine broader concerns such as overconsumption, environmental impact, or the ethics of mystery-based toy buying for children.

[推测] This episode is especially suitable for listeners interested in consumer trends, retail strategy, TikTok-driven commerce, toy industry economics, and the way small companies can compete with larger brands through speed and timing.
