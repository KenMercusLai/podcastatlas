# A Polite Word for Liar

## Overview

This episode of *Revisionist History* examines the instability of memory through harmonica virtuoso Larry Adler and his friend John Weitz, a former OSS agent. Their sharply conflicting accounts of a postwar raid in Munich raise a deceptively simple question: when two people remember the same event differently, must one of them be lying?

Adler openly admits that better stories can displace less exciting truths in his memory. Yet his courage during the McCarthy era complicates the idea that his errors merely reveal vanity or weak character.

Weitz responds to emotionally charged memories in the opposite way, retreating into secrecy and restraint. The episode ultimately argues that people manage uncertainty differently—and that memory errors do not reliably reveal character.

## 分段落总结

[01:26] **Larry Adler and the Unreliability of Stories**

[事实] Larry Adler jokes that “raconteur” is a polite word for “liar.”

[事实] Adler disputes a biography’s claim that he confessed his affair with Ingrid Bergman to her husband, but concedes that he cannot categorically rule it out.

[事实] Drawing on *War and Peace*, Adler explains how a person can improve a story, repeat it, and eventually lose track of the original truth.

[事实] Malcolm Gladwell introduces the episode as an investigation into myths about memory and the games memory plays.

[07:53] **The Making of a Celebrated Raconteur**

[事实] Adler was born in Baltimore in 1914 to Jewish immigrants from Ukraine and became a harmonica prodigy.

[事实] At fourteen, he traveled to New York and persuaded Rudy Vallée to let him perform at the Paramount Theatre.

[事实] His extraordinary career brought him into the company of celebrities including Charlie Chaplin, Greta Garbo, Salvador Dalí, and Prince Philip.

[事实] Adler’s wit, musical brilliance, and constant name-dropping made storytelling central to his public persona.

[11:00] **John Weitz: From German Refugee to OSS Agent**

[事实] John Weitz was born Hans Werner Weitz in Berlin in 1923 to a wealthy, assimilated Jewish family.

[事实] His family fled Germany in 1938, and Weitz eventually reached the United States, joined the Army, and was recruited into the OSS.

[事实] He operated undercover in occupied Europe, participated in the liberation of Dachau, and later impersonated a German prisoner of war.

[事实] His sons describe lasting effects of the war, including poor sleep, rigid habits, heavy drinking, volatility, and an expectation that events could suddenly become catastrophic.

[17:15] **The Munich Raid and Two Opposing Memories**

[事实] After the war, Weitz was working against a Nazi guerrilla organization called Werewolf and had infiltrated a Munich cell under the name Hans Werner.

[事实] According to Adler, Weitz invited Jack Benny, Ingrid Bergman, and Adler to accompany an OSS raid; only Adler agreed.

[事实] Weitz remembered armed resistance, two Germans killed, and six captured.

[事实] Adler remembered entering the house, finding two old women, and witnessing no shooting or injuries.

[事实] Another published account placed a similar raid in April 1946, involved eighteen Nazi soldiers, and again identified Adler as accompanying “Werner.”

[22:14] **How a Better Story Becomes a Memory**

[事实] Adler recalled flying in a glider during the war and described the exhilaration of being released over Gander Airport.

[事实] A diary later showed that Adler had actually been in the aircraft towing the glider.

[事实] Adler concluded that his wish to have occupied the more exciting position eventually became “fact” in his mind.

[事实] Gladwell questions why disagreements and errors in memory are so often treated as evidence of ego, dishonesty, or defective character.

[25:27] **Principle, Blacklisting, and Personal Courage**

[事实] During the McCarthy era, Adler opposed anti-communist hysteria, was accused of being a communist, lost his career, and went bankrupt fighting the accusation.

[事实] Roy Cohn offered Adler a route back to work if he named people already known to investigators, but Adler refused on principle.

[事实] Adler moved his family to London and rebuilt his life after losing his homes and Hollywood career.

[事实] He later joined a public reading of *The Satanic Verses* while Salman Rushdie was under a fatwa, an action his son describes as motivated by conviction rather than publicity.

[推测] Adler’s willingness to accept severe personal risks complicates an explanation of his Munich memory based solely on vanity or self-aggrandizement.

[27:47] **Reconsidering Adler’s Motive for Joining the Raid**

[事实] Gladwell suggests that Adler, a Jewish man in Munich—the birthplace of the Nazi Party—may have regarded the raid as important rather than merely adventurous.

[事实] Adler insisted that his version was less dramatic and scarcely worth telling, but that he repeated it because it was what he remembered.

[推测] If Adler was serious about the mission, the anticlimactic discovery of old women would weaken rather than enhance his story, making a simple ego-based explanation less persuasive.

[28:39] **Weitz’s Silence and the Burden of Germany**

[事实] Weitz’s novels and historical biographies repeatedly examined privileged Germans who accommodated or served Hitler.

[事实] In a 1997 interview with Charlie Rose, Weitz resisted giving details of his wartime operations even after more than half a century.

[事实] Weitz cited an OSS instruction to protect people connected with German resistance for as long as any of them remained alive.

[事实] Gladwell contrasts Adler’s chatter and whimsy with Weitz’s silence and retreat, describing both as different responses to emotionally significant memories.

[事实] The episode’s central conclusion is that memory does not reveal character and that treating it as though it does is a mistake.

[32:57] **A Third Version Reconciles the Friends**

[事实] In the early 1990s, Weitz gave historian Perry Biddiscombe another account of the Munich operation.

[事实] In this version, the Werewolf members offered no resistance, nobody was shot, and the arrests were followed by the arrival of distressed mothers and grandmothers.

[事实] This account combined elements of Weitz’s earlier story about arrests with Adler’s memory of older women.

[推测] Gladwell interprets the merged version as Weitz choosing friendship over strict fidelity to a single memory and abandoning “memory fundamentalism.”

[34:14] **Adler’s Final Performance and a Life Oriented Toward Experience**

[事实] Adler gave his final concert at the Royal Albert Hall in May 2001 for Prince Philip’s eightieth birthday celebration.

[事实] Although ill and hospitalized, he checked himself out, was wheeled onto the stage, performed from a wheelchair, and received a standing ovation.

[事实] Gladwell initially says Adler died a few days later, then corrects himself: Adler died two and a half months later.

[推测] Gladwell presents Adler as someone who privileged present experience over uncertain memories, from pursuing Broadway as a child to performing near the end of his life.

[36:24] **A Final Demonstration of Embellished Memory**

[事实] Adler tells an elaborate story about repeatedly performing “Sharm El Sheikh” for Israeli soldiers after the Six-Day War and receiving a comic heckle.

[事实] He then admits that the punchline came from an opera anecdote and that he had played the song only once.

[事实] Adler says that many surrounding details were true, but borrowing and reshaping the other story made his version much better.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s chief strength is its refusal to reduce faulty memory to a simple moral failure. By placing Adler’s admitted embellishments alongside his documented willingness to sacrifice his career for principle, it shows why the label “liar” can obscure more than it explains.

Its structure is especially effective: biography, wartime history, oral testimony, family recollections, and archival interviews gradually turn a colorful dispute into a broader argument about memory and identity. The final anecdote lets Adler demonstrate the phenomenon in his own words, with both candor and humor.

[推测] The episode’s limitation is that it cannot establish exactly what happened during the Munich raid; its conclusion is therefore interpretive rather than forensic. It is best suited to listeners interested in memory, oral history, moral judgment, storytelling, and the uncertain boundary between deception and sincere recollection.
