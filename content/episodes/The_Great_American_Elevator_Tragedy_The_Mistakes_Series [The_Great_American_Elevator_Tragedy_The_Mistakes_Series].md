# The Great American Elevator Tragedy

## 概览

The United States invented the modern commercial elevator, yet it now has fewer elevators per capita than any other high-income country the host could identify. Compared with Europe, American elevators can cost several times more and are often too large for small apartment buildings.

Drawing on research by Stephen Smith of the Center for Building in North America, the episode traces this outcome to specialized labor practices and, more importantly, building rules that mandate unusually large elevator cabins. Requirements intended to accommodate wheelchairs and fully extended emergency stretchers can make elevators prohibitively expensive or physically impossible to install.

The episode culminates in a building-code amendment proposed by Glendale fire inspector Gregory A. Victor. His successful seven-foot stretcher requirement made already oversized elevators larger without documenting any cost impact. The broader conclusion is that pursuing perfect performance in rare emergencies can reduce everyday accessibility by leaving many buildings with no elevator at all.

## 分段落总结

[02:21] **A YIMBY Search for a Consequential Mistake**

[事实] Host Malcolm Gladwell identifies himself as a recent and enthusiastic YIMBY who supports abundant housing and opposes rules that unnecessarily raise construction costs.

[事实] He introduces the episode as the story of a seemingly minor building-code decision about elevators that produced far-reaching consequences.

[事实] Stephen Smith reports that he could not find a high-income country with fewer elevators per capita than the United States; Greece, despite its much smaller population, has roughly half as many elevators as the US.

[推测] The YIMBY framing treats elevator policy as part of the wider housing debate: obscure technical standards can influence affordability, density, and who is able to live in a building.

[06:28] **From American Invention to American Shortage**

[事实] Elisha Otis developed early modern elevators in the mid-19th century and installed the first commercial elevator in Manhattan’s Haughwout Building.

[事实] The elevator helped make New York’s vertical development possible, but American leadership in adopting the technology later declined.

[事实] Smith founded the Center for Building in North America partly because building codes receive little attention from academia and other researchers.

[推测] The episode presents America’s elevator shortage as an institutional reversal: the country that enabled vertical urbanism now imposes rules that restrict it.

[08:17] **Why a European Elevator Costs So Much Less**

[事实] Smith began investigating after seeing controversy over a new three-story Minneapolis apartment building that omitted an elevator to remain affordable.

[事实] While a small elevator for a three-story Italian building was estimated at €20,000–€30,000, Smith estimated a comparable American installation at about $120,000–$150,000.

[事实] Smith says European designers can fit compact elevators into very small buildings, whereas current American rules would make that impossible in his five-story Brooklyn building.

[事实] Smith’s experience with POTS and severe fatigue sometimes prevented him from leaving his third-floor apartment because the building had no elevator.

[推测] The example shows that omitting elevators is not merely an inconvenience: it can exclude disabled and older residents from otherwise desirable housing.

[14:43] **Labor Practices Raise Installation Costs**

[事实] Smith describes the American elevator workforce as small, specialized, highly unionized, and more highly paid than its European counterpart.

[事实] European elevators can be assembled in a factory and installed at the site as a finished unit.

[事实] In the United States, union rules can require factory-assembled elevators or escalators to be disassembled at the building site and rebuilt inside the shaft.

[事实] Smith found legal and arbitration records describing practices used to document that workers had actually taken components apart.

[推测] These labor arrangements add expense and inefficiency, but the episode argues that they do not fully explain a price difference as large as fivefold.

[17:57] **Oversized Cabins and the Accessibility Paradox**

[事实] For a small six-story apartment building, Smith says a typical European elevator cabin is roughly half the size of an American or Canadian one.

[事实] European cabins can accommodate a wheelchair, although the user may need to enter or exit backward; American standards appear to allow more maneuvering space.

[事实] Larger American elevators may accommodate fully extended stretchers, but their size and cost can prevent installation in small or affordable buildings.

[事实] Where no elevator is installed, wheelchair users cannot live in upper-floor apartments and emergency workers may have to carry patients down several flights of stairs.

[推测] A rule designed to maximize accessibility in a rare scenario can reduce accessibility overall by causing buildings to have no elevator whatsoever.

[20:00] **When Perfect Becomes the Enemy of Good**

[事实] Smith acknowledges that a large elevator can help emergency workers continue chest compressions while transporting a patient on a fully flat stretcher.

[事实] He contrasts that uncommon situation with the widespread need of older people who cannot manage stairs but could use a small elevator while standing with a walker.

[事实] Smith argues that making elevators three or four times more expensive inevitably results in far fewer elevators.

[事实] Gladwell characterizes the pursuit of perfection when “good” would suffice as the central mistake behind American “mega elevators.”

[推测] The policy failure comes from evaluating the benefit of an ideal elevator without weighing it against the loss of all elevator access in buildings where that ideal is unaffordable.

[23:34] **How Building Codes Acquire National Reach**

[事实] Most American construction rules are based on the International Building Code maintained by the nonprofit International Code Council.

[事实] Although the code is called international, Smith describes it as fundamentally American and says it now forms the vast majority of local building codes across the country.

[事实] Interested parties can propose amendments, which may be decided by a technical committee or the ICC’s broader voting membership.

[事实] Gregory A. Victor, a Glendale, Arizona fire inspector who later became the city’s fire marshal, used this process to propose a new elevator requirement.

[推测] Because the model code is adopted widely, a narrowly motivated amendment can reshape construction economics far beyond the jurisdiction where it originated.

[26:34] **The Seven-Foot Stretcher Amendment**

[事实] Victor argued that elevators designed around six-foot-four-inch stretchers did not adequately accommodate the equipment used by his jurisdiction.

[事实] His initial proposal would have required elevators to fit whatever stretcher a jurisdiction possessed, but a technical committee rejected it because it lacked standardization and did not demonstrate a cost-benefit case.

[事实] Victor returned to the broader ICC membership with a fixed seven-foot requirement and supported it with an emotional appeal about patients experiencing medical emergencies.

[事实] The revised proposal passed during a period when, according to information relayed to Smith, many elevator-industry representatives were absent at their own event.

[推测] The amendment succeeded because a vivid individual emergency was more persuasive than the diffuse future costs imposed on housing projects nationwide.

[29:03] **A Cost Impact of “None”**

[事实] American elevators were already among the world’s largest and most expensive before Victor’s amendment.

[事实] The new rule made them larger still, increasing the pressure on builders of small or affordable apartment projects to omit elevators.

[事实] The amendment form for International Building Code change G143-03/04 included sections for the proposed change, its justification, and its cost impact.

[事实] Victor entered “None” in the cost-impact section.

[推测] That single answer encapsulates the episode’s central warning: regulatory benefits are easy to state, while indirect costs—including projects never built and elevators never installed—can remain invisible.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s chief strength is its transformation of an obscure technical standard into a clear account of how housing costs and accessibility are shaped. Personal experience, international comparison, and the history of a specific code amendment give the argument both human and institutional dimensions.

Its most valuable insight is the distinction between the quality of an individual elevator and the availability of elevators across society. A smaller elevator may perform less perfectly in exceptional circumstances, yet widespread installation could serve far more wheelchair users, older residents, and people with temporary or chronic mobility limitations.

[推测] The treatment is deliberately polemical and gives limited attention to counterarguments from firefighters, disability advocates, elevator unions, or code officials. Some claims—particularly the account of industry representatives being absent during the vote—are explicitly presented as information Smith was told rather than independently established in the transcript.

[推测] The episode is especially suitable for listeners interested in housing policy, urbanism, accessibility, regulation, or unintended consequences. Its broader lesson applies beyond elevators: standards should be judged not only by the risk they address, but also by the useful projects they make unaffordable or impossible.
