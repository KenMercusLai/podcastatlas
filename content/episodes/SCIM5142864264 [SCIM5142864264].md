# Chris Voss on Negotiation, Tactical Empathy, and Hard Conversations

## 概览

This episode features Andrew Huberman interviewing Chris Voss, former FBI lead crisis negotiator and author of *Never Split the Difference*, about how negotiation works across business, relationships, scams, hostage situations, and everyday difficult conversations.

The central theme is that negotiation is less about forcing agreement and more about diagnosing reality: whether a deal exists, whether the other side can be trusted, what emotions are driving the exchange, and whether both sides actually feel heard.

Voss repeatedly returns to tactical empathy, labels such as “sounds like,” calibrated “how” and “what” questions, mirroring, calm vocal tone, and concise bad-news delivery as tools that make people think, reduce defensiveness, and improve outcomes.

## 分段落总结

[00:00] **Episode Setup and Guest Background**

[事实] Huberman introduces Chris Voss as a former FBI agent, lead crisis negotiator, Joint Terrorist Task Force member, negotiation teacher, and author of *Never Split the Difference*.
[事实] The episode frames negotiation as relevant to business, romantic relationships, family relationships, work relationships, deception, stamina, and heated conversations.
[推测] The introduction positions negotiation as a general life skill rather than a narrow law-enforcement specialty.

[03:08] **Diagnosing Whether a Deal Is Worth Pursuing**

[事实] Voss says his first goal is to figure out what is really going on and whether there is a deal, no deal, or a bad deal.
[事实] He says it is not a sin to fail to get a deal, but it is a problem to take too long to fail or to take too long to get a bad deal.
[事实] He emphasizes early diagnosis of whether the other side is trustworthy or cutthroat.

[06:07] **Playfulness as a Negotiation Advantage**

[事实] Voss says some of his biggest personal negotiation wins came when he was playful and in a good mood.
[事实] He describes recovering lost luggage after joking with an airport employee that he needed her to “wave a magic wand.”
[推测] The story illustrates that playful framing can invite cooperation by letting the other person step into a helpful role without feeling pressured.

[10:18] **Calm Voice and Emotional State**

[事实] Voss says his “late night FM DJ voice” calms the other person and also calms him.
[事实] He believes negative emotions interfere with his ability to process information.
[事实] Huberman explains that low-frequency vocal sounds can entrain low-frequency neural activity and shift emotional tone toward calm.
[推测] The discussion suggests that vocal delivery is not just style; it can change the physiology of the interaction.

[17:07] **Benevolent Negotiation and the Risk of “Win-Win” Language**

[事实] Huberman asks about friendly or benevolent negotiations, such as travel plans, shared finances, or relationships.
[事实] Voss says “win-win” should mean collaboration, but people who announce it early often correlate with trying to take advantage.
[事实] He warns that people who emotionally believe in “win-win” can make themselves vulnerable if the other side is predatory.

[20:35] **Feeling Heard Through Hypothesis Testing**

[事实] Voss says people in friendly negotiations often mainly want to be heard out.
[事实] His method is to describe his best guess of the other person’s perspective so they can correct him.
[事实] He says correction is satisfying and often produces more candid information than direct questioning.
[推测] This works like hypothesis testing: being wrong is useful because it reveals the other person’s actual priorities.

[26:46] **Generosity as a Better Opening Move**

[事实] Voss says people he wants to work with often begin by giving something valuable without asking for anything.
[事实] He cites Joe Polish as someone whose generosity created a strong basis for future collaboration.
[事实] Huberman connects this to a lab culture where lending equipment without demanding repayment built goodwill.

[31:20] **High-Stakes Cases and Failed Collaboration**

[事实] Voss discusses the Burnham-Sabaro case involving Abu Sayyaf, where hostages were killed and the case became a major learning experience.
[事实] He says poor collaboration among government-side factions contributed to critical information not being shared.
[事实] He learned not to assume others would volunteer necessary information, even basic contextual details like holidays.
[推测] The case becomes a lesson that your own side’s coordination can be as important as communication with the adversary.

[37:22] **Reading Threats by Specificity**

[事实] Voss says threat seriousness can often be judged by specificity: who, what, when, and where.
[事实] In one kidnapping case, a vague threat about a father losing “an egg” did not specify timing or method, so Voss judged there was room to continue the process.
[事实] Later, when the kidnappers said the son would die the next day if not paid, Voss judged the threat as specific and serious.

[42:00] **Scams, Urgency, and Proof of Leverage**

[事实] Huberman and Voss discuss online scams, hacked accounts, fake kidnapping claims, and urgent demands for payment.
[事实] Voss says the first issue is whether the other side actually has leverage, such as access to an account, data, money, or a loved one.
[事实] Voss describes testing a suspicious text from a friend’s number by introducing false shared details to see whether the sender could confirm them.
[推测] Urgency is treated as a warning signal because it pushes people to act before verifying reality.

[55:36] **How and What Questions Against Aggression**

[事实] Voss says “how” and “what” questions force deeper thinking and are useful for judging how the other side responds.
[事实] He says the answer matters less than whether the other side thinks carefully or fires back reflexively.
[事实] Against a cutthroat or aggressive counterpart, he uses calibrated questions to slow and wear them down without direct confrontation.

[60:02] **Implementation and Truth Testing**

[事实] Voss recommends asking questions such as “How do I know you’re going to follow through?” to make the other side describe implementation.
[事实] He says “vision drives decision,” meaning someone who intends to comply should have some picture of what compliance looks like.
[事实] In kidnapping negotiations, his team would sometimes seek a final verbal promise after enough conversation to know what the person sounded like when telling the truth.

[64:20] **Gut Signals, Deception, and Alignment**

[事实] Huberman describes sensing that something was off about a person years before discovering lies.
[事实] Voss says people should learn the difference between gut signals and fear responses, and that the gut can be highly accurate.
[事实] Voss focuses on whether words, tone, facial expression, and behavior line up rather than assigning fixed meanings to isolated body-language cues.
[推测] The practical advice is to notice shifts, then test them with observations like “seems like something crossed your mind.”

[77:11] **Text Communication and Bad News**

[事实] Voss says texts and emails should usually make one move at a time, like chess, rather than bundle many points together.
[事实] He describes sending a two-line text before a difficult call: “It’s now bad time to talk” and “I got something you don’t want to hear.”
[事实] He says this prepared the other person for problem solving and helped resolve the issue quickly.
[推测] The example favors concise context-setting over long softening preambles.

[83:22] **Breakups, Firings, and Painful Endings**

[事实] Voss says people who postpone hard endings are often trying to save themselves rather than the other person.
[事实] He advises giving bad news quickly but gently, warning that it is coming, pausing briefly, and then delivering it.
[事实] For firings, he says Monday is better than Friday because the person can begin taking action during the workweek.
[推测] His approach treats clarity and speed as more humane than prolonged cushioning.

[89:09] **Ego Depletion and Agreement Durability**

[事实] Huberman explains ego depletion as the fatigue that comes from defending one’s position over time.
[事实] Voss says ego depletion may work in contained hostage situations where the person will immediately come out and be handcuffed.
[事实] He warns that in business or personal negotiations, agreements produced only by exhaustion may fail once the other person rests and regains energy.

[95:57] **Small-Stakes Practice for Readiness**

[事实] Voss says “small stakes practice” prepares people for high-stakes results.
[事实] He practices labels and questions with Lyft drivers, hotel staff, TSA agents, grocery clerks, and other everyday interactions.
[事实] He uses questions like “What do you love about what you do?” to trigger a state change and learn quickly about the other person.

[101:50] **Handling Venting Without Amplifying It**

[事实] Voss says he is wary of simply letting people vent because it can spiral.
[事实] He interprets venting as a sign that someone feels unheard, ignored, frustrated, or blocked.
[事实] He recommends labels such as “sounds like this is driving you crazy because nobody listens to you” instead of correcting, advising, or arguing.
[推测] The goal is to deactivate negative emotion by naming it rather than feeding the rant.

[106:36] **Spiritual, Physical, and Recovery Practices**

[事实] Voss says he uses a morning gratitude exercise, small amounts of meditation, prayer, and spiritual practice.
[事实] He says fitness has long been part of his life and currently mentions cold plunge, sauna, diet, and managing joint issues.
[事实] Huberman and Voss agree that taking care of oneself supports the ability to show up effectively for others.

[114:12] **Long Crises and Tactical Responsibility**

[事实] Voss describes the 2003 Dwight Watson tractor incident in Washington, D.C., which lasted about three days.
[事实] He says he could sleep after handing the shift to another skilled negotiator and trusting the process.
[事实] He recounts the Princess Gate siege as an example where a negotiator kept a hostage taker on the phone while an assault occurred.
[推测] The discussion presents negotiation work as requiring stamina, delegation, and acceptance of difficult tactical roles.

[120:28] **Hostage Survival Through Humanization**

[事实] Voss says anything a hostage can do to humanize themselves while complying can improve survival chances.
[事实] He suggests saying something like “I’ll do whatever you say. I’m Chris” so the captor knows a first name.
[事实] He says people are easier to harm when dehumanized and harder to harm when humanized.

[125:24] **Tactical Empathy Defined**

[事实] Voss defines tactical empathy as actively demonstrating understanding of another person’s perspective.
[事实] He distinguishes empathy from agreement, liking, sympathy, and compassion.
[事实] He says empathy is about transmitting information, while compassion is a response to that transmission.
[事实] He gives an example of acknowledging Arab Muslim witnesses’ view of American governments without agreeing or disagreeing.

[132:00] **Mirroring as a Listening Tool**

[事实] Voss defines mirroring as repeating roughly one to three words someone just said.
[事实] He says mirroring replaces phrases like “what do you mean?” or “please repeat that” and often gets the person to expand.
[事实] He says mirroring can help people hear themselves and recover their train of thought.

[138:55] **Proactive Listening and Labeling Negativity**

[事实] Voss prefers “proactive” or “interactive” listening over the overused term “active listening.”
[事实] He says hostage negotiators were trained to label presenting emotions, which were usually negative.
[事实] He says calling out predictable negativity, such as “this is probably going to sound greedy,” can reduce or inoculate against that reaction.
[推测] The method works by naming the emotional obstacle before it controls the conversation.

[146:21] **Using Family Members Carefully in Crises**

[事实] Voss says family members can be helpful in contained crises but must be used carefully.
[事实] In the Dwight Watson case, he wanted a family statement recorded because the exact wording could land, while a live call might reopen old wounds.
[事实] He says family members may unintentionally trigger pain they do not even know exists.

[152:03] **Mental Health After Heavy Work**

[事实] Voss says humor, friendship, emotional reinforcement, and genuine understanding helped him process difficult work.
[事实] He says he does not mainly feel pride in his accomplishments, but he has felt privilege and satisfaction.
[事实] He emphasizes avoiding people who run you down while accepting good-natured humor.

[155:36] **Current Projects and Teaching the Tools**

[事实] Voss describes Fireside as an interactive podcast and group coaching platform where people can ask questions and get real-time feedback.
[事实] He says his team is working on a tactical empathy operations manual, online training, a weekly newsletter, and a real-estate-focused book.
[事实] Huberman closes by emphasizing the power of “sounds like” as a simple tool adults and children could learn to improve listening.

[推测] Its strongest moments are the concrete examples, especially the lost-luggage story, scam verification, calibrated questions, concise bad-news text, and hostage humanization advice.

[推测] A limitation is that some claims about gut signals, energy, and physiological intuition are discussed partly from experience and plausibility rather than fully established mechanisms within the transcript.

[推测] This episode is especially useful for listeners who handle difficult conversations, leadership, sales, legal disputes, relationship conflict, crisis work, or any situation where being heard and reading the other side accurately matter.
