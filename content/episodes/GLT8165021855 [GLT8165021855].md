# Coronations: Ritual, Religion, and Legitimacy in English History

## 概览

This episode traces English coronations from the Anglo-Saxon period through the Stuarts, arguing that coronations are not timeless ceremonies so much as rituals repeatedly reshaped by political insecurity, religious conflict, and dynastic need.

Tom Holland and Dominic Sandbrook use scandalous, chaotic, and symbolically loaded coronation stories to show how later writers often read a monarch’s reign back into the coronation itself. Bad omens, disorder, special oils, processions, and oaths all become ways of explaining or legitimating power.

The central thread is that vulnerable rulers, usurpers, child kings, religiously contested monarchs, and restored regimes tend to invest most heavily in coronation spectacle. The episode ends with William and Mary’s post-Glorious Revolution settlement and Queen Anne’s continuation of sacral monarchy through the royal touch.

## 分段落总结

[01:25] **Coronations as Political and Religious Statements**

[事实] The episode opens with a Protestant broadside ballad celebrating the 1689 coronation of William III and Mary II after the Glorious Revolution.
[事实] The hosts frame coronations as rituals that appear timeless but actually reflect changing political and religious passions.
[事实] They connect Charles III’s coronation oath to the legacy of the Glorious Revolution.

[03:00] **Dunstan, Edwy, and Biased Coronation Stories**

[事实] Tom discusses Edwy’s 956 coronation at Kingston upon Thames and the hostile account associated with supporters of Saint Dunstan.
[事实] The account claims Edwy abandoned the post-coronation proceedings, threw off his crown, and behaved disgracefully with a noblewoman and her daughter.
[推测] The hosts treat this story as likely pro-Dunstan and pro-Edgar propaganda rather than neutral reporting.
[事实] Ethelred the Unready is also presented through ominous stories, including the claim that he urinated in the baptismal font.

[06:23] **William the Conqueror’s Chaotic Coronation**

[事实] William I’s coronation in Westminster Abbey went badly when Norman soldiers mistook acclamation inside the abbey for disorder and began setting fires in London.
[事实] Orderic Vitalis reports that monks and bishops stayed in the abbey nervously, while William trembled through the ceremony.
[事实] Tom explains that anointing mattered to William because it elevated him among European kings and helped secure his legitimacy as king of England.
[推测] The disorder could have deepened William’s anxiety about whether God truly blessed his rule.

[09:05] **The Church Pushes Back Against Sacral Kingship**

[事实] The Gregorian Revolution is described as a period when the papacy and church tried to separate secular rule from priestly status.
[事实] The church sought to limit kings’ claims to sacred authority and emphasized the role of bishops and archbishops in coronation.
[事实] Richard I’s coronation triggered anti-Jewish violence and fires in London, while John was said to have laughed through his ceremony.
[推测] The hosts suggest John’s laughter may be a later story projected backward from his disastrous reign.

[10:57] **Henry III and the Need for a Proper Coronation**

[事实] Henry III became king at age nine while Westminster Abbey was occupied by the French.
[事实] He was hastily crowned at Gloucester using his mother’s crown because King John had lost the crown jewels.
[事实] Henry later received papal permission for a second coronation and anointing at Westminster Abbey.
[事实] He subsequently redeveloped Westminster Abbey into the building that substantially stands today.

[13:07] **Usurpers, Holy Oil, and Manufactured Legitimacy**

[事实] Henry IV’s coronation used oil associated with a prophecy of Thomas Becket and the Virgin Mary.
[事实] This story cast Henry IV not as a usurper but as the fulfillment of sacred prophecy.
[事实] Under Henry V, the story was expanded to connect the oil with reconquest in Normandy and Aquitaine.
[推测] The hosts see these stories as examples of insecure rulers strengthening coronation ritual to stabilize legitimacy.

[15:05] **Henry VI and the English Claim to France**

[事实] Henry VI inherited claims to both England and France while still a very young child.
[事实] After Joan of Arc helped Charles VII be crowned at Reims, the English arranged for Henry VI to be crowned in Notre Dame in Paris.
[事实] French observers complained about the English cooking at Henry VI’s coronation feast.
[事实] Henry VI became the only king formally crowned and anointed as king of both England and France in separate ceremonies.
[推测] His double coronation did not prevent the political instability that led into the Wars of the Roses.

[17:20] **Henry VII and the Imperial Crown**

[事实] Henry VII’s coronation followed his defeat of Richard III at Bosworth.
[事实] His mother, Margaret Beaufort, reportedly wept when she saw him crowned.
[事实] Henry VII chose an imperial crown, with arches and an orb symbolizing rule with imperial authority.
[推测] The imagery anticipated Henry VIII’s later claim that England was an empire outside papal jurisdiction.

[18:39] **Henry VIII, Catherine of Aragon, and Anne Boleyn**

[事实] Henry VIII and Catherine of Aragon were crowned together in 1509 after a major London procession.
[事实] Henry swore to protect the law, canon, and Holy Church, which the hosts note ironically in light of the later Reformation.
[事实] Spectators tore up pieces of the ceremonial carpet as souvenirs.
[事实] Anne Boleyn received a separate coronation in 1533, presided over by Thomas Cranmer, but reportedly complained that the crowds did not cheer enough.

[22:09] **Edward VI and the First Protestant Coronation**

[事实] Edward VI’s coronation was shortened because he was young, though it still lasted about seven hours.
[事实] The ceremony retained anointing and oath-taking but was reinterpreted in Protestant terms.
[事实] Cranmer presented Edward as a second Josiah, a young king expected to remove idolatry and oppose Rome.
[推测] The ritual shifted emphasis from anointing as making the king holy to anointing as recognizing authority already held by descent.

[25:53] **Mary I Restores Catholic Coronation Ritual**

[事实] Mary I’s coronation reversed Edward VI’s Protestant direction and centered Catholic ritual.
[事实] Thomas Cranmer was imprisoned, while Catholic clergy such as George Day and Stephen Gardiner were restored to prominence.
[事实] Mary’s court rejected the oil left from Edward VI’s coronation as Protestant and ordered new oil.
[事实] The hosts later note that Elizabeth I’s coronation oil was rancid.

[27:50] **Elizabeth I’s Procession and Religious Ambiguity**

[事实] Elizabeth I’s public procession included pageants emphasizing her Englishness, Tudor descent, virtues, and Protestant true religion.
[事实] A figure of Truth presented Elizabeth with an English Bible, which she kissed as the crowd cheered.
[事实] The hosts say less is clearly known about the abbey ceremony and that there was confusion over Catholic elements such as mass.
[推测] Elizabeth’s team may have deliberately emphasized the public procession while leaving the religious meaning of the coronation ambiguous.

[31:20] **Shakespeare and the Meaning of Anointing**

[事实] The hosts discuss how Shakespeare’s history plays repeatedly explore whether anointing makes a monarch sacred.
[事实] Richard II expresses the idea that sacred balm cannot be washed from an anointed king, yet he is deposed.
[事实] Henry V presents another idea: that crown, mace, robes, swords, and sceptres matter less than the person of the king.
[推测] Shakespeare captures a wider tension between sacred monarchy and character-based kingship in post-Reformation England.

[33:40] **James I, Security, Plague, and Protestant Settlement**

[事实] James VI of Scotland became James I of England in 1603 and arrived with security concerns about how English crowds would receive a Scottish king.
[事实] Soldiers were paid to guard against disorder in Westminster.
[事实] A plague outbreak curtailed the procession, though crowds still gathered.
[事实] The coronation service was translated into English and adjusted to remove material considered too Catholic, while James took the oath in Latin, English, and French.
[事实] Anne of Denmark refused communion, joining a pattern of Catholic or Catholic-leaning queens who resisted Protestant coronation ritual.

[36:20] **Charles I and Retrospective Omens**

[事实] Henrietta Maria refused to participate in Charles I’s Protestant coronation and watched from a window.
[事实] Later accounts treated details of Charles I’s coronation as ominous after his execution in 1649.
[事实] Stories included a broken golden dove, an awkward sermon text, and Charles wearing white satin.
[推测] These omens are presented as examples of later writers reading tragedy backward into the coronation.

[38:12] **Cromwell’s Quasi-Coronation**

[事实] Oliver Cromwell’s second investiture as Lord Protector in 1657 took place in Westminster Hall.
[事实] He wore robes, received symbols such as a Bible, sceptre, robe, and sword, and swore an oath resembling a coronation oath.
[事实] Heralds and crowds acclaimed him as Lord Protector.
[推测] The ceremony shows that even a republic or protectorate borrowed monarchical forms when it needed public legitimacy.

[40:00] **Charles II’s Restoration Coronation**

[事实] Charles II had already been crowned in Scotland in 1651 and was crowned again after the Restoration in 1661.
[事实] Samuel Pepys described the coronation but struggled to see or hear much because of the crowd and noise.
[事实] Charles II used the occasion to announce a general pardon for Civil War conduct.
[事实] Pepys later watched the banquet, received food from acquaintances, went drinking, and recorded a drunken end to the day.

[43:48] **James II and the Road to William and Mary**

[事实] James II had both a private Catholic coronation and a public Westminster Abbey coronation.
[事实] The hosts mention lavish records of his coronation feast, including French-style dishes.
[事实] James II’s reign ended with his replacement by his daughter Mary and her husband William.
[推测] The discussion treats James’s Catholicism and perceived absolutism as central to the political crisis that followed.

[45:00] **William and Mary and the Glorious Revolution Oath**

[事实] William III and Mary II were crowned as joint monarchs, an unusual arrangement in English history.
[事实] Their coronation had to be organized quickly to establish legitimacy after the Glorious Revolution.
[事实] Mary used a less prestigious crown because time and resources were limited.
[事实] Their coronation oath required defense of the Protestant reformed religion established by law.
[事实] The hosts connect this oath directly to the one inherited by Charles III.

[46:45] **Queen Anne and the Royal Touch**

[事实] Queen Anne was crowned in 1702 and had to be carried into the ceremony.
[事实] She wore elaborate clothing, jewels, and a special crown described as covered with diamonds.
[事实] The hosts discuss the royal touch for scrofula as a sign of sacral monarchy.
[事实] The future Dr. Johnson was reportedly touched by Anne as a child and wore the touch piece for the rest of his life.
[事实] Charles II is said to have touched 92,000 people for the king’s evil.

[49:25] **Closing Reflections and Next Episode**

[事实] The hosts choose coronations they would most like to have attended, including Edwy’s, Richard II’s, Cromwell’s investiture, and Charles II’s Restoration celebrations.
[事实] They preview a following episode covering the Hanoverian Georges, later coronation mishaps, Elizabeth II’s 1953 coronation, and the new Carolean age.
[推测] The ending reinforces the episode’s lighter style: serious historical argument is carried through anecdotes, jokes, and vivid ceremonial detail.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s value lies in showing coronations as political technology rather than mere pageantry. The strongest through-line is that ritual becomes most elaborate when legitimacy is weakest.

[推测] Its main strength is the range of examples: scandalous Anglo-Saxon stories, Norman violence, medieval holy oil, Tudor religious reversals, Shakespearean kingship, Cromwell’s quasi-coronation, and the Glorious Revolution oath all connect to the same theme.

[推测] The limitation is that the episode moves quickly and often relies on colorful anecdotes, some of which the hosts themselves flag as biased or retrospective. It is best suited to listeners who enjoy British political and religious history told through ceremony, symbolism, and comic detail.
