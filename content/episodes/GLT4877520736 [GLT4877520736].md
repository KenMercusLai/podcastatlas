# The Troubles: Northern Ireland Before the Storm

## 概览

This episode sets up a new series on the early years of the Troubles by arguing that the violence of the late 1960s and early 1970s did not erupt from nowhere. The hosts trace the roots of the conflict through partition, the creation of Northern Ireland, entrenched Protestant unionist control, Catholic exclusion, and the long afterlife of earlier Irish conflicts.

The discussion stresses that Northern Ireland was built with a two-thirds Protestant majority but also contained a large Catholic minority that often experienced the state as hostile, discriminatory, and politically closed. At the same time, many Protestants felt besieged on a Catholic-majority island and deeply suspicious of Dublin, London, and Catholics within Northern Ireland.

The episode then moves into the 1960s, focusing on Terence O’Neill’s attempt to modernize Northern Ireland and Ian Paisley’s rise as a charismatic anti-Catholic Protestant hardliner. It ends with the emergence of loyalist paramilitary violence in 1966, including early killings linked to the UVF, and frames these events as a prelude to the civil rights marches, street fighting, British troops, and IRA resurgence to come.

## 分段落总结

[00:11] **Ian Paisley as the Voice of Hardline Protestant Ulster**

[事实] The episode opens with a dramatic reading from Ian Paisley’s 1965 sermon “Why I Am a Protestant,” presenting him as a central voice of anti-Catholic Ulster Protestant fundamentalism.

[事实] The hosts describe Paisley as invoking Reformation-era anti-Roman Catholic language in the middle of the supposedly modern, “groovy” 1960s.

[推测] The opening uses Paisley to signal that the Troubles cannot be understood only as modern politics; older religious and historical identities remained active political forces.

[04:15] **The Troubles as a Dark Chapter in British and Irish History**

[事实] The hosts define the Troubles as a conflict that began with sectarian rioting and developed into a low-level civil war involving paramilitary groups, the British Army, and bombing campaigns in Ireland and Britain.

[事实] They state that thousands were killed, including almost 2,000 innocent civilians, and that at the peak in the early 1970s almost 500 people were being killed every year.

[事实] The series will focus on the first years of the Troubles and how the violence reached its peak by the end of 1972.

[推测] The hosts are explicitly trying to avoid a one-sided account by acknowledging both their British perspective and the existence of serious historical scholarship from different backgrounds.

[07:11] **Where to Begin the Story**

[事实] The hosts say that choosing a starting point is itself political: a British account might begin in the 1960s, while Irish nationalists might begin with the Normans, Elizabeth I, Cromwell, or the Plantation of Ulster.

[事实] They choose to begin around the early 20th century, where their previous Irish series ended.

[推测] This framing suggests that the episode wants enough historical depth to explain the conflict without attempting a complete history of Ireland.

[08:04] **Home Rule, Unionism, and Armed Precedent**

[事实] In 1900, Ireland had about 4.5 million people and was part of the United Kingdom; roughly three quarters were Catholic and one quarter Protestant, with Protestants concentrated in the northeast.

[事实] Nationalists, mostly Catholic, wanted home rule or independence, while unionists, typically Protestant, wanted to remain in the United Kingdom.

[事实] In 1912, nearly half a million unionists led by Edward Carson signed a covenant pledging to resist home rule, and around 100,000 organized into paramilitary volunteers.

[推测] The covenant and armed mobilization created a model later paramilitaries could present as heroic precedent rather than criminal violence.

[10:00] **Two Rival Memories: Somme and Easter Rising**

[事实] Many Ulster volunteers fought in the First World War, especially at Gallipoli and the Somme, while in 1916 Irish nationalists launched the Easter Rising in Dublin.

[事实] The hosts contrast Protestant murals around the Shankill Road commemorating the Somme with Catholic murals around the Falls Road commemorating the Easter Rising.

[推测] These rival public memories show how the same historical period became two incompatible identity stories in Belfast.

[11:35] **Partition and the Creation of Northern Ireland**

[事实] The 1922 treaty allowed Northern Ireland to opt out of the Irish Free State and remain in the United Kingdom.

[事实] Northern Ireland consisted of six counties, had its own parliament at Stormont, and had a two-thirds Protestant majority.

[事实] The hosts note that calling Northern Ireland “Ulster” is politically sensitive because it includes only six of Ulster’s nine historic counties.

[推测] The naming dispute reflects the broader point that almost every geographical term in this history carries political meaning.

[14:00] **Partition as Inevitable but Unstable**

[事实] The hosts say nationalists regarded partition as the original sin of modern Irish history.

[事实] They also say many historians would regard partition as probably inevitable because armed Ulster unionists would not accept rule from a united Ireland without civil war.

[事实] Nationalists initially hoped Northern Ireland would eventually wither away and join a united island, but it proved more resilient than expected.

[推测] Partition solved one immediate crisis while preserving a structural conflict inside the new state.

[15:02] **A State with a Built-In Catholic Minority**

[事实] Northern Ireland’s unionist leaders chose six counties that gave them a two-thirds Protestant majority, leaving roughly one-third of the population Catholic.

[事实] Between summer 1920 and summer 1922, more than 500 people were killed in violence, most of them Catholic civilians; thousands of Catholics were driven from jobs and homes.

[事实] Some Catholics later involved in the IRA had family memories of violence from 1920 and 1921.

[推测] Early violence gave many Catholics reasons to see the state as unsafe from its beginning.

[16:23] **History as Living Context**

[事实] The hosts argue that history in Northern Ireland was woven into daily life through stories, parades, marches, and commemorations of older battles.

[事实] They contrast this with politicians in London, who increasingly misunderstood or ignored Irish history as the Irish question receded from British politics.

[推测] The gap between local historical memory and British indifference made later crises harder for London to interpret.

[19:34] **Protestant Siege Mentality**

[事实] The hosts say Protestants were a majority in Northern Ireland but a minority on the island of Ireland, and many felt surrounded by Catholics.

[事实] They feared the Republic of Ireland wanted to undermine or destroy Northern Ireland and regarded Catholics inside Northern Ireland as a potential fifth column.

[事实] Concerns about Catholic birth rates and demographic change existed through the 20th century.

[推测] This siege mentality helped sustain slogans such as “no surrender” and made compromise appear threatening to many unionists.

[21:35] **Interwar Violence and the IRA’s Persistence**

[事实] The hosts reject the idea that nothing happened between the 1920s and the 1960s, citing major Belfast rioting in 1935 with deaths, injuries, and people driven from homes.

[事实] The IRA did not disappear after the civil war; a few hundred active members remained capable of violence.

[事实] In 1939, the IRA launched the S-Plan bombing campaign in Britain, including attacks in London, Birmingham, Bristol, Liverpool, Blackpool, and Coventry.

[事实] During the Second World War, both British and Irish governments cracked down on the IRA, and Ireland executed six IRA men.

[25:10] **A Protestant State and Its Security Apparatus**

[事实] Northern Ireland’s first prime minister, James Craig, openly described the polity as a Protestant parliament and Protestant state.

[事实] Judges, magistrates, civil servants, the RUC, and the B Specials were overwhelmingly Protestant.

[事实] The RUC had wide powers, including searching, arresting, internment without trial, curfews, march bans, and newspaper bans.

[推测] The state’s security structure made Catholic distrust of policing and justice predictable rather than incidental.

[27:51] **Orange Order Power and Catholic Second-Class Status**

[事实] The Orange Order was founded in 1795 to defend Protestantism and the union with Britain.

[事实] The hosts say almost every B Special and almost every unionist MP belonged to an Orange lodge.

[事实] A 1967 Times investigation described Catholics in Northern Ireland as second-class citizens.

[事实] Catholics were disadvantaged in employment, with Roman Catholics twice as likely to be unemployed as Protestants.

[29:59] **Voting, Housing, and Gerrymandering**

[事实] Northern Ireland used first-past-the-post elections and boundaries were gerrymandered to maintain unionist power at Stormont.

[事实] At the local level, voting depended on being a ratepayer or spouse of a ratepayer, which excluded many poorer Catholics.

[事实] Housing allocation mattered politically because giving a couple a house could also give them votes.

[事实] Unionist councils were reluctant to build or allocate houses in Catholic areas, while the hosts note that nationalist councils could also favor Catholics.

[32:32] **Derry as an Emblem of Discrimination**

[事实] Derry had a two-to-one Catholic majority around 1965 but was controlled by Protestants through ward boundary manipulation.

[事实] The hosts say almost every Protestant family wanting a house in Derry received one, while about 2,000 Catholic families were on the housing waiting list.

[事实] They identify Derry as the place where the Troubles would soon kick off in earnest.

[推测] Derry’s combination of Catholic majority, Protestant control, and housing inequality made it especially combustible.

[34:11] **Limited Options for Young Catholics**

[事实] The hosts ask what hope a young Catholic in early-1960s Northern Ireland had if he lacked housing, a local vote, and possibly work.

[事实] The Northern Irish Nationalist Party was small, ineffective, and won only nine of 52 Stormont seats in 1962 and 1965.

[事实] The IRA’s 1956 border campaign, Operation Harvest, had failed, and both Belfast and Dublin had used internment to break the organization.

[事实] By 1962, the IRA had called off the campaign and dumped its weapons.

[36:22] **Cathal Goulding and the IRA’s Marxist Turn**

[事实] Cathal Goulding became IRA chief of staff and argued that the idea of the IRA rising to free Ireland was unrealistic.

[事实] He was influenced by Marxist academics and British Communist Party figures, who encouraged a turn toward socialist activism and non-sectarian workers’ politics.

[事实] Older, culturally conservative IRA veterans disliked this turn and expected a future showdown.

[推测] The IRA’s quietness in the mid-1960s helped create space for loyalist fears to focus on an enemy that was present but not yet actively campaigning.

[38:36] **Dublin’s Formal Claim and Practical Inaction**

[事实] The Irish constitution claimed the national territory consisted of the whole island of Ireland.

[事实] The hosts say Dublin governments had done little in practice to advance unification since the 1920s.

[事实] During the Second World War, Britain twice suggested a united Ireland if Dublin abandoned neutrality and joined the Allies, but de Valera did not accept.

[事实] Sean Lemass privately accepted that partition would continue for a long time.

[40:53] **London’s Indifference**

[事实] Some Labour MPs and newspapers campaigned or reported on discrimination against Catholics in Northern Ireland.

[事实] Most British politicians assumed the Irish question had been solved by giving Northern Ireland its own parliament.

[事实] Northern Ireland rarely appeared in British newspapers or television news, and many MPs knew very little about it.

[推测] British neglect allowed discriminatory structures to persist until crisis made them impossible to ignore.

[44:49] **Optimism Before the Breakdown**

[事实] After the break, the hosts quote a 1965 Times view that modernization and the welfare state gave Northern Ireland grounds for optimism.

[事实] They contrast that optimism with what would soon follow: bombs, British soldiers on the streets, hundreds killed each year, and violence spreading to Britain.

[推测] The episode uses the 1965 optimism to show how badly many observers misread the pressures building inside Northern Ireland.

[46:14] **Terence O’Neill and Economic Modernization**

[事实] Terence O’Neill became Northern Ireland’s fourth prime minister on 25 March 1963.

[事实] He saw Northern Ireland’s main challenge as economic, especially the decline of textiles and shipbuilding, higher unemployment, and lower earnings than in Britain.

[事实] O’Neill sought foreign investment from companies such as Michelin, Ford, and ICI.

[事实] Much of this investment favored Belfast and the east, while more Catholic western areas felt left behind.

[50:11] **O’Neill’s Political Weakness**

[事实] The hosts describe O’Neill as an Eton-educated, London-raised unionist with an English accent and an aloof manner.

[事实] Working-class conservative Protestants often saw him as soft, stuck-up, and detached from their concerns.

[事实] David Trimble later said O’Neill never spoke or behaved as if he was “one of us.”

[推测] O’Neill’s modernization agenda may have required political skills he did not possess.

[52:04] **The Lemass Visit and Paisley’s Opportunity**

[事实] In 1965, O’Neill became the first Northern Irish prime minister to welcome the Taoiseach of the Republic of Ireland, Sean Lemass.

[事实] O’Neill framed the visit as bridge-building with the Republic.

[事实] Ian Paisley attacked the visit and used it to portray O’Neill as a traitor to Protestant Ulster.

[推测] The Lemass visit gave Paisley a clear symbol around which to mobilize distrust of compromise.

[52:47] **Ian Paisley’s Religious and Public Persona**

[事实] Ian Paisley was born in Armagh in 1926, the son of a Baptist minister who had served in Carson’s volunteers.

[事实] Paisley preached his first sermon at 16 and founded the Free Presbyterian Church of Ulster in 1951.

[事实] He believed in the literal truth of the Bible and regarded mainstream Ulster churches as weak toward Rome.

[事实] He later had links with Bob Jones University in South Carolina.

[56:14] **Anti-Catholic Rhetoric and Charisma**

[事实] Paisley’s rhetoric was intensely anti-Catholic, and his Protestant Telegraph published lurid anti-Catholic headlines.

[事实] The hosts emphasize that Paisley was also a powerful speaker whose biblical language and performance impressed listeners.

[事实] Liam Neeson, who grew up Catholic in County Antrim, later said Paisley’s performances helped inspire him to become an actor.

[推测] Paisley’s appeal lay not only in his message but in his ability to embody Protestant fear, anger, and historical memory.

[60:10] **Publicity Stunts and Defiance**

[事实] Paisley gained attention in 1956 by helping a 15-year-old Catholic girl who wanted to join his church leave for Scotland against her parents’ wishes.

[事实] He denounced royal visits to the Pope and attacked O’Neill’s decision to lower the Union Jack after Pope John XXIII died.

[事实] In later life, he disrupted Pope John Paul II’s address to the European Parliament.

[推测] Paisley understood that spectacle and provocation could turn religious protest into political power.

[63:14] **The 1964 Tricolor Riot**

[事实] In the 1964 Westminster election, independent Republican candidate Liam McMillen displayed an Irish tricolor in his West Belfast campaign office.

[事实] Under pressure from Paisley, the RUC removed the flag on 28 September, triggering major rioting.

[事实] The riot involved stone-throwing, petrol bombs, a burned trolley bus, armored cars, water cannons, and dozens hospitalized.

[事实] The incident gave Paisley publicity and helped him present himself as a defender of Protestant liberty.

[65:19] **Paisley Against O’Neill, Easter Rising Memory, and Marches**

[事实] Paisley attacked O’Neill over the Lemass visit with the slogan “no mass, no Lemass.”

[事实] In 1966, O’Neill allowed nationalist commemorations of the 50th anniversary of the Easter Rising.

[事实] Paisley denounced this as support for murder, treason, and sedition.

[推测] The 50th anniversary sharpened the clash between unionist memory of the Somme and nationalist memory of the Easter Rising.

[66:35] **Paisley’s Imprisonment and O’Neill’s Crackdown**

[事实] In June 1966, Paisley led about 1,500 supporters against the mainstream Presbyterian assembly and deliberately took them through a Catholic area.

[事实] Fighting followed, and Paisley refused to sign a pledge to keep the peace, leading to a three-month prison sentence.

[事实] O’Neill banned Protestant and Catholic marches for three months and gave the RUC power to shut down meetings of more than three people.

[事实] O’Neill denounced Paisley’s claim to loyalism, but the hosts say O’Neill was losing momentum while Paisley was gaining it.

[68:23] **Paisley’s Political Networks and Loyalist Mobilization**

[事实] Paisley’s political wing, Ulster Protestant Action, recruited more people amid economic anxiety and sectarian tension.

[事实] Noel Doherty, a Paisley protégé, founded the Ulster Protestant Volunteers in April 1966.

[事实] The group evoked Carson’s 1912 volunteers, used the motto “For God and Ulster,” and recruited among B Specials because they had access to weapons.

[推测] Loyalist organizing combined fear of the IRA, economic insecurity, religious identity, and the prestige of earlier armed unionism.

[71:00] **Gusty Spence and the UVF**

[事实] The Ulster Volunteer Force was founded by men meeting in pubs in the working-class Protestant Shankill area.

[事实] Gusty Spence, a former British Army soldier and Harland and Wolff scaffolder, became a key early figure.

[事实] Spence’s father had been in Carson’s volunteers and served in the First World War.

[推测] The hosts present Spence and similar men as drawn to a romanticized tradition of armed action and cause-driven violence.

[72:57] **Violence as a Choice**

[事实] The hosts argue there was nothing inevitable about the Troubles and that people chose or willed violence into existence.

[事实] They link paramilitary appeal to unemployment, disappointment, fear of the other community, songs, stories, and inherited heroic narratives.

[推测] The episode’s moral argument is that historical grievance mattered, but individuals and groups still made decisions that escalated conflict.

[74:12] **The First Loyalist Killings of 1966**

[事实] In May 1966, Spence and associates were already petrol-bombing Catholic schools and shops.

[事实] On 7 May, they targeted what they thought was a Catholic-owned pub but hit the house next door, fatally injuring 77-year-old Protestant grandmother Matilda Gould.

[事实] Two weeks later, the UVF declared war on the IRA and warned authorities against appeasement.

[事实] On 27 May, UVF men failed to find a Republican activist and instead attacked John Patrick Scullion, who later died in hospital.

[76:47] **Peter Ward and the Malvern Arms Shooting**

[事实] On 25 June 1966, four young Catholic men went for a drink at the Malvern Arms, a Protestant pub near the Shankill Road.

[事实] Gusty Spence and his gang were also there and wrongly identified them as IRA men.

[事实] When the four men left, gunmen opened fire; 18-year-old Peter Ward was killed and Andrew Kelly was wounded.

[事实] Spence and two others were sent to prison.

[78:30] **Paisley’s Distance from Violence and the Politics of Denial**

[事实] Paisley condemned shootings of this kind while continuing to use highly inflammatory rhetoric.

[事实] The hosts say many figures in the Troubles publicly denied supporting violence while behaving in ways that encouraged it.

[推测] Dominic’s personal judgment is that there was considerable cynicism among leaders who disavowed violence while benefiting politically from escalation.

[80:34] **O’Neill’s Warning and the Road Ahead**

[事实] Most people in Britain paid little attention to the 1966 killings because the public mood was focused on Swinging London and England’s World Cup win.

[事实] O’Neill returned from France to denounce the UVF as criminals and warned Northern Ireland must choose progress or a return to pointless violence.

[事实] The Belfast Telegraph was less optimistic than the London press and warned that Ulster could be thrown back into a dark past.

[事实] The next episode will cover the 1968 civil rights marches, fighting in Derry, British troops, and the IRA’s re-emergence.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its layered explanation of the Troubles’ origins. It does not treat the conflict as spontaneous irrational hatred, but as the result of partition, institutional discrimination, Protestant insecurity, British neglect, Irish inaction, economic change, and charismatic agitators.

[推测] A major strength is the way the hosts connect big constitutional history with concrete local grievances: voting rules, housing allocation, policing powers, Derry’s ward boundaries, and the symbolism of flags and marches. This makes the political background easier to understand without reducing it to abstract sectarianism.

[推测] The episode’s limitation is that it is primarily a setup episode. It ends before the civil rights marches and before the IRA’s major re-emergence, so listeners looking for the familiar peak years of the Troubles will need the next installment.

[推测] This episode is especially useful for listeners who know the names Paisley, IRA, UVF, or Bloody Sunday but want to understand the earlier structures and choices that made the later explosion of violence possible.
