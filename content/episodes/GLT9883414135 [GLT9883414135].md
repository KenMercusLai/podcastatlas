# The Second Ku Klux Klan: Film, Fraternalism, and Fear

## 概览

This episode explains how the Second Ku Klux Klan, founded in Georgia in 1915, differed from the Reconstruction-era Klan. It borrowed robes, hoods, and white supremacist symbolism, but became a public mass-membership fraternal organization shaped by advertising, entertainment, Protestant respectability, and modern sales techniques.

The central argument is that the Second Klan’s power came from combining “100% Americanism” with anxieties about Catholics, Jews, immigrants, Prohibition, sexuality, and social change. Its appeal was not limited to marginal figures: the speakers stress its strength among respectable white Protestant middle-class Americans, clergy, businessmen, and local officials.

The episode moves from Thomas Dixon’s racist fiction and *The Birth of a Nation* to William Joseph Simmons’s relaunch of the Klan, then to the marketing operation run by Edward Young Clark and Elizabeth Tyler. It closes with the Klan’s rapid growth, political influence in Oregon and Texas, and the violence hidden behind its public image.

## 分段落总结

[00:13] **Opening Creed and Frame**

[事实] The episode opens with a reading from the Klan’s 1922 creed, which explicitly invokes Christianity, Americanism, racial hierarchy, and white supremacy.

[事实] The hosts use the creed’s pseudo-archaic language to introduce the strangeness of the Second Klan’s ritual world.

[02:11] **The Second Klan as a Different Organization**

[事实] The Second Klan was born in Georgia in 1915 and is described as a different movement from the Reconstruction-era Klan, though both were racist.

[事实] It was a public fraternal association with between two and five million members, strongest in the North and Midwest rather than only the South.

[事实] Its main targets were often white Catholics, Jews, immigrants, and bootleggers, not only African Americans.

[07:17] **Thomas Dixon and Lost Cause Fiction**

[事实] Thomas Dixon, born in North Carolina in 1864, grew up in a family connected to the original Klan and later became a lecturer and novelist.

[事实] His novels promoted a racist Lost Cause view of Reconstruction, especially *The Leopard’s Spots* and *The Clansman*.

[事实] *The Clansman* was popular but controversial, and its stage version was linked in reports to public disorder and racial violence.

[13:01] **The Birth of a Nation**

[事实] D.W. Griffith adapted Dixon’s material into *The Birth of a Nation*, released in February 1915.

[事实] The film is described as technically foundational for cinema while also intensely racist and central to popularizing a heroic image of the Klan.

[事实] Civil rights groups including the NAACP protested it, but it became a huge commercial success and was shown at the White House under Woodrow Wilson.

[18:43] **William Joseph Simmons**

[事实] William Joseph Simmons came from Alabama, had failed as a Methodist preacher, and became deeply involved in American fraternal organizations.

[事实] He belonged to many clubs, including the Masons and the Woodmen of the World, and used fraternal culture as the template for the revived Klan.

[推测] Simmons’s Klan project is presented less as a coherent ideology at first than as a mixture of nostalgia, fantasy, status-seeking, and money-making.

[24:00] **Stone Mountain Relaunch**

[事实] Simmons launched the new Klan on Thanksgiving Eve at Stone Mountain near Atlanta, using a burning cross ceremony.

[事实] The hosts emphasize that cross-burning was not original to the first Klan but came through Dixon’s fiction and Griffith’s film.

[事实] Early recruitment was weak, with only a small number of members before the First World War and Red Scare created more favorable conditions.

[29:24] **Clark, Tyler, and Salesmanship**

[事实] Simmons hired Edward Young Clark and Elizabeth Tyler of the Southern Publicity Association to market the Klan.

[事实] Their deal gave them and their recruitment network 80 cents of every dollar, while Simmons received 20 cents.

[事实] They divided the country into sales regions, used invented titles, recruited salesmen called Klegels, and turned membership into a pyramid-style recruitment business.

[35:25] **Rituals, Costumes, and Community**

[事实] New members paid a fee, underwent a ritual before a burning cross, swore allegiance to the flag, Constitution, public schools, and white supremacy, and bought official robes at a markup.

[事实] Klan meetings mixed ritual language, Protestant hymns, networking, and status titles.

[推测] The appeal lay partly in giving ordinary local men a sense of belonging, drama, and importance.

[40:10] **“100% Americanism” and Enemies Within**

[事实] Clark and Tyler framed the Klan as defending “100% Americanism” against sinister internal enemies.

[事实] The hosts connect the Klan’s growth to early-1920s anxieties about cultural, political, religious, and social change.

[推测] The Klan’s marketing worked by identifying local resentments and turning them into a national moral crusade.

[41:00] **Masons, Methodists, and Respectability**

[事实] Klan recruiters often approached Masonic networks first, and early meetings were sometimes held in Masonic or other fraternal halls.

[事实] Methodist ministers were a major constituency, and some were offered free membership or local religious roles in the Klan.

[事实] The hosts stress that many members were respectable middle-class professionals rather than only socially marginal people.

[47:24] **Race, Jews, and Catholics**

[事实] The Second Klan remained committed to white supremacy, but the episode says anti-Black hatred was not always its dominant organizing theme.

[事实] The hosts say the Klan was not involved in the Tulsa massacre and note that the Second Klan was strongest in many states with small Black populations.

[事实] The episode identifies Jews and especially Catholics as central targets of Klan rhetoric, with Catholics portrayed as alien, anti-American, and linked to immigration and religious conspiracy.

[52:23] **Schools and Protestant Americanism**

[事实] The Klan defended public schools because it opposed Catholic schools and wanted children assimilated into a Protestant definition of American identity.

[事实] The hosts discuss anti-Catholic claims that drew on very old Protestant conspiracy traditions.

[推测] The school issue shows how the Klan could present intolerance as civic reform and patriotism.

[53:47] **Prohibition as a Core Issue**

[事实] The episode argues that Prohibition was crucial to the Klan’s success.

[事实] Temperance organizations and the Klan shared many constituencies, especially Methodists, Baptists, and other Protestant groups.

[事实] Weak enforcement of the Volstead Act gave the Klan an opening to portray itself as a defender of law, morality, and the Constitution.

[56:48] **Moral Vigilantism**

[事实] Klan members joined or imitated law-enforcement campaigns against bootleggers, speakeasies, and alleged moral offenders.

[事实] The episode describes attacks on accused abortion providers, alleged adulterers, negligent parents, and unfaithful spouses.

[事实] In Mer Rouge, Louisiana, Klansmen kidnapped five men, tortured and murdered two, and local officials were implicated, but no convictions followed.

[59:13] **Exposure and Congressional Hearings**

[事实] By 1921 the Klan had grown rapidly, and its leaders had become wealthy from recruitment.

[事实] The *New York World* published a major exposé of Klan violence, and Congress held hearings on the organization.

[事实] Simmons defended the Klan theatrically, the committee found no case to answer, and the publicity appears to have helped recruitment rather than destroying the movement.

[62:38] **Political Breakthroughs in Oregon and Texas**

[事实] In 1922 the Klan endorsed candidates and claimed influence over many congressional races.

[事实] In Oregon, Klan-backed candidates won major offices, and a referendum banning Catholic schools passed before later being struck down by courts.

[事实] In Texas, the Klan recruited through Confederate memory, evangelical churches, and respectable networks, reaching around 200,000 members by 1922.

[66:52] **Texas Respectability and Violence**

[事实] The Texas Klan both raised money for civic causes such as Hope Cottage in Dallas and engaged in severe vigilante violence.

[事实] The episode describes kidnappings, floggings, branding, attacks on lawyers representing Black clients, and organized whipping sites in Dallas.

[推测] Texas illustrates the episode’s central tension: public charity and local respectability could coexist with terror and coercion.

[68:17] **Goose Creek**

[事实] In Goose Creek, Texas, Klansmen attacked Mrs. Harrison and Mr. Armand after entering her home and dragging them away.

[事实] Investigators later found that Klan vigilantes had terrorized the town for about 18 months, attacking at least 20 people over alleged immorality or opposition to the Klan.

[推测] The case suggests that Klan power depended partly on silence, fear, and sympathy or complicity among local authorities.

[70:33] **Closing Question**

[事实] The episode ends by asking which face of the Klan was more revealing: respectable civic organization or violent reign of terror.

[事实] It sets up the next episode with the Klan still growing in 1923 and preparing to influence the 1924 presidential election.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its clear distinction between the first and second Klans. It shows the Second Klan not simply as a survival of Civil War violence, but as a modern mass movement built through film, branding, sales networks, churches, clubs, and politics.

[推测] Its strongest section is the explanation of how fantasy became reality: Dixon’s fiction and Griffith’s film supplied symbols that Simmons and his marketers turned into rituals, recruitment tools, and public spectacle.

[推测] The limitation is that the episode is the middle part of a larger series, so the fall of the Second Klan and the promised 1924 political climax are mostly deferred.

[推测] It is especially useful for listeners interested in 1920s America, political extremism, religious nationalism, media influence, moral panics, and how respectable institutions can provide cover for violent movements.
