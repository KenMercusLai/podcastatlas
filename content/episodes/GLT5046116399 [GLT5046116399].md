# Evita: Illness, Martyrdom, and the Politics of Mourning

## 概览

This episode follows Eva Perón’s final years, focusing on how her illness became inseparable from Peronist politics, public devotion, and national melodrama. The hosts frame Evita not simply as a dying political figure, but as the center of a cult that fused Catholic imagery, populist sentiment, and theatrical self-sacrifice.

The discussion traces her refusal or avoidance of treatment, the pressure around the 1951 vice-presidential nomination, and the huge public rallies that turned her suffering into a mass political spectacle. Her body, both living and dying, is repeatedly described as a symbol onto which Argentines projected hopes, grief, resentment, and ideological conflict.

The episode ends with her death in July 1952, the extraordinary public mourning that followed, and the beginning of the bizarre story of her embalmed body, which the hosts preview as the subject of the next episode.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **Evita as Sacred Figure**

[事实] The episode opens with a 1951 poem by Peronist writer José María Castiñeira de Dios that links Eva Perón with Maria and casts her as hope, faith, and national consolation.

[事实] The hosts describe the poem as strange and excessive, while noting that its Catholic and Marian imagery reflects the established cult of Evita by 1951.

[推测] The poem shows how Evita’s image had already moved beyond ordinary politics into a quasi-religious register before her death.

[02:42] **The Cult Before Death**

[事实] The hosts explain that “Evita” is an affectionate diminutive meaning something like “dear little Eva.”

[事实] By 1951, supporters were calling her names such as “the lady of hope” and “the bridge of love,” and her foundation helped build this image.

[推测] Her later illness had added emotional force because supporters already imagined her as a maternal and protective figure for the nation.

[03:25] **Collapse and Hidden Illness**

[事实] At the beginning of 1950, Evita collapsed after visiting Avellaneda to open a taxi drivers’ union office and then disappeared from newspapers for four days.

[事实] The public explanation was appendicitis, but the hosts say she was probably already suffering from the cancer that would kill her.

[事实] A doctor, Oscar Ivanissevich, later said he examined her and recommended a hysterectomy after identifying cancer of the uterus.

[推测] The official account may have concealed the severity and nature of her illness because cancer, especially gynecological illness, was difficult to discuss publicly in that context.

[05:48] **Refusal of Treatment**

[事实] Evita was suffering hemorrhages and carried cotton wool on tours to manage them.

[事实] The hosts say she refused or resisted medical intervention, even though her mother and Perón’s first wife had undergone similar operations.

[事实] Perón had a skeptical attitude toward doctors and may have encouraged distrust of medical advice, though the hosts say there is no direct evidence for this.

[推测] Her refusal may have combined denial, political self-dramatization, fear, and the symbolic difficulty of accepting a hysterectomy while being cast as “mother of the nation.”

[07:18] **Work, Sacrifice, and Martyrdom**

[事实] After recovering from her 1950 illness, Evita threw herself back into work intensely and said she had no time for medical treatment.

[事实] She framed treatment as something for “oligarchs,” giving her illness a political meaning.

[事实] Her biographers Fraser and Navarro are quoted as saying the least outrageous distortion around her life is that she chose to die for Perón and Peronism.

[推测] The hosts suggest her public persona, shaped by soap opera melodrama and ideas of sacrifice, made self-destruction politically legible to her supporters.

[08:39] **Peronism Under Pressure**

[事实] By the early 1950s, Argentina’s economic conditions were worsening: export returns were falling, prices were rising, and unions were becoming restless.

[事实] A railway strike created an unusual conflict between Perón’s government and organized labor.

[事实] Perón had changed the constitution and faced an election scheduled for November 1951.

[推测] The mounting political pressure made Evita’s role more important as a source of emotional legitimacy for the regime.

[09:15] **The Vice-Presidential Question**

[事实] Evita’s allies in the CGT trade union movement and the women’s movement pushed for her to be Perón’s running mate.

[事实] The hosts note that no woman had been vice president in any country at that point, and Evita had not even been able to vote in the previous election.

[事实] By the time the campaign for her nomination grew, she was visibly ill, thin, and suffering severe blood loss.

[推测] Her physical decline may have strengthened her symbolic authority among supporters who saw her as embodying suffering.

[10:51] **Army Resistance**

[事实] The army opposed the idea of Evita becoming vice president and had long been wary of her influence.

[事实] The hosts say Perón could not afford to alienate the army because it was the institution most capable of overthrowing him.

[事实] Perón allowed the factions beneath him to struggle rather than immediately deciding whether Evita would be nominated.

[推测] Perón’s hesitation may have been a political tactic to balance his popular base against the military.

[13:22] **Cabildo Abierto**

[事实] The CGT organized a vast public meeting called the Cabildo Abierto to pressure for Evita’s nomination.

[事实] The name evoked the 1810 gathering in Buenos Aires associated with throwing off Spanish rule.

[事实] An estimated two million people were brought into central Buenos Aires, with transport, food, hotels, and even cinema tickets organized for union members.

[推测] The event fused labor politics, nationalism, mass spectacle, and Peronist emotional theater.

[15:22] **The Balcony Performance**

[事实] On 22 August 1951, Perón and Evita appeared before the huge crowd on Avenida 9 de Julio.

[事实] Evita was dressed austerely, appeared gaunt and ill, and had tears in her eyes.

[事实] She gave rambling speeches saying she wanted only to be Evita, while the crowd demanded that she accept the vice-presidential role.

[推测] The hosts interpret the moment as a situation where Perón and Evita had created a public appetite for melodrama that they could no longer fully control.

[18:48] **Renunciation**

[事实] On 31 August 1951, Evita went on the radio and announced that she would not accept the vice-presidential nomination.

[事实] The hosts say this refusal occupies a major place in the Evita Museum in Buenos Aires and is treated there as a moment of self-sacrifice.

[事实] Some Peronists believed Perón had yielded to pressure from the army.

[推测] The emotional power of the renunciation came less from a clear political argument than from supporters’ investment in Evita’s sacrificial story.

[22:00] **Tests, Coup, and Public Illness**

[事实] After the Cabildo Abierto, Evita fainted and then suffered severe abdominal pain, sometimes being unable to get out of bed.

[事实] On 24 September, doctors told Perón she had advanced cancer and needed an operation.

[事实] While she was being treated, Benjamin Menéndez led a disorganized coup attempt against Perón, which was defeated.

[事实] Evita then spoke publicly about her ill health for the first time and prayed for health so she could support Perón and the descamisados.

[24:14] **Religious Mobilization**

[事实] After Evita’s illness became public, Argentines held masses, candlelit vigils, and pilgrimages for her health.

[事实] Some sick people reportedly made pilgrimages to Luján, Argentina’s major Marian shrine, carrying images and inscriptions asking for Evita’s health.

[事实] Street altars with candles and religious images appeared for her.

[推测] The political energy around the failed vice-presidential nomination was redirected into a religious campaign to save her life.

[26:44] **The Last Loyalty Rally**

[事实] At the 17 October loyalty rally, around one and a half million people gathered, and Evita appeared after being given morphine.

[事实] Perón paid tribute to her while she stood beside him, and she broke down crying in his arms.

[事实] The hosts say many people were speaking about her as if she were already dead.

[推测] Evita’s final public appearances turned dying itself into a political and theatrical act.

[28:43] **Voting and Decline**

[事实] In the November 1951 election, Evita voted from hospital using a special ballot box brought to her.

[事实] Writer David Viñas described women outside kneeling, praying, touching, and kissing the ballot box containing her vote.

[事实] Perón won the election.

[推测] The ballot box scene shows how ordinary democratic ritual had become entangled with devotion to Evita as a sacred object.

[29:42] **Darker Final Speeches**

[事实] In early 1952, Evita continued appearing on the balcony of the Casa Rosada despite worsening health and heavy pain medication.

[事实] Her speeches became more violent, including threats to execute justice with Peronists’ own hands and declarations of fanaticism for Perón until death.

[事实] Tom says Evita also ordered pistols and machine guns for trade unions from her sickbed; Dominic says he had not heard that detail.

[推测] Her late rhetoric helped later radicals in the 1960s and 1970s claim a more militant Evita.

[31:52] **Desperate Treatments and Dark Rumors**

[事实] The hosts discuss radiotherapy that was applied incompetently and burned her.

[事实] Tom says a debated story later emerged that an American surgeon performed a lobotomy on Evita, probably authorized by Perón; he says the balance of opinion may support that it happened.

[事实] Rumors circulated in wealthy Buenos Aires neighborhoods that children’s blood was being taken to keep Evita alive.

[推测] The rumors show how hatred of Evita imagined her not as a saintly sufferer but as a vampiric figure sustained by others.

[33:47] **National Frenzy Before Death**

[事实] By summer 1952, Argentina saw constant processions, masses, and public displays around Evita.

[事实] Congress named her the spiritual leader of the nation, cities changed their names to Eva Perón, and plans were approved for a huge monument.

[事实] On 20 July, a major public mass was held at the Obelisk in Buenos Aires, but Evita was too ill to attend and listened from bed until the hospital cut the radio connection.

[推测] The state and the public were treating Evita’s approaching death as a national ritual before it had actually happened.

[34:49] **Death of Evita**

[事实] On 26 July 1952, Evita reportedly told her maid that she had never been happy and that she could not have endured an ordinary life.

[事实] She declined over the day, received the last sacraments, and doctors told Perón at 8:23 p.m. that she had died.

[事实] Her brother Juan Duarte screamed that there was no God, while Perón was described as impassive.

[推测] Her reported final reflections fit the episode’s portrait of a person who understood life as struggle, performance, and escape from ordinariness.

[35:31] **Official Mourning Begins**

[事实] The government changed the official time of death from 8:23 to 8:25 so news bulletins could begin exactly five minutes early with five minutes of silence.

[事实] Cinemas, theatres, restaurants, and public transport stopped.

[事实] The silence and mourning rituals continued daily for weeks.

[推测] The regime used timing, media, and shutdowns to turn grief into a disciplined national performance.

[36:17] **Pedro Ara and Embalming**

[事实] Dr. Pedro Ara, an anatomy professor and embalming specialist, was waiting to prepare Evita’s body for public display.

[事实] He had a reputation for replacing blood with glycerin and carried an embalmed head as an example of his work.

[事实] Perón had planned for Evita’s body to be displayed publicly and later preserved permanently, rather like Lenin.

[推测] The treatment of the body continued the episode’s theme that Evita’s political role did not end with death.

[37:49] **The Body and the Crowds**

[事实] Evita’s body was placed in a coffin with a glass lid and an Argentine flag.

[事实] When the body was moved to the Labour Ministry, eight people were killed in the crush and more than 2,000 were taken to hospital with injuries within 24 hours.

[事实] The planned three days of mourning extended by another 13 days because the crowds were so large.

[推测] The mourning became a mass event whose scale exceeded the authorities’ initial plans.

[38:45] **A Bacchanal of Mourning**

[事实] The CGT required its members to wear mourning for a month.

[事实] Around a million or more people may have queued to see Evita’s body, compared by the hosts with the queue for Queen Elizabeth II.

[事实] Every morning included a compulsory 15-minute silence, with passages from Evita’s ghostwritten autobiography read over loudspeakers.

[推测] The hosts present the mourning as both genuine popular grief and a coercive culture of public sentiment.

[40:38] **Decay and Final Procession**

[事实] Condensation formed between Evita’s body and the glass coffin lid, and the body began to decompose before Dr. Ara could complete his work.

[事实] Workers drilled holes in the coffin lid at night to clear the mist, worrying Ara because it disrupted his embalming plan.

[事实] On 9 and 10 August, huge parades carried the coffin through Buenos Aires to the CGT headquarters, where Ara had prepared a secret laboratory.

[推测] The struggle to preserve the “incorruptible” body becomes a physical version of the political effort to preserve Evita’s myth.

[42:02] **Preview of the Next Episode**

[事实] The hosts preview the next episode as covering the extraordinary fate of Evita’s body.

[事实] They mention themes including vampirism, body snatching, terrorism, death squads, and people returning politically from beyond the grave.

[推测] The episode deliberately ends by shifting Evita from historical person into posthumous political myth and gothic spectacle.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its ability to connect medical history, populist politics, Catholic symbolism, and mass emotion without treating Evita’s illness as merely private tragedy. It shows how a leader’s body can become a national and ideological object.

[推测] Its strongest moments are the close readings of public ritual: the Cabildo Abierto, the hospital ballot box, the vigils, and the funeral queues. These scenes make clear why Evita’s story remained politically potent after death.

[推测] The episode’s limitation is that some disputed details, such as the lobotomy and the weapons order, are discussed conversationally rather than resolved with firm evidence. The hosts usually signal uncertainty, but listeners looking for a strictly documented medical account may want additional sources.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in modern political mythology, Latin American history, charismatic populism, and the strange afterlives of political figures.
