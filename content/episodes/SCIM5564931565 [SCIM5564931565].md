# David Eagleman on Neuroplasticity, Memory, Dreams, Time & Polarization

## 概览

This episode of the Huberman Lab Podcast features neuroscientist and science communicator Dr. David Eagleman in a wide-ranging discussion about how the brain changes, learns, remembers, perceives time, constructs dreams, and reacts to social identity.

The central thread is neuroplasticity: the brain is described as a model-building system that rewires itself through experience, challenge, novelty, attention, and practice. Eagleman emphasizes that useful plasticity is not unlimited or random change, but goal-directed change that helps the brain become faster, more efficient, and better adapted.

The conversation moves from individual learning and self-control to broader social behavior. Topics include Ulysses contracts for managing the future self, why fear can make events seem to unfold in slow motion, why memories are unreliable in legal contexts, how sensory substitution reveals the brain’s flexibility, and how in-group/out-group labels can alter empathy and fuel polarization.

## 分段落总结

[00:00] **Episode Frame**

[事实] Huberman introduces Dr. David Eagleman as a neuroscientist, bestselling author, and long-time public educator in science.

[事实] The episode is framed around neuroplasticity, learning and unlearning, memory formation, stress and time perception, dreams, false memories, déjà vu, and cultural or political polarization.

[推测] The episode is designed as both a neuroscience explainer and a practical guide for thinking about learning, habits, memory, and social division.

[03:08] **Neuroplasticity as the Brain’s Core Human Advantage**

[事实] Eagleman defines plasticity as the brain constantly reconfiguring itself through experience, culture, language, neighborhood, and other inputs.

[事实] He argues that DNA is only part of what makes a person; the other major part is the lived world that wires the brain.

[事实] He says humans are distinctive because each generation absorbs prior discoveries early in life and then builds beyond them.

[推测] In this framing, human culture is partly an accumulated product of past neuroplasticity being handed to new brains.

[06:31] **Cortex, Brain Real Estate, and Sensory Remapping**

[事实] Eagleman says humans have much more cortex than nearby species, and he emphasizes cortex broadly rather than only prefrontal cortex.

[事实] He describes cortex as using similar circuitry across regions, with its function shaped by the inputs it receives.

[事实] He cites work in which visual input was routed into auditory cortex and the tissue became visually responsive.

[事实] He explains that in people born blind or deaf, unused cortical territory can be taken over by other senses or cognitive functions.

[13:28] **Practice, Specialization, and Efficient Skill**

[事实] Huberman raises the question of early specialization versus diversified childhood experience.

[事实] Eagleman says repeated practice burns a skill into the brain’s circuitry, making performance faster and more energy efficient.

[事实] He uses elite athletes and chess champions as examples of skills that become highly efficient through extensive training.

[事实] He notes that bilingual or trilingual children may have lower vocabulary in each language than monolingual children because practice time is divided.

[19:23] **Curiosity, the Internet, and Learning at the Right Moment**

[事实] Eagleman is optimistic about internet-based learning because young people can get information when curiosity is active.

[事实] He says plasticity depends on the right neurotransmitter state, and curiosity or engagement helps create that state.

[事实] Huberman notes that immediate information access may let people spend more time implementing what they learn instead of only searching for it.

[推测] The discussion treats timely access to information as beneficial when it serves active curiosity rather than passive consumption.

[22:21] **Building a Well-Rounded Nervous System**

[事实] Eagleman says people should try to develop along many axes, including athletics, scholarship, and social life.

[事实] He identifies critical thinking and creativity as especially important because future careers are hard to predict.

[事实] He proposes AI debate as a way for students to argue both sides of difficult issues and develop a broader view.

[事实] He defines creativity as remixing, bending, breaking, and blending foundational knowledge into something new.

[26:37] **Attention Hygiene and Novelty**

[事实] Huberman describes keeping social media on a separate phone and sometimes locking it away to reduce reflexive use.

[事实] Eagleman says the key to broadening plasticity is to seek novelty and stay in the zone between frustrating and achievable.

[事实] He cites the Religious Orders Study, saying some nuns had Alzheimer’s pathology at death but showed no obvious cognitive deficits while alive because their daily lives remained socially and cognitively active.

[事实] Eagleman says crossword puzzles are useful only until someone becomes good at them; then the brain needs a new challenge.

[32:04] **Frustration, Neuromodulators, and Plasticity**

[事实] Huberman connects the agitation of learning something new with catecholamines such as adrenaline and norepinephrine.

[事实] Eagleman says neuromodulators work together, and he expects future neuroscience to understand them more like combination locks than isolated switches.

[事实] Eagleman identifies acetylcholine as especially important for plasticity, with broad release in early life and more local release in adulthood.

[事实] He warns that altering neurotransmitter systems can have unexpected outcomes, citing dopamine medication in Parkinson’s and compulsive gambling risk.

[36:00] **Goal-Directed Plasticity**

[事实] Eagleman says parts of the brain differ in how plastic they remain across life.

[事实] He says primary visual cortex locks down early, while downstream areas involved in recognizing things remain plastic because new data keeps arriving.

[事实] Huberman and Eagleman distinguish plasticity itself from goal-directed plasticity.

[事实] Eagleman says he would not want total infant-like plasticity in adulthood because identity depends on accumulated memories and skills.

[39:36] **The Future Self and Ulysses Contracts**

[事实] Eagleman says humans spend much of life remembering the past and simulating possible futures.

[事实] He links future-oriented thinking to the prefrontal cortex and describes it as central to human self-regulation.

[事实] He introduces the Ulysses Contract as a strategy in which the present self constrains the future self before temptation appears.

[事实] Examples include locking away a phone, freezing cash, or otherwise removing access to a future temptation.

[45:01] **Commitment Devices in Everyday Life**

[事实] Eagleman says social pressure can help people keep commitments, such as agreeing to meet someone at the gym.

[事实] He gives examples of putting money or social consequences on the line to support behavior change.

[事实] He says people in recovery are advised to remove alcohol from the home or limit cash because the future self may not act like the present, rational self.

[事实] For positive goals, he mentions reducing friction, such as putting running shoes near the door before sleep.

[51:32] **Inner Voice, Aphantasia, and Mental Imagery**

[事实] Eagleman says traits such as inner voice and visual imagery exist on spectrums.

[事实] He describes aphantasia as having no visual image in the mind and hyperphantasia as seeing imagery almost like a movie.

[事实] Eagleman says he is aphantasic and has interviewed many people about the experience.

[事实] He describes Ed Catmull’s finding that many strong Pixar directors and animators reported aphantasia.

[推测] The discussion suggests that lacking vivid internal imagery may push some people to develop stronger external drawing or design practices.

[56:54] **Predispositions, Life Paths, and Pruning**

[事实] Eagleman says people enter life with genetic predispositions, childhoods, cultures, languages, and eras they do not choose.

[事实] He compares life paths to a cone of possible trajectories, with choices opening some paths and closing others.

[事实] He says a parent’s job, quoting his father, is to open doors for a child.

[事实] He agrees that early brain development involves many connections that are later pruned and strengthened by experience.

[60:45] **Attention Across Space and Time**

[事实] Huberman describes a personal perceptual exercise of shifting attention from interoception to distant objects to a cosmic-scale perspective.

[事实] Eagleman says time perception is not handled by one brain area; different mechanisms operate for long eras, seconds, and sub-seconds.

[事实] Eagleman says children are not as good at large-scale time understanding, and people improve with age and practice.

[推测] The exchange frames flexible attention across spatial and temporal scales as a possible way to gain perspective during stress.

[66:43] **Why Fear Feels Like Slow Motion**

[事实] Eagleman says people are generally poor at perceiving time accurately.

[事实] He describes a freefall experiment in which participants fell from a tall tower while a wrist device tested whether they could perceive faster during fear.

[事实] The experiment found that participants did not see faster during the frightening event, even though afterward their own fall felt longer.

[事实] Eagleman explains this as a memory effect: in life-threatening situations, the amygdala helps record more detail, so later the event feels longer.

[70:35] **Memory Density Equals Perceived Time**

[事实] Eagleman says subjective time can be explained by how much memory is written down.

[事实] He explains that childhood summers feel long because children are encoding many new experiences.

[事实] He says adulthood can feel faster because familiar patterns produce less new memory.

[事实] Novel and attentive experiences can make a weekend or life period feel longer in retrospect.

[76:11] **Novelty, Attention, and Living Longer Subjectively**

[事实] Eagleman says novelty leads to more memory formation, whether through a new activity, a new route home, or rearranging an office.

[事实] He says attending carefully to experiences can make life seem longer without changing biological longevity.

[事实] Huberman asks how much time people should spend being present, and Eagleman emphasizes being smart about what one pays attention to.

[推测] The practical takeaway is to deliberately design daily life to include small disruptions of routine and focused attention.

[80:19] **Presence and Choosing What Deserves Attention**

[事实] Eagleman says Ulysses contracts can reduce future-oriented worry and allow more presence.

[事实] He says people should think clearly about what they want to devote attention and memory to.

[事实] Huberman discusses how attention can be captured at unhelpful scales, including pathological perseveration or being lost in abstraction.

[推测] The discussion implies that mental health may involve the ability to shift between time scales and attention scales rather than becoming stuck in one.

[86:03] **Addiction, Heartbreak, Social Media, and AI**

[事实] Eagleman describes addiction as plasticity: the brain adapts to repeated exposure and comes to expect the drug or stimulus.

[事实] He compares heartbreak to withdrawal because the brain has adapted to another person’s presence and must readjust after loss.

[事实] Huberman asks whether repeated social media use may create similar expectation circuits.

[事实] Eagleman says he is torn because online content can be useful and well matched to interests, but scrolling often leaves people drained and resembles addiction in that way.

[90:43] **Sensory Substitution and New Channels Into the Brain**

[事实] Eagleman discusses Neosensory, a wristband that converts sound into patterns of vibration on the skin.

[事实] He says deaf users can learn to interpret these patterns because the brain correlates vibration with events such as mouth movement or environmental sounds.

[事实] He reviews earlier sensory substitution work, including tactile visual systems and tongue-based visual input devices for blind users.

[事实] Some users report that substituted input feels like hearing or seeing rather than like vibration or tongue stimulation.

[100:33] **Plug-and-Play Senses**

[事实] Eagleman argues that the brain can interpret many kinds of peripheral input, which supports sensory substitution, enhancement, and addition.

[事实] He describes a belt that gives directional information through vibration, allowing people to learn a sense of north.

[事实] He says this shows the brain can incorporate information that humans do not normally sense.

[推测] This section extends the plasticity theme from repairing lost senses to potentially adding new perceptual abilities.

[102:38] **Dreams as Defense of Visual Cortex**

[事实] Eagleman says his theory of dreaming is tied to brain plasticity.

[事实] He describes studies showing that after 60 to 90 minutes of blindfolding, touch or sound can start producing activity in visual cortex.

[事实] He hypothesizes that dreams help defend visual cortex from being taken over during nightly darkness.

[事实] He says REM circuitry sends periodic activity into primary visual cortex, and the brain turns that activity into visual stories.

[106:06] **REM Sleep, Plasticity, and Blind Dreams**

[事实] Eagleman says infants spend about 50% of their time in REM sleep, and REM sleep declines as the brain becomes less plastic.

[事实] He says species with extended infancy and more learning demands tend to have more REM sleep than species born more mature.

[事实] He says REM dreams are more visually elaborate than dreams from other sleep stages.

[事实] He says blind people still dream, but their dreams are built from nonvisual experiences such as touch and sound.

[110:43] **Stress, Eyewitness Memory, and the Law**

[事实] Eagleman says victims can show weapon focus, remembering the weapon while missing details such as a face.

[事实] He says even amygdala-linked traumatic memories are not necessarily accurate.

[事实] He describes Elizabeth Phelps’s post-9/11 memory work, saying traumatic memories drifted over time much like ordinary memories.

[事实] He says memory changes each time it is recalled and can be altered by new information.

[116:31] **False Memory and Suggestibility**

[事实] Eagleman says children can be more susceptible to memory manipulation.

[事实] He discusses Elizabeth Loftus’s “lost in the mall” work, where people came to believe a fabricated childhood event.

[事实] He says this has implications for therapy aimed at uncovering repressed memories.

[事实] He suggests modern children may have more photo-based repetition of their past, which could make some memories more anchored.

[119:22] **Polarization, Identity, and Empathy**

[事实] Huberman raises polarization and asks whether the world is more polarized now.

[事实] Eagleman says he does not think polarization is new, citing major 20th-century examples before social media.

[事实] He says social media may make people more aware of other groups’ beliefs rather than creating polarization from scratch.

[事实] Eagleman says beliefs cannot be separated from identity and team membership.

[121:07] **In-Groups and Out-Groups in the Brain**

[事实] Eagleman describes fMRI experiments where participants saw labeled hands being stabbed.

[事实] He says people showed stronger empathic responses for in-group members and weaker responses for out-group members.

[事实] He says the same effect appeared with religious identities, atheist identity, sports teams, and arbitrary assigned groups.

[事实] He emphasizes that this low-level neural response does not necessarily determine how a person acts.

[125:38] **Reward, Fairness, and Zero-Sum Thinking**

[事实] Eagleman says experiments from his lab and others show that reward systems can activate when something bad happens to someone a person dislikes.

[事实] Huberman connects this to media interpretation, where the same event can be read differently depending on who is treated as protagonist or antagonist.

[事实] They discuss whether people experience another person’s gain as their own loss.

[事实] Eagleman says people differ in whether they see resources as zero-sum or more abundant.

[129:38] **Propaganda and Dehumanization**

[事实] Eagleman says propaganda often works by dehumanizing an out-group with labels that make them seem nonhuman.

[事实] He says this can reduce activation in networks involved in thinking about other humans.

[事实] He cites historical examples in which dehumanizing language helped enable violence.

[事实] He argues that education should help people recognize this propaganda pattern when it appears.

[133:31] **Bias, Internal Models, and Relationship Complexity**

[事实] Eagleman says people have limited internal models shaped by thin personal trajectories through space and time.

[事实] He argues that education should teach people to see the limits of their own models and understand other people’s models.

[事实] He gives blind orchestra auditions as an example of structurally blocking bias.

[事实] He proposes “complexification of relationships”: surfacing shared interests before divisive positions so people are more willing to listen across disagreement.

[139:46] **Current Work and Closing**

[事实] Eagleman says he is working on the podcast Inner Cosmos.

[事实] He mentions two books: The Ulysses Contract and Empire of the Invisible.

[事实] He describes a documentary project with Craig Ferguson asking whether AI can be funny.

[事实] Huberman closes by praising Eagleman’s ability to combine science, experiments, and storytelling.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] This episode is most valuable for listeners who want neuroscience connected to everyday learning, habit design, memory, attention, and social behavior. Eagleman repeatedly translates mechanisms into concrete examples, which makes abstract ideas easier to apply.

[推测] A major strength is the breadth of experimental anchors: cortical remapping, sensory substitution, freefall time perception, eyewitness memory, REM sleep, and empathy studies all support the larger theme that the brain is adaptive but not perfectly reliable.

[推测] The main limitation is that the episode covers many domains, so some topics are necessarily treated at a high level. Several ideas, including the dream theory, relationship-complexification approach, and some social media interpretations, are presented as hypotheses or developing views rather than settled conclusions.

[推测] The episode is especially suitable for educators, students, clinicians, creators, and listeners interested in behavior change or public discourse. It is less suited for someone seeking a narrow protocol-only episode, because much of its value comes from broad conceptual framing.
