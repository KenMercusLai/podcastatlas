# Martin Shkreli：Big Pharma 的盲点、做空量子计算与 AI 的未来

## 概览

Martin Shkreli argues that quantum computing is widely misunderstood as simply “faster computing.” In his view, its practical advantage is confined to narrow classes of problems, while today’s machines remain slow, lack quantum memory, and offer little near-term commercial value. Despite losing millions shorting quantum-computing companies, he remains skeptical of the sector and sees photonic computing as a more promising route for accelerating AI workloads.

The conversation then turns to Shkreli’s career in biotech. He describes finding undervalued drug rights, repositioning molecules for underserved diseases, and creating value through scientific and regulatory development. His central claim is that large pharmaceutical companies often overlook assets that smaller, more focused teams can recognize and commercialize.

On AI, Shkreli rejects catastrophic narratives associated with effective altruism but remains bullish on the economic demand for intelligence. He expects AI to create and operate businesses, while warning that efficiency breakthroughs could disrupt today’s infrastructure assumptions. The episode closes with reflections on Sam Bankman-Fried, intellectual giftedness, ego, prison, and Shkreli’s realization that his need to be seen as an exceptional trader contributed to his downfall.

## 分段落总结

[00:50] **The case against commercial quantum computing**

[事实] Shkreli says his understanding of quantum computing was shaped by his wife Chelsea, who studied at MIT and taught the subject with Scott Aaronson.

[事实] He argues that quantum computers should not be treated as generally faster machines: their exceptional advantage applies to specialized problems such as integer factorization and elliptic-curve discrete logarithms.

[事实] He says current quantum systems operate at hundreds or thousands of hertz, are poorly suited to ordinary tasks such as sorting, and lack an equivalent of conventional RAM.

[事实] Shkreli contends that these limitations leave quantum computing with few compelling business applications outside highly specialized computational problems.

[04:21] **Early technology versus investable business**

[事实] The hosts propose that quantum computing may simply be at an early stage comparable to the beginnings of transistors, logic gates, and software systems.

[事实] Shkreli accepts that future progress is possible but distinguishes technological potential from a sound investment, noting that many once-important computing technologies ultimately disappeared.

[事实] He characterizes both the technology and its business model as problematic because major institutions justify current spending largely through the possibility of future discoveries.

[事实] Shkreli says he and Chelsea tested publicly accessible quantum computers and could not perform even a basic multiplication example on them.

[事实] He reports losing millions of dollars while shorting quantum-computing companies, illustrating the difficulty of timing a market even when conviction is high.

[06:03] **Photonic computing as an alternative AI architecture**

[事实] Shkreli describes modern AI accelerators as machines that spend most of their time performing matrix multiplication.

[事实] After reading research on analog optical computing, he became interested in performing matrix multiplication with light, which he says could be dramatically faster than conventional GPU execution.

[事实] He mentions startups pursuing photonic matrix-multiplication cores and says he and Chelsea have considered investigating or starting a company in the field.

[事实] He acknowledges that optical approaches still face challenges involving nonlinearity, storage, and the rest of the computing system beyond matrix multiplication.

[推测] His broader thesis is that improving computational architecture may be more valuable than continuously expanding the number and energy consumption of conventional data centers.

[07:48] **Finding overlooked value in pharmaceutical assets**

[事实] Shkreli says he began working at Jim Cramer’s hedge fund at age 16, later joined a Tiger Cub, started his own fund, and concluded that he was better suited to entrepreneurship and private equity than short-term trading.

[事实] He found the market for individual drug molecules less efficient than the public-equity market, claiming that major pharmaceutical companies sometimes sold rights to potentially valuable drugs for relatively small sums.

[事实] He describes buying a Bristol Myers drug for $2 million and says the company associated with that asset is now publicly traded at a valuation of roughly $6 billion.

[事实] The work required reading scientific papers and patents, visiting universities and drug companies, building industry relationships, and evaluating far more research than a small human team could process.

[事实] Shkreli says he started a natural-language-processing company in 2015 to help examine approximately 40 million medical papers, although the available technology did not perform as well as hoped.

[10:08] **Repositioning drugs for unmet medical needs**

[事实] Shkreli cites sparsentan as an example of transforming an overlooked asset: Bristol Myers had developed it for hypertension, but his team redirected it toward focal segmental glomerulosclerosis, a disease without an approved treatment at the time.

[事实] He says the drug was later approved and became commercially successful.

[事实] Shkreli presents indication selection—finding a disease in which a molecule has greater clinical and commercial value—as a central skill in biotech entrepreneurship.

[事实] The discussion distinguishes this model from royalty investing, where firms acquire discounted claims on future drug cash flows rather than directly repositioning and developing the molecules.

[11:13] **AI infrastructure, efficiency, and semiconductor risk**

[事实] Shkreli predicts that today’s leading GPUs will eventually become obsolete as better computing architectures emerge.

[事实] He calls proposals to place computing infrastructure in space or rely heavily on nuclear-powered expansion excessive if faster and more efficient machines can instead be developed.

[事实] He remains long on semiconductors and invokes Jevons paradox—the idea that improved efficiency can increase total consumption—to explain why efficiency gains do not necessarily reduce overall compute demand.

[事实] At the same time, he warns that a transformer architecture offering a thousandfold efficiency improvement could be negative for Nvidia if demand failed to expand proportionally.

[事实] He compares the uncertainty around current AI leaders with the difficulty of identifying durable winners such as Yahoo or Cisco during the dot-com era.

[13:05] **AI fear, effective accelerationism, and drug discovery**

[事实] Shkreli criticizes Dario Amodei as a fearmonger and questions the circumstances in which founders left OpenAI to create competing companies, while the hosts note that employee mobility is common in Silicon Valley and differs from documented trade-secret theft.

[事实] He identifies with effective accelerationism, which he describes as a counter-movement to effective altruism and as a philosophy of moving quickly with AI development.

[事实] Shkreli argues that present AI systems are software that can still be shut down, making immediate extinction rhetoric disproportionate to their current capabilities.

[事实] He rejects predictions of near-term mass unemployment and says he does not expect an AI-relevant drug to emerge within the next decade.

[事实] Drawing on his drug-development experience, he argues that faster computers are useful but that AI currently offers limited help with the hardest parts of discovering and securing approval for medicines.

[推测] His skepticism is directed less at AI itself than at claims that computational improvements can bypass biology, clinical trials, regulation, and the tacit knowledge involved in pharmaceutical development.

[16:35] **The economic demand for intelligence**

[事实] Shkreli agrees that venture-capital funding is helping finance AI token consumption but does not interpret this alone as a fatal valuation trap.

[事实] He argues that demand for intelligence and productive work is effectively unbounded and should not be measured only against the existing knowledge-worker market.

[事实] He expects AI systems eventually to start and operate companies across industries such as pharmaceuticals, automobiles, and aviation.

[事实] As an example, he suggests that a large company such as McDonald’s could rationally spend billions on computation if AI produced sufficiently valuable decisions about products or strategy.

[推测] This outlook implies that AI’s addressable market may expand through newly created economic activity, rather than merely replacing the cost of work already performed by humans.

[18:31] **Who captures the value created by AI?**

[事实] Shkreli compares AI with the internet, arguing that consumers and businesses may capture most of the long-term value even if infrastructure providers earn exceptional profits during particular periods.

[事实] He names Meta as a potential winner because of its distribution and product capabilities.

[事实] He says he would buy stakes in OpenAI and Anthropic, while praising OpenAI’s move into hardware as a way to control how its models reach users.

[事实] Shkreli nevertheless describes chatbots as an early form of AI that may eventually appear primitive.

[推测] His investment framework separates broad social value from shareholder returns: a transformative technology can benefit nearly everyone without guaranteeing lasting excess profits for today’s leading suppliers.

[20:11] **AI safety and the critique of effective altruism**

[事实] Asked about competitor peer review as a compromise between safety and speed, Shkreli responds by criticizing the intellectual culture behind existential-risk arguments.

[事实] He attributes some AI fear to Nick Bostrom’s *Superintelligence* and argues that a sense of self-importance among effective-altruist thinkers has amplified catastrophic narratives.

[事实] He claims that Meta, Jensen Huang, and political leaders have shown greater willingness to dismiss such concerns as exaggerated.

[事实] Shkreli acknowledges that figures associated with effective altruism may be highly intelligent, but argues that expertise in one domain does not guarantee good judgment in another.

[推测] The discussion does not provide a detailed safety framework; it primarily challenges the motives, scale, and urgency of prevailing risk claims.

[21:25] **Brilliance, unconventional thinking, and failure**

[事实] The hosts ask whether unusually intelligent people gain advantages from unconventional thinking but also struggle to respect conventional legal or ethical boundaries.

[事实] Shkreli says Sam Bankman-Fried made investments that appeared brilliant in 2021 and 2022, including early exposure to Anthropic and Robinhood.

[事实] He argues that intellectually gifted people can feel compelled to live up to an exceptional self-image, citing Bankman-Fried, Elizabeth Holmes, Raj Rajaratnam, and himself.

[事实] Shkreli says his refusal to admit that he was not the world’s best hedge-fund manager—despite being a stronger entrepreneur—was a central factor that put him in prison.

[推测] He interprets spectacular failure partly as a psychological problem: preserving an identity of exceptional competence can become more important than acknowledging mistakes.

[23:16] **Prison, identity, and shared human needs**

[事实] Shkreli says prison taught him that behavior is often driven by psychological forces people do not fully understand.

[事实] He realized that his fear of not being viewed as a great trader had narrowed his identity, even though he could have defined success through investing, entrepreneurship, science, or family.

[事实] He says prisoners from very different backgrounds shared ordinary emotional concerns, including missing loved ones and feeling hurt when friends stopped staying in contact.

[事实] Shkreli concludes that status and achievement do not erase basic human needs or the desire to fulfill personal roles such as being a good parent.

[推测] These reflections present self-acceptance and a broader identity as the episode’s implicit counterweight to ambition, ego, and intellectual exceptionalism.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s strongest feature is Shkreli’s ability to connect technical architecture, market structure, and investor incentives. His distinction between a scientifically remarkable technology and an investable business is especially useful, as is his account of how biotech entrepreneurs create value by identifying neglected molecules and better clinical applications.

Its principal limitation is that many forceful claims—particularly about quantum computing, AI safety, and the prospects for AI-assisted drug development—are presented through Shkreli’s personal conviction rather than systematically tested against competing evidence. The hosts offer some counterarguments, but several provocative assertions remain unresolved.

The closing discussion adds value by moving beyond markets and technology. Shkreli’s admission that attachment to an identity as a great trader contributed to his imprisonment provides a candid psychological theme connecting ambition, unconventional thinking, and self-destructive behavior.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners interested in contrarian technology investing, biotech strategy, AI infrastructure, and founder psychology. It is more valuable as a source of challenging hypotheses and mental models than as a neutral or comprehensive account of the subjects discussed.
