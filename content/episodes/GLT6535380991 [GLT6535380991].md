# The French Revolution: How France Slid Into Crisis Before 1789

## 概览

This episode argues that the French Revolution did not simply begin with the storming of the Bastille in July 1789. The hosts frame it as the result of a chain of crises: financial collapse, social and economic distress, and a new political culture obsessed with virtue, public opinion, rights, and the possibility of rebuilding society from scratch.

The discussion emphasizes that Louis XVI was not portrayed as a monstrous tyrant, but as a mild, indecisive monarch trapped inside a complicated, privilege-based state. France was wealthy, dynamic, and intellectually vibrant, yet its tax system, debt burden, and institutions made reform extremely difficult.

A central theme is that the language later associated with revolution first emerged from elites defending old privileges. Courts, nobles, clergy, lawyers, and local notables invoked liberty, the nation, patriotism, and citizens while resisting royal tax reforms. By 1788, that language had escaped elite control and was being taken up by crowds, provincial activists, and ambitious young lawyers.

The episode ends with France effectively bankrupt in August 1788, the Estates General promised but not yet defined, and the hosts arguing that the Revolution may already have begun in Grenoble with the Day of the Tiles.

## 分段落总结

[01:12] **Thatcher, The Terror, And The Meaning Of Revolution**

[事实] The episode opens with Margaret Thatcher’s 1989 criticism of the French Revolution, in which she contrasted it with Britain’s 1688-89 Glorious Revolution and emphasized terror, murder, and the loss of fraternity.

[事实] The hosts say the French Revolution remains divisive and controversial, with historians still split between those who stress its violence and those who defend its ideals.

[事实] They describe it as a central political event in modern history because it shaped conservatism, nationalism, left-right politics, French national symbols, and later historical writing.

[推测] The opening frames the episode less as a celebration of revolution than as an inquiry into why attempts to remake society can become violent and destabilizing.

[05:45] **Three Causes: Money, Hunger, And Political Culture**

[事实] Dominic Sandbrook identifies three major ingredients behind the Revolution: a financial and high political crisis, a social crisis caused by bad weather and hunger, and a cultural moment centered on progress, purity, virtue, and starting anew.

[事实] The hosts distinguish the French Revolution from the Glorious Revolution and the American Revolution by stressing its ambition to reboot society rather than merely adjust institutions.

[事实] They argue that chronology and contingency matter, comparing this approach to narrative explanations of the road to the First World War.

[推测] The episode’s causal model rejects a single-origin explanation and instead treats the Revolution as a cascading failure of state, society, and political imagination.

[08:04] **Louis XVI And The Fragile Power Of Absolutism**

[事实] Louis XVI became king in 1774 at age 19 and is described as mild, affable, fond of locks and hunting, and not an obvious object of hatred.

[事实] France under Louis XVI had around 28 million people, was the largest kingdom in continental Europe, and was a major economic and military power.

[事实] Despite being called an absolute monarch, Louis depended on judges, nobles, officials, intendants, institutions, customs, and constitutional conventions.

[推测] The hosts suggest that Louis’s weakness was not cruelty but a failure to impose or sustain reform when faced with elite resistance.

[09:39] **A Powerful But Fragmented France**

[事实] Paris was the second-largest city in Europe after London and combined poverty, crime, prostitution, coffee houses, books, newspapers, theaters, and political ideas.

[事实] France also had prosperous provincial centers such as Bordeaux and Lyon, while around eight out of ten French people worked on the land.

[事实] The kingdom was linguistically and regionally diverse; many subjects would not have spoken French as Louis understood it.

[推测] This diversity made France difficult to govern uniformly and made local institutions and privileges politically sensitive.

[12:36] **Enlightenment Modernity And The Desire To Fix The State**

[事实] Louis XVI is described as a product of the Enlightenment era, someone who respected reason and believed problems could have rational solutions.

[事实] As a boy, Louis wrote that citizens had rights including life, honor, liberty, and property.

[事实] The hosts stress that the so-called Ancien Régime was not experienced by its inhabitants as old and static, but as modern, energetic, and changing.

[事实] They cite French advances in trade, industry, chemistry, mathematics, and ballooning as signs of excitement and innovation.

[推测] The monarchy’s problem was partly that a dynamic society expected reform from institutions that were too tangled to reform smoothly.

[15:29] **America, Public Opinion, And A New Political Culture**

[事实] The American Republic fascinated French observers, and Benjamin Franklin was popular in Paris.

[事实] Dominic says the French Revolution would still have happened without America, though the French and American revolutions shared some political language.

[事实] France’s support for the American War worsened its finances, but the hosts say French leaders were mainly interested in hurting Britain.

[事实] A new political culture had emerged in Paris through coffee houses, pamphlets, street readings, newspapers, and the phrase “public opinion,” coined in 1750.

[推测] The American example mattered less as a direct cause than as part of a wider Atlantic language of experiment, rights, and political possibility.

[18:04] **Reason, Sensibility, Virtue, And Violence**

[事实] The hosts contrast an older Enlightenment culture of wit, skepticism, and reason with a younger culture influenced by Rousseau, sensibility, intense feeling, tears, nature, friendship, family, and authenticity.

[事实] They identify virtue as a central revolutionary idea and compare its social force to a modern abstract moral ideal that becomes difficult to question.

[事实] They also describe revolutionary politics as strongly masculine and chauvinist, linking virtue to the Latin virtus.

[事实] France before the Revolution was already marked by riots, lynchings, looting, executions, troops firing on crowds, and public violence.

[推测] The episode implies that revolutionary violence did not appear from nowhere; it intensified patterns already present in eighteenth-century French public life.

[21:55] **Debt, War, And The Limits Of French Finance**

[事实] The hosts say money was at the center of the crisis, as early modern monarchies struggled to fund larger armies, navies, and global competition.

[事实] France had been accumulating large debts since the 1720s and lacked a national bank, partly because of earlier financial trauma linked to John Law.

[事实] By the 1760s France was around 2 billion livres in debt and spending about 60% of its budget servicing that debt.

[事实] Britain had even greater debt, but could raise taxes through the House of Commons, while France had no regular elected assembly and had not called the Estates General since 1614-15.

[推测] The comparison with Britain shows that France’s problem was not only debt itself, but the absence of trusted mechanisms for raising revenue.

[24:46] **A Tax System Built On Privilege And Friction**

[事实] France raised revenue through complicated taxes including property taxes with many exemptions, a lucrative salt tax that encouraged smuggling, and internal customs duties.

[事实] These internal duties made commerce difficult because goods could be taxed repeatedly while moving across the kingdom.

[事实] Louis XVI appointed Turgot, a liberal reformer who tried to deregulate and liberalize bread prices, but unrest followed and Louis dismissed him.

[事实] Dominic argues that Louis repeatedly gestured toward change and then backed down under pressure.

[推测] The hosts present Louis’s desire to be loved as politically damaging in a period that required sustained confrontation with vested interests.

[27:22] **The American War And The Deepening Deficit**

[事实] Jean-Jacques Necker supported French involvement in the American War and organized short-term loans to pay for it.

[事实] The war was mainly naval and therefore extremely expensive; France added about 1.5 billion livres in debt, mostly through loans.

[事实] France hoped to gain rich Caribbean prizes such as Jamaica, but Britain kept its Caribbean possessions.

[事实] Simon Schama is cited as saying that the cost of the American War brought on the terminal crisis of the French monarchy.

[推测] The American War gave France symbolic revenge against Britain but produced a fiscal disaster without sufficient strategic compensation.

[28:24] **Calonne’s Warning And The Privilege Problem**

[事实] In 1786, finance minister Charles Alexandre de Calonne told Louis XVI that France faced an annual deficit of 80 million livres, with half of revenue going to interest payments.

[事实] Calonne warned that Necker’s short-term loans were coming due and that France could not rely on new loans to pay old ones.

[事实] He argued that the constitution and tax system had to be reformed because nobles and clergy were largely exempt from taxation.

[事实] The hosts explain that many privileged groups did not see exemptions as corruption, but as ancestral liberties and rights.

[推测] Calonne’s diagnosis exposed a contradiction: reform required attacking the very privileges that many elites equated with liberty.

[30:47] **Parlements, Liberty, And Resistance To Reform**

[事实] The parlements were high courts, not elected parliaments, and they registered royal edicts to check whether they violated French rights.

[事实] Since the 1750s, Louis XV and Louis XVI had repeatedly faced parlementary resistance to new taxes.

[事实] The parlements framed their resistance as defending liberty, the nation, and the ancient French constitution against royal despotism.

[事实] The physical separation between Versailles and Paris strengthened resentment toward a court seen as isolated, corrupt, and out of touch.

[推测] Institutions defending privilege gained public legitimacy by presenting themselves as guardians against arbitrary monarchy.

[32:34] **The Assembly Of Notables Opens Pandora’s Box**

[事实] Calonne proposed bypassing the parlements by calling an Assembly of Notables, hoping leading figures would approve reform.

[事实] On 22 February 1787, the notables gathered at Versailles and heard proposals for a land tax regardless of status, freer internal markets, and elected local assemblies.

[事实] The notables rejected the plans, objected to sitting with the Third Estate, and argued that only the Estates General could approve new taxes.

[事实] Calonne went public with pamphlets and public readings, using language such as national will, patriotism, justice, and sacrifice of privilege.

[事实] Louis dismissed Calonne after the notables objected to his appeal to public opinion.

[推测] By taking fiscal reform to the public, Calonne helped make national politics a matter for crowds, pamphleteers, and public debate rather than court management.

[40:11] **Brienne Discovers The Deficit Is Worse**

[事实] Louis appointed Etienne Charles de Lomenie de Brienne, the Archbishop of Toulouse, as finance minister after Calonne.

[事实] Brienne had doubted Calonne’s warnings, but after examining the books he found the deficit was worse: about 140 million livres rather than 80 million.

[事实] Brienne proposed a land tax and a stamp tax, but the notables again refused and demanded the Estates General.

[事实] Louis became increasingly depressed, spending time hunting, eating, and drinking while facing only bad options.

[推测] Brienne’s reversal shows how opposition figures could become reformers once forced to confront the actual finances of the monarchy.

[42:37] **Parlementary Revolt And The Language Of Citizens**

[事实] In summer 1787, Brienne went to the Paris Parlement seeking approval for new taxes.

[事实] The Paris Parlement rejected the taxes and declared that the nation must consent through regular meetings of the Estates General.

[事实] The Parlement used the word citoyens, or citizens, rather than simply subjects.

[事实] The hosts stress the irony that revolutionary language was first used to defend the privileges of nobles and clergy.

[推测] The language of citizenship and national consent began as an elite weapon against royal taxation, but it contained implications that would later undermine the old order itself.

[45:26] **Riots, Exile, And The Breakdown Of Royal Control**

[事实] Brienne exiled the Paris Parlement to Troyes in August 1787, using the Swiss Guard to seal it off and remove its members.

[事实] Paris erupted in disorder, with riots, attacks on officials, the killing of a suspected police agent, and infantry deployed to secure the streets.

[事实] The government shut down clubs and societies, including chess clubs, to stop subversive ideas from spreading.

[事实] From 1787 onward, the hosts say Paris had to be managed with troops on the streets and that royal control had partly slipped.

[推测] The monarchy’s attempts to discipline institutions made itself look despotic and further legitimized opposition.

[46:52] **Brienne’s Court Reform And Provincial Resistance**

[事实] In April 1788, Brienne proposed stripping the parlements of their role in policing royal edicts, strengthening lower courts, and creating a new plenary court to approve royal taxes.

[事实] This plan provoked riots not only in Paris but also in provincial capitals such as Lyon, Aix, and Dijon.

[事实] The hosts emphasize that the Revolution should not be understood as Paris simply dragging the rest of France behind it.

[事实] Provincial courts mattered because they embodied regional identity, local status, and livelihoods.

[推测] Reform from the center threatened provincial communities that saw their courts as part of their distinct rights and identity.

[48:23] **Grenoble And The Day Of The Tiles**

[事实] Dominic argues that the French Revolution began in 1788 in Grenoble, not in Paris in 1789.

[事实] Grenoble was the capital of Dauphine, had a parlement, and contained many lawyers, journalists, clerks, and writers whose livelihoods depended on that court.

[事实] The city’s glove-making industry was also suffering from economic downturn, adding social anger.

[事实] On 7 June 1788, Brienne’s edict was read in the marketplace, troops tried to remove parlement leaders, protests became a general strike and riot, and townspeople threw roof tiles at soldiers.

[事实] Soldiers fired, killing a 12-year-old boy and a hatter; the crowd then seized the city, sacked the governor’s house, and paraded bloodied clothes.

[推测] Grenoble matters because it shows elite constitutional conflict being overtaken by mass urban unrest.

[51:24] **Crowds, Judges, And The First Revolutionary Leader**

[事实] Simon Schama is cited as arguing that the parlement judges carried by the crowd were less leaders of the riot than trophies or prisoners of it.

[事实] Some older judges became anxious and left Grenoble, while younger figures saw opportunity in the collapse of royal authority.

[事实] Jean-Joseph Mounier, a 29-year-old judge from Grenoble and son of a cloth merchant, is described as possibly the first true French revolutionary leader.

[事实] Mounier was upwardly mobile, ambitious, a lawyer, and had bought the office of royal judge, placing him at the lowest rank of nobility.

[推测] The episode suggests that revolutions are often driven not by the poorest people alone, but by ambitious middling figures whose social advancement feels blocked.

[53:12] **Vizille, Majority Rule, And A New Revolutionary Language**

[事实] On 21 July 1788, Mounier convened a large meeting at the Chateau de Vizille outside Grenoble.

[事实] The meeting demanded free elections, provincial Estates General for Dauphine, equal numbers for the Third Estate compared with clergy and nobility combined, and a single chamber.

[事实] The hosts say many historians see this as a seismic starting point for the Revolution.

[事实] The language at Vizille invoked patriotic fire, sacrifice, the general will, and the idea that opponents were traitors to the patrie.

[推测] The hosts see an early totalitarian danger in claiming to embody the nation, because that makes opponents appear illegitimate by definition.

[55:35] **From Ancient Liberties To Starting Again**

[事实] By 1788, political language was shifting from defending old French liberties to demanding a new constitution based on natural rights and the general will.

[事实] The hosts cite Volney’s argument that the actions of past generations did not matter compared with the essential rights of man and natural relations in society.

[事实] They contrast this with Britain’s evolved constitution and with the American Constitution, which emphasized checks, qualifications, and separation of powers.

[事实] French radicals increasingly spoke in stark terms: virtue, patriotism, liberty, the people, and traitors.

[推测] The episode presents this simplification of politics as one of the key reasons the French Revolution became more radical than its British or American models.

[58:13] **The Estates General And Bankruptcy**

[事实] Brienne told Louis XVI that the Estates General had to be called, even though Louis feared it could overturn the state and monarchy.

[事实] In the second week of August 1788, the government announced that the Estates General would be called, though its rules and election procedures remained unclear.

[事实] On 16 August 1788, Brienne announced that the royal treasury was virtually empty and that people would be paid in IOUs or paper money.

[事实] The hosts end by saying France was bankrupt, setting up the next episode on the Estates General.

[推测] The promise of representation and the admission of bankruptcy together made the old system’s survival doubtful before the famous events of 1789.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s strongest value is its careful reconstruction of the pre-1789 crisis. Rather than treating the Bastille as a sudden beginning, it shows how financial failure, elite resistance, public opinion, local unrest, and ideological language accumulated over several years.

Its most interesting argument is that revolutionary language did not first belong to radicals in the streets. In the telling here, terms such as liberty, nation, citizens, patriotism, and the general will were sharpened through conflicts among monarchy, courts, notables, lawyers, and local elites before being taken up more widely.

The episode is especially useful for listeners interested in political breakdown, state finance, constitutional conflict, and the unintended consequences of reform. It is less focused on peasants’ lived experience or Marie Antoinette herself, except where those topics connect to public opinion, violence, and the monarchy’s legitimacy.

[推测] The tone and references suggest the hosts are skeptical of romanticized accounts of revolution and particularly interested in how ideals of virtue and renewal can coexist with coercion, exclusion, and violence.
