# Troy and the Trojan War

## 概览

This episode uses Troy as a way to ask why one ancient war story has remained so powerful. The hosts move from the basic myth of Helen, Paris, Achilles, and the wooden horse into the harder historical question of whether any real Bronze Age conflict lies behind Homer’s poetry.

A central conclusion is that Troy matters less because of the recoverable facts than because of the stories later cultures built from it. Homer, Greek tragedy, Roman origin myths, medieval British legends, Ottoman claims, modern film, and even comparisons with the world wars all become part of Troy’s afterlife.

The discussion repeatedly contrasts mythic meaning with historical explanation: abducted women versus trade routes, heroic glory versus pointless suffering, Greek victory versus Trojan victimhood, and modern moral readings versus ancient aristocratic values.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Opening With Troy’s Enduring Appeal**

[事实] The episode opens with a quote from the 2004 film *Troy*, attributed in the dialogue to Sean Bean playing Odysseus.

[事实] The hosts introduce Troy as the subject of the episode, prompted partly by Stephen Fry’s new book on Troy.

[事实] Dominic says he is reading a children’s version of the *Iliad* to his son, while Tom says he is writing a children’s history of ancient Greece in which the gods remain central.

[01:38] **Listener Feedback and Divisive Prime Ministers**

[事实] Before turning fully to Troy, the hosts read comments about their previous episode on 1981.

[事实] A listener asks who the last “undivisive” British prime minister was, leading to discussion of Margaret Thatcher, Harold Wilson, Jim Callaghan, David Cameron, Tony Blair before Iraq, and Stanley Baldwin.

[推测] This opening segment functions as the show’s conversational warm-up before shifting from modern political history to the Bronze Age.

[04:32] **The Trojan War in Brief**

[事实] Tom summarizes the Trojan War: Helen, daughter of Zeus, is taken by Paris to Troy, and the Greek heroes sail to retrieve her because of an oath.

[事实] The Greeks spend ten years outside Troy, great heroes fight and die, and the city is finally taken through the trick of the wooden horse.

[事实] The Greeks hide warriors inside the horse, the Trojans bring it into the city, and Troy is burned.

[06:07] **Did Troy Really Happen?**

[事实] Greek historians dated the Trojan War to roughly 1250-1150 BC, near the end of the Bronze Age.

[事实] Tom says the major question is whether Homer and later Greek writers preserved authentic memories of real events.

[事实] Greeks and Romans generally believed the war had happened, while by the eighteenth and early nineteenth centuries many people treated it as a fairy tale.

[事实] Heinrich Schliemann went to Hisarlik in modern Turkey, dug extensively, damaged much archaeology, and claimed to have found Homer’s Troy.

[事实] Tom says the archaeological consensus is that Hisarlik probably was Troy.

[07:24] **Troy’s Strategic and Symbolic Location**

[事实] Troy is described as near Gallipoli, commanding the entrance to the straits between the Aegean and the Black Sea.

[事实] Alexander the Great visited what he believed was Achilles’ tomb and performed rituals there.

[事实] Xerxes also visited the site of Troy and paid respects to the heroes before invading Greece.

[事实] The hosts say ancient leaders used Troy as part of a larger story about conflict between Asia and Europe.

[09:33] **Herodotus, Thucydides, and Rational Explanations**

[事实] Herodotus framed the Trojan War within a wider pattern of Greeks and Asians stealing each other’s princesses.

[事实] Herodotus thought Helen’s abduction happened but treated the Greek response as a massive overreaction.

[事实] Thucydides also believed the war happened but explained it in terms of Agamemnon’s power, wealth, and control of trade routes.

[推测] The episode presents Thucydides’ interpretation as closer to how many modern historians would explain a Bronze Age conflict.

[11:52] **Hittite Evidence and Bronze Age Diplomacy**

[事实] Tom says Hittite royal correspondence discovered in the early twentieth century may shed light on the Trojan War.

[事实] One Hittite reference mentions Wilusa, which may connect linguistically to Ilium, another name for Troy.

[事实] A king of Wilusa named Alexandu may connect to Alexander, another name for Paris.

[事实] Another Hittite term, Ahhiyawa, may connect to Achaea or the Achaeans, Homer’s name for the Greeks.

[推测] These clues are suggestive rather than conclusive, and the hosts stress that they are interesting largely because Homer made Troy culturally important.

[14:02] **Why the Story Still Works**

[事实] Dominic says the *Iliad* appeals partly because of its personalities and the disconnect between heroic status and petty or questionable behavior.

[事实] Achilles is described as spending much of the story sulking in his tent.

[事实] Tom notes that the *Iliad* covers only a short period of the war and does not include the Trojan Horse.

[事实] The hosts discuss the wooden horse as a famous story of Greek cunning, while also joking about how suspicious the Trojans should have been.

[16:21] **Ancient Trivia and Listener Questions**

[事实] Tom says Emperor Tiberius held quiz-like sessions on Capri about difficult Trojan War questions.

[事实] Tiberius asked questions such as what name Achilles used when disguised as a girl and what song the Sirens sang.

[事实] Tom says one guest cheated by obtaining questions in advance and was killed.

[事实] The hosts then move into listener questions about Troy’s wider meaning.

[17:57] **Troy Compared With Modern Western Wars**

[事实] Dominic suggests World War II is the obvious modern Western equivalent to Troy because it functions as a moral myth about evil, heroism, and appeasement.

[事实] Tom argues that the First World War also belongs in the comparison because the Trojan War has a strong sense of pointlessness and ruin.

[事实] Achilles chooses a short life with eternal fame over a long obscure life, but in the *Odyssey* his ghost regrets that choice.

[事实] Tom says Greek tragedy often presents the Trojan War as bleak, destructive, and lacking clear purpose.

[20:24] **Helen, Beauty, and Historical Consequence**

[事实] The hosts discuss Helen as a woman placed at the center of a war story.

[事实] Tom links the theme to Herodotus’ opening account of people abducting or raping each other’s women.

[事实] A listener asks about historically beautiful people whose beauty changed history.

[事实] Dominic gives Anne Boleyn as an example, arguing that Henry VIII’s attraction to her helped shape England’s break with Catholicism.

[事实] Cleopatra is discussed as a figure later imagined as a fatal seductress, though Tom notes that she is known to have slept with Julius Caesar and Mark Antony, both extremely powerful men.

[24:26] **Film, History, and Myth**

[事实] A listener asks whether film is useful for exploring history or does more damage than good.

[事实] Tom calls the Brad Pitt film *Troy* the worst film about the ancient world because it removes the gods.

[事实] Tom argues that trying to make the Trojan myth historically grounded misses what makes Homer interesting.

[事实] The hosts discuss whether films can do harm as propaganda by shaping collective imagination.

[事实] Tom contrasts *Troy* with *300*, which he says accurately presents the Spartans as proto-fascist and unsettling.

[26:49] **Trojans as Ancestors and Political Prestige**

[事实] Dominic raises medieval British stories in which Brutus, a relative of Aeneas, brings Trojan refugees to Britain and founds Britannia.

[事实] In this tradition, Brutus founds New Troy, which becomes London, and divides his kingdom among three sons.

[事实] Tom explains that identifying with Trojans also links a people to Rome, because Romans traced their origins to Aeneas.

[事实] Tom says Roman claims to Trojan descent also helped provide a rationale for conquering Greece.

[事实] Mehmet II is said to have presented himself as an heir of the Trojans when he captured Constantinople.

[30:14] **Hector, Achilles, and Sympathy for the Losers**

[事实] Dominic says both he and his son were drawn to Hector and the Trojans despite knowing they lose.

[事实] The hosts discuss whether modern audiences sympathize with Trojans because they are underdogs and victims.

[事实] Tom describes Achilles as the great hero because he is the best at killing people, and says Alexander modelled himself on Achilles.

[事实] Tom argues that modern sensibilities are strongly shaped by Christianity and are inclined to identify with sufferers and victims.

[32:33] **Greek Tragedy and the Suffering of War**

[事实] Tom says Greek tragedy also gives major attention to the suffering of the defeated.

[事实] Euripides’ *Trojan Women* is described as a devastating portrayal of Trojan queens and princesses being led into slavery after the sack of the city.

[事实] Tom contrasts the sack of Troy with the sack of Jerusalem in the Old Testament, arguing that Greek tragedy lacks the same redemptive framework.

[事实] He says Greek tragedy became prominent again during and after the Gulf War, when productions often cast Greeks as coalition forces.

[事实] The episode closes with the hosts saying there are many unanswered questions and that they may return to Troy later.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode is valuable because it treats Troy not simply as a myth or a historical puzzle, but as a story whose meanings keep changing. The strongest sections are the movement from Bronze Age archaeology to Homer, Herodotus, Greek tragedy, Rome, medieval Britain, modern war memory, and cinema.

Its main limitation is also part of its style: the discussion is wide-ranging and comic, so some major topics are introduced quickly rather than developed in detail. The historical evidence for Troy, especially the Hittite material, is presented as suggestive rather than fully unpacked.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners who want an accessible, lively introduction to why the Trojan War matters culturally, rather than a technical archaeological or philological account.
