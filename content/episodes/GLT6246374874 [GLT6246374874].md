# May 1968: De Gaulle Returns

## 概览

This episode continues the story of May 1968 in Paris, shifting the focus from student and worker unrest to Charles de Gaulle’s response. The hosts place de Gaulle against his long career: the First World War, the Free French, liberation, the Fifth Republic, and his near-monarchical idea of presidential authority.

The central drama is de Gaulle’s apparent loss of nerve as strikes and demonstrations spread, opposition politicians prepare for a post-Gaullist future, and the communists organize a major march. His secret trip to Baden-Baden to test the loyalty of the French army becomes the hinge of the episode.

The conclusion is that May 1968 failed politically: de Gaulle dissolved the National Assembly, framed the election as order versus communism, and won a huge legislative majority. The hosts then debate whether the movement’s deeper legacy was cultural and intellectual, especially through Foucault and later Anglo-American theories of power, repression, and institutions.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Paris Setting and Recap**

[事实] The hosts are in Paris, beginning in the Latin Quarter, and present this as the second part of their episode on the events of 1968.  
[事实] They recap the earlier discussion: student protests, the Sorbonne, police violence, student violence, spreading strikes, and the sense that the French state had lost control.  
[事实] They say roughly two-thirds of the country was on strike and that “revolution” seemed to be in the air.

[01:36] **De Gaulle as the Embodiment of France**

[事实] De Gaulle is introduced as the key figure not yet fully discussed: president of France, in power for ten years, but with a much longer public career.  
[事实] The hosts summarize his biography: born in Lille in 1890, captured at Verdun, leader of French resistance from London, and marcher down the Champs-Élysées during liberation in 1944.  
[事实] They describe his belief in the glory of France as a “necessary fiction” and link him to the Fifth Republic’s strong presidency.

[03:09] **The Fifth Republic and Patriarchal Authority**

[事实] The hosts argue that de Gaulle’s Fifth Republic placed the president in a near-monarchical role.  
[事实] They describe him as embodying a paternalistic, patriarchal, and authoritarian public culture in France.  
[推测] This framing makes the student rebellion not just a political challenge, but a symbolic revolt against de Gaulle’s whole idea of France.

[04:13] **De Gaulle’s Early Reaction to the Protests**

[事实] De Gaulle initially dismisses the protests as childishness and uses harsh language about how to discipline unruly children.  
[事实] When Nobel Prize winners urge leniency toward the students, he is angered and tells ministers that an interior minister must know how to order firing if necessary.  
[事实] The hosts also say he is increasingly out of touch with the mood in French universities despite being literate and culturally cultivated.

[05:41] **Romania, Anxiety, and a Failed Speech**

[事实] De Gaulle continues with a state visit to Romania, partly because of his wider foreign policy vision of positioning France between NATO and the Warsaw Pact.  
[事实] He returns on May 18 after sleepless anxiety about events in Paris and describes the unrest with the term “chienlit.”  
[事实] On May 24 he gives a rambling speech about participation and councils, but the hosts say it does not work and protests continue.

[06:56] **Cohn-Bendit and Escalating Violence**

[事实] Daniel Cohn-Bendit, “Danny the Red,” becomes a face of the student radical wing.  
[事实] The French government bans him from returning to France, which inflames students further.  
[事实] The hosts note that two people die during the unrest, while also stressing that the death toll is far lower than in other violent crises of 1968.

[09:14] **Fear of Commune-Style Collapse**

[事实] French authorities do not know in the moment whether the unrest is theatrical protest or a genuine revolutionary crisis.  
[事实] The army prepares contingency plans to occupy or retake Paris, echoing fears of the Paris Commune.  
[推测] The memory of earlier French revolutions shapes how politicians and police interpret the danger.

[09:43] **Grenelle Accords and Rejected Compromise**

[事实] The government negotiates with unions in the Grenelle Accords on May 25 and 26.  
[事实] Jacques Chirac is mentioned as one of the negotiators.  
[事实] The deal offers wage and benefit gains, but workers at the Renault plant in Billancourt reject it as insufficient.

[11:01] **The Intoxication of Protest**

[事实] The hosts describe the movement as acquiring its own momentum, with people enjoying occupations, slogans, philosophy, and the demand for the impossible.  
[事实] They argue that protest and revolution can become intoxicating and difficult for participants themselves to explain.  
[推测] This helps explain why material concessions did not immediately end the movement.

[11:35] **Opposition Politicians Sense an Opening**

[事实] By late May, the government seems to have run out of options and de Gaulle appears old, irrelevant, and politically exhausted.  
[事实] François Mitterrand says there is no more state and declares himself ready to assume power.  
[事实] Pierre Mendès France also presents himself as ready to govern, while the communists begin positioning themselves for what might come next.

[12:42] **De Gaulle’s Night of Despair**

[事实] On the night of May 28, de Gaulle is at the Élysée Palace as unrest moves closer to the seat of power.  
[事实] Julian Jackson is cited describing de Gaulle’s state as “apocalyptic despair.”  
[事实] De Gaulle compares the crisis to 1940, says he lacks the energy to deal with it, and reflects on deep French historical defeats such as Waterloo and Sedan.

[14:22] **The Communist March and De Gaulle’s Departure**

[事实] On May 29, communists and the CGT organize a major march through Paris, with estimates ranging from police expectations of 50,000 to communist claims of around 400,000.  
[事实] De Gaulle postpones meetings, prepares personal papers, and says he does not want blood shed defending an empty palace.  
[事实] He tells aides he is leaving Paris for Colombey-les-Deux-Églises, but secretly has Baden-Baden in mind.

[15:48] **Baden-Baden and the Army**

[事实] De Gaulle sends word to General Massu, commander of French forces in Baden-Baden, to determine whether the army can be relied on.  
[事实] Massu had served with the Free French and remained personally loyal to de Gaulle despite disagreement over Algeria.  
[事实] De Gaulle flies by helicopter with his wife, then diverts from Colombey to Baden-Baden without telling Pompidou.

[18:03] **Panic in Paris and Reassurance in Germany**

[事实] In Paris, Pompidou and others panic when they discover de Gaulle has disappeared, with officials burning documents, planning escape routes, and fearing revolutionary takeover.  
[事实] De Gaulle lands in Baden-Baden at 2:40 p.m. while the communist march is underway in Paris.  
[事实] Massu reassures him that the troops will remain loyal if force is needed.

[20:07] **Return and Lingering Fear**

[事实] De Gaulle decides not to address the nation from Germany or Strasbourg and instead returns to Colombey, then Paris.  
[事实] His wife Yvonne leaves family jewels with relatives at Baden-Baden, suggesting the family still sees Germany as a possible refuge.  
[推测] The trip both restores de Gaulle’s confidence and reveals how close the regime believed it might be to collapse.

[23:05] **A Transformed President**

[事实] On May 30, aides find de Gaulle transformed at the Élysée Palace: decisive rather than anxious.  
[事实] Jacques Foccart advises him to speak before the planned Gaullist counter-demonstration, not after it.  
[事实] Pompidou urges dissolving the National Assembly and holding elections, and de Gaulle agrees.

[24:10] **The Radio Speech**

[事实] De Gaulle speaks at 4:30 p.m. on radio rather than television, evoking his 1940 broadcasts instead of showing an old man on screen.  
[事实] The speech is short, direct, and frames the choice as de Gaulle versus communist intimidation and totalitarianism.  
[事实] He dissolves parliament, calls elections, and warns that further disruption could lead him to use emergency powers under Article 16.

[26:00] **The Gaullist Counter-Demonstration**

[事实] A huge pro-Gaullist crowd gathers and marches down the Champs-Élysées, with estimates ranging up to several hundred thousand.  
[事实] The hosts compare the crowd to a “silent majority” and note that it includes conservative students as well as older supporters.  
[事实] The setting echoes de Gaulle’s 1944 liberation march down the same avenue.

[28:43] **The Movement Deflates**

[事实] The pro-Gaullist demonstration shocks radicals by revealing a large public constituency for order and for de Gaulle.  
[事实] The hosts argue that the approaching Whitsun holiday also helped weaken the movement, as people left Paris or paused the protests.  
[事实] The Sorbonne is cleared by June 13, and the occupied Odéon theater is cleared the next day.

[29:35] **Election Landslide and Political Failure**

[事实] De Gaulle frames the election as order versus communism.  
[事实] Mitterrand and Mendès France both lose their seats.  
[事实] De Gaulle’s party wins 353 of 486 seats, while the communists win 34 and the socialists 57.  
[推测] Politically, the hosts treat May 1968 as a cul-de-sac rather than a successful revolution.

[31:26] **Aftermath and Cultural Legacy**

[事实] De Gaulle steps down a year later, but the hosts say he was tired and may have done so anyway.  
[事实] Pompidou succeeds him, and the left does not immediately take power.  
[事实] The hosts debate whether May 1968’s real influence came through culture and ideas rather than politics.

[32:37] **Foucault and the Language of Power**

[事实] The hosts discuss Foucault’s later interest in prisons, surveillance, repression, and the state, especially in relation to Discipline and Punish.  
[事实] They describe the idea of “docile bodies” and institutions that pacify revolutionary energy.  
[事实] They argue that Foucault’s influence was especially powerful in America and the English-speaking world.

[36:07] **Conservative Reactions**

[事实] Roger Scruton is presented as a British student who witnessed the Paris unrest and later described the protesters as middle-class hooligans using Marxist jargon.  
[事实] The hosts say Scruton’s reaction helped shape his conservatism.  
[事实] They contrast Paris with Prague, where the suppression of the Prague Spring had far greater moral consequences for communism.

[37:31] **1968 in Global Perspective**

[事实] The hosts compare Paris with Prague, Mexico City, and American riots after Martin Luther King Jr.’s assassination.  
[事实] They argue that Paris remains famous partly because Paris is historically imagined as the home of revolution.  
[推测] The episode suggests May 1968 may have been the last time Parisian unrest reverberated globally in that older revolutionary style.

[38:59] **Memory, Later France, and the End of the Episode**

[事实] The hosts argue that later French presidencies and later riots are less internationally remembered than May 1968.  
[事实] They mention urban unrest in 2005 and say the language of 1968 meant little to many later rioters in the banlieues.  
[事实] They close by saying they may return to 1968 through American presidential politics and to French history through other subjects.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its combination of narrative tension and historical interpretation: de Gaulle’s disappearance, Baden-Baden, the army, and the radio speech are presented almost as a political thriller, while still being tied to longer French history.

[推测] Its strongest analytical point is the distinction between political failure and cultural afterlife. The hosts make clear that May 1968 did not win power in France, but they still treat its intellectual legacy as a serious question.

[推测] A limitation is that the discussion is strongly shaped by the hosts’ skeptical tone toward the protesters and toward later claims of 1968’s significance. Listeners wanting a more sympathetic account of the students, workers, or unions would need another perspective.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in de Gaulle, modern French politics, revolutionary memory, and the global meaning of 1968 rather than a granular social history of every group involved in the protests.
