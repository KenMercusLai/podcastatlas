# The Cultural Revolution: Mao, Youth Revolt, and the War on the Past

## 概览

This episode examines the Cultural Revolution as a catastrophe rooted in both Mao Zedong’s political struggle and a sincere, destructive ideology of revolutionary renewal. The discussion begins with the Red Guards’ 1966 assault on the “old” world, then moves backward to explain how the Chinese Communist revolution, the Great Leap Forward, Mao’s sidelining, and the Sino-Soviet split shaped the crisis.

Rana Mitter argues that the Cultural Revolution was not simply a coup or a burst of irrational violence. It combined Mao’s personal need to reassert authority, his fear of Soviet-style “revisionism,” a long-standing cult of Mao’s thought, resentment among young people and marginalized groups, and a radical rejection of China’s pre-revolutionary past.

The episode follows the movement from student mobilization and Mao worship to struggle sessions, torture, religious-style rituals, factional violence, military intervention, and post-Mao efforts to condemn the Cultural Revolution without fully blaming Mao. It ends by connecting the trauma of the period to Deng-era reform and to Xi Jinping’s later emphasis on order, control, poverty, and inequality.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **The Red Guards Declare War on the Old World**

[事实] The episode opens with posters in Beijing on 19 August 1966 calling for the destruction of old ideas, old culture, old customs, and old habits.
[事实] Red Guards targeted old street names, barbershops, high heels, flower beds, pets, monuments, classical literature, paintings, and temples.
[事实] A cited estimate says between 1.5 and 2 million people were killed, with many more lives ruined by denunciations, false confessions, struggle meetings, and persecution campaigns.
[推测] The opening frames the Cultural Revolution as an assault not only on people but on memory, social practice, and historical continuity.

[03:41] **Why Historians Find It Especially Chilling**

[事实] Rana Mitter says historians are particularly disturbed by the systematic removal, destruction, and erasure of the pre-revolutionary past.
[事实] He gives the example of Tibetan Lama temples being taken apart brick by brick and reused for mundane purposes.
[推测] The horror for historians lies in the Cultural Revolution’s attempt to make the past not merely rejected but almost invisible.

[06:23] **China’s Road to 1966**

[事实] Mitter traces the background through early 20th-century turmoil, foreign encroachment, Japanese invasion, civil war, and the Communist victory in 1949.
[事实] He describes the Great Leap Forward as a failed economic experiment that caused a massive famine, with 20 to 30 million deaths mentioned in the conversation.
[事实] After the Great Leap Forward, Mao was not formally disgraced but was pushed somewhat aside by colleagues who wanted less of him in daily leadership.
[推测] The Cultural Revolution emerges in the discussion as both Mao’s return to center stage and his attempt to prevent the revolution from becoming bureaucratic and moderate.

[09:16] **Mao’s Fear of Revisionism**

[事实] Mitter says Mao believed by the early 1960s that the Chinese revolution had lost its fire.
[事实] Mao saw Khrushchev’s denunciation of Stalin and the moderation of the Soviet Union as hostile signals with implications for his own position.
[事实] Mao feared China might become what he called a bourgeois revisionist revolution like the Soviet Union.
[推测] Mao’s anxiety combined ideological conviction with personal fear that he could suffer a fate like Stalin’s posthumous discrediting.

[11:06] **The Cult of Mao and the Meaning of Maoism**

[事实] Mitter says Mao had placed his own personality at the center of the Chinese revolution since the 1940s rectification movement.
[事实] During that movement, party recruits were pushed psychologically and assigned key texts, most of which were by Mao.
[事实] Maoism is described as including peasant revolution, guerrilla tactics, philosophical ideas, and the glorification of violent renewal.
[推测] The inability of other leaders to remove Mao is explained less by officeholding than by the sacred status attached to Mao’s thought.

[14:03] **Violence, the Gun, and the New Revolutionary Elite**

[事实] The conversation notes Mao’s phrase about political power growing from the barrel of a gun, while also stressing the second half: the party must control the gun.
[事实] Mitter says Mao saw bureaucratic privilege as a danger and criticized institutions such as the health ministry for serving urban elites.
[事实] The discussion links this concern to barefoot doctors, who were peasants given basic medical training for rural areas.
[推测] Mao’s attack on bureaucratic privilege gave ideological cover to people who already felt excluded or resentful.

[16:03] **Resentment from Below and Xenophobia from Above**

[事实] Mitter says some people from “bad class backgrounds,” including families linked to the former Nationalists, had reasons to resent the Communist social order.
[事实] The Cultural Revolution is described as one of the most xenophobic periods in Chinese history, when foreign languages, clothes, or associations could become politically dangerous.
[事实] The Sino-Soviet split made fear of Soviet attack a major concern for parts of the leadership.
[推测] The movement drew energy from both domestic grievances and a siege mentality toward the outside world.

[18:17] **The Yangtze Swim and the Theatre Review**

[事实] Mao’s swim in the Yangtze is presented as a political signal of physical vigor and authority.
[事实] Mitter connects Mao’s emphasis on bodily strength to social Darwinist ideas that had influenced many East Asian intellectuals in the 20th century.
[事实] A theatre review attacking a Ming-dynasty play about a dismissed official became politically charged because it was read as an analogy for Peng Dehuai’s removal after criticizing the Great Leap Forward.
[推测] Small cultural signals mattered because in Mao’s China art, criticism, and political loyalty were tightly fused.

[22:02] **From Summer Campaign to National Upheaval**

[事实] Mitter says Mao likely did not initially know he was launching ten years of turmoil, violence, and destruction.
[事实] The initial idea was to mobilize educated youth over the summer to attack seniors, teachers, and authorities who were seen as complacent or revisionist.
[事实] The movement quickly grew far beyond what Mao expected.
[推测] The Cultural Revolution became uncontrollable because Mao’s top-down signal unlocked bottom-up resentments across schools, workplaces, and local society.

[25:05] **Youth Rebellion and Social Fractures**

[事实] Mitter says young people were told by the ruler of the country to challenge those who claimed authority over them, especially teachers.
[事实] Workers, students, and marginalized groups could redirect personal and social grievances against managers, teachers, and local elites.
[事实] In some areas, people previously stigmatized by class background became among the most radical rebel groups.
[推测] The episode presents teenage rebellion as politically authorized, socially explosive, and locally specific rather than simply commanded from Beijing.

[28:12] **Struggle Sessions and the “Airplane Position”**

[事实] Mitter describes the opening phase as including some of the most brutal acts under Mao’s rule.
[事实] Victims such as teachers and bureaucrats were humiliated before crowds, forced to wear accusatory signs, and made to hold painful stress positions for hours.
[事实] The “airplane position” is described as a form of torture used in re-education and denunciation.
[推测] The violence worked by reversing authority in public: those who had once commanded others were made physically helpless before them.

[30:35] **Free Travel, Mass Rallies, and Mao Worship**

[事实] Mao invited students from across China to Beijing, and huge numbers came, including mass rallies in Tiananmen Square.
[事实] Mitter describes “exchange” travel, with free train travel allowing young people to move around China, meet others, and participate in the revolutionary moment.
[事实] Red Guards were encouraged to think of themselves not as students or workers but as soldiers.
[事实] One young man recorded that seeing Mao made him change that day into his birthday.
[推测] The rallies functioned like a conversion experience, giving political mobilization a religious emotional charge.

[34:38] **Mangoes as Sacred Relics**

[事实] The hosts discuss mangoes given to Mao by Pakistan’s foreign minister in 1968 and then distributed as symbolic gifts.
[事实] At one factory, workers displayed a mango, read Mao quotations, and later consumed water made from the fruit when it began to rot.
[事实] Replicas of Mao’s mangoes were sent around factories.
[推测] The mango episode shows how Mao’s authority turned ordinary objects into relic-like carriers of political devotion.

[35:49] **Religion Inside Revolutionary Politics**

[事实] Mitter agrees that Mao worship had religious qualities and links it to Chinese syncretic traditions.
[事实] He mentions Christian missionary influence, the Taiping precedent, and Buddhist millenarian traditions as part of the wider religious vocabulary available in China.
[事实] He contrasts the Cultural Revolution’s popular appeal with Chiang Kai-shek’s New Life Movement, which he says failed to take off.
[推测] The Cultural Revolution succeeded emotionally because it fused ideology, ritual, purification, and mass participation.

[38:12] **Was the Cultural Revolution Unique?**

[事实] Mitter cites the argument that the Cultural Revolution was unique because a Communist Party leader mobilized forces to overthrow his own party’s bureaucratic order.
[事实] He also identifies the May Fourth Movement and the New Culture Movement as important precedents for rejecting China’s inherited Confucian past.
[事实] Mao’s generation had seen China’s weakness, foreign domination, internal warlordism, and republican instability as evidence that tradition had failed.
[推测] China’s ancient civilization did not prevent iconoclasm; in this telling, its weight helped provoke a radical desire to smash the inherited order.

[42:52] **The Iron Cage of Tradition**

[事实] Mitter says Mao wrote about a young woman, Miss Zhao, who killed herself because she was being forced into marriage.
[事实] Mao used the image of Confucian society as an iron cage that could not be escaped gently.
[事实] Lu Xun is also mentioned as using similar metaphors and accusing Confucianism of cannibalism.
[推测] The Cultural Revolution’s destructiveness drew on an older conviction that Chinese tradition had to be broken, not reformed.

[44:07] **Extreme Violence and Local Escalation**

[事实] The hosts discuss reports of ritual cannibalism in Guangxi, including attacks on class enemies.
[事实] Mitter says at least some of the evidence in the cited research appears accurate, while noting that cannibalism is also a recurring trope in Chinese accounts of extreme disorder.
[事实] He gives examples of youth breaking into arsenals, seizing tanks, and even attempting to make a nuclear bomb from an experimental station.
[推测] These examples show how revolutionary permission, local factionalism, and access to weapons could push violence beyond any planned campaign.

[47:38] **The Army Takes Control**

[事实] Mitter periodizes the classic Red Guard phase from 1966 to 1969.
[事实] Mao eventually judged that three years of destruction was enough, and the army was brought in to shut the Red Guard phase down.
[事实] Lin Biao, the defense minister and Mao ally, played a central role before later being accused of a coup attempt and dying in a 1971 plane crash in Outer Mongolia.
[推测] The movement’s later phase shifted from youth revolt to a more militarized and factional struggle inside the state.

[49:29] **Nixon, Zhou Enlai, and the Gang of Four**

[事实] Mitter says Zhou Enlai wanted to bring in the Americans because China could not remain isolated and needed foreign investment to repair Cultural Revolution damage.
[事实] The group later known as the Gang of Four, including Jiang Qing, opposed opening to the Americans.
[事实] From 1969 to Mao’s death in 1976, there was less street violence than in the Red Guard phase, but arrests, executions, and score-settling continued under military control.
[推测] Foreign policy debates became part of the same palace politics that shaped the Cultural Revolution’s final years.

[50:23] **Why Senior Leaders Did Not Resist**

[事实] The discussion notes that Zhou Enlai’s adopted son and daughter were tortured to death, and Deng Xiaoping’s son Deng Pufang was thrown from a window and left in a wheelchair.
[事实] Mitter says many surviving victims and perpetrators remain reluctant to speak in detail because of trauma or guilt.
[事实] He points to Liu Shaoqi, president of the People’s Republic of China, who was removed and left to die neglected in a windowless basement.
[事实] Mitter says Mao’s charisma created motivational and coercive power that other leaders lacked.
[推测] Even high-ranking figures were trapped by unpredictability, fear, and Mao’s personal cult.

[53:23] **After Mao: Blaming the Gang of Four**

[事实] The Cultural Revolution ended quickly after Mao’s death in 1976, when Hua Guofeng had the Gang of Four arrested.
[事实] Jiang Qing tried to shift blame onto Mao, saying she had only acted as his instrument.
[事实] Mitter says the post-Mao party condemned the Cultural Revolution as a grave error and crime while avoiding central personal blame on Mao.
[事实] Scar literature from the late 1970s to early 1980s allowed some public expression of suffering.
[推测] The party “sterilized” Maoism by rejecting the Cultural Revolution’s radicalism while preserving Mao’s usable symbolic authority.

[56:29] **Xi Jinping and the Long Shadow of the Cultural Revolution**

[事实] Mitter says Xi Jinping spent teenage years in Shaanxi because his father Xi Zhongxun had fallen out of political favor.
[事实] Mitter connects Xi’s later politics to fear of disorder and chaos from the 1960s and 1970s.
[事实] He also says Xi’s time in a very poor region may have shaped his concern with poverty reduction and economic inequality.
[推测] The Cultural Revolution’s legacy in contemporary China may include both stronger surveillance and control, and a continuing official emphasis on poverty and inequality.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it avoids reducing the Cultural Revolution to madness or simple tyranny. It presents the disaster as a convergence of Mao’s ambition, ideological conviction, youth mobilization, social resentment, historical iconoclasm, and institutional breakdown.

[推测] Its strongest sections are the explanations of Mao worship, the religious quality of political ritual, and the link between May Fourth anti-traditionalism and Cultural Revolution iconoclasm. These parts help listeners see why the movement could feel meaningful to participants while producing catastrophic violence.

[推测] The limitation is breadth: the hosts acknowledge that many topics are compressed into one hour, so some regional experiences, victim testimony, and policy details remain only briefly sketched. The episode is best suited to listeners who want a historically grounded overview of the Cultural Revolution and its long afterlife in Chinese politics.
