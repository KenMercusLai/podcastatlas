# From the Alma to Balaclava: The Road to the Charge of the Light Brigade

## Overview

This episode traces the opening phase of the Allied invasion of Crimea in 1854, beginning with Britain and France’s decision to attack Sevastopol after the immediate Russian threat to Constantinople had already receded. The intended quick destruction of Russia’s Black Sea Fleet was undermined from the outset by poor intelligence, inadequate logistics, confused command arrangements, and uncertainty over where the army should land.

After an inefficient but unopposed landing, the Allies marched south and won the Battle of the Alma. Their victory owed more to French initiative, British infantry discipline, and the superior Minié rifle than to effective high command. Russian errors were even more damaging, especially Prince Menshikov’s mistaken belief that his defensive position was virtually impregnable.

The commanders then failed to exploit their victory by advancing immediately on the weakly defended Sevastopol. The delay allowed the Russians to strengthen the city and block its harbour, turning a proposed raid into a prolonged siege.

The episode ends at Balaclava, the vulnerable British supply port. With Russian forces massing nearby, strained supply lines, exposed redoubts, and bitter divisions among the cavalry commanders, the stage is set for the Thin Red Line and the charges of the Heavy and Light Brigades.

## Section-by-section summary

[02:57] **The Light Brigade’s long-awaited war**

[Fact] Tennyson’s poem introduces the Charge of the Light Brigade as a heroic but devastating episode in British military history.

[Fact] Lord Cardigan and most of his men had never experienced active service and were eager to put years of cavalry training into practice.

[Fact] Britain and France entered the war to halt Russia’s advance toward Constantinople, but Ottoman forces had already forced the Russians back from the Danube before the Allies could fight.

[Fact] Newspaper coverage, particularly that of *The Times* correspondent William Howard Russell, intensified public pressure for the expedition to achieve something dramatic.

[08:10] **An expedition sails without a coherent plan**

[Fact] On 7 September, roughly 60,000 Allied troops sailed from disease-ridden Varna toward Crimea, while around 30,000 remained behind because transport capacity was insufficient.

[Fact] The British Heavy Brigade was among the units initially left behind, leaving only the Light Brigade available during the opening campaign.

[Fact] British and French commanders Lord Raglan and Saint-Arnaud could communicate only through intermediaries because Raglan had lost an arm and the terminally ill Saint-Arnaud was confined by severe pain.

[Fact] Although the original threat to Constantinople had passed, the Allies decided to attack Sevastopol to destroy Russia’s Black Sea Fleet and secure the Ottoman capital for the longer term.

[Inference] The expedition’s momentum was driven partly by strategic reasoning and partly by the political and emotional need to justify the war.

[13:13] **A campaign built on inadequate intelligence**

[Fact] Allied commanders lacked reliable knowledge about Russian troop numbers, Crimean geography and climate, Sevastopol’s defences, and suitable landing sites.

[Fact] They assumed the operation would be completed quickly and therefore made little provision for a winter campaign.

[Fact] A proposed landing beach proved unsuitable because the officer sent to inspect it was severely short-sighted.

[Fact] Raglan personally reconnoitred the Crimean coast and selected Calamita Bay, an undefended beach approximately 40 miles north of Sevastopol.

[Inference] The choice reduced the immediate risk of an opposed landing but sacrificed any realistic prospect of surprising Sevastopol.

[17:45] **The landing at Calamita Bay**

[Fact] The Allied landing began on 14 September, the anniversary of Napoleon’s entry into Moscow in 1812.

[Fact] The French completed their landing efficiently in about one day, while the British required approximately five days because they lacked carts and struggled to unload horses and equipment.

[Fact] The only Russian official present was a customs officer who demanded that the disease-ridden invaders enter quarantine; the French ignored him.

[Fact] Once assembled, some 60,000 Allied soldiers began marching south toward Sevastopol in conspicuous columns of French blue and British red.

[20:22] **Disease, thirst, and restrained cavalry**

[Fact] Cholera, dysentery, heat, and lack of water quickly eroded the army’s initial enthusiasm, with sick men collapsing along the route.

[Fact] Russian forces avoided an immediate attack but shadowed the Allies with Cossack scouts and destroyed local supplies.

[Fact] Raglan refused to use the Light Brigade for extensive reconnaissance because its horses and men had been weakened by disease and he feared exposing it to superior Russian cavalry.

[Fact] Cardigan resented this restraint, while his hostile brother-in-law Lord Lucan closely supervised and repeatedly quarrelled with him.

[25:29] **The Light Brigade’s humiliating first encounter**

[Fact] Near the Bulganic River, the Light Brigade encountered approximately 2,000 Cossacks and was outnumbered by at least two to one.

[Fact] Cardigan and Lucan argued over the brigade’s formation while the Cossacks approached and began firing.

[Fact] Raglan ordered the brigade to withdraw after observing substantial Russian infantry and artillery beyond the next ridge.

[Fact] Two Light Dragoons were wounded, becoming the first British casualties of the Crimean campaign described in the episode.

[Fact] Although Raglan’s withdrawal order probably saved the brigade, the cavalry blamed Lucan and mockingly renamed him “Lord Look-on.”

[30:29] **The Russians await the Allies at the Alma**

[Fact] Prince Menshikov established a formidable defensive position on the heights above the River Alma and was confident that the Allies would be destroyed.

[Fact] Thirty young women from Sevastopol were invited to picnic on the heights and watch the expected Russian victory.

[Fact] As the battle turned against Russia, the women were told to flee, abandoning their food and champagne.

[Fact] By the end of the battle, the Russian army was retreating toward Sevastopol.

[37:28] **Victory through courage, weapons, and enemy mistakes**

[Fact] The episode characterizes the Battle of the Alma as a succession of serious mistakes by commanders on both sides, with Russia losing because its errors were worse.

[Fact] Raglan issued ambiguous orders and rode so far forward that he became separated from his army, while the gravely ill Saint-Arnaud relied heavily on opiates merely to remain in the saddle.

[Fact] Menshikov wrongly assumed that cliffs protecting his left flank were impassable; French forces under Pierre Bosquet climbed them and struck the Russian flank.

[Fact] Colin Campbell’s Highland regiments displayed discipline under fire and used the newly introduced Minié rifle to devastating effect against Russian troops equipped with inferior muskets.

[Fact] The combination of the French flanking movement and British rifle fire forced the Russians from the field.

[43:17] **A victory the cavalry could not exploit**

[Fact] The Light Brigade remained inactive throughout the three-hour battle because Raglan considered the ground unsuitable and feared committing it without the Heavy Brigade.

[Fact] When the Russian retreat became a rout, Lucan wanted to unleash the cavalry, but Raglan repeatedly forbade him from pursuing.

[Fact] Cavalry officers nevertheless blamed Lucan for their inaction, further damaging his reputation.

[Inference] Raglan’s caution preserved the Light Brigade but also deepened the frustration and resentment that would shape later decisions at Balaclava.

[46:45] **Captain Nolan and the doctrine of decisive cavalry**

[Fact] Captain Louis Edward Nolan, a member of Raglan’s staff, publicly condemned the failure to pursue the defeated Russians.

[Fact] Nolan had served in Austrian cavalry, studied cavalry systems across Europe and Russia, and written influential books on horse training and cavalry tactics.

[Fact] He advocated kinder treatment of horses, designed an improved saddle, and argued that properly handled cavalry could successfully attack infantry and artillery.

[Fact] Nolan despised the passivity of Raglan and regarded the aristocratic Cardigan and Lucan as emblematic of incompetent purchased command.

[Fact] Unable to attack Raglan openly, Nolan directed much of his criticism at Lucan, and Russell’s reporting amplified that criticism.

[53:00] **The squandered chance to capture Sevastopol**

[Fact] Immediately after the Alma, Sevastopol had only about 5,000 troops and weak land-facing defences; Menshikov initially believed it should be evacuated.

[Fact] The Allies remained on the battlefield tending casualties instead of advancing directly on the city.

[Fact] The delay allowed Sevastopol’s defenders to repair their fortifications and sink five ships and two frigates across the harbour entrance, blocking an Allied naval attack.

[Fact] By 17 October, the Allies had begun a formal bombardment and become committed to a prolonged siege.

[Fact] Saint-Arnaud died after relinquishing command and was replaced by the more capable François Canrobert.

[Inference] The failure to follow the Alma victory transformed a potentially brief campaign into the central and exceptionally lengthy siege of the war.

[56:29] **Balaclava and the fragile British supply line**

[Fact] Balaclava became the port connecting the Royal Navy with the British siege force outside Sevastopol.

[Fact] Raglan committed roughly 20,000 men to the siege but only 5,000 to Balaclava’s defence, despite the port’s essential logistical role.

[Fact] There was no railway or telegraph, and the single metalled Vorontsov Road was linked to Balaclava by rough tracks vulnerable to autumn mud.

[Fact] Six redoubts were constructed along the Causeway Heights and staffed by about 1,000 Ottoman soldiers under British artillery officers, but only four received naval guns.

[Inference] The imbalance between the siege force and the port’s defenders left the entire British campaign dependent on a dangerously exposed logistical corridor.

[60:19] **The cavalry camp and Cardigan’s yacht**

[Fact] Both the Light and Heavy Brigades were now stationed near Balaclava in cold, poorly supplied conditions with inadequate tents and insufficient horse fodder.

[Fact] Lucan required the cavalry to stand to horse before dawn, while Raglan continued to prevent it from conducting aggressive reconnaissance.

[Fact] Cardigan worked with his men by day but slept and dined aboard his yacht *Dryad*, where he enjoyed a French chef and feather beds with his companion Hubert de Burgh, known as “the Squire.”

[Fact] Other officers mocked Cardigan as the “noble yachtsman,” and his comforts damaged his standing among the troops.

[64:22] **The Battle of Balaclava begins**

[Fact] Russian forces began concentrating beyond the Causeway Heights on 18 October, but Raglan refused to send the cavalry against them.

[Fact] By 24 October, approximately 25,000 Russian troops with infantry, cavalry, and 78 guns had assembled to attack Balaclava.

[Fact] Capturing the port would sever the British siege force’s link to outside supplies and could end the siege of Sevastopol.

[Fact] Before dawn on 25 October, the sound of guns from the redoubts announced the beginning of the Battle of Balaclava.

[Fact] The episode ends before the Thin Red Line and the charges of the Heavy and Light Brigades, reserving those events for the next instalment.

## Podcast review/summary

The episode’s chief strength is its explanation of how the celebrated Charge of the Light Brigade emerged from a wider chain of strategic confusion, logistical weakness, personal rivalry, and missed opportunities. The narrative connects operational decisions with vivid human details, from the abandoned Russian picnic to Cardigan dining comfortably aboard his yacht.

Its account of the Alma is especially effective because it avoids presenting victory as the simple product of national courage. French manoeuvring, Scottish infantry discipline, the Minié rifle, and Russian misjudgements all receive attention, while the shortcomings of Allied commanders remain central.

The humorous treatment of Cardigan, Lucan, Raglan, Nolan, and Saint-Arnaud makes a complicated campaign accessible, though it can blur the distinction between comic characterization and formal military assessment. The episode also stops at the opening of Balaclava, so its central promise—the Charge of the Light Brigade—remains unresolved.

[Inference] This instalment is best suited to listeners interested in military history as a study of command, technology, logistics, reputation, and institutional failure, rather than as a simple chronicle of battlefield heroism.
