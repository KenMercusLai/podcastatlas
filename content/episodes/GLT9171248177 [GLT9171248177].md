# Brexit Parallels: Britain’s Island History and European Entanglements

## 概览

Dominic Sandbrook and Tom Holland discuss Brexit through historical analogies rather than arguing for or against it. They frame the episode as a historian’s tour of earlier “Brexits”: moments when Britain was separated from, detached from, excluded by, or imagined itself apart from continental Europe.

The discussion moves from deep geography to political, religious, imperial, and wartime examples. Doggerland explains Britain’s island condition; Roman Britain, King John, and the Reformation explore sovereignty and supranational authority; the Seven Years War, Napoleon, and “splendid isolation” examine Britain’s global and maritime self-image.

Their main conclusion is that none of the parallels is exact, but some illuminate why Brexit arguments drew so heavily on history. They identify Doggerland, the Reformation, and Dunkirk or the Second World War as the most powerful “mirrors” for understanding British identity and its complicated relationship with Europe.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Setting Up Brexit as a Historical Question**

[事实] The hosts introduce the episode around the uncertainty of Brexit and say they do not know whether there will be a deal.
[事实] They explicitly say they will not debate whether Brexit is good or bad, and will instead focus on historical comparisons.
[事实] Tom presents the idea of a “top 10” list of earlier or analogous Brexits.
[推测] The episode treats Brexit less as a policy argument than as a test case for Britain’s long-running historical self-image.

[02:00] **Doggerland and the First Island Brexit**

[事实] Tom identifies the drowning of Doggerland about 8,200 years ago as the first “Brexit.”
[事实] Britain had once been attached to the continent, but rising sea levels and a tsunami linked to landslips near Norway helped make it an island.
[事实] The hosts discuss whether Britain’s island geography underpins a lasting consciousness of separation from mainland Europe.
[事实] Tom says Britain is too close to Europe to separate completely, but the impulse to do so is always present.
[推测] Doggerland becomes the episode’s foundational example because it makes geography the starting point for later political and cultural separateness.

[05:04] **Roman Britain and the Collapse of Imperial Connection**

[事实] The hosts discuss comparisons between the EU and the Roman Empire, including a listener’s reference to A. C. Grayling.
[事实] They reject Carausius as a full parallel because he did not want to leave the Roman Empire but wanted power within it.
[事实] Around 409 or 410, the Britons are described as defecting from Roman rule and living independently from Roman laws.
[事实] Tom says the end of Roman rule damaged Britain’s economy, with coinage disappearing for centuries.
[事实] He argues that Britain’s later Germanic language, unlike the Romance languages of former Roman provinces such as France, Spain, and Italy, shows how seismic the break was.

[10:02] **King John, Rome, and Humiliating Re-entry**

[事实] King John loses Normandy and other continental Angevin lands, with Normandy falling in 1204.
[事实] After a dispute over the Archbishop of Canterbury, Pope Innocent III imposes an interdict in 1208 and excommunicates John in 1209.
[事实] In 1213, under threat of French invasion, John submits and effectively makes England a papal fief.
[事实] The hosts compare this jokingly to a hard Brexit going wrong, followed by Britain rejoining under worse terms.
[推测] This parallel is used to show that breaking from supranational authority can end in dependence rather than sovereignty.

[12:45] **The Reformation as the Strongest Sovereignty Parallel**

[事实] The hosts trace England’s earlier religious ties to Rome through the Synod of Whitby in 664 and the Norman Conquest.
[事实] They identify Henry VIII’s Reformation in the 1530s as a major Brexit comparison.
[事实] Parliament declared England an empire, rejected the authority of foreign priests and prelates, and made the king supreme head of the English church.
[事实] Dominic says this created a lasting sense of English distinctiveness, later reinforced by anti-Catholic memory around events such as the Spanish Armada and Gunpowder Plot.
[推测] The Reformation is treated as one of the clearest parallels because it combined sovereignty, law, national identity, and a break with continental authority.

[15:21] **How English Was the English Reformation?**

[事实] Tom notes Diarmaid MacCulloch’s point that the Reformation was pan-European, unlike Brexit, which was distinctively British.
[事实] The English Reformation followed developments in Germany and Switzerland and had Protestant allies in the Dutch Republic.
[事实] Dominic argues that the English version was distinctive because it was driven by Henry VIII’s marital and political needs and became bound up with monarchy.
[事实] He says English Protestantism became tied to patriotic loyalty to the state in a way that differed from some continental examples.
[推测] The hosts complicate the Reformation analogy by showing that even a famous English break from Rome was also part of a broader European movement.

[17:17] **Cromwell, Milton, and England as a Model for Europe**

[事实] The hosts discuss the Protectorate under Oliver Cromwell after the execution of Charles I.
[事实] They say the revolutionaries were defying the wider European order by killing a king.
[事实] Dominic argues that Cromwell’s England had a stronger ideological belief in being chosen by God and becoming a “new Jerusalem.”
[事实] Tom compares this to some Brexiteers’ hope that Brexit might trigger a wider European chain reaction.
[事实] Milton is discussed as Cromwell’s secretary and as someone explaining England’s revolution to Europe.

[20:27] **The Seven Years War and Global Britain**

[事实] Dominic introduces “global Britain” and the Seven Years War, from 1756 to 1763, as a key moment in Britain’s global self-image.
[事实] The hosts say Britain’s major theatres of war included North America, Canada, India, and the oceans.
[事实] Tom says this helped establish the idea that Britain looks to the seas.
[事实] They also note that Britain still had continental links and allies, including Frederick the Great and the Hanoverian connection.
[推测] The Seven Years War is used to explain the emotional appeal of a maritime, global Britain, even if the hosts doubt that most Brexit voters were motivated by detailed imperial nostalgia.

[24:01] **Napoleon, Exclusion, and British Resistance to Rational Europe**

[事实] The hosts describe the Napoleonic Wars as a kind of Brexit forced on Britain by Napoleon’s continental system.
[事实] Napoleon excluded British goods from European markets, while Britain’s army could not easily land on the mainland except through Portugal.
[事实] They say this analogy runs in reverse because Britain wanted access to Europe while Napoleon wanted to keep it out.
[事实] Dominic discusses Napoleon’s association with rational systems such as the Napoleonic Code and decimalization, from which Britain long stood apart.
[推测] This section presents Britain’s anti-Napoleonic identity as part of a broader suspicion of continental standardization and centralizing rationalism.

[27:55] **Splendid Isolation and the Victorian Myth of Apartness**

[事实] Dominic describes “splendid isolation” as a late 19th-century theme rather than a single event.
[事实] Britain saw itself as a top imperial power, with the empire and the defense of India central to its political imagination.
[事实] The hosts mention Britain staying out of the Franco-Prussian War of 1870-71.
[事实] They also acknowledge that Britain was still involved in European politics, including as a guarantor of Belgium and through dealings with Bismarck.
[推测] The hosts treat splendid isolation as partly myth and partly aspiration, but one that left a deep mark on British political imagination.

[30:10] **Historical Myth-Making on Both Sides**

[事实] The hosts say Britain has always been part of the European system while also feeling pulled away from it.
[事实] They argue that both Brexit supporters and Remainers intensify their arguments through historical myth-making.
[事实] Brexiteers emphasize British difference, while Remainers emphasize Britain’s European entanglements.
[推测] The discussion suggests that Brexit history is not just about facts, but about which inherited myths people choose to foreground.

[31:01] **Ireland Leaving the United Kingdom**

[事实] A listener compares the ERG versus Theresa May to de Valera versus Michael Collins.
[事实] The hosts discuss Ireland leaving the United Kingdom as leaving a union of nations and a kind of single market.
[事实] Tom says Irish independence was economically difficult and that Ireland remained within the pull of the British economy until joining the Common Market.
[事实] Dominic argues that once a country secedes, the new status quo becomes hard to reverse.
[事实] They note a major difference: Ireland’s relationship with Britain carried a legacy of overt imperialism, which is not equivalent to Britain’s relationship with the EU.

[34:24] **Dunkirk and the Myth of Standing Alone**

[事实] Tom calls Dunkirk a literal Brexit because British forces left European soil.
[事实] He notes that the evacuation also involved French and Belgian soldiers and depended on French and Belgian forces shielding the retreat.
[事实] The hosts discuss the “very well then, alone” mythology and its large role in British self-understanding.
[事实] They say comparisons between the EU and Nazi Germany are not accurate, even though such comparisons have appeared in Brexit discourse.
[推测] Dunkirk matters less as a precise analogy than as an emotional memory of survival, isolation, and national defiance.

[36:40] **Choosing the Most Revealing Parallels**

[事实] The hosts agree that none of the historical parallels is fully accurate.
[事实] They choose Doggerland, the Reformation, and Dunkirk or the Second World War as the most interesting mirrors.
[事实] Tom says Doggerland matters because island geography is fundamental.
[事实] They say the Reformation is strong because it involved rejecting a body of laws, assumptions, and continental authorities.
[事实] Dominic argues that the Second World War matters because Britain tells the 20th-century story differently from countries that were occupied or defeated.

[39:19] **Returning to Roman Britain and the No-Deal Fear**

[事实] The hosts say Roman Britain might become a better comparison if Brexit produced catastrophic outcomes.
[事实] Tom explains that Roman commanders stripped Britain of garrisons while trying to become emperor on the continent.
[事实] By the early 5th century, Britain had few Roman forces left but still had to pay taxes.
[事实] They compare this loosely to paying budget contributions without receiving enough return.
[推测] The Roman example is used as a warning scenario about institutional collapse, rather than as the main explanation for Brexit.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s value lies in turning a heated political topic into a historical exercise. The hosts avoid direct advocacy and instead test analogies, showing why some comparisons illuminate British identity while others collapse under scrutiny.

Its strongest moments come when it separates historical reality from historical memory. Doggerland, the Reformation, splendid isolation, and Dunkirk are not treated as simple precedents for Brexit, but as stories that shaped how Britons imagine their country’s relationship with Europe.

The limitation is that the format is deliberately playful and selective. Many comparisons are introduced quickly, and some are jokes as much as arguments, so the episode works better as an interpretive essay than as a comprehensive history of Britain and Europe.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners interested in British identity, political myth, and the historical imagination behind Brexit, rather than listeners looking for policy analysis of trade, treaties, or the mechanics of EU withdrawal.
