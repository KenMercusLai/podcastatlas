# Replaying History in Real Time on Social Media

## 概览

Marketplace Tech interviews content creator Manny Morota, whose X accounts recount events from 25, 50, and 100 years ago as though they were unfolding in the present. His minute-by-minute recreation of September 2001 recently attracted hundreds of thousands of new followers.

Morota manually researches historical newspapers, archived websites, and wire-service reports, publishing 10–20 stories per day. He argues that social media’s real-time format can make history more immediate, allowing audiences to encounter both unfolding facts and contemporary confusion without the clarity of hindsight.

The project resonates differently across generations: older users recover details and emotions they had forgotten, while younger users gain insight into what their parents experienced. Morota hopes this approach can shift X toward accurate, educational discussion rather than conspiracy theories, despite the platform’s toxicity.

## 分段落总结

[01:05] **Turning social media into a historical news feed**

[事实] Manny Morota went viral by recounting news from 25 years ago on X as if it were happening in real time.

[事实] He also publishes day-by-day accounts of events from 50 and 100 years ago while working as a law clerk for a nonprofit.

[事实] Morota wants older audiences to remember past events and younger people to experience periods they did not live through.

[02:25] **A fully manual research and publishing process**

[事实] Morota does not use scheduling or automation; he researches and posts everything manually.

[事实] His sources include century-old and 50-year-old newspapers, archived websites accessed through the Wayback Machine, and reporting from CNN, AP, and Reuters.

[事实] He typically publishes 10–20 stories per day to convey the broader mood of a historical period, combining major events with international, religious, and pop-culture news.

[推测] The breadth of his selections helps reconstruct the texture of everyday news rather than reducing an era to its most famous event.

[03:05] **Why real-time social media suits historical storytelling**

[事实] Morota chose Twitter, now X, because people already use it to follow breaking news in real time.

[事实] He presents historical developments through the same stream of incremental updates used for contemporary stories.

[事实] During the September 11 recreation, he posted roughly twice per minute between the first plane strike and the second tower’s collapse.

[事实] The feed included both accurate reports and misinformation circulating at the time, recreating the uncertainty people originally experienced.

[推测] This format can produce a stronger sense of immediacy than a retrospective documentary because audiences receive fragmented information without advance knowledge of how events connect.

[04:48] **Platform revenue and the ethics of monetizing tragedy**

[事实] X’s previous creator revenue-sharing program ended on September 7, and Morota was accepted into a replacement program whose future rewards remained uncertain.

[事实] He described the approximate compensation rate as $20 per million views.

[事实] Morota felt uncomfortable earning money from engagement with his September 11 coverage.

[事实] He said any revenue from that coverage would be donated to Tunnel to Towers, a charity serving September 11 survivors and others affected by the attacks.

[05:28] **Different generations respond through memory and discovery**

[事实] Older millennials told Morota that the feed reminded them of events they had forgotten or news that was overshadowed by September 11 coverage.

[事实] Younger Gen Z users said the project helped them relate to the emotional experience of their parents’ generation.

[事实] Morota believes younger audiences should understand the horror, uncertainty, confusion, and fear that persisted after the attacks.

[推测] The project functions as a form of intergenerational historical memory, connecting personal recollection with audiences who have no firsthand experience of the period.

[08:17] **Creating an educational space on a toxic platform**

[事实] The host characterizes X as combative and frequently described as toxic, while noting that Morota has created a distinct community around his work.

[事实] Morota hopes his coverage can make discussion on X more educational and less conspiratorial.

[事实] He specifically contrasts his historical account with discredited theories alleging that the United States or another government engineered the September 11 attacks.

[事实] He wants to attract people sincerely interested in learning while minimizing participation from users who bring toxicity into the shared discussion.

[推测] The project suggests that a platform’s dominant culture does not entirely determine how individual communities use it.

[09:32] **Future historical events and continuing research**

[事实] Morota plans to cover Charles Lindbergh’s 1927 transatlantic flight on his century-old history account.

[事实] For his 2002 timeline, he is researching the buildup to the Iraq War, including claims about weapons of mass destruction and appeals to the United Nations.

[事实] He also expects to cover the October 2002 D.C. Beltway sniper attacks.

[事实] At the time of the interview, he was researching the years 2002, 1977, and 1927.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode offers a concise and compelling example of social media being used for public history. Its strongest insight is that chronology and delivery format shape historical understanding: receiving reports one fragment at a time can restore uncertainty that conventional retrospectives often erase.

The discussion also addresses the ethical tensions surrounding historical content, including misinformation, platform toxicity, and revenue generated from traumatic events. Morota’s decision to donate proceeds from the September 11 coverage gives the monetization question a concrete response, though the broader economics of sustaining such labor-intensive work remain largely unexplored.

[推测] This episode will be most useful to listeners interested in digital media, public history, online communities, and new forms of historical storytelling. Its main limitation is its short length: it introduces questions about sourcing, editorial judgment, archival accuracy, and exposure to disturbing material without examining them in depth.
