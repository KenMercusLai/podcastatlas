# Sell Me a Sasquatch: Naming the Planet Money Game

## 概览

This episode follows Planet Money’s attempt to turn its economics-inspired card game into a mass-market retail product. The game is based on asymmetric information from “The Market for Lemons,” but the team learns that good mechanics are only half the job: the name, theme, and box appeal may decide whether big retailers and shoppers notice it.

Exploding Kittens presents several possible themes, including misfit mascots, magical creatures, and cats as salespeople. The Planet Money team is drawn to “Sell Me a Sasquatch,” partly because it feels memorable, fun, and nostalgic for millennial shoppers.

The episode then complicates that choice with international market concerns: European customers may not know what a Sasquatch is. After trying alternatives, the team returns to “Sell Me a Sasquatch” for the U.S. edition and invites listeners to submit creature ideas for the final game.

## 分段落总结

[00:31] **From Yacht to Yahtzee**

[事实] The episode opens with the story that Yahtzee began as a dice game called “Yacht,” and its commercial success depended partly on getting a better name.
[事实] The hosts use this story to frame the central issue: a strong or weak title can determine whether a good game succeeds.
[推测] The anecdote sets up naming as a business decision rather than a cosmetic detail.

[01:33] **The Planet Money Game Concept**

[事实] Planet Money is working with Exploding Kittens to design a game inspired by the Nobel Prize-winning economics paper “The Market for Lemons.”
[事实] The game uses partial information in card trading, allowing players to hide parts of a deal and create distrust, deception, and chaos.
[事实] The team intentionally chose a mass-appeal party game instead of a complex educational economics game.
[推测] The goal is to make economics feel experiential and entertaining, while also gaining access to big-box retail reporting.

[03:01] **Why Naming Matters**

[事实] Game consultant Jamie Wolanski previously praised the game mechanic but warned that the name and theme were critical.
[事实] The hosts say this episode focuses on learning what it takes to name and theme a game for major retailers like Walmart and Target.
[事实] They also say the episode will end with a name, a theme, and an invitation for listeners to contribute ideas.

[05:40] **Prototype Versus Product**

[事实] The team had done extensive playtesting and had a working rule set, but they did not yet have a sellable retail product.
[事实] Exploding Kittens’ process was to make sure the game worked mechanically before worrying about theme, card art, and name.
[推测] This separates gameplay validation from market packaging, reflecting how retail products need both substance and shelf appeal.

[07:04] **The High-Stakes Naming Meeting**

[事实] Exploding Kittens co-founder Elan Lee tells the team that they have completed about half the process and now need to figure out how to sell the game.
[事实] He says a great game can still fail if the box does not catch shoppers’ attention.
[事实] The Planet Money team initially wants a used-car theme because of the original “Market for Lemons” paper.
[事实] Exploding Kittens warns that car-themed games can create misleading expectations about racing or moving pieces.

[09:15] **Three Early Theme Options**

[事实] Stephanie Pesta from Exploding Kittens’ art team presents three possible themes and names.
[事实] The first is “Misfit Mascots,” where players trade odd mascot characters like King Ketchup and Liverwurst Lad.
[事实] The second is “Sell Me a Sasquatch,” where players collect and trade cryptids or magical creatures.
[事实] The third involves cats in suits acting as salespeople, traders, and vendors.

[13:05] **The Gut-Driven Process**

[事实] Exploding Kittens describes this phase as exploratory, focused on what “feels right” and what “sticks.”
[事实] The hosts are uncomfortable with how unscientific the process feels compared with their usual data-oriented instincts.
[事实] Exploding Kittens says this approach works better for them than formal focus groups that may create too much data.
[推测] The episode presents creative retail judgment as partly empirical, partly instinctive, and difficult to reduce to metrics.

[14:46] **Three Feet and Three Seconds**

[事实] Jamie Wolanski explains that shoppers are choosing among hundreds of games on a shelf.
[事实] She says retail teams often think in terms of having three feet and three seconds to get a customer to pick up or buy a product.
[事实] Jamie says the core big-box game customer is generally a millennial woman aged roughly 28 to 44 who controls household purchasing dollars.
[事实] She says shoppers often buy games either for that night’s game night or as a gift.

[18:24] **Settling on Sell Me a Sasquatch**

[事实] After letting the options sit for a few weeks, both Planet Money and Exploding Kittens gravitate toward “Sell Me a Sasquatch.”
[事实] The hosts say the name is fun to say and the theme feels enjoyable to spend time inside.
[事实] The Sasquatch theme reminds them of 1980s and 1990s paranormal culture such as The X-Files and Unsolved Mysteries.
[推测] The theme may appeal to millennial nostalgia while still feeling playful enough for a party game.

[20:53] **Jamie’s Reaction**

[事实] Jamie initially reacts thoughtfully to the name “Sell Me a Sasquatch.”
[事实] After hearing that the cover art features a cute Sasquatch in a vending machine, she says Sasquatch, Bigfoot, and Yeti are trending and “super hot.”
[事实] Jamie rates the idea a nine out of ten, then jokes that adding a fairy makes it a ten out of ten.
[推测] Her approval reassures the team that the name could work for big-box retail.

[23:21] **The International Problem**

[事实] Exploding Kittens later tells the team there is a problem with international markets because some international teams do not think European customers know what a Sasquatch is.
[事实] They say international sales are typically around 20 percent at first, then Yuri Hosta later says international business is closer to 30 percent of Exploding Kittens sales and growing.
[事实] Translating “Sasquatch” could damage the name, while leaving it in English could confuse shoppers.
[推测] The strongest U.S. retail name may create friction in global markets.

[26:03] **International Market Differences**

[事实] Yuri Hosta manages Exploding Kittens’ international business outside North America.
[事实] He says France tends to be more of a card-game market and may like layered, complex rules.
[事实] He says Spain and Italy respond well to dynamic games like Throw Throw Burrito.
[事实] He says a therapy-themed Exploding Kittens game has done well in Germany, and the Freud Museum contacted them about selling it.

[28:57] **Searching for Alternatives**

[事实] The team considers whether the game should launch under different names in the U.S. and Europe, but that would slow down production and require separate art, naming, and factory setup.
[事实] Exploding Kittens tries alternatives including a swap-meet theme called “Sell Me Something Stupid.”
[事实] The hosts reject that direction because the random objects no longer feel like a coherent market.
[事实] They also revisit car-themed titles, including “Running on Empty,” “A Lot of Lemons,” “Wheeling and Dealing,” “Rust in Peace,” “Buy, Sell, Regret,” and “Dumpster Drive.”

[32:46] **The Final Decision**

[事实] Exploding Kittens says the game needs to be ready by July 2026 to be on shelves for Christmas 2026.
[事实] In a decisive meeting, they present one theme: “Sell Me a Sasquatch.”
[事实] They say they tried many other titles and pitched some to major retailers, but nothing beat “Sell Me a Sasquatch.”
[事实] They decide to move forward with the name for the U.S. edition and let the international team solve the Europe issue separately.

[35:54] **Listener Contributions**

[事实] The hosts ask listeners to submit creature ideas for “Sell Me a Sasquatch.”
[事实] They already have classic cryptids and magical creatures such as Bigfoot, the Loch Ness Monster, a tooth fairy, and a golden goose.
[事实] They ask for local legendary cryptids and invented economics-themed creatures.
[事实] Examples include Toxy the toxic asset, a literal black swan, and a slow deer representing stagflation.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode is valuable because it shows the hidden commercial work behind turning an idea into a retail product. The strongest material is the contrast between Planet Money’s data-driven instincts and Exploding Kittens’ more intuitive approach to naming, art, and shelf appeal.

A highlight is how the episode makes product positioning concrete: three feet, three seconds, gift buyers, game-night shoppers, and international translation risk. The story also keeps the economics connection alive without turning the episode into a technical lesson.

[推测] The main limitation is that the episode depends heavily on the team’s internal reactions and consultant judgment, so listeners looking for rigorous consumer testing or detailed sales data may find the process unsatisfying.

[推测] This episode is best suited for listeners interested in product design, retail strategy, board games, branding, or the practical business decisions behind creative projects.
