# The Neuroscience of Grief, Attachment, and the Grieving Body

## 概览

This episode features Andrew Huberman speaking with Dr. Mary Frances O'Connor about grief as a natural psychological, physical, and neurobiological response to loss. The central frame is that grief cannot be understood apart from attachment: the brain has encoded loved ones as enduring, needed figures, so death creates a conflict between knowing someone is gone and still feeling them as everlasting.

A major conclusion is that grieving is not about “letting go,” but about integration. O'Connor describes grieving as a learning process in which people slowly adapt to a world where the person is no longer physically present, while the internal relationship continues to change.

The conversation moves from neuroscience to practical coping: yearning and dopamine, protest and despair, social support, medical risks during bereavement, rumination after suicide loss, religious and philosophical meaning-making, body-based tools, avoidance, memory, and the value of learning to oscillate between loss and restoration.

## 分段落总结

[00:00] **Episode Frame**

[事实] Huberman introduces Dr. Mary Frances O'Connor as a professor of clinical psychology and psychiatry at the University of Arizona, where she directs a grief, loss, and social stress laboratory.

[事实] The episode focuses on the neuroscience of attachment, why grief can feel physically painful, and how grief can follow deaths, relationship loss, job loss, or loss of a role.

[事实] Huberman previews O'Connor's view that dopamine-driven yearning is central to grieving, and that protest and despair both need to be acknowledged.

[03:25] **Grief vs. Grieving**

[事实] O'Connor defines grief as the natural physical, emotional, and mental response to the death of someone close.

[事实] She distinguishes grief from grieving: grief is the wave felt in a given moment, while grieving is how those waves change over time.

[事实] She describes grieving as a form of learning: learning how to live with the loss of the person.

[05:00] **Attachment and the “Gone but Everlasting” Conflict**

[事实] O'Connor says grief cannot be separated from love and bonding, because the impact of loss depends on what was attached.

[事实] Attachment carries an implicit belief that “I will always be there for you” and that absence means the loved one should be sought.

[事实] In death, the brain has to reconcile two conflicting streams of information: the person is gone, yet the attachment system treats them as everlasting.

[10:32] **Anticipated Loss and Sudden Loss**

[事实] O'Connor says sudden losses are harder to understand because the mind has not rehearsed that scenario.

[事实] Closure conversations in hospice, including saying love, thanks, forgiveness, and goodbye, can help people reflect after the loss.

[事实] She also says anticipatory knowledge does not necessarily change attachment biology; people may still feel shocked or reach for the phone after a long-expected death.

[15:01] **Integration, Not Letting Go**

[事实] O'Connor rejects the framing that healthy grieving means letting go of attachment.

[事实] She describes adjustment as integration: developing a new internal relationship with the deceased while adapting to their physical absence.

[事实] She shares that her mother died after years of breast cancer, and that her own relationship with her mother continued to evolve internally after death.

[20:29] **Yearning and Dopamine**

[事实] O'Connor identifies yearning or pining as a hallmark of grief and connects it to wanting.

[事实] In neuroimaging research, people who reported more yearning showed more activity in the nucleus accumbens when viewing photos of their deceased loved one.

[事实] She says grief is not merely one more stressor added to life; it can feel as though a bonded part of oneself has been amputated.

[25:14] **Attachment Needs Are Not Addiction**

[事实] O'Connor cautions against describing yearning for a loved one as addiction-like.

[事实] She compares yearning to thirst: attachment figures are needed in a basic homeostatic way, like food and water.

[事实] Grieving requires finding new ways to meet attachment needs, because the deceased person can no longer serve that role in the same way.

[31:00] **Grief Literacy and Rituals**

[事实] O'Connor says many cultures and religions historically gave people structures for mourning, such as wakes, Shiva, masses, or ancestor practices.

[事实] She argues that many modern people lack shared grief literacy and are left without modeling or philosophical structure for intense grief reactions.

[事实] She connects attachment to neurobiology involving dopamine, oxytocin, cortisol, adrenaline, and specific brain receptors.

[36:06] **Protest and Despair**

[事实] Drawing on John Bowlby, O'Connor describes two broad grief responses: protest and despair.

[事实] Protest is the activated search response, like realizing a child is missing in a grocery store.

[事实] Despair is the withdrawal response that comes with recognizing the person is truly gone and cannot be found.

[事实] She says despair can serve a function by stopping costly searching, but despair also falsely implies that the pain will last unchanged forever.

[44:17] **Transmutation and Attachment Hierarchy**

[事实] Huberman frames despair as a “no-go” pathway that can be transformed into new context-dependent action.

[事实] O'Connor says withdrawal has its own biological cost, including likely inflammation and shifts in hormones such as prolactin and oxytocin.

[事实] She argues humans have an innate capacity to shift attachment hierarchies, as seen developmentally when children move from parents as primary attachment figures toward peers or partners.

[事实] She says bereaved people need support because they often face this attachment transition alone rather than as part of a shared cohort.

[55:12] **Bereavement as a Public Health Risk**

[事实] O'Connor describes a public health model of bereavement support, including grief literacy, social support, and medical attention.

[事实] She says the day a loved one dies, a person is 21 times more likely to have a heart attack than on other days.

[事实] She says in the first three months after a wife's death, men are nearly twice as likely to have a fatal heart attack as men who remain married; for women the figure is about 1.8 times.

[事实] Her lab found that blood pressure rises during waves of grief, and that people with more intense grief had larger increases and poorer recovery in a German replication study.

[事实] She says about one in ten people develop disordered grieving and may need specific evidence-based psychotherapy.

[65:23] **Alcohol, Medical Care, and the Grieving Body**

[事实] O'Connor describes alcohol as one possible tool in a larger coping toolkit, using her own example of having a beer for breakfast after her mother died.

[事实] She distinguishes limited use in a specific moment from chronic use to avoid feelings.

[事实] She argues that “dying of a broken heart” is not just a metaphor, citing large epidemiological findings on increased mortality among newly bereaved people.

[事实] Her lab and an Australian replication conducted proof-of-concept work using baby aspirin in the first two weeks after loss, but she clearly says this was not a randomized clinical trial or medical advice.

[75:28] **Compartmentalization and Body-Based Tools**

[事实] O'Connor says emotions are outputs of physical and mental states, so coping can begin by working with the body and the demands placed on it.

[事实] She gives examples such as running when protest energy is high, or yoga and breathing to soothe the body.

[事实] In her lab's intervention study with widows and widowers, mindfulness helped, but progressive muscle relaxation helped grief even more.

[事实] She says suppression can be useful in specific moments, such as before a work meeting, but it cannot be the only coping strategy.

[84:01] **Beyond the Five Stages**

[事实] Huberman and O'Connor discuss Elisabeth Kübler-Ross's five-stage model as historically important but often misused as a prescription.

[事实] O'Connor says modern longitudinal grief research shows trajectories such as acceptance increasing and yearning decreasing, but not in a linear stage-by-stage sequence.

[事实] She says a person is not failing at grief because they never feel one expected emotion, such as anger.

[87:19] **Permission for Joy and Change**

[事实] O'Connor says people often feel judged by social expectations around how grief “should” look.

[事实] She gives an example of an older widow who was not sad ten days after her husband's death and feared others would see something wrong with her.

[事实] She says people may feel guilty for joy, fun, vacation, or falling in love because part of the brain still treats the deceased attachment as everlasting.

[事实] She suggests asking who is judging the person and how permission can be found.

[96:01] **Grief After Suicide**

[事实] O'Connor states that she is not an expert in suicide itself.

[事实] She says grief after sudden, violent, unexpected death, including many suicides, is more challenging.

[事实] She discusses “would have, could have, should have” thoughts, such as believing one phone call or action would have saved the person.

[事实] She says the task is not to answer infinite guilt questions, but to learn how to live given that the person is gone.

[107:47] **Rumination and the Present Moment**

[事实] O'Connor describes “if only” thoughts as rumination and perseverative thinking.

[事实] She says guilt can feel more tolerable than accepting that terrible events can happen randomly and without control.

[事实] She recommends shifting attention from whether a thought is true to whether the thought is helpful in ongoing life.

[事实] She gives changing environment, such as getting up and walking outside, as one skill for interrupting rumination.

[111:05] **Religion, Spirituality, and Meaning**

[事实] O'Connor says religion can offer community and social support during grief.

[事实] She also says loss can challenge beliefs, as in asking how a benevolent God could allow the death.

[事实] She describes attachment itself as transcendent in a neuroscientific sense, because loved ones become physically encoded in the brain.

[事实] She cites prairie vole research showing epigenetic changes in the nucleus accumbens after pair bonding.

[115:55] **Prospective Evidence on Belief Systems**

[事实] O'Connor describes the Changing Lives of Older Couples study as a rare prospective bereavement study.

[事实] The study interviewed older couples before any death, then re-interviewed surviving spouses after one partner died.

[事实] She says people who already had a way to understand death's role in life, whether religious, philosophical, or cycle-of-life based, showed less grief severity after loss.

[119:59] **Facing Death Before It Happens**

[事实] Huberman describes a daily relaxation practice in which he imagines that one day he will take a final exhale.

[事实] He also describes writing a document for loved ones about what he would want them to know if he died.

[事实] O'Connor agrees such permission can be valuable, while emphasizing that loved ones will still grieve because attachment makes grief unavoidable.

[事实] She discusses terror management theory as evidence that people use psychological strategies to avoid awareness of death.

[128:08] **Energy, Motivation, and the Grieving Body**

[事实] O'Connor says her experience with multiple sclerosis shaped her empathy for grieving people living in unpredictable bodies.

[事实] She distinguishes motivation from energy: a person may lack motivation but still have energy, or may truly lack the energy to act.

[事实] She says grief can bring days of fogginess, exhaustion, or very limited functioning, and that people do not get to choose when those days occur.

[131:25] **Oscillation as Health**

[事实] O'Connor says there is no way to optimize grieving.

[事实] She describes the dual process model of bereavement, where mental health involves oscillating between loss-oriented feelings and restoration-oriented demands.

[事实] She shares an example of a widower who could cry about his deceased wife and also enjoy a new relationship, seeing both the past and present as good.

[136:01] **Avoidance and Re-Entering Life**

[事实] O'Connor says avoidance and rumination can both make grieving harder.

[事实] Examples of avoidance include avoiding the hospital, avoiding a closet, or avoiding dinner with couple friends after a spouse dies.

[事实] She says actions often come before motivation or good feelings; people may need to do the hard thing first and feel differently later.

[139:55] **Memory, Perspective, and Professional Help**

[事实] O'Connor says people are often zoomed in on the final moments, guilt, or what was not said around the time of death.

[事实] Over time, people may zoom out and remember the life in broader context rather than only the death.

[事实] She says sleep and dreams may help detach some emotional intensity from memories.

[事实] She says if troubling memories worsen over time or grief consistently worsens, it is time to seek professional help.

[143:08] **Time Perception and Attention Near Death**

[事实] Huberman asks about people near death seeming to notice smaller slices of time and small meaningful moments.

[事实] O'Connor says there is some research on this, though terminal illness and end-of-life periods are difficult to study.

[事实] She connects the question to attention regulation and practices such as yoga nidra, religious practices, and philosophical practices.

[推测] The discussion suggests that awareness of finitude can make smaller experiences feel more abundant and meaningful.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode's strongest value is that it makes grief legible without reducing it to a simple self-help formula. O'Connor repeatedly resists tidy advice and instead offers a biologically grounded model: grief is painful because attachment is real, bodily, and survival-relevant.

[推测] A major highlight is the integration of neuroscience, clinical insight, culture, ritual, and personal experience. The conversation is especially useful for listeners who have felt confused by old stage models of grief or guilty for having mixed reactions such as sadness, anger, relief, joy, or new love.

[推测] The episode's limitation is that some practical topics remain broad. Suicide prevention itself is explicitly outside O'Connor's expertise, and the aspirin discussion is carefully framed as proof-of-concept rather than guidance.

[推测] This is best suited for listeners dealing with bereavement, supporting someone who is grieving, or trying to understand why grief affects sleep, blood pressure, motivation, memory, rumination, relationships, and the body as a whole.
