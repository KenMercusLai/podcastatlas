# Qin Shi Huangdi: The First Emperor of China

## 概览

This episode uses Jorge Luis Borges’ essay “The Wall and the Books” to frame Qin Shi Huangdi as both a historical ruler and a mythic figure: the man associated with the Great Wall, book burning, tyranny, and the founding of imperial China.

The hosts argue that his legacy is deeply ambivalent. He was remembered by Confucian historians as a brutal despot, yet he also created a model of centralized imperial rule that shaped Chinese political imagination for centuries.

The discussion moves from the Warring States background and older Chinese ideas of harmony, history, and legitimacy, through Qin conquest and administration, to later legends about the Great Wall, immortality, the terracotta army, and Maoist-era rehabilitation of the First Emperor.

## 分段落总结

[00:15] **Borges, the Wall, and the Books**

[事实] The episode opens with Borges’ 1950 essay “The Wall and the Books,” which links the building of the Great Wall with the destruction of earlier books.

[事实] The hosts introduce Qin Shi Huangdi as a ruler remembered for walls, book burning, alleged executions of scholars, and a late-life quest for immortality.

[推测] Borges’ appeal lies in how the First Emperor seems to turn history, memory, architecture, and power into one symbolic project.

[03:16] **The Terracotta Army and Western Familiarity**

[事实] The hosts say many Western listeners may know ancient China mainly through the Great Wall and the terracotta army.

[事实] The terracotta soldiers were discovered in 1974 near the First Emperor’s capital and are described as one of the great archaeological discoveries after Tutankhamun.

[推测] The army gives a vivid visual form to a ruler who otherwise often appears through hostile later accounts.

[04:31] **An Ambivalent Founder**

[事实] Qin Shi Huangdi is presented as both an archetypal tyrant and the founder of China’s imperial state.

[事实] The hosts say he unified seven warring kingdoms into a single order and established a template that endured into the twentieth century.

[事实] In modern Chinese culture, including the film *Hero* and television series, he can be treated as a heroic founder rather than only a despot.

[07:52] **The Warring States and Qin’s Rise**

[事实] Around the third century BC, what became China was divided among seven warring states.

[事实] Qin was seen by the other states as rough, warlike, and somewhat barbaric, but its militarism helped make it successful.

[事实] Under the King of Qin, the other six states were conquered.

[10:07] **Was It Year Zero?**

[事实] The hosts ask whether Qin Shi Huangdi truly abolished history or drew on older Chinese traditions.

[事实] Borges noted that Chinese chronology was already thousands of years old and included figures such as the Yellow Emperor, Laozi, and Confucius.

[事实] Daoist and Confucian traditions both valued harmony, but they did not obviously provide a practical route to violent imperial conquest.

[13:28] **Confucian Harmony and Political Order**

[事实] Confucius is described as seeking balance, hierarchy, and proper conduct, summed up by the idea that ruler, subject, father, and son should each fulfill their roles.

[事实] The hosts say this Confucian ideal helped make unified order seem cosmically meaningful in Chinese history.

[推测] The First Emperor’s later centralization partly answered the desire for unity, but by methods Confucians found morally unacceptable.

[16:09] **Zhou Memory and the Mandate of Heaven**

[事实] Later Chinese historians sometimes portrayed history as moving from many states toward one unified empire.

[事实] Warring States thinkers also looked backward to the Zhou dynasty, which the hosts say ruled for 789 years.

[事实] The Zhou justified rule through the Mandate of Heaven, meaning a dynasty that failed to maintain order could lose legitimacy.

[22:51] **Jia Yi’s Critique of Qin**

[事实] After the break, the hosts quote Jia Yi’s “The Faults of Qin,” which depicts the First Emperor as conquering, wall-building, burning writings, melting weapons, and policing movement.

[事实] Jia Yi wrote about 50 years after the First Emperor’s death and is described as a Confucian critic.

[推测] Because later history was often written by Confucians, the surviving image of Qin Shi Huangdi is filtered through moral hostility.

[25:48] **Origins and Accession**

[事实] The First Emperor’s birth name is given as Zheng, and he became King of Qin in 246 BC at age 13.

[事实] Hostile later traditions questioned his legitimacy by linking his mother and possible paternity to a merchant.

[事实] Qin is described as militarily formidable, with advanced crossbows, large armies, and a hard martial culture.

[28:04] **Lord Shang’s Harsh State Ideology**

[事实] The hosts describe Lord Shang’s ideology as rejecting Confucian virtues such as ritual, benevolence, filial piety, and shame about fighting.

[事实] This system emphasized household registration, taxation, military service, forced labor, magistrates, and severe punishments.

[事实] Soldiers could advance by collecting enemy heads, reflecting an extremely militarized order.

[31:41] **Conquest and the Imperial Title**

[事实] By 221 BC, Qin had conquered the other six kingdoms.

[事实] The hosts say the conquest was associated with claims of massive slaughter, though they note such large round numbers are difficult to verify.

[事实] Zheng then took the title Qin Shi Huangdi: Qin from his homeland, Shi meaning first, and Huangdi carrying august and divine associations.

[35:01] **Centralization and Standardization**

[事实] After conquest, the First Emperor divided his realm into 36 provinces and extended centralized control through subdivisions and officials.

[事实] He ordered 120,000 wealthy families to move to his capital and had weapons melted into bells and bronze statues.

[事实] He standardized coinage, weights, measures, chariot axles, and built roads, including a central lane reserved for himself.

[39:23] **Punishment, Books, and Intellectual Control**

[事实] The hosts describe punishments ranging from shaving and tattooing to castration and amputation.

[事实] They say the consensus is probably that books were burned, while the story of burying Confucian scholars alive probably did not happen.

[事实] In 213 BC, an adviser urged destroying books that encouraged appeals to the past; texts on divination, medicine, and agriculture were spared.

[41:07] **The Great Wall as Defense and Power**

[事实] The Great Wall project is said to have begun around 214 BC under General Meng.

[事实] The hosts stress that this was not the later stone wall familiar from modern images, but earthworks, fortresses, ravines, and defensive stretches.

[推测] The wall functioned both as a serious defense against northern peoples and as a statement of imperial control over space.

[43:18] **The Human Cost of the Wall**

[事实] A poem quoted in the episode says the long wall is propped up on skeletons.

[事实] The hosts recount a legend of a woman whose husband dies building the wall; her tears dissolve the wall and reveal the bones of dead laborers.

[推测] These stories express popular memory of forced labor, suffering, and resentment under Qin rule.

[44:49] **The Quest for Immortality**

[事实] The First Emperor is described as obsessed with avoiding death and interested in magical or necromantic writings.

[事实] Magicians told him of islands of immortals across the sea and took groups of boys and girls on expeditions that did not return.

[事实] In 211 BC, a meteor reportedly bore a prophecy that the First Emperor would die and his lands be divided.

[47:00] **Death and Myth**

[事实] The hosts tell a story that after his death, aides hid his corpse in a covered chariot and surrounded it with wagons of fish to mask the smell.

[事实] They immediately question the story’s truth and describe it as having the quality of folktale or parable.

[推测] The death stories reinforce the theme that a ruler who tried to control everything could not control mortality or his posthumous reputation.

[49:34] **The Terracotta Army and the Tomb**

[事实] The terracotta army includes thousands of archers, crossbowmen, foot soldiers, officers, horses, and chariots.

[事实] Frances Wood is cited as describing the army as an industrial rather than purely artistic project, using repeated parts to create an illusion of individuality.

[事实] The tomb itself has not been excavated; the hosts mention accounts of rivers of mercury and modern evidence of high mercury levels.

[52:32] **Maoist Rehabilitation and Modern Legacy**

[事实] The hosts say early communist China initially condemned emperors, including the First Emperor.

[事实] After Lin Biao’s death in a 1971 plane crash, Maoist propaganda reframed Qin Shi Huangdi positively as a state-builder opposed by Confucian reactionaries.

[事实] A state-sanctioned popular biography in the 1970s presented him as a farsighted founder and sold nearly two million copies within two years.

[事实] The hosts connect this rehabilitation to modern Chinese presentations of Qin Shi Huangdi as a founder whose harshness created unity.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its ability to connect ancient Chinese history with broader questions about empire, memory, legitimacy, and state violence. The comparisons with Rome, Egypt, Assyria, Stalin, and Mao help make Qin Shi Huangdi legible to listeners less familiar with Chinese antiquity.

[推测] Its strongest theme is ambivalence: the First Emperor appears as both tyrant and founder, destroyer of books and creator of durable order, historical ruler and mythic symbol.

[推测] A limitation is that several memorable anecdotes, especially around immortality and death, are acknowledged as legendary or uncertain, so the episode works best as an interpretation of reputation and legacy rather than a purely factual biography.

[推测] This episode is especially suited to listeners interested in ancient empires, authoritarian political traditions, Chinese historical memory, and the long afterlife of founding rulers.
