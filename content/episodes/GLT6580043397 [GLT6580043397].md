# Jean-Paul Marat, Charlotte Corday, and the Road to the Reign of Terror

## Overview

This episode traces how the assassination of radical journalist Jean-Paul Marat became a decisive prelude to the Reign of Terror. It begins with Marat’s transformation from a frustrated physician and rejected experimental scientist into a populist revolutionary who portrayed France as the victim of an immense conspiracy.

The hosts place his rise within the crises of 1793: inflation and food shortages, war against much of Europe, revolt in the Vendée, military betrayal, and an increasingly violent struggle between the Girondins and Montagnards. Emergency institutions created in response—including the Revolutionary Tribunal and Committee of Public Safety—provided the machinery later used during the Terror.

The final part follows Charlotte Corday, a young Girondin sympathizer who believed killing Marat would save France from civil war. Instead, his murder turned him into a revolutionary martyr, intensified fears of conspiracy, hastened the destruction of the Girondins, and helped clear the way for Robespierre’s ascendancy.

## Segment-by-Segment Summary

[03:10] **The Assassination That Heralded the Terror**

[Fact] Charlotte Corday, a 24-year-old woman from Normandy, was arrested in Paris on 13 July 1793 after assassinating Jean-Paul Marat.

[Fact] She entered Marat’s apartment, found him working in his bath, and stabbed him in the chest with a kitchen knife.

[Fact] Jacques-Louis David’s subsequent painting made the murder one of the defining images of the French Revolution.

[Inference] The episode treats the assassination not merely as a dramatic crime but as a catalyst that accelerated France’s descent into the Reign of Terror.

[06:16] **Marat’s Reinvention as a Revolutionary Journalist**

[Fact] Born in what is now Switzerland in 1743, Marat lived in Britain, practiced medicine, promoted questionable treatments, and pursued experimental science.

[Fact] The French Royal Academy of Sciences rejected his attacks on Newtonian theories, deepening his sense of humiliation and exclusion.

[Fact] After the Revolution began in 1789, he reinvented himself as a radical polemicist and founded *L’Ami du peuple*—*The Friend of the People*—partly inspired by British journalist and politician John Wilkes.

[Inference] His personal experience of rejection appears to have reinforced a political worldview centered on corrupt elites, persecution, and betrayal.

[10:32] **Marat as an Early Modern Populist**

[Fact] Historian Keith Michael Baker characterizes Marat as the first modern populist.

[Fact] Marat invoked “the People” as a virtuous collective perpetually endangered by traitors, priests, royalists, and other conspirators.

[Fact] He believed provocative, confrontational, and violent language was necessary to awaken the public from political complacency.

[Fact] Marat rejected civility and polish as signs of falseness or treason while repeatedly presenting himself as a persecuted defender of ordinary people.

[Inference] The hosts draw parallels between his rhetoric and modern political cultures built around conspiracies, elite betrayal, privileged access to hidden truth, and status gained through attracting hostility.

[14:11] **Bloodshed, the September Massacres, and Political Power**

[Fact] Marat suffered from a severe chronic skin condition that produced sores and blisters and forced him to spend long periods in medicinal baths.

[Fact] Other leading revolutionaries, including Danton and Robespierre, regarded him as excessively bloodthirsty and personally unpleasant.

[Fact] Marat repeatedly demanded executions, with the number of proposed victims rising from hundreds to hundreds of thousands.

[Fact] In 1792 he encouraged militants to enter Parisian prisons and kill inmates, contributing to massacres in which roughly 1,500 people died.

[Fact] Despite widespread hatred outside Paris, he remained popular among the capital’s radicals and was elected to the National Convention.

[18:00] **Girondins and Montagnards Divide the Convention**

[Fact] The National Convention contained roughly 750 predominantly young, idealistic republican deputies, many of them lawyers and writers influenced by Rousseau.

[Fact] The Girondins drew much of their support from prosperous provincial cities, favored relatively liberal economic policies, distrusted the Parisian crowds, and sought to restrain revolutionary violence.

[Fact] The more radical Montagnards were powerful in Paris, idealized the people, accepted violence as necessary, and included Robespierre, Danton, and Marat.

[Fact] By spring 1793, hostility between the factions had become so extreme that Girondin orator Pierre Vergniaud warned that the Revolution might imitate Saturn by devouring its own children.

[Inference] Vergniaud’s warning was undermined by the fiercely partisan attack in which he delivered it, illustrating how even those who recognized the danger continued to intensify it.

[23:26] **Economic Collapse and War Radicalize France**

[Fact] Poor harvests, a failing grain market, wartime expenditure, and the mass printing of assignats produced severe inflation, shortages, and rapidly rising food prices.

[Fact] Working-class women became an important force in Parisian street politics, demanding action on food prices and poverty.

[Fact] The Enragés called for price controls, redistribution, and a more radical revolution on behalf of the poor.

[Fact] France had initiated war with Austria in 1792 and subsequently fought Britain, the Dutch Republic, Prussia, Austria, and Spain while also confronting revolution in Saint-Domingue.

[Fact] Revolutionary leaders combined universal claims about liberating humanity with expansive French nationalism.

[Inference] Because the Revolution had failed to improve daily life, deprivation made claims of sabotage and internal betrayal increasingly persuasive.

[28:40] **Conscription Ignites the Vendée Rebellion**

[Fact] In February 1793, the Convention ordered the recruitment of 300,000 additional soldiers, provoking intense resistance in rural France.

[Fact] In the Vendée, resentment already existed over attacks on the clergy, higher taxes, the execution of the king, and interference from Paris.

[Fact] Peasants, priests, and landowners formed the Catholic and Royal Army and fought a guerrilla campaign against republican forces.

[Fact] Parisian radicals depicted the insurgents as backward tools of aristocrats and priests, even though many were peasants whom revolutionary ideology supposedly identified with “the people.”

[Fact] The ensuing conflict involved massacres, reprisals, and scorched-earth warfare; the hosts cite an estimate of approximately 200,000 deaths, perhaps a quarter of the Vendée’s population.

[Fact] Historians continue to debate whether the repression should be regarded as genocidal or as a precursor to twentieth-century forms of mass violence.

[33:46] **Dumouriez’s Defection Validates Fears of Conspiracy**

[Fact] General Charles-François Dumouriez, formerly a Girondin foreign minister, suffered defeat against Austria and withdrew French forces from Belgium and Holland.

[Fact] He attempted to persuade his army to march on Paris and establish military control, but his soldiers refused.

[Fact] On 5 April 1793 he defected to the Austrians, confirming Marat’s earlier allegation that he was a traitor.

[Fact] The defection devastated the Girondins and strengthened the belief that enemies were concealed within the revolutionary leadership itself.

[Fact] The Convention subsequently made it possible to remove deputies’ immunity when they were suspected of conspiring against republican government.

[Inference] A genuine act of betrayal gave sweeping credibility to Marat’s conspiratorial worldview, making it harder to distinguish real threats from politically useful accusations.

[38:22] **The Machinery of Terror Takes Shape**

[Fact] Historian Timothy Tackett identifies March and April 1793 as a major turning point in the emergence of the Terror.

[Fact] Deputies on mission were dispatched to impose central authority in the provinces, using force when necessary.

[Fact] The Revolutionary Tribunal was established to try political crimes; death sentences were to be carried out within 24 hours and could not be appealed.

[Fact] Surveillance committees recorded citizens and foreigners and encouraged denunciations of counter-revolutionary activity.

[Fact] The Committee of Public Safety was created on 6 April to manage military emergencies, initially with nine members and Danton as its dominant figure.

[Fact] These bodies were assembled in response to immediate crises rather than according to a single blueprint, but together they formed the institutional structure of the Terror.

[44:04] **Marat’s Trial Becomes a Political Triumph**

[Fact] The Girondins used the removal of parliamentary immunity to indict Marat for inciting an uprising against the Convention.

[Fact] After briefly hiding, Marat surrendered because popular opinion in Paris strongly favored him.

[Fact] At his trial on 24 April, the Montagnard-aligned prosecutor allowed him to present himself at length as a faithful defender of the nation.

[Fact] The jury acquitted him, and supporters carried him back to the Convention crowned with roses while chanting in his honor.

[Inference] The failed prosecution converted Marat from a controversial agitator into a victorious symbol of popular sovereignty and exposed the Girondins’ political weakness.

[48:58] **The Paris Insurrection Destroys the Girondins**

[Fact] On 1 May, working-class women entered the Convention and demanded price controls and relief from food shortages.

[Fact] On 26 May, Robespierre publicly called for popular insurrection when good faith and decency had been trampled underfoot.

[Fact] Beginning on 31 May, armed militants and National Guards surrounded the Convention, closed Paris’s gates, and declared the city in insurrection.

[Fact] On 2 June, approximately 50,000 people and thousands of armed guards compelled the Convention to suspend and place under house arrest 29 Girondin members.

[Fact] Marat supported surrendering the Girondins and was widely believed to have helped select names for the purge, although the hosts note that this cannot be confirmed.

[Inference] The purge marked a decisive transfer of power from elected representatives to armed Parisian pressure and left the Montagnards dominant.

[56:09] **Provincial Revolt and the Federalist Crisis**

[Fact] Marat withdrew from public life because his worsening skin condition confined him to a bath in his apartment, where he continued producing his newspaper.

[Fact] The purge of the Girondins horrified provincial cities whose administrations were more sympathetic to them.

[Fact] Lyon’s artisans, shopkeepers, and other former supporters of the Revolution rebelled against what they viewed as domination by Parisian extremists.

[Fact] Similar “federalist” resistance emerged in cities including Marseille, Bordeaux, and Caen.

[Fact] About 20 fugitive Girondin deputies gathered in Caen and proposed raising an army to march on Paris.

[Inference] The Revolution was increasingly consuming its original social base, turning former supporters into enemies as authority became concentrated in radical Paris.

[58:55] **Charlotte Corday Chooses Her Target**

[Fact] Charlotte Corday came from a minor aristocratic Norman family and was descended from playwright Pierre Corneille.

[Fact] Educated in a convent, she read Plutarch, Voltaire, and Rousseau and initially supported the Revolution.

[Fact] She became horrified by revolutionary violence, including the execution of a priest close to her family, and embraced the Girondin cause.

[Fact] Marat was especially hated in Caen because he had demanded that its resistance be crushed without mercy.

[Fact] Influenced by classical stories of republican heroism and sacrifice, Corday decided that killing Marat would save lives and serve France.

[Inference] Her political imagination resembled that of her enemies: both sides believed the removal of one supposedly monstrous individual could redeem the nation.

[62:29] **The Journey to Paris and the Assassination Plan**

[Fact] Corday left Caen on 9 July 1793, telling her father that she was going to England, and carried a copy of Plutarch on the journey.

[Fact] She originally planned to kill Marat publicly in the Convention but discovered that illness had kept him at home.

[Fact] She wrote a manifesto arguing that Marat stood outside the law and that her act would awaken France against bloodshed and extremism.

[Fact] On 13 July she bought a black-handled kitchen knife with a blade approximately 15 centimeters long.

[Fact] After being refused entry to Marat’s home earlier that day, she returned in the evening claiming to possess vital information about Girondin traitors in Caen.

[67:20] **The Murder of Marat**

[Fact] Marat admitted Corday after hearing her claim that she possessed the names of Girondin conspirators.

[Fact] She spoke with him for roughly ten minutes and recited the names of 18 people in Caen.

[Fact] According to the account presented, Marat said they would all be guillotined within days.

[Fact] Corday then drew the knife from her clothing and stabbed him once deeply in the chest; he called to his wife before dying in the bath.

[Fact] Members of the household restrained Corday, and authorities protected her from a hostile crowd because they wanted to uncover what they assumed was a wider plot.

[71:38] **Interrogation, Trial, and Execution**

[Fact] Revolutionary officials repeatedly questioned Corday because they were convinced that she must belong to a Girondin or foreign conspiracy.

[Fact] Corday consistently maintained that she alone had conceived and carried out the assassination.

[Fact] The interrogators struggled to accept both that a young woman had acted independently and that people outside Paris might regard Marat as a monster.

[Fact] After a six-hour trial on 17 July, Corday was sentenced to immediate execution by guillotine.

[Fact] She requested that an officer finish her portrait, gave him a lock of her hair, and reportedly faced execution calmly because she had anticipated death from the beginning.

[Inference] The investigation revealed how completely conspiratorial thinking had narrowed the revolutionary authorities’ ability to understand independent political action.

[75:55] **The Cult of Marat**

[Fact] Marat’s supporters immediately represented him as a martyr whose murder demanded vengeance.

[Fact] Jacques-Louis David organized an elaborate public funeral modeled on Roman spectacle, though the heat and the condition of Marat’s body made the planned display difficult.

[Fact] Marat’s heart was placed in an urn, and revolutionary rhetoric explicitly compared him to Jesus while declaring him a god.

[Fact] Locks of hair, commemorative objects, hairstyles, and renamed towns formed a political cult around his memory.

[Inference] Although radical revolutionaries rejected Christianity, their treatment of Marat reproduced Christian patterns of martyrdom, relics, sacred hearts, and devotional worship.

[81:38] **From Marat’s Death to Robespierre’s Ascendancy**

[Fact] Radicals interpreted Marat’s assassination as further proof that enemies and assassins might be concealed everywhere.

[Fact] Girondin deputies remaining in Paris were moved from house arrest into prison, while war, rebellion, blockade, and food scarcity continued.

[Fact] Danton appeared too pragmatic and willing to compromise for the new political atmosphere and was removed from the Committee of Public Safety.

[Fact] Seventeen days later, Maximilien Robespierre joined the committee and would become the central embodiment of the Reign of Terror.

[Inference] Corday intended to stop bloodshed by killing Marat, but his martyrdom strengthened the politics of suspicion and helped make terror appear both necessary and morally justified.

## Podcast Review/Summary

The episode’s greatest strength is its integration of biography, political conflict, economic distress, war, and popular activism. Marat’s murder is presented not as an isolated sensational event but as the product of overlapping national emergencies and the trigger for further radicalization.

Another highlight is the comparison between revolutionary rhetoric and modern populism: appeals to a virtuous people, accusations of elite betrayal, rejection of civility, and demands that followers “wake up.” These comparisons make the political psychology accessible, although some of the modern analogies and comic asides simplify complex ideological distinctions.

The discussion is particularly effective in showing that the Terror was assembled gradually through emergency measures rather than implemented from a complete master plan. It also demonstrates the tragic symmetry between Marat and Corday: each believed that exemplary violence against an enemy could protect the nation.

[Inference] The episode is especially suitable for listeners interested in the French Revolution, political radicalization, populism, conspiracy thinking, and the transformation of temporary emergency powers into organized state violence.
