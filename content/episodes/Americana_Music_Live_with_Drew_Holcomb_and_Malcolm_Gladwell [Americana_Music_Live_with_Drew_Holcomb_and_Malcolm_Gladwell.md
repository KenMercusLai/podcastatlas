# Drew Holcomb on Americana, Faith, Family, and Finding a Voice

## Overview

Recorded live at New York’s Cherry Lane Theatre, this episode pairs Malcolm Gladwell with Nashville singer-songwriter Drew Holcomb for a conversation interwoven with acoustic performances. Holcomb explains why “Americana” gives him a musical home without imposing the commercial conventions of country or rock.

The discussion traces Holcomb’s creative life through Memphis, Nashville, Scotland, family, Christianity, grief, and marriage. He describes how losing his brother at 17 led to a crisis of faith, while music became a way to process emotions that religious language could not fully resolve.

Holcomb and Gladwell also examine the songs and artists that shaped them, including Amy Grant, Bruce Springsteen, Emmylou Harris, Glen Campbell, Patsy Cline, Willie Nelson, Dolly Parton, George Jones, Tom Petty, and Paul Simon. Across these examples, they return to the power of emotionally direct storytelling.

The live set culminates in the debut of Holcomb’s “Cormac McCarthy’s Black Ferrari” and a communal performance of “Dance with Everybody,” reinforcing his view of music as a bridge between grief and joy, private experience and shared life.

## 分段落总结

[01:11] **From a Touring Variety Show to Cherry Lane Theatre**

[事实] Gladwell met Holcomb while participating in Lee Camp’s traveling variety show, where spoken stories about the suffrage and anti-slavery movements were accompanied by live music.

[事实] Impressed by Holcomb’s thoughtfulness and vitality, Gladwell invited him to the newly reopened Cherry Lane Theatre for a live conversation.

[事实] Gladwell asked Holcomb to keep his guitar beside him throughout the interview, making performances an integral part of the episode.

[02:50] **“I’m Gonna Fly” and the Freedom of Turning Forty**

[事实] Holcomb opens with his favorite song of his own, explaining that playing something he loves helps him manage nervousness.

[事实] He wrote the song around January 2022, shortly after turning 40, during a season when he typically becomes introspective and creative.

[事实] The song embraces the tension between childhood wonder and growing older, including a memory of watching other children play outside after he had been sent to bed.

[事实] Holcomb says performing it feels like wrapping himself in a blanket and that he has finally allowed himself to admit he enjoys his own music.

[08:21] **Americana as a Musical Home Without Many Rules**

[事实] Holcomb’s early listening combined folk, rock and roll, country, soul, gospel, Motown, Bob Dylan, and Amy Grant.

[事实] Before “Americana” became a common label, he described his music as folk rock.

[事实] He sees Americana as an institutional home for musicians who do not fit commercial country or contemporary rock radio.

[事实] Holcomb values the genre’s flexibility: songs need not follow fixed structures or instrumentation, provided the music feels genuinely made by people.

[09:30] **Memphis, Nashville, and Competing Southern Identities**

[事实] Holcomb grew up in Memphis with a cultural suspicion of Nashville, recalling that his family repeatedly drove through Nashville without stopping.

[事实] He describes Memphis as a hometown-oriented, majority-Black city shaped by long-standing families, blue-collar companies, soul music, and distinctive inexpensive food.

[事实] He characterizes Nashville as a more transient city attracting young people through expanding music and healthcare industries.

[事实] Holcomb says Nashville changed dramatically during his nearly two decades there, particularly by developing into a much stronger food city.

[14:25] **A Religious Childhood and the Music That Survived**

[事实] Christianity was central to Holcomb’s upbringing in a large extended family associated with an independent Bible church.

[事实] Members of the church encouraged his parents to discard records considered spiritually inappropriate, including albums Holcomb later wished had survived.

[事实] Bob Dylan’s evangelical-era albums remained in the household and helped make Dylan’s other work acceptable as well.

[事实] Holcomb’s mother played hymns on the piano to wake the children for school, while his later job at a Memphis arena exposed him to performers ranging from Boyz II Men to ZZ Top.

[20:26] **Parental Influence and Permission to Pursue Music**

[事实] Gladwell proposes a theory of “asymmetrical parenting,” suggesting that people often foreground one parent at a time when explaining their development.

[事实] Holcomb says his father left dentistry after discovering that its monotony did not suit his extroverted personality, gradually transitioning into financial advising.

[事实] Because Holcomb’s grandfather had suppressed his father’s own musical ambitions, his father strongly supported Holcomb when he showed serious interest in music.

[事实] His father offered to help him obtain a good guitar on two conditions: that he work hard and recognize when it might be time to walk away.

[25:49] **A Grandfather’s Stories and “Dragons”**

[事实] Holcomb grew up five houses from a grandfather whose extraordinary stories sometimes seemed difficult to separate from fiction.

[事实] One story—riding around the Queen’s estate in her Land Rover—was later confirmed when the family received a condolence letter from the Queen’s secretary.

[事实] Holcomb wrote “Dragons” years after his grandfather’s death as a tribute to his influence.

[事实] The song’s advice includes taking chances, pursuing worthwhile relationships, ignoring critics, bearing witness, and confronting whatever stands in one’s way.

[31:34] **Grief, Faith, and a Search for a Third Way**

[事实] Holcomb says people raised in his religious world often either remain fully inside it or depart through a process of deconstruction, but he sought a third path.

[事实] His brother, who had spina bifida and other health problems, died suddenly when Holcomb was 17 and away in the Dominican Republic.

[事实] The loss precipitated a crisis of faith because familiar sacraments and religious language no longer made sense to him.

[事实] Van Morrison’s *Moondance* and David Gray’s *White Ladder* became grief records for Holcomb, even though they were not explicitly about grief.

[事实] He did not wholly reject his faith community because he had experienced genuine love there, but he eventually rejected much of American evangelical culture while retaining central elements of Christian orthodoxy.

[推测] Holcomb’s “third way” treats faith as an open-ended practice of bridge-building rather than either unquestioning allegiance or total repudiation.

[35:52] **Scotland and the Beginning of a Songwriting Life**

[事实] Holcomb was drawn to Edinburgh during a high-school trip and later studied in Scotland, including a seminary program at the University of St Andrews.

[事实] Traditional Scottish music, local pub performances, the landscape, and the weather contributed to an environment in which he began writing seriously.

[事实] While in Scotland, he worked on an oral history of his late brother, interviewing doctors, teachers, relatives, and others about why his short life mattered so deeply to them.

[事实] Holcomb says he was alone but not necessarily lonely, and that this solitude helped him begin composing songs.

[事实] His early work passed through an imitative phase influenced by Steve Earle, Bruce Springsteen, and Ryan Adams before he found his own voice.

[41:53] **Springsteen, Brotherhood, and Emotional Storytelling**

[事实] Gladwell identifies Bruce Springsteen’s “Highway Patrolman” as a model for constructing an emotionally powerful narrative.

[事实] The song tells of a law-enforcement officer who allows his troubled brother to escape after a violent incident.

[事实] Holcomb connected the song to years when he feared losing his own brother to addiction; that brother was eight years sober at the time of the conversation.

[事实] Holcomb says he would play the song on nights when he had not heard from his brother.

[推测] Their exchange shows how narrative songs can become private emotional tools whose significance extends far beyond their original fictional characters.

[46:16] **Anger as Information and “Ring the Bells”**

[事实] Holcomb was taught that anger itself was bad, but later concluded that anger and rage are different: anger can signal injustice, loneliness, sadness, or hurt.

[事实] He now treats anger as a warning light that prompts him to investigate what is happening instead of suppressing the feeling.

[事实] Holcomb co-wrote “Ring the Bells” with Abner and Amanda Ramirez shortly after the Charlottesville white-supremacist rally.

[事实] The song responded in part to prominent American Christians treating the opposing sides of that event as morally equivalent.

[事实] Holcomb argues that sometimes anger needs a forceful, embodied release rather than quiet containment.

[50:18] **Country Songs, Grief, and Melodrama**

[事实] Gladwell selects Emmylou Harris’s “Boulder to Birmingham,” written about Gram Parsons, as an especially powerful articulation of grief and longing.

[事实] Holcomb names Glen Campbell’s “Wichita Lineman” as his favorite or greatest country song, praising its simple portrayal of a working man missing the person he loves.

[事实] His other selections include Patsy Cline’s “Crazy,” written by Willie Nelson, and Dolly Parton’s “Jolene.”

[事实] Holcomb recalls repeatedly sharing the stage with Willie Nelson and describes Nelson and Parton as the remaining living legends of that country tradition.

[事实] Gladwell chooses George Jones’s “The Grand Tour” and acknowledges his own attraction to extravagant melancholy and country melodrama.

[57:35] **Influence, Imitation, and the Musician’s Library**

[事实] Holcomb compares songwriting to drawing from a library that expands throughout a musician’s life.

[事实] He says young writers often begin through imitation, improve as they discover their own voices, and later transform cues from their influences into something personal.

[事实] Artists such as Paul Simon and Tom Petty matter to him because their records helped him see and feel the world.

[事实] He admires artists who resist a single formula and allow different producers, eras, and musical approaches to leave distinct marks on their work.

[60:04] **Paul Simon’s “America” and Reverence for the Ryman**

[事实] Holcomb chooses Paul Simon’s “America” as his requested cover because he and Gladwell had connected over the song when they first met.

[事实] He considers its opening one of the finest first lines in songwriting and performs the full song acoustically.

[事实] Holcomb had recently watched Simon perform at Nashville’s Ryman Auditorium shortly after playing two nights there himself.

[事实] Seeing one of his heroes display the same reverence for the venue that he feels made the concert emotional, joyful, and overwhelming.

[65:24] **The Debut of “Cormac McCarthy’s Black Ferrari”**

[事实] Holcomb describes Cormac McCarthy as one of his favorite authors and was surprised to learn that McCarthy owned a black Ferrari late in life.

[事实] The contrast between McCarthy’s dark literary image and the glamorous car inspired a song about driving through the desert amid an existential crisis.

[事实] Holcomb says contemporary life feels defined by rapidly accumulating crises that make it difficult to know where to place anger, joy, or attention.

[事实] He performs “Cormac McCarthy’s Black Ferrari” publicly for the first time during the episode.

[事实] Holcomb explains that the image ultimately represents the possibility that even a chronicler of violence and despair could find temporary pleasure in speed and a beautiful machine.

[推测] The Ferrari becomes a symbol of brief release from life’s accumulated weight rather than merely an incongruous celebrity possession.

[73:25] **Turning a Solo Song Into a Band Arrangement**

[事实] Holcomb had shared the new song with his band but had not yet developed its full arrangement.

[事实] The group’s process begins with practical decisions about key and tempo before exploring instrumentation and rhythmic approach.

[事实] They may test acoustic guitar, piano, bass, drums, and other configurations until all five musicians recognize an approach that feels right.

[事实] Holcomb cannot predict in advance how the finished band version will differ from the solo performance.

[74:40] **“Dance with Everybody” and Music as Common Ground**

[事实] Holcomb wrote “Dance with Everybody” with Ketch Secor of Old Crow Medicine Show after a casual conversation while dropping their children off at school.

[事实] Both musicians had recently returned to live performance and wanted to capture how much they had missed playing for audiences.

[事实] The NCAA selected the song as a March Madness theme for two consecutive years, which amused Holcomb because the song is not about basketball and he considers himself a poor player.

[事实] The closing performance invites saints, sinners, poets, prophets, fools, and strangers to put aside differences and meet on the dance floor.

[推测] Ending with this song turns Holcomb’s bridge-building ideal into a participatory musical statement: community emerges through shared movement rather than ideological agreement.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s greatest strength is the seamless movement between conversation and performance. Holcomb does not merely explain his ideas about grief, anger, faith, and influence; the audience hears those ideas embodied in songs ranging from intimate family tributes to protest music and communal celebration.

Gladwell’s questions are most effective when they draw connections between biography and artistic technique. Discussions of Springsteen, country melodrama, Paul Simon, and Holcomb’s unfinished band arrangements make the episode valuable both as a personal portrait and as an accessible examination of how songwriters transform memory into narrative.

The conversation covers many subjects quickly, so some themes—particularly Holcomb’s evolving theology and the broader cultural meaning of Americana—remain suggestive rather than fully developed. The live setting also favors anecdote, humor, and emotional immediacy over sustained critical analysis.

This episode is especially suited to listeners interested in songwriting, Americana and country traditions, creative responses to grief, or the complicated relationship between religious inheritance and personal belief. Its central insight is that music can hold tensions that ordinary categories cannot: youth and age, faith and doubt, anger and mercy, sorrow and celebration.
