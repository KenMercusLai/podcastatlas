# Lee Harvey Oswald and the Kennedy Assassination

## 概览

This episode closes a multi-part discussion of John F. Kennedy’s assassination by focusing on Lee Harvey Oswald. The hosts frame the case through Don DeLillo’s *Libra*, using the novel’s concern with plotting, narrative, and unknowability as a way into the historical debate.

Dominic’s central argument is that Oswald is the most plausible killer and that the Warren Commission’s broad conclusion is more convincing than conspiracy explanations. He argues that Kennedy’s politics and character make a CIA-style motive implausible, while Oswald’s life, politics, resentment, violence, and movements make him a strong suspect.

The discussion follows Oswald from a troubled childhood through Marxism, the Marines, his failed Soviet adventure, his marriage to Marina, his return to Dallas, his fascination with Cuba, the Edwin Walker shooting attempt, and finally the evidence around the Texas School Book Depository. The episode ends by asking why Kennedy conspiracy theories became culturally and politically powerful.

## 分段落总结

[00:33] **DeLillo, narrative, and the Warren Commission**

[事实] The episode opens with a passage from Don DeLillo’s *Libra* about Oswald’s Marxist books giving his life a sense of historical meaning.

[事实] The hosts discuss how *Libra* treats the Kennedy assassination as a story about plotting, conspiracy, and the boundary between fiction and nonfiction.

[事实] They note that the Warren Commission concluded Oswald acted alone, did not know Jack Ruby, and was not part of a conspiracy.

[03:26] **The central argument: victim and killer**

[事实] Dominic says the key to the crime lies in the personalities of both Kennedy and Oswald.

[事实] He argues that Kennedy’s conservatism, wartime experience, patriotism, and commitment to America’s global role make it implausible that powerful government agencies had a meaningful motive to murder him.

[事实] Dominic states directly that he believes Lee Harvey Oswald killed John F. Kennedy and that no other explanation carries as much evidentiary weight.

[05:05] **Oswald’s childhood and family background**

[事实] Oswald was born in New Orleans in September 1939 to Robert Oswald and Margaret Claverie; his father died of a heart attack two months before he was born.

[事实] The hosts describe his mother Margaret as troubled and unstable, and note that Oswald grew up as a shy, withdrawn, difficult boy.

[事实] School reports described him as sullen and aggressive, while the hosts emphasize that his reading and politics became important to his self-image.

[06:41] **Loneliness, resentment, and early psychological assessments**

[事实] Dominic compares Oswald with later would-be or actual assassins such as John Hinckley and Arthur Bremer, describing a pattern of troubled childhoods, loneliness, and frustrated dreams.

[事实] At age twelve, Oswald moved with his mother to New York, attended school in the Bronx, played truant, and was sent for psychiatric assessment.

[事实] A caseworker described him as detached, withdrawn, emotionally starved, and lacking affection; a psychiatrist diagnosed personality disturbance with schizoid features and passive-aggressive tendencies.

[推测] The hosts treat these early reports as important because they resemble later descriptions of Oswald after his arrest in Dallas.

[10:18] **Marxism as explanation and dignity**

[事实] The hosts say Oswald became a socialist and then a Marxist at a young age, writing to the Socialist Party of America that he had studied socialist principles for over fifteen months.

[事实] Dominic argues that Marxism gave Oswald answers, dignity, and a sense of being part of a sweeping historical process.

[推测] The discussion suggests that if Oswald had not found Marxism, another militant political or religious creed might have served a similar emotional function for him.

[12:30] **The Marines, marksmanship, and “Red Lee”**

[事实] Oswald left school and joined the U.S. Marines, serving in places including Mississippi, California, Japan, and the Philippines.

[事实] Dominic says Oswald was trained to shoot, qualified as a sharpshooter in 1956, and later scored lower but still qualified as a marksman in 1959.

[事实] Oswald studied Russian, read left-wing material, called himself a communist or Marxist, and was known among fellow Marines as “Red Lee.”

[事实] He was described as unpopular and insubordinate, then left the Marines in 1959 after falsely saying his mother was ill.

[16:01] **The Soviet defection**

[事实] Oswald traveled through Europe to Helsinki and then by train to Moscow in October 1959, where he told Soviet officials he wanted to become a Soviet citizen.

[事实] Soviet and KGB officials were bewildered by him, and after being told he could not stay, Oswald attempted suicide by cutting his wrist.

[事实] He later went to the U.S. Embassy and said he wanted to give up his American citizenship.

[事实] The hosts say the KGB eventually concluded Oswald was too inept to be a CIA spy and sent him to Minsk, where he worked in an electronics factory as a lathe operator.

[20:16] **Marina, Minsk, and the return to America**

[事实] In Minsk, Oswald met Marina Prusakova, a nineteen-year-old pharmacology student from Archangel, and they married after six weeks in April 1961.

[事实] KGB wiretaps recorded a tempestuous relationship, and their daughter June was born in February 1962.

[事实] Oswald became eager to return to the United States, reclaimed his American citizenship in May 1962, and received a $435 loan from the U.S. Embassy for repatriation expenses.

[事实] The hosts say both Soviet and American authorities treated him as unreliable, difficult, and not useful as an intelligence asset.

[24:38] **Dallas, frustration, and Cuba**

[事实] After returning to the Dallas-Fort Worth area in 1962, Oswald worked as a sheet metal worker and then at a photo print company, where he was fired for being insubordinate and difficult.

[事实] Dominic describes him as aggressive, sullen, and violent, including beating Marina.

[事实] Oswald remained committed to communism, and his main political fascination became Cuba.

[推测] The hosts suggest Cuba may have looked more romantic to Oswald than the disappointing Soviet reality he had experienced in Minsk.

[27:04] **The rifle and Edwin Walker**

[事实] On March 12, 1963, Oswald bought a mail-order rifle under the false name A.J. Hidell.

[事实] The hosts discuss his use of a false name as consistent with his hostility to authority and fantasy of living in a world of espionage.

[事实] Oswald later tried to shoot Major General Edwin Walker, a right-wing segregationist in Dallas, but missed.

[事实] He had left Marina a note suggesting he might not return, and Marina later said he told her what he had done.

[30:40] **New Orleans, Mexico City, and the Book Depository**

[事实] Oswald moved to New Orleans, formed a local branch of the Fair Play for Cuba Committee, and was briefly arrested after scuffling with anti-Castro activists.

[事实] In September 1963, he took a bus to Mexico City and went to the Cuban and Soviet consulates to seek a visa for Cuba, but did not obtain one.

[事实] After returning to Dallas, he learned through Ruth Paine of a job at the Texas School Book Depository and began working there on October 14.

[事实] The FBI already knew of Oswald and had spoken to Marina and the Paines about him, which made him angry.

[32:47] **The motorcade as opportunity**

[事实] Dallas newspapers began reporting around November 7 and 8 that Kennedy and his wife would visit the city.

[事实] On November 15, the papers said there probably would not be a motorcade, but on November 19 they reported the motorcade route would pass the Book Depository.

[事实] Dominic argues that Oswald could not have planned the assassination before knowing Kennedy would pass the building where he worked.

[推测] Dominic suggests November 19 may have been when Oswald saw a chance to make himself historically significant by killing a man who represented American capitalism, power, and policy toward Cuba.

[34:52] **Evidence from the Book Depository**

[事实] Dominic says investigators compiled around fifty to sixty pieces of evidence pointing to Oswald.

[事实] The night before the murder, Oswald unusually visited Marina on a weeknight and collected a package he later called curtain rods; the hosts identify this as the rifle.

[事实] Witnesses saw someone on the sixth floor of the Book Depository, and police later found a sniper’s nest and a gun there.

[事实] Oswald was the only employee who at any point placed himself on the sixth floor, and he was also the only employee who left the building immediately after the shooting.

[36:35] **Flight, Tippit, arrest, and interrogation**

[事实] After leaving the Book Depository, Oswald took a bus, then a taxi, went home, and collected his revolver.

[事实] Police officer J.D. Tippit stopped him, and Oswald shot and killed Tippit.

[事实] Oswald was arrested in a movie theater, violently resisted arrest, and tried to pull his gun.

[事实] Under interrogation, he lied about the curtain rods, buying the rifle, and the photograph of himself with the rifle; Marina and his brother Robert both thought his behavior suggested guilt.

[39:40] **Shots, the “magic bullet,” and motive**

[事实] The hosts address the objection that Oswald could not have fired the shots quickly enough, saying similar performances have been duplicated and improved upon by other marksmen using a similar rifle.

[事实] They discuss the “magic bullet” objection and say many experts believe the bullet could have passed through Kennedy and into John Connally because Connally was lower, to the left, and turned away.

[事实] Dominic rejects the CIA explanation as lacking both plausibility and obvious motive, while describing Oswald as a violent man who had already tried to kill Walker and had killed Tippit.

[推测] The hosts suggest Kennedy may have been an especially satisfying target for Oswald because Kennedy was rich, charismatic, successful, and seemingly favored by fate.

[42:40] **Assassin profiles and conspiracy appeal**

[事实] Dominic compares Oswald to later assassins or attempted assassins and to the Travis Bickle archetype from *Taxi Driver*: alienated men who feel life has gone against them.

[事实] He argues that Oswald fits the known profile of such killers and that Occam’s razor points to Oswald rather than a more complex conspiracy.

[事实] The hosts say conspiracy theories are emotionally satisfying because they make believers feel they possess secret knowledge that explains not just one crime but American political history.

[44:05] **Kennedy’s afterlife and conspiracy politics**

[事实] The hosts say the assassination occurred at the moment when the optimism of the 1960s was beginning to turn, before Vietnam, civil rights conflict, law-and-order politics, and later cultural anxieties intensified.

[事实] They mention Jim Garrison’s investigation, Clay Shaw, and a wave of assassination books in 1966, 1967, and 1968.

[事实] Dominic argues that even if Kennedy had lived, he would still have faced Vietnam, civil rights, economic problems, law-and-order concerns, drugs, and campus unrest.

[事实] Dominic says Kennedy conspiracy theories helped feed later American populism, “deep state” thinking, and paranoid conspiracy politics, including QAnon.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its clear narrative synthesis: it does not simply list conspiracy claims, but reconstructs Oswald’s biography to explain why the hosts find him a plausible lone assassin.

[推测] Its strongest sections are the links between Oswald’s childhood, Marxism, failed Soviet adventure, Cuba obsession, and later violence. The episode is especially useful for listeners who want a historically grounded argument against elaborate Kennedy assassination conspiracies.

[推测] Its limitation is that the case is presented through the hosts’ interpretation of selected evidence rather than a full courtroom-style examination of every contested detail. Listeners looking for a neutral catalog of all theories may find the episode argumentative.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners interested in political history, conspiracy culture, presidential assassination, and the psychology of ideological violence.
