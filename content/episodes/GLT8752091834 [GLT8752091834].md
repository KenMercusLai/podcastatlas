# American Crusades in the American Century

## 概览

This episode examines why religion remained so prominent in American political culture while much of Western Europe became less overtly religious, and how that difference shaped U.S. foreign policy through the twentieth century.

Andrew Preston argues that American exceptionalism, church-state separation, and a competitive “marketplace of faith” helped keep religion politically vital. The discussion then follows how Catholics and Jews were folded into a “Judeo-Christian” national story during World War II and the Cold War.

The episode’s main through-line is that American foreign policy repeatedly framed conflicts as moral and even religious struggles: against Nazism, atheistic communism, terrorism, and later through pro-Israel politics and evangelical support for Donald Trump.

## 分段落总结

[00:09] **Religion and the American Century**

[事实] The episode brings back Professor Andrew Preston to discuss America’s relationship with religion at home and abroad in the twentieth century.
[事实] The hosts frame the discussion around American exceptionalism, the Cold War, Nixon, Reagan, George W. Bush, and Donald Trump.
[事实] The opening question asks why Americans remained more overtly religious than many Western Europeans.

[01:57] **American Exceptionalism and Religious Persistence**

[事实] Preston says religion is still deeply embedded in American political culture in ways that differ from many other countries.
[事实] He defines American exceptionalism as the belief that America is different from other countries and, for many Americans, better.
[事实] He argues that modernization theory struggled to explain the United States because America became highly modern while remaining strongly religious.
[事实] He links American religiosity to church-state separation, which meant churches were not discredited when political authorities were discredited.
[事实] He says the lack of state support created a competitive “marketplace of faith,” encouraging religious innovation and new movements.

[04:51] **The Invention of the Judeo-Christian Tradition**

[事实] Preston says Americans did not speak of a “Judeo-Christian” tradition before the late 1930s.
[事实] He says Franklin Roosevelt began invoking Judeo-Christian heritage as the United States positioned itself against Nazi Germany.
[事实] World War II propaganda presented Catholics, Protestants, and Jews as fighting together for the same American cause.
[事实] During the Cold War, the key divide shifted from Protestant versus Catholic or Jew to religious versus nonreligious.

[07:10] **Crusade, Isolationism, and American Engagement**

[事实] Preston rejects the term “isolationism,” arguing that the United States had long been active and often aggressive abroad.
[事实] He distinguishes between reluctance to make political commitments to other countries and actual isolation from the world.
[事实] He describes two religious impulses in American foreign policy: a warlike “sword of the spirit” and a more pacifist, reformist “shield of faith.”
[推测] The episode presents American “crusading” as compatible with both interventionist and restraint-oriented religious arguments.

[09:20] **The Religious Flavor of the Cold War**

[事实] Preston says the American Cold War was unusually religious compared with Western Europe.
[事实] He argues that religion was not just rhetorical decoration but part of what many Americans understood their national cause to be.
[事实] Truman wanted a major religious summit of world leaders as the Cold War intensified.
[事实] The anecdote about Turkey’s response highlights the tension between America’s constitutional church-state separation and its religiously framed foreign policy.

[13:39] **Catholics, Jews, Israel, and Cold War Pluralism**

[事实] Truman recognized the state of Israel despite warnings from many diplomats that it would anger the Arab world.
[事实] Preston says Truman identified with Cyrus as a liberator of the Jews, while many professional diplomats did not understand the biblical reference.
[事实] The recognition of Israel is presented as part of a wider Cold War commitment to religion against irreligion, tyranny, and aggression.
[推测] The discussion suggests that foreign policy and domestic interfaith relations reinforced each other during the Cold War.

[15:31] **Reagan and the Revival of Evangelical Language**

[事实] The hosts describe Reagan as personally complex and religiously hard to pin down, while also embraced by evangelicals.
[事实] Preston says Nixon was the first president to start ending speeches with “God bless America.”
[事实] Preston argues Reagan was not a total break but revived religious language in a politically skillful way.
[事实] Reagan called the Soviet Union an “evil empire” in a speech to the National Association of Evangelicals.
[事实] Preston places Reagan’s anti-communism within the “sword of the spirit” tradition.

[18:14] **Religious Freedom and the End of the Cold War**

[事实] Preston says Reagan also used religion more subtly as he sought a way out of the Cold War.
[事实] The Siberian Seven, a group of Pentecostals who sought asylum in the U.S. embassy in Moscow, became a major political issue.
[事实] Reagan quietly negotiated their departure and kept his promise not to exploit it politically.
[事实] Preston describes this as an early confidence-building measure between the United States and the Soviet Union.
[事实] Reagan continued to press Gorbachev on religious freedom and individual religious choice.

[20:11] **The End of History as Secularized Providence**

[事实] Tom notes that Francis Fukuyama, author of the “end of history” idea, was the son of a Protestant minister.
[事实] Tom interprets Fukuyama’s vision as a secularized version of the end times, with liberal democracy spreading universally.
[事实] The discussion connects post-Cold War America to the idea of defending what is good against what is evil.
[推测] This section frames liberal democratic universalism as carrying traces of older Protestant and providential ideas.

[21:13] **9/11, George W. Bush, and Islam**

[事实] Preston says George W. Bush’s “crusade” comment after 9/11 was a genuine slip of the tongue.
[事实] He also says the word reflected existing American attitudes toward foreign policy.
[事实] Bush is described as comfortable with Protestant evangelical language.
[事实] Preston says Bush also invoked religious pluralism, arguing that al-Qaeda did not represent true Islam.
[事实] Preston says Obama continued a similar pluralist message in his Cairo speech in June 2009.

[24:05] **Trump, Evangelicals, and Secularized Religion**

[事实] Preston says Trump was very pro-Israel partly because pro-Israel politics mattered to his political base.
[事实] White evangelicals supported Trump in higher numbers and proportions than they had supported George W. Bush or Reagan.
[事实] Preston describes Trump himself as very irreligious but able to say and do the things expected by the religious right.
[事实] The discussion connects Trump to Norman Vincent Peale and the “power of positive thinking.”
[推测] The episode’s closing idea is that American culture can remain religiously shaped even when its political figures are not personally devout.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its clear connection between domestic religious structures and foreign policy behavior. It does not treat religious rhetoric as merely decorative; it shows how religious assumptions shaped American ideas of freedom, democracy, evil, pluralism, and national mission.

[推测] The strongest moments are the concrete anecdotes: Truman seeking a global religious summit, Truman identifying with Cyrus, Reagan’s handling of the Siberian Seven, and Trump’s relationship with evangelical politics despite his personal irreligion.

[推测] A limitation is that the transcript focuses mainly on high-level political rhetoric and presidential examples. It gives less space to dissenting religious voices, non-Christian perspectives inside the United States, or the experiences of people affected by American foreign policy.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in U.S. political culture, religion and diplomacy, the Cold War, and the religious background to modern American conservatism.
