# How the U.S. Lost Rare Earths and Is Trying to Win Them Back

## 概览

This episode traces the rise, collapse, and attempted revival of the U.S. rare earths industry through the story of the Mountain Pass mine in California and the company Molycorp.

The central argument is that rare earths became essential to modern technology, but China built dominance not only through mining, but through processing, financing, technical expertise, supply-chain control, and state-backed industrial policy.

The episode moves from the 1949 discovery at Mountain Pass, to Molycorp’s monopoly, China’s takeover, the 2010 Japan export shock, Molycorp’s failed comeback, and the U.S. government’s current effort to rebuild domestic rare earth capacity.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **The Accidental Discovery at Mountain Pass**

[事实] Prospectors in 1949 were searching for uranium in the mountains between Los Angeles and Las Vegas when their Geiger counters detected something unusual.

[事实] They had found a major deposit of rare earths, including elements such as lanthanum, cerium, neodymium, praseodymium, and europium.

[事实] The episode says rare earths later became critical for products ranging from iPhones and microwaves to fighter jets.

[事实] China now processes about 90% of the world’s rare earths.

[03:00] **Mountain Pass as the Center of the Story**

[事实] The hosts identify the site as the Mountain Pass Rare Earths Mine, located near the California-Nevada border.

[事实] For decades, Mountain Pass was one of the world’s most important producers of europium and other rare earth metals.

[推测] The episode uses Mountain Pass as a narrative anchor because its history mirrors the broader shift of rare earth power from the United States to China.

[05:20] **Molycorp’s Rise and the Color TV Boom**

[事实] By the 1960s, Mountain Pass had become a successful mining operation run by Molycorp.

[事实] Rare earths found commercial uses in polishing camera lenses and mirrors, refining oil, and producing the red color in color televisions.

[事实] Mark Smith says every color television made during one period used europium from the Mountain Pass deposit to create red color.

[事实] Molycorp had a rare earths monopoly by the 1960s and was the world’s key supplier for years.

[07:47] **Early Signs of Decline**

[事实] Mark Smith joined Molycorp around 1987 or 1988, when the company was still significant but facing pressure.

[事实] He recalls meetings about unsold cerium and lanthanum, suggesting demand for Molycorp’s products was weakening.

[事实] Smith suspected customers were buying cheaper rare earth products from China.

[推测] The episode presents this as the first visible sign that China was beginning to displace the U.S. rare earths industry.

[09:41] **The China Visit to Molycorp**

[事实] Smith recounts a story that, in the 1960s, Molycorp’s CEO invited visitors from China to observe the company’s mining and processing operations.

[事实] According to Smith, those visitors could walk around, take pictures, and bring information back to China.

[事实] Emily Fang explains that China at the time was not yet the economic powerhouse it later became and may not have looked like a threat to Molycorp.

[推测] The episode treats the visit as symbolically important, while acknowledging that it may not fully explain China’s later dominance.

[11:27] **China’s Long Industrial Buildout**

[事实] Professor Rod Eggert co-authored a paper on China’s rare earth policies from 1975 to 2018.

[事实] His research suggests Chinese leaders saw rare earths as useful inputs for engineered high-tech products.

[事实] Eggert says rare earths were a small part of China’s broader strategy to become a global manufacturing hub.

[事实] China established a rare earth office around 1975, though the early industry was described as dirty, unregulated, and small-scale.

[14:50] **How China Built Dominance**

[事实] China used policies such as low-cost financing, training scientists and engineers, and encouraging domestic competition among rare earth companies.

[事实] China restricted large-scale export of rare earth ore and pushed refining and processing to happen inside China.

[事实] Foreign companies that wanted access to China’s rare earth industry had to set up facilities and move supply chains into China.

[事实] By the 1990s, China could mine and process rare earths as well as the United States, but more cheaply.

[推测] The episode suggests China’s real advantage was not simply geology, but control over processing capacity and supply-chain structure.

[18:12] **Molycorp Shuts Down Mountain Pass**

[事实] Mark Smith says Molycorp shut down its Mountain Pass operation after it could not find enough buyers for its products.

[事实] He says that when Mountain Pass shut down, there was effectively no rare earth production left outside China.

[推测] The shutdown marked the point where U.S. dependence on China became a structural vulnerability rather than just a business problem.

[19:35] **The 2010 Japan Shock**

[事实] In 2010, a Chinese fishing boat captain was detained after an incident near disputed islands called Diaoyu by China and Senkaku by Japan.

[事实] China informally stopped selling rare earth products to Japan after the incident.

[事实] Japan’s car and electronics industries were heavily dependent on rare earth products.

[事实] The episode says the incident showed that the world had become highly reliant on rare earths and that China had major leverage over the global economy.

[22:07] **Price Spike and Molycorp’s Comeback Attempt**

[事实] Around the Japan dispute, China also reduced overall rare earth exports, saying it needed more for domestic manufacturing.

[事实] Rare earth prices rose dramatically, with the episode citing increases of about 600% to 700%.

[事实] Mark Smith, then CEO of Molycorp, saw an opportunity to reopen Mountain Pass.

[事实] The reopening plan was called Project Phoenix.

[23:39] **Project Phoenix Expands Too Fast**

[事实] The first phase of Project Phoenix was to restart mining and processing at Mountain Pass on a smaller scale.

[事实] Molycorp received significant media attention, including interviews on Bloomberg.

[事实] Smith then considered a second phase to double production capacity, aiming for 40,000 tons by the end of 2012.

[事实] Smith now says he should have focused on phase one and delayed expansion.

[26:11] **The Price Crash and Molycorp’s Demise**

[事实] Smith says that after Molycorp announced plans to double output, China released a large amount of rare earth product onto the global market.

[事实] Prices for the products Molycorp planned to refine dropped sharply.

[事实] Smith acknowledges that his claim about China’s intent is an allegation rather than a proven fact.

[事实] The episode says there is no smoking gun, but China’s state-owned rare earth sector can coordinate at a high level.

[推测] The failed expansion became a warning about trying to rebuild supply in a market where a dominant producer can influence prices.

[27:29] **Mark Smith’s Warning**

[事实] Smith says the more important rare earths become, the riskier it is to depend completely on China.

[事实] He has spent years trying to explain rare earth risks and benefits to people in many settings, including planes and airports.

[事实] When the producers first spoke with him nearly a year earlier, he said it was still difficult to get people’s attention.

[推测] Smith is portrayed as someone whose warnings seemed niche until geopolitics made the supply-chain risk obvious.

[30:03] **The 2025 U.S. Wake-Up Call**

[事实] The episode says President Trump announced “Liberation Day” tariffs in April 2025, including large tariffs on Chinese goods.

[事实] Emily Fang says China later limited exports of some critical rare earths needed by the United States.

[事实] The Defense Department realized it lacked replacements for some Chinese-made magnets and products used in defense equipment.

[推测] The episode frames this as a delayed U.S. recognition of a vulnerability that Japan had already experienced in 2010.

[31:17] **The U.S. Turns to State-Driven Policy**

[事实] The U.S. government has started putting billions of dollars into companies developing rare earth mines and refining facilities.

[事实] The episode mentions grants, loans, government equity stakes, and efforts to coordinate a rare earth price floor with Mexico, Europe, and Japan.

[事实] Emily Fang says this resembles the kind of state-driven planning China used to dominate rare earths.

[推测] The U.S. is trying to reduce China’s ability to crash prices and discourage non-Chinese competitors.

[32:21] **Mark Smith’s New Role at NioCorp**

[事实] Mark Smith now works at NioCorp, which is trying to develop a rare earth mine in Nebraska.

[事实] NioCorp has received millions of dollars in government loans.

[事实] Smith says the company is meeting with the Department of War, Department of Commerce, Department of Interior, and the White House.

[事实] Smith says he would view the U.S. government as a strong equity partner if it invested directly in the company.

[34:05] **Mountain Pass’s Strange Ending**

[事实] Analysts cited in the episode estimate it may take at least five years, and possibly up to a decade, to put the American rare earths industry on solid footing.

[事实] Mountain Pass was eventually sold to MP Materials, and a Chinese state company bought a stake in MP Materials.

[事实] Until last year, MP Materials sent rare earth ore to China for refining and processing.

[事实] The Department of Defense took a 15% stake in MP Materials last year, and the company is now looking to refine everything in the United States.

[推测] The mine’s shared ties to both a Chinese state-backed company and the U.S. government underline how entangled the rare earth supply chain remains.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s main value is that it turns a technical supply-chain topic into a clear economic story: rare earth dominance depends on processing knowledge, financing, market structure, and government strategy, not just mineral deposits.

Its strongest section is the comparison between China’s long industrial policy and the United States’ current response. The episode makes the point concrete through Mark Smith’s career, Molycorp’s rise and collapse, and the repeated use of rare earths as geopolitical leverage.

[推测] The limitation is that some of the most consequential claims, especially whether China deliberately crashed prices to undermine Molycorp, remain difficult to prove from the transcript alone. The episode handles that uncertainty by labeling the claim as an allegation and noting the absence of a smoking gun.

[推测] This episode is best suited for listeners interested in industrial policy, U.S.-China competition, strategic minerals, manufacturing supply chains, and the economic history behind technologies that seem ordinary only after the supply chain works.
