# The Fall of the Aztecs, Part 1: Cortes Before Mexico

## 概览

This episode introduces the conquest of the Aztecs as both a gripping adventure story and a deeply contested historical problem. It begins with the famous meeting of Hernan Cortes and Montezuma on November 8, 1519, then steps back to explain why almost every part of that story is debated.

The hosts frame Cortes not as a simple caricature of greed, but as a product of late medieval Spain: frontier violence, chivalric romances, Christian militancy, legal training, and the restless search for fortune across the Atlantic.

The episode then follows the Spanish expansion from Hispaniola to Cuba and toward the Mexican mainland. It ends with Diego Velazquez choosing Cortes to lead the next expedition west, a choice presented as the decisive mistake that will set the larger conquest in motion.

## 分段落总结

[00:31] **The Meeting at Tenochtitlan**

[事实] The episode opens with a dramatic reading set in Tenochtitlan as the city waits for the arrival of strangers from across the sea.
[事实] Dominic identifies the scene as the meeting of Cortes and Montezuma on November 8, 1519, outside Tenochtitlan, now Mexico City.
[事实] The hosts describe the meeting as one of the most famous encounters in history and a collision between Europe and Mesoamerica.
[推测] The opening frames the episode as a story of wonder, tension, and impending catastrophe.

[02:45] **A Contested Adventure Story**

[事实] The hosts say the episode is tied to colonialism, European imperialism, and the Columbian exchange of viruses, foods, habits, and other forces.
[事实] Dominic says the story is an adventure, but also a tragedy and a story of horror.
[事实] They emphasize that recent revisionist scholarship has challenged many traditional claims about the conquest.
[推测] The episode asks listeners to treat the familiar conquest narrative as unstable rather than settled.

[04:22] **Problems With the Traditional Narrative**

[事实] Dominic says the traditional claims that the Aztecs thought the Spanish were gods or surrendered out of fear are probably largely wrong.
[事实] He says Spanish sources may be lying, self-deceiving, or simply misunderstanding what was happening.
[事实] Tom notes that questions about Montezuma’s surrender and Aztec beliefs remain politically sensitive today.
[推测] The hosts are warning that the available evidence reflects power, translation problems, and retrospective storytelling.

[06:15] **Names and Terminology**

[事实] The hosts decide to use “Aztecs,” while noting that the people themselves did not call themselves that.
[事实] They explain that “Aztec” was coined or popularized later to distinguish the Mexica from modern Mexicans.
[事实] They discuss whether to say Montezuma or Moctezuma, and note that neither is exactly the ruler’s original Nahuatl name.
[事实] They also note that Hernan Cortes may more properly have been Hernando or Fernando.

[08:12] **Cortes’s Spanish Background**

[事实] Cortes was probably born in 1484 or 1485 in Extremadura.
[事实] His father claimed Hidalgo status, though the family was not wealthy.
[事实] His mother was related to the Pizarro family, later connected to the conquest of Peru.
[事实] Dominic describes Medellin as a violent, factional, lawless place.
[推测] Cortes’s upbringing prepared him for a world in which status, violence, and opportunity were closely linked.

[11:02] **Reconquista and Christian Militancy**

[事实] Cortes grew up during the period of the Reconquista and would have heard of the fall of Granada in 1492.
[事实] Ferdinand and Isabella are described as carrying both Christianity and Spanish power southward.
[事实] The hosts mention forced conversions of Muslims and the expulsion of Jews.
[推测] The conquest of Mexico is presented as partly shaped by older Iberian models of holy war and conquest.

[13:31] **Books, Romance, and Knightly Ambition**

[事实] Cortes was literate and likely familiar with popular chivalric romances.
[事实] The hosts mention Tirant lo Blanc and Amadis de Gaula as part of the imaginative world of the conquistadors.
[事实] Bernal Diaz later compared the Valley of Mexico to scenes from such romances.
[推测] Cortes and his peers may have understood their own actions through stories of knights, castles, infidels, and heroic fortune-seeking.

[15:12] **Leaving Spain**

[事实] Cortes went to Salamanca and studied law, though it is unclear whether he formally graduated.
[事实] His family seems to have expected him to become a notary, clerk, lawyer, or royal bureaucrat.
[事实] Instead, he chose to seek his fortune across the Atlantic after Columbus’s monopoly had been broken.
[事实] He eventually sailed to Hispaniola in 1506.
[推测] Cortes’s decision shows a preference for risk and opportunity over secure status.

[17:33] **Hispaniola and Colonial Violence**

[事实] Cortes arrived in Santo Domingo, where a family connection was governor.
[事实] The Spanish were brutally repressing the Taino people, forcing them into gold mines and using violence against them.
[事实] Taino communities were dying in large numbers from disease, ecological collapse, and trauma.
[事实] The Spanish had also begun bringing in African slaves.
[推测] Cortes’s early colonial experience exposed him to extreme violence before he reached Mexico.

[19:46] **Boredom and Frustrated Ambition**

[事实] Cortes became notary of a new town on Hispaniola.
[事实] Dominic describes his life there as boring and administratively routine.
[事实] The hosts describe Cortes as a gambler, womanizer, and restless young man.
[推测] His Atlantic gamble had not yet produced the fortune or independence he wanted.

[20:49] **Cuba as the Next Step**

[事实] Dominic compares Spanish expansion to links in a chain, moving from one island to the next.
[事实] In 1511, Cortes joined Diego Velazquez’s move toward Cuba.
[事实] Velazquez was pursuing the Taino rebel Hatuey and seeking to conquer and colonize Cuba.
[推测] The conquest of Cuba became a stepping stone toward the later expedition to the Mexican mainland.

[22:24] **Hatuey and Spanish Greed**

[事实] A famous story says Hatuey warned Cuban Tainos that the Spanish worshipped a god contained in a basket of gold.
[事实] Another story says Hatuey refused baptism before execution because he did not want to spend eternity with Spaniards.
[事实] The hosts present these as memorable stories while noting uncertainty about whether such stories happened exactly as told.
[推测] Hatuey’s stories function in the episode as an early moral indictment of Spanish violence and greed.

[23:26] **Narvaez, Las Casas, and Cuba’s Brutality**

[事实] Panfilo Narvaez had been involved in violent conquests in Jamaica and Cuba.
[事实] Dominic recounts a scene in which Narvaez ordered villagers killed despite their having prepared food for the Spanish.
[事实] Bartolome de las Casas appears as a friar who protested against such brutality.
[事实] The hosts note that Las Casas later became an important critic of Spanish treatment of Indigenous peoples.

[25:12] **Encomiendas and the Colonial Economy**

[事实] After Cuba’s conquest, Velazquez distributed encomiendas to his comrades.
[事实] Europeans brought pigs, sheep, crops, and other things that transformed the landscape.
[事实] Gold shipments were sent back to Europe for the Spanish monarchs, who expected returns on these ventures.
[推测] The hosts present conquest as both ideological and entrepreneurial, driven by religion, status, and material return.

[26:37] **Cortes Under Velazquez**

[事实] Cortes became Velazquez’s secretary and later chief magistrate of Santiago de Cuba.
[事实] Cortes had a conflict over Catalina Suarez, whom he was accused of promising to marry.
[事实] Velazquez imprisoned and humiliated Cortes, who then had to marry her.
[事实] Cortes seems to have resented being subordinate to Velazquez.
[推测] This humiliation helps explain why Cortes would later be eager to act independently.

[29:21] **Armed Entrepreneurs and the Wider Atlantic World**

[事实] The hosts quote Keats’s poem about “Stout Cortez” seeing the Pacific, then explain that Keats got the figure wrong.
[事实] Vasco Nunez de Balboa, not Cortes, was the European who saw the Pacific from Panama in 1513.
[事实] Matthew Restall’s phrase “armed entrepreneurs” is used for men seeking new territories from the Caribbean.
[事实] The hosts mention other explorers, including Juan Ponce de Leon and Florida.
[推测] The Spanish Caribbean is portrayed as a competitive launchpad for private ambition under royal and colonial authority.

[31:05] **Rumors of Lands to the West**

[事实] In Cuba, Spaniards heard rumors of land to the west from sailors and surviving Taino accounts.
[事实] Some reports suggested that people could cross from the mainland by canoe in about six days.
[事实] The Spanish did not yet fully understand the geography of the American mainland.
[推测] Curiosity, rumor, and competition pushed the Spanish toward further expeditions.

[32:14] **Hernandez de Cordoba and the Yucatan**

[事实] In 1517, Francisco Hernandez de Cordoba sailed west and reached islands and coastlines near what is now Cancun.
[事实] The hosts tell the story that “Yucatan” may have come from a misunderstanding of Maya speech.
[事实] The Spanish saw stone buildings, thatched roofs, villages, towns, and pyramid temples.
[事实] Dominic says it was clear this world differed from Cuba and Hispaniola.
[推测] The expedition revealed to the Spanish that they were encountering a more urban and materially complex society.

[33:48] **Sacrifice, Source Problems, and Violence**

[事实] At Campeche, the Spanish saw temples and what they understood as evidence of sacrifice.
[事实] The hosts stress that communication with local people was deeply unclear.
[事实] They say human sacrifice did exist, but Spanish accounts of it are controversial.
[事实] Hernandez de Cordoba was later attacked, badly wounded, and died after returning to Cuba.
[推测] The discussion uses sacrifice to show how Spanish fear, projection, and justification complicate the record.

[36:15] **Grijalva’s Expedition**

[事实] In January 1518, Velazquez sent his nephew Juan de Grijalva with four ships and about 200 men.
[事实] Grijalva had cannon, crossbows, arquebuses, and dogs.
[事实] He followed the Yucatan route, visited Cozumel and Campeche, and reached Tabasco.
[事实] At Tabasco, local people traded food and small gold items with the Spanish.
[推测] Gold quickly turned the expedition from exploration into a major political and commercial opportunity.

[38:32] **Greed, Faith, and Dread**

[事实] The hosts say the Spanish were excited by gold and also genuinely Christian in their desire to convert people.
[事实] They also describe the Spaniards as frightened by unfamiliar temples, blood, and customs.
[事实] Tom compares the experience to science fiction or astronauts landing on an alien planet.
[推测] The episode stresses that Spanish motives were mixed rather than reducible to one cause.

[39:10] **The Totonacs and News of the Aztecs**

[事实] Grijalva encountered the Totonacs on the coast.
[事实] The Totonacs spoke Nahuatl, a language unfamiliar to the Spanish.
[事实] They told the Spanish about a powerful inland people, the Mexica or Aztecs, who demanded tribute.
[事实] They described a rich, ordered society with stone houses, laws, and public justice.
[推测] The Totonacs’ complaints suggested to the Spanish that Indigenous political divisions could become strategically important.

[42:03] **Gold and Spanish Expectations**

[事实] The Totonacs showed the Spanish gold ornaments, tiaras, figures, and other items.
[事实] A Totonac spokesman claimed there was much more gold available from the hills and streams.
[事实] Grijalva understood this as evidence of enormous wealth.
[推测] The boasting about gold likely increased Spanish appetite for a larger expedition.

[43:03] **The Aztec Envoy Arrives**

[事实] Rumors of the Spanish reached the great inland city.
[事实] An envoy named Teocla Macazki, described as “keeper of the house of darkness,” came from that city to meet the Spanish.
[事实] He brought gold necklaces, bracelets, feathers, and jewels.
[事实] The Spanish gave him wine, beads, and ship’s biscuits.
[推测] The gift exchange shows both hospitality and mutual misunderstanding before direct confrontation.

[45:15] **Grijalva Does Not Go Inland**

[事实] Grijalva thought the encounter had gone extremely well.
[事实] He did not follow the envoys inland to the great city.
[事实] The hosts say he had not been ordered to do so and had only a few hundred men.
[推测] His caution created the opening for Cortes to become the central figure in the next phase.

[46:33] **Alvarado Returns With the News**

[事实] Grijalva sent Pedro de Alvarado back to Cuba with gold and reports of what had been found.
[事实] Alvarado reached Santiago in early autumn 1518.
[事实] Velazquez was delighted by the news of rich lands and organized societies to the west.
[推测] The return of gold and intelligence made delay dangerous for Velazquez because rivals might move first.

[47:20] **Velazquez Chooses Cortes**

[事实] Velazquez wanted to secure the new lands before competitors, especially Diego Colon of Hispaniola.
[事实] His preferred relatives refused the risky command.
[事实] Velazquez wanted someone capable but controllable.
[事实] He chose Hernan Cortes.
[推测] The hosts present this as Velazquez’s greatest mistake and the cliffhanger that launches the next episode.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it makes a familiar conquest story feel uncertain again. Instead of treating Cortes versus Montezuma as a simple clash of heroes and victims, it foregrounds mistranslation, self-serving sources, later myths, and political sensitivity.

[推测] Its strongest feature is the combination of narrative momentum and historiographical caution. The hosts keep the story vivid, but repeatedly remind listeners that many famous details may be formulaic, exaggerated, misunderstood, or contested.

[推测] The limitation is that this installment is largely setup: it does not yet narrate Cortes’s march inland, the meeting with Montezuma, or the fall of Tenochtitlan. It is best suited to listeners who want context before the better-known dramatic events.
