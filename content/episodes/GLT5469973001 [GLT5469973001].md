# Anglo-Ukrainian Relations, Football, and the Many Histories of Ukraine

## 概览

This episode is a Euro 2020 special prompted by England’s quarter-final against Ukraine in Rome. After joking about their failed prediction that England would lose to Germany, the hosts use the match as an excuse to explore whether there is enough historical material for an “Anglo-Ukrainian relations” episode.

The discussion moves quickly from comic football analogies into deeper history: Indo-European language origins on the Ukrainian steppe, Kievan Rus, Viking routes, Byzantine Christianity, Crimea’s sacred status, the Crimean War, and the British memory of heroic failure.

The second half focuses on more concrete British-Ukrainian links: the Welsh industrialist John Hughes and the origins of Donetsk as Huzovka, Ukraine’s violent 20th-century history, and Gareth Jones’s reporting on the Holodomor. The episode closes by returning to football, with jokes about England players as historical archetypes and a sympathetic note about Ukraine’s difficult recent and modern history.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **England, Germany, and the Setup for Ukraine**

[事实] The hosts open by admitting that their previous football-themed prediction, that England would lose humiliatingly to Germany, was comprehensively wrong.

[事实] England’s victory over Germany is described as the first knockout win over Germany since 1966.

[事实] Tom missed watching the match live because he was attending his son’s school play, but he successfully avoided learning the score before watching it later.

[推测] The opening establishes the episode’s pattern: football serves as a light entry point into historical discussion.

[02:47] **Football, Fukuyama, and the End of History**

[事实] Tom says he had to interview Francis Fukuyama shortly after the England-Germany match and had delayed the interview by an hour.

[事实] The hosts connect Fukuyama’s “end of history” idea to international sport, suggesting that sporting rivalry can channel national passions away from violence.

[事实] Dominic challenges this by mentioning the Football War in Central America and football-related tensions before the breakup of Yugoslavia.

[推测] The exchange frames football as both a substitute for conflict and, at times, a warning sign of political violence.

[04:33] **Ukraine as a Historically Rich Subject**

[事实] The hosts introduce England’s upcoming match against Ukraine in Rome as the reason for the special episode.

[事实] Dominic initially presents Anglo-Ukrainian relations as a harder subject than Anglo-German relations.

[事实] Ukraine is described as an “unbelievably interesting country,” with references to Kievan Rus, Mongol destruction, the Polish-Lithuanian Commonwealth, and later Austrian and Russian imperial divisions.

[推测] The hosts use this overview to argue that Ukraine’s history is broader and more complex than a simple football preview would suggest.

[05:48] **The Indo-European Roots of “Football”**

[事实] Tom jokingly argues that if Ukraine beats England, football will be “going home” because English is an Indo-European language.

[事实] He says the widely accepted homeland of Indo-Europeans was around southern Russia and the Ukrainian steppe, above the Caspian and Black Seas.

[事实] He traces “foot” and “ball” back through reconstructed Indo-European roots, while Dominic points out that the same argument could be made for many English words.

[推测] This is presented as a deliberately stretched but playful historical-linguistic argument rather than a serious claim about the invention of football.

[08:20] **Kievan Rus, Byzantium, and the Varangian Guard**

[事实] The hosts discuss the Viking origins of Kiev, describing the Rus as Swedish rowers who came down the great rivers and controlled trade routes.

[事实] Vladimir of Kiev converts to Byzantine Christianity after being impressed by Hagia Sophia and the beauty of Constantinople’s Christian worship.

[事实] The Kievan Rus become connected to Byzantium, and Vikings are recruited into the emperor’s Varangian Guard.

[事实] After 1066, displaced Anglo-Saxon nobles are said to have migrated to Constantinople and joined the Varangian Guard.

[10:10] **A “New England” in Crimea**

[事实] Tom cites historian Caitlin Green’s work on the possibility that Anglo-Saxon Varangians were granted land in Crimea by the Byzantine emperor.

[事实] Medieval saga material is said to describe towns in this “New England” being named after English towns such as London and York.

[事实] Italian charts from the 14th to 16th centuries are described as mentioning places called Londina and Sasako.

[推测] The evidence is treated as “tantalizing” rather than conclusive, but strong enough for the hosts’ Anglo-Ukrainian theme.

[12:03] **Crimea’s Sacred Significance**

[事实] The hosts discuss Crimea from a medieval perspective and note that Vladimir the Great was baptized there.

[事实] Crimea is described as significant to both Ukrainians and Russians because it is linked to the origins of their Christianity.

[事实] The Russian annexation of Crimea is connected to religious symbolism, including priests blessing Russian forces.

[事实] The hosts compare Crimea’s religious-political resonance to Canterbury, Rome, Jerusalem, and Kosovo for the Serbs.

[14:37] **The Crimean War as a British Memory**

[事实] The Crimean War is described as a conflict from 1853 to 1856 involving a Turkish, British, and French alliance against Russia.

[事实] The trigger is linked to disputes over administration of holy places in Ottoman-controlled Israel and Palestine, along with broader fears about Russian expansion and control of the Straits.

[事实] The war is described as one of the first modern wars because of railways, telegraphs, photography, Florence Nightingale, and war correspondents.

[事实] Sevastopol is presented as the central theater of the conflict.

[16:39] **The Light Brigade, the Thin Red Line, and Football Tactics**

[事实] The hosts compare two Crimean War images to football styles: the Charge of the Light Brigade as reckless attack, and the Thin Red Line as disciplined defense.

[事实] The Thin Red Line is identified as involving the Sutherland Highlanders under Colin Campbell facing Russian cavalry.

[事实] Gareth Southgate is jokingly compared to a “thin red line” defensive figure.

[事实] The Charge of the Light Brigade is described as a disastrous cavalry attack caused by poor communication among Lord Raglan, Lord Lucan, Lord Cardigan, and Nolan.

[20:14] **Heroic Failure and British Football Culture**

[事实] The hosts discuss how British culture often mythologizes bloody disasters and heroic failure.

[事实] Tennyson’s poem on the Charge of the Light Brigade is said to help establish this template.

[事实] The hosts connect this to English football memories such as Paul Gascoigne crying after England were knocked out.

[推测] The discussion suggests that England’s calm progress in Euro 2020 feels culturally unusual because English football has often been narrated through noble failure.

[22:00] **Crimean War Legacies in Britain**

[事实] Dominic describes public discontent during the Crimean War, including snowball riots and criticism of management.

[事实] The fall of Prime Minister Aberdeen and the rise of Palmerston are mentioned in connection with the war.

[事实] Captured Russian guns from Sevastopol were distributed to British towns and colonies, with examples including Hartlepool, Abingdon, and Canada.

[事实] The hosts mention the claim that Victoria Cross medals are made from melted-down Sevastopol guns, while Dominic notes that this is controversial.

[23:42] **Nightingale, Mary Seacole, and Mythmaking**

[事实] Florence Nightingale and Mary Seacole are discussed as legacies of the Crimean War.

[事实] Dominic says Mary Seacole has become an icon in British school history, even though he says she was not a nurse and “basically just ran a hotel.”

[事实] Tom comments that Seacole’s modern reputation is an example of continued mythologizing of people from British imperial history.

[推测] The hosts treat both Victorian and modern historical memory as shaped by symbolic figures rather than only by documented roles.

[25:18] **Huzovka and the British Origins of Donetsk**

[事实] In the second half, Dominic introduces Huzovka, the settlement that became Donetsk.

[事实] He says Donetsk began as a British foundation after the Russian Empire brought in industrial talent in the late 1860s.

[事实] The Millwall Ironworks and Shipbuilding Company sent John Hughes, a Welshman from Merthyr Tydfil, to develop iron and coal industries in what is now Ukraine.

[事实] Hughes brought over hundreds of largely Welsh skilled workers, engineers, and miners.

[28:29] **A Welsh and English Industrial Community in Ukraine**

[事实] By the end of the 19th century, Huzovka had about 30,000 residents, including many Welsh or English people.

[事实] The settlement had an Anglican church and an English-speaking school.

[事实] Huzovka became a major industrial center and later changed names to Stalino and then Donetsk.

[事实] After the Russian Revolution, the British community was expelled, with some returning to Wales after losing their wealth.

[30:31] **Ukraine’s Violent 20th Century**

[事实] Dominic describes Ukraine’s 20th-century history as exceptionally grim.

[事实] After the First World War, he says multiple armies fought for control of Ukraine, including White Russians, Bolsheviks, anarchists, Poles, and Ukrainian nationalist groups.

[事实] The discussion mentions the early 1930s famine under Stalin, the Nazi invasion, collaboration and resistance, the Red Army’s return, and conflict involving Poles.

[事实] Timothy Snyder’s book *Bloodlands* is cited as framing Ukraine as central to a territory of mass killing.

[31:37] **Gareth Jones and the Holodomor**

[事实] Gareth Jones, a Welsh British journalist, is described as exposing the Holodomor, the famine in early 1930s Ukraine.

[事实] Jones’s mother had worked in Huzovka as tutor to John Hughes’s son.

[事实] Jones reported on the famine in *The Times* after traveling to Russia and Ukraine.

[事实] Walter Duranty of *The New York Times* is accused in the discussion of falsely denying Jones’s reports despite knowing they were true.

[35:02] **History, Footballers, and Comic Digressions**

[事实] The hosts joke that England players should avoid discussing history with Ukrainian players, because Crimea would be a sensitive topic.

[事实] Harry Maguire’s arrest in Greece is discussed through a comic claim that he had insulted Greek civilization.

[事实] The hosts compare England players to historical types: Maguire to wartime newsreel figures, Jack Grealish to Dickens or the First World War, and Harry Kane to a Habsburg prince.

[推测] This section mainly returns the episode to comic football banter after the heavier material on Ukraine’s modern suffering.

[37:48] **Possible Future Episodes and Sympathy for Ukraine**

[事实] The hosts say they should predict England will lose to Ukraine because their previous pessimistic prediction worked before the Germany match.

[事实] They discuss possible future episodes on Anglo-Danish or Anglo-Czech relations if England progresses.

[事实] Dominic says a Ukrainian victory would probably bring Ukrainians greater joy than an English victory would bring England fans.

[事实] The episode ends by noting that Ukraine has had a bad decade and a dreadful century.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is that it turns a topical football fixture into a wide-ranging historical tour without pretending that Anglo-Ukrainian relations are a simple or continuous story.

Its strongest sections are the concrete historical connections: the possible Anglo-Saxon “New England” in Crimea, the Crimean War’s afterlife in British memory, John Hughes and Huzovka, and Gareth Jones’s reporting on the Holodomor.

[推测] The main limitation is that several links are intentionally playful or speculative, especially the linguistic “football going home” argument and the football-tactics comparisons to Crimean War episodes.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners who enjoy history presented through banter, unexpected connections, and football-adjacent cultural references, rather than a tightly structured survey of Ukrainian history.
