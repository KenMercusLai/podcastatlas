# The Rise and Fall of Johnson Products

## 概览

This Planet Money episode tells the story of Johnson Products, the Black-owned hair-care company behind Ultra Wave, Ultra Sheen, and Afro Sheen. The episode frames the company’s rise and decline through three hairstyles: the Conk, the Afro, and the Jerry Curl.

The central argument is that George and Joan Johnson built a major business by paying close attention to Black consumers when many mainstream companies ignored them. Their products, hiring practices, bank investments, and Soul Train sponsorship tied Black culture directly to Black business.

The episode also follows how going public brought wealth and prestige but reduced control and exposed Johnson Products’ profitable market to larger competitors. By the end, the story becomes more complicated: Johnson Products helped create a multibillion-dollar Black hair-care industry, yet much of that market is now owned by non-Black companies.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **Olivia Joan Golly’s TikTok Opens the Story**

[事实] Olivia Joan Golly posts TikToks trying on couture and vintage clothing that belonged to her grandmother, Joan Johnson.

[事实] The clothing includes custom Chanel, Givenchy, and Yves Saint Laurent, which highlights the wealth and status Joan Johnson had achieved.

[事实] Olivia Joan says she kept talking about the collection because it showed that wealthy Black entrepreneurs like her grandparents existed.

[推测] The TikTok archive functions as a personal doorway into a broader economic history that many listeners may not know.

[02:26] **George Johnson and Afro Sheen**

[事实] Joan Johnson’s husband, George E. Johnson, created Afro Sheen, described as one of the most iconic Black hair products of the 20th century.

[事实] The episode says Johnson Products connected Black culture and Black business, and that its money helped fund the civil rights movement and Soul Train.

[事实] The hosts introduce the episode as the rise and fall of Johnson Products, told through the Conk, the Afro, and the Jerry Curl.

[推测] The episode uses hair not only as a style history but as a way to track changing politics, consumer needs, and business strategy.

[05:39] **The Conk and 1950s Assimilation Pressure**

[事实] In the early 1950s, many Black workers were moving to northern cities during the second wave of the Great Migration.

[事实] Nat King Cole represented a polished Black style of the era, including shiny straight hair.

[事实] The episode says straightened hair was tied to respectability and better chances in the workforce.

[推测] The demand for straightened hair reflected both consumer preference and the pressure Black people faced to fit white-dominated professional norms.

[07:05] **George Johnson’s Early Work and Chemistry Training**

[事实] George Johnson moved to Chicago from Mississippi with his mother and brothers, and his family was extremely poor.

[事实] He began working as a child, collecting bottles, paper, rags, and cigarette-package foil for money.

[事实] He attended Wendell Phillips High School, where he met Joan, but he did not graduate because he needed money.

[事实] By the 1950s, he worked at a Black-owned cosmetics company and learned chemistry on the job.

[09:00] **Orville Nelson and the Straightener Problem**

[事实] George met barber Orville Nelson, who had developed a hair straightener but was frustrated that it did not work reliably.

[事实] Orville’s mixture included ingredients such as egg, potato, and sodium hydroxide, also called lye.

[事实] George observed Orville’s barbershop and saw that the product separated and needed to be emulsified.

[推测] George’s advantage was not inventing Black hair straightening from scratch, but making an existing practice safer, more consistent, and commercially scalable.

[11:32] **Ultra Wave and the Birth of Johnson Products**

[事实] George spent nine months experimenting with formulas before creating a thicker, more stable product.

[事实] In 1954, Orville and George went into business together with Ultra Wave Hair Culture.

[事实] Ultra Wave was not the first hair straightener, but it was shelf-stable and reliable.

[事实] After George and Orville’s relationship soured, Orville left and George and Joan took over what became Johnson Products Company.

[13:41] **Joan Johnson, Expansion, and Community Business**

[事实] Joan quit a good-paying government job to help with books, labeling, jars, trucks, and collections.

[事实] George described Joan as a tough businesswoman, including a story where she waited in a barbershop until a customer paid his bill.

[事实] Johnson Products expanded from Chicago into Indianapolis, Cleveland, Detroit, Memphis, St. Louis, and beyond.

[事实] The company built a headquarters, lab, and factory on Chicago’s South Side that became a symbol of Black entrepreneurship.

[14:45] **Black Employment and Independence Bank**

[事实] George hired mostly Black workers and paid them well across divisions, from janitors to executives.

[事实] George and other mostly Black businessmen took over a failed neighborhood bank and renamed it Independence Bank.

[事实] The bank was intended to help Black entrepreneurs and families get loans.

[推测] The episode presents Johnson Products as part of a wider Black economic ecosystem, not just a consumer goods company.

[15:34] **Civil Rights, Natural Hair, and Afro Sheen**

[事实] By the mid-1960s, young people were losing interest in straightening their hair.

[事实] The civil rights movement encouraged rejection of white beauty standards and acceptance of natural hair.

[事实] Johnson Products responded by creating Afro Sheen, a moisturizer for afros.

[推测] The company’s success depended on detecting cultural change early and turning that change into products.

[16:32] **Martin Luther King Jr. and Black Power**

[事实] In 1966, Martin Luther King Jr. visited Johnson Products’ research facility.

[事实] George said King’s organization could not make payroll and needed financial help.

[事实] Independence Bank loaned King’s organization more than $100,000.

[事实] George recalled King looking at the company’s headquarters and saying, “this is Black power.”

[17:44] **Soul Train as a Marketing Engine**

[事实] George saw Soul Train live in the studio and believed the show should be broadcast in color.

[事实] He funded a 30-minute color pilot and later wrote a check to help make Soul Train a national program.

[事实] Ads for Afro Sheen and Ultra Sheen appeared on every show as part of the sponsorship deal.

[事实] George attributed sales growth from about $11.2 million in 1971 to $39 million in 1975 to Soul Train.

[19:32] **Going Public and Becoming Wealthy**

[事实] In 1971, Johnson Products became the first Black-owned company listed on the American Stock Exchange.

[事实] George said the listing felt like the moment he had made it.

[事实] After going public, George had more personal money, bought property, a boat, and a house, while Joan shopped in Paris and designer stores.

[推测] The public listing gave the Johnsons prestige and capital, but it also marked the beginning of a less controlled phase of the business.

[22:27] **Board Pressure and Competitive Exposure**

[事实] After going public, George and Joan had to answer to a board.

[事实] George said the board pushed him to hire a “real marketing director” instead of relying on himself and his brother.

[事实] George said sales went flat under the new white marketing director.

[事实] Johnson Products’ public reports disclosed detailed profit information, which George later said helped larger white-owned companies understand the Black hair-care market.

[推测] Transparency that was meant to prove credibility also made the company’s market easier for competitors to enter.

[24:13] **Revlon and Bigger Competitors**

[事实] Revlon created its own hair straightener after larger companies recognized the profitability of Black hair care.

[事实] George said Revlon had a good product and wondered why it did not wipe Johnson Products out.

[事实] The episode says Revlon did not destroy Johnson Products by itself.

[推测] The threat from Revlon showed that Johnson Products had opened a market that it could no longer protect from companies with far greater resources.

[24:39] **The Jerry Curl and Falling Behind**

[事实] The Jerry Curl became hugely popular after Michael Jackson appeared with one during the Thriller era.

[事实] Johnson Products did not have a leading Jerry Curl product ready at the right time.

[事实] George acknowledged that the company was no longer at the cutting edge.

[事实] He said Johnson Products lost many customers and experienced its first losses.

[26:03] **Decline, Health Warnings, and Sale**

[事实] The episode names several factors in the company’s decline, including seeming dated, losing money, regulatory warning labels for relaxers, and later lawsuits over health risks.

[事实] George and Joan had marriage troubles, divorced, and eventually remarried.

[事实] Joan took charge of the company and turned things around.

[事实] In 1993, Joan sold Johnson Products to a white-owned pharmaceutical company for $70 million and personally made $32 million from the sale.

[27:27] **Legacy in the Modern Hair-Care Market**

[事实] The episode says the global Black hair-care market is worth about $4 billion today.

[事实] George said he was happy to see newer entrepreneurs in the business, especially women running companies.

[事实] Olivia Joan still uses products her family created, including Ultra Sheen.

[事实] Olivia Joan said it breaks her heart to see how many products aimed at Black consumers are founded or run by white people.

[29:27] **Johnson Products as a Symbol**

[事实] Sonari Glinton remembers the Johnson Products building near the Dan Ryan Expressway in Chicago.

[事实] He says the building symbolized Blackness, prosperity, Black power, and optimism about the future.

[事实] The episode ends by noting that most Black hair-care companies today have white owners.

[推测] The closing contrast suggests that Johnson Products’ legacy is both triumphant and unresolved.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s strongest value is how it connects style, business, race, and ownership without treating hair care as a minor consumer category. By organizing the story around three hairstyles, it makes shifts in Black politics and consumer demand easy to follow.

[推测] Its highlight is the use of personal archive and memory: Olivia Joan’s clothing, George Johnson’s interviews, Soul Train ads, and memories of the South Side building give the business history emotional weight.

[推测] A limitation is that the episode moves quickly through the company’s later decline, including product safety issues and lawsuits, so listeners looking for a deeper legal or health analysis may need more context.

[推测] This episode is especially suitable for listeners interested in Black entrepreneurship, consumer markets, cultural history, beauty industries, and how community-focused businesses change once they become public companies.
