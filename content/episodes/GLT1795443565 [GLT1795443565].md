# Valentine's Day: Saints, Chaucer, Cards, and Vinegar Valentines

## 概览

This episode traces Valentine’s Day from uncertain saintly origins to medieval English poetry, then to Georgian and Victorian card culture. The central question is whether the holiday is pagan, Christian, commercial, or something more tangled.

The hosts argue that the link between Valentine’s Day and the Roman Lupercalia is weak, while the connection between Valentine, birds, spring, and romantic choice seems to emerge strongly in late 14th-century English literary culture.

The second half shifts from romance to commerce and satire: printed cards, the Penny Post, anonymous correspondence, and the now-lost tradition of insulting “vinegar valentines.” The episode closes by noting other events that happened on February 14, including the St Valentine’s Day Massacre.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Victorian Valentines and the Core Question**

[事实] The episode opens with a Victorian post office clerk describing huge quantities of Valentine letters, parcels, and hampers.
[事实] The hosts note that many Victorian valentines were ornate and delicate, while a significant portion were coarse or offensive.
[事实] The main question is why Valentine’s Day is celebrated and whether it comes from pagan ritual, Christian tradition, or modern commercial invention.

[03:09] **Multiple Saint Valentines**

[事实] The Roman martyrology includes a St Valentine associated with Rome, the Via Flaminia, and the Milvian Bridge.
[事实] Another Valentine is described as Bishop of Terni, and the martyrology now treats him as possibly the same person.
[事实] A further Valentine, Bishop of Genoa, has a feast day on May 2, which later matters for debates about the romantic association.
[推测] The uncertainty around these figures makes Valentine’s Day harder to explain than major Christian feasts such as Christmas or Easter.

[05:08] **Claudius Gothicus and Martyr Legends**

[事实] Later traditions place Valentine’s martyrdom in the reign of Claudius Gothicus, who ruled from 268 to 270.
[事实] The hosts describe Claudius Gothicus as a tough military emperor who fought the Alemanni and Goths and died during plague.
[事实] Medieval legends say Valentine converted a judge, became connected to stories about a judge’s daughter, and was eventually beaten to death.
[事实] The hosts emphasize that the earliest form of the story contains nothing clearly erotic or romantic.

[08:23] **Lupercalia and the Weak Pagan Link**

[事实] The Lupercalia was a Roman mid-February festival involving animal sacrifice, fertility ritual, and public rites around the Palatine.
[事实] Pope Gelasius banned the Lupercalia near the end of the 5th century, around 496.
[事实] The hosts say the direct line from Lupercalia to modern Valentine’s Day is not obvious.
[推测] The holiday’s romantic meaning is therefore better explained through later medieval and social developments than through a simple pagan survival.

[13:13] **Chaucer, Birds, and English Origins**

[事实] The hosts say current thinking sees Valentine’s Day as probably English in its romantic form.
[事实] Chaucer’s *Parliament of Fowls* is presented as the first English poetic mention connecting St Valentine’s Day with birds choosing mates.
[事实] John Gower also describes St Valentine presiding over an assembly of birds choosing mates.
[推测] The association may have grown from ideas of spring, mating, courtly love, and late 14th-century English literary culture.

[17:49] **Courtly Love and Political Context**

[事实] One theory connects Chaucer’s poem to the marriage negotiations between Richard II and Anne of Bohemia in 1381.
[事实] The hosts note uncertainty over whether the relevant Valentine date was February 14 or May 2.
[事实] They place the Valentine tradition within the wider language of courtly love, chivalric romance, and the English court.

[19:14] **The First Surviving Valentines**

[事实] The first surviving Valentine poem is attributed to Charles, Duke of Orléans, who was captured at Agincourt and held in the Tower of London.
[事实] He addressed his wife as his Valentine, showing that the term had taken on romantic meaning by the 15th century.
[事实] John Lydgate and the Paston Letters also show people using Valentine language and choosing Valentines.
[事实] The feast day was abolished under Edward VI, but the social custom of Valentine’s Day continued.

[22:01] **Seventeenth-Century Valentine Customs**

[事实] By the 17th century, people could choose Valentines in several ways, including romantic selection, drawing by lot, or meeting someone first in the morning.
[事实] The future James II gave Lady Frances Stuart a jewel worth £800 as a Valentine.
[事实] A Dutch visitor in 1663 reported that the first suitable person met in the morning could become one’s Valentine, though this could be planned in advance.
[推测] Valentine’s Day at this stage functioned as both romance and social game rather than only private sentiment.

[24:31] **Eighteenth-Century Doubts and Folk Tradition**

[事实] Catholic writers such as Butler tried to explain Valentine’s Day by linking it to supposed pagan customs.
[事实] The hosts say this explanation required imagining a pagan festival that did not actually match the evidence.
[事实] By the mid-18th century, February 14 was widely recognized as Valentine’s Day in popular culture.
[事实] Georgian newspapers reported unusual Valentine’s Day marriages and anecdotes.

[28:13] **Relics in Dublin**

[事实] The hosts discuss Valentine relics kept at Our Lady of Mount Carmel on Whitefriar Street in Dublin.
[事实] The relics were sent after the Irish Carmelite preacher John Spratt impressed church authorities in Rome.
[事实] They arrived in Dublin on December 10, 1836.
[事实] Visitors can write in a book there about loneliness, unhappy love, or hopes for love.

[30:56] **Printed Cards and Georgian Beginnings**

[事实] In late Georgian Britain, people began using pre-printed Valentine cards, described as “mechanical” because they were machine-made.
[事实] One early surviving example is held at York Castle Museum and was sent by Catherine Moss Day to Mr Brown of London.
[事实] A book called *The Young Man’s Valentine Writer* appeared in 1797 to help men compose Valentine messages.
[事实] Early cards were often folded papers decorated with lace, thread, Cupid imagery, and verse.

[33:01] **The Penny Post and Anonymous Romance**

[事实] The Penny Post was introduced in 1840 through Rowland Hill’s postal reforms.
[事实] The sender paid a penny, making communication cheaper and removing the need for the recipient to pay.
[事实] The reform helped create stamps, letter boxes, pillar boxes, and more anonymous correspondence.
[事实] The hosts describe a boom in love letters and Valentine cards after the 1840s.

[35:30] **Victorian Card Industry**

[事实] Printed Valentine cards became popular because people did not always want to write poems or copy from manuals.
[事实] Jonathan King, a London stationer, became a major producer of Victorian Valentine cards.
[事实] The Museum of London holds around 1,700 examples of cards associated with King.
[事实] The hosts describe highly elaborate cards, including one involving human hair arranged as a moustache.

[38:09] **Britain, America, and Commercial Expansion**

[事实] The hosts say Britain led the world in Valentine cards during the 19th century.
[事实] American stationers imported British Valentine cards and listed categories such as comic, sentimental, lovesick, burlesque, romantic, matrimonial, and suicidal.
[事实] Esther Howland of Worcester, Massachusetts, built a major American Valentine card business by adapting British models.
[事实] The Hallmark Company, founded in Kansas City in 1910, later overtook this earlier American Valentine card industry.

[40:02] **Vinegar Valentines**

[事实] Vinegar valentines were commercially produced cards designed to insult people.
[事实] The hosts cite scholarship by Annabella Pollen on this tradition.
[事实] These cards mocked gossip, vulgarity, boasting, bad singing, drunkenness, and other perceived faults.
[事实] The custom appears to have faded around the end of the 19th century, though the reason is not clear.
[推测] The hosts treat vinegar valentines as an earlier form of anonymous social trolling.

[44:51] **The St Valentine’s Day Massacre**

[事实] The St Valentine’s Day Massacre took place on Thursday, February 14, 1929, in Lincoln Park, Chicago.
[事实] Seven men were shot during Prohibition-era conflict between the North Side Gang and Al Capone’s Chicago Outfit.
[事实] Some attackers disguised themselves as police officers and lined the victims up before machine-gunning them.
[事实] The massacre helped push federal action against Capone and damaged the reputation of Chicago mayor William W. Thompson.

[49:02] **Other February 14 Events and Closing**

[事实] The hosts mention Captain Cook’s death on February 14.
[事实] They also mention Gregory VII excommunicating Henry IV on February 14 and describe the split papal throne story as doubtful.
[事实] They note that Ayatollah Khomeini issued the fatwa against Salman Rushdie on February 14.
[事实] The episode ends by framing the podcast itself as a Valentine to listeners and closing with another Victorian-style insulting Valentine verse.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s value lies in showing that Valentine’s Day is not easily reduced to either pagan survival or modern marketing. Its history is presented as a layered development involving saints, medieval poetry, postal reform, commerce, and social satire.

[推测] A major highlight is the contrast between tender romantic customs and the much harsher world of vinegar valentines. This gives the episode energy and challenges the assumption that older Valentine traditions were simply innocent or sentimental.

[推测] The main limitation is that several origin questions remain unresolved, especially the exact route from St Valentine to romantic love. The hosts are clear about this uncertainty rather than forcing a single answer.

[推测] This episode suits listeners interested in cultural history, medieval literature, Victorian popular culture, and the strange ways ordinary customs become commercial, emotional, and comic over time.
