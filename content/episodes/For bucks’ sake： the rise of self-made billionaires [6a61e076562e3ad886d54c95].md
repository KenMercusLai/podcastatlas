# For bucks’ sake: the rise of self-made billionaires

## 概览

This episode of *The Intelligence* opens with a discussion of whether today’s billionaires are becoming more economically legitimate, despite rising political hostility toward extreme wealth. Economist writer Callum Williams argues that a growing share of billionaire wealth now comes from competitive sectors that provide goods, services, jobs or consumer value, rather than from inheritance, natural-resource rents or political favour.

The second story examines Cambodia’s crackdown on scam compounds. Su-Lin Wong explains that the industry remains vast, adaptive and politically protected, even after raids, sanctions and the extradition of a major alleged scam boss.

The final segment uses Larry the Cat, Downing Street’s long-serving chief mouser, as a comic guide to political survival: cultivate a strong backstory, maintain public perception, master media attention and tolerate no rivals.

## 分段落总结

[01:13] **Episode Opening**

[事实] Rosie Blore introduces the episode as covering self-made billionaires, scam compounds in Cambodia, and political lessons from Larry the Cat.

[事实] The first main segment begins with the observation that billionaires are widely disliked and often discussed by politicians as evidence of policy failure.

[01:46] **Anti-Billionaire Sentiment**

[事实] The episode cites political criticism of billionaires, including concerns about oligarchy, inequality and tax systems.

[事实] Callum Williams says there is a large surge in anti-billionaire sentiment.

[事实] Williams argues that more billionaires are deriving wealth from useful goods and services or from employing many people, rather than from exploiting the system.

[03:11] **How The Economist Classifies Billionaire Wealth**

[事实] Williams says the research is not about whether billionaires are good or bad, but whether the source of billionaire wealth is improving or worsening over time.

[事实] The “worst” category includes wealth from sectors such as mining, natural resources and casinos, where political favours may matter, as well as inherited wealth.

[事实] The “better” category includes billionaires who built companies, goods or services that people use and value.

[事实] The research finds that competitive billionaire wealth is becoming more important over time, while uncompetitive wealth is becoming less important.

[04:33] **Self-Made Wealth Is Not Just Tech**

[事实] Williams says tech has produced many billionaires, including Elon Musk, but the tech share of billionaire wealth is not at an all-time high.

[事实] He says the tech share is slightly lower now than it was in the early 2000s.

[事实] Billionaires have also emerged from entertainment, sports, finance, restaurants and clothing retail.

[事实] Examples mentioned include Taylor Swift, Lionel Messi, Panda Express and Uniqlo.

[05:48] **Why Self-Made Billionaires Have Risen**

[事实] Williams identifies China’s rapid growth and expanding consumer sector over the past 25 years as one factor.

[事实] He also points to strong market returns, especially in the United States, which boosted hedge funds, private equity and company valuations.

[事实] He says mobile-first internet helped businesses scale ideas very quickly from the mid-2010s onward.

[事实] Examples of fast-scaling firms include ByteDance, Spotify and Stripe.

[07:17] **Taxes, Competition And Gaming The System**

[事实] Williams says the most egregious forms of billionaire accumulation have become less powerful.

[事实] He says post-Soviet Russian billionaires are around 20% less wealthy than at their peak in the late 2000s.

[事实] He says evidence is unclear on whether the global economy has become meaningfully less competitive over the past 20 years.

[事实] Williams says that, in the United States, the top 400 to 500 people by wealth have an effective tax rate of 45% to 50% a year.

[09:19] **Implications For Wealth Taxes**

[事实] Williams says the political-influence argument for taxing wealth does not depend on how billionaires made their money.

[事实] He says the justice argument for taxing someone heavily is stronger when wealth was accumulated illegitimately.

[事实] He argues that taxing competitive entrepreneurs can have higher economic costs if they leave a country.

[推测] The segment’s central implication is that the case for wealth taxes may become more complicated as more billionaire wealth comes from competitive activity.

[10:28] **Elon Musk Interview Promotion**

[事实] The host says The Economist’s editor-in-chief Zanny Minton Beddoes interviewed Elon Musk for *The Insider*.

[事实] Subscribers are told they can watch the interview or hear an audio version in the podcast feed.

[12:16] **Cambodia’s Scam Compound Crackdown**

[事实] The episode says Cambodia faces growing international pressure to crack down on industrial-scale scamming.

[事实] The scams are often run by organised criminal gangs from China.

[事实] Police and military raids have increased, but Su-Lin Wong says selective enforcement has not dismantled the system protecting scam compounds.

[事实] Operations are shifting to Laos, Myanmar, Sri Lanka and Indonesia.

[13:13] **Scam Inc As An Industry**

[事实] Wong says scam messages on WhatsApp and similar platforms often come from a sophisticated organised industry, not isolated individuals.

[事实] She says the industry is estimated to make more than $500bn a year globally.

[事实] The victims are targeted worldwide, including in Europe, the United States and elsewhere.

[事实] Wong says the industry is roughly the same size as the global illicit drug trade.

[14:16] **Why Cambodia Became A Hub**

[事实] Online scams in Cambodia alone are estimated to generate up to $19bn a year.

[事实] Wong says that would equal about 40% of Cambodia’s formal GDP and exceed the country’s garment manufacturing industry.

[事实] Scamming began largely in China, where Chinese criminals targeted Chinese citizens, before a crackdown pushed operations across Southeast Asia.

[事实] Scam operations are often run from casinos, which can also help launder proceeds.

[15:13] **Raids And Humanitarian Fallout**

[事实] Cambodia’s information minister says the government has closed more than 90 casinos over the past year.

[事实] Wong says tens of thousands of lower-level scammers have been released from prison-like compounds.

[事实] Many released workers now have nowhere to go and no money to buy tickets home.

[推测] The crackdown is creating a humanitarian crisis even as it disrupts some criminal sites.

[16:00] **Chen Zhi And International Sanctions**

[事实] Chen Zhi is described as believed to be the boss of one of Asia’s most powerful criminal organisations.

[事实] His organisation was headquartered in Cambodia, and he was an adviser to Hun Sen.

[事实] The United States and Britain sanctioned Chen, but he was extradited to China.

[事实] The extradition frightened other scam bosses because it suggested even Hun Sen could not protect his own adviser.

[17:00] **Financial Pressure On Scam Networks**

[事实] Hong Kong, Singapore, South Korea, Taiwan and Thailand also imposed sanctions on Chen Zhi and his associates.

[事实] The U.S. government seized around $15bn in cryptocurrency from Chen Zhi, described as the largest seizure in U.S. history.

[事实] The United States also sanctioned Cambodian senator Khok An.

[事实] In June, the United States blocked subsidiaries of Hui Wan Group, described as the world’s largest illicit marketplace, from America’s financial system.

[18:05] **Damage To Cambodia’s Tourism**

[事实] Wong says Chinese tourism to Cambodia has fallen sharply because of fears of being trafficked into scam camps.

[事实] Cambodia received 2.4m Chinese tourists in 2019 and 850,000 in 2024.

[事实] Cambodia is offering visa-free entry to Chinese tourists to revive tourism.

[18:39] **Why The Problem Persists**

[事实] Amnesty International counted 86 scam compounds in Cambodia this year, up from 53 a year earlier.

[事实] Wong says the industry is adapting rather than dying.

[事实] She compares the pattern to earlier selective crackdowns on sand mining, illegal logging and land grabbing.

[事实] Wong says no ministers protecting or enabling Scam Inc have yet been sanctioned, detained or arrested by their own government.

[20:14] **Larry The Cat At Downing Street**

[事实] The final segment follows Chris Impey trying to see Larry the Cat outside Number 10 Downing Street.

[事实] Larry is the chief mouser to the Cabinet Office and has held the post for 15 years.

[事实] Larry has been present through the premierships and resignations of several British prime ministers.

[22:56] **Lesson One: Have A Good Backstory**

[事实] The segment says Larry came from Battersea Cats and Dogs Home before arriving at Downing Street.

[事实] His arrival attracted press attention, though he was initially hidden under a blanket.

[推测] The segment uses Larry’s origin story as a humorous analogy for political image-building.

[23:52] **Lesson Two: Do The Job Or Manage Perception**

[事实] The page also says he tests antique furniture for napping quality.

[24:27] **Lesson Three: Steal The Spotlight**

[事实] Larry has more than 900,000 followers on his unofficial X account.

[事实] He receives gifts from fans and is described as media savvy.

[事实] He has photobombed world leaders and drew attention in 2019 by sheltering under Donald Trump’s armoured limousine.

## 播客点评/总结

[事实] The episode’s strongest segment is the first, because it gives a clear framework for separating inherited or politically connected wealth from competitive, self-made wealth. It does not claim billionaires are good, but it challenges a simple anti-billionaire narrative.

[事实] The Cambodia segment adds investigative weight by connecting scam compounds to tourism, sanctions, extradition, cryptocurrency seizures and political protection. Its main limitation is that it describes the system’s resilience more than it offers solutions.

[推测] The Larry the Cat segment works as a lighter closing piece after two heavy economic and criminal-justice stories. It is more comic essay than policy analysis, so listeners looking only for hard news may find it less substantive.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in political economy, organised cybercrime and The Economist’s mix of analysis with dry humour.
