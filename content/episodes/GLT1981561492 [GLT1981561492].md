# Pope Joan: A Medieval Legend and the Anxiety of Female Authority

## 概览

This episode examines the medieval legend of Pope Joan, a woman said to have disguised herself as a man, risen through the papal court, become pope, and been exposed when she gave birth in public.

The hosts argue that Joan almost certainly did not exist, but that the story mattered because it was accepted for centuries and later became an embarrassment to the Catholic Church during the Reformation.

The discussion moves from failed historical explanations to a broader account of Gregorian reform, papal ritual, clerical masculinity, women’s exclusion from priesthood, and the radical case of Guglielma and Maifreda.

## 分段落总结

[01:41] **The Legend Is Introduced**

[事实] Jean de Mailly, writing in the early 13th century, described a “popess” who disguised herself as a man and became secretary, cardinal, and finally pope.
[事实] In that version, she gave birth in public and was punished by being dragged behind a horse and stoned.
[推测] The episode uses this shocking story to ask both whether it happened and why medieval Christians found it meaningful.

[03:35] **Martin of Poland’s Canonical Version**

[事实] Martin of Poland placed Joan in the early 9th century and described her as an English woman born in Mainz who followed her lover by disguising herself as a man.
[事实] She studied, went to Rome, entered the papal court, became a cardinal, and was elected pope under the name John.
[事实] Martin said she gave birth during a procession from St Peter’s to the Lateran, after which popes avoided that route.
[推测] The added names, places, and rituals helped the story sound historically grounded.

[06:56] **A Story Believed for Centuries**

[事实] The hosts say the legend was widely believed for two to three centuries, up to the 16th century.
[事实] Pope Joan appeared in accepted papal lists, in Roman tourist traditions, and in Siena Cathedral as “John VIII, a woman from England.”
[事实] In 1601 Clement VIII definitively declared the legend untrue.
[推测] Protestant use of the story during the Reformation made it much more embarrassing for the Roman Church.

[09:23] **Why Historians Reject It**

[事实] David Blondel’s 1647 dissertation argued against the historical reality of Pope Joan by examining the sources.
[事实] The first references appear long after the supposed events, and contemporary sources from 1099 or the 9th century do not mention her.
[事实] The real John VIII ruled for ten years and died by violence from rivals, not in childbirth.
[推测] The legend collapses because it cannot be fitted into the known papal chronology.

[13:30] **The Pornocracy Explanation**

[事实] Later historians used the term “pornocracy” for a period when the papacy was dominated by Roman factions and powerful women.
[事实] Marozia was described as a major Roman power broker whose son became Pope John XI and was said to be the son of Pope Sergius III.
[事实] Another theory linked the legend to John XII, a notorious teenage pope with many scandals and a mistress supposedly named Joan.
[推测] These scandals may echo the legend’s themes, but the hosts argue they do not explain how the Pope Joan story developed.

[17:50] **Gregorian Reform as Context**

[事实] Tom argues that the story emerged from the world of the Gregorian Revolution rather than from a real hidden female pope.
[事实] The Gregorian reformers wanted to free the Church from the control of kings, emperors, and earthly rulers.
[事实] By the late 11th century, cardinals increasingly became the people who elected popes.
[推测] A legend about false papal identity made sense in a period obsessed with papal legitimacy, purity, and authority.

[20:47] **The Chairs With Holes**

[事实] Alain Boreau’s interpretation focuses on two ancient-looking chairs used in papal ritual at the Lateran.
[事实] Both chairs had holes in the seats, and later writers connected them to a supposed test of the pope’s sex.
[事实] By 1290, Geoffrey de Coulomb wrote that Romans verified the elected pope’s sex through an opening in the stone throne.
[推测] The hosts treat this sex-verification ritual as fictional, but useful for explaining how the Pope Joan legend attached itself to papal ceremony.

[25:24] **The Anxiety About Papal Masculinity**

[事实] The hosts ask why it mattered that a celibate pope be visibly male.
[事实] Tom says the Pope Joan story reflects an obsessional concern with the masculinity of the pope.
[推测] The legend turns the symbolic tension of male celibate authority into a bodily scandal.

[28:01] **Why Women Cannot Be Priests in Catholic Doctrine**

[事实] Tom explains that Catholic teaching rests on Christ having summoned men, not women, as apostles.
[事实] Priests act in persona Christi, in the person of Christ, and Catholic doctrine holds that a woman cannot personate Christ in that role.
[事实] The hosts connect this doctrine to the exclusion of women from both priesthood and papacy.
[推测] The episode presents doctrinal reasoning and cultural misogyny as connected but not identical.

[29:42] **Aristotle, Misogyny, and the Bible**

[事实] Aristotle’s idea that women were defective or inadequate men entered Latin Christian scholarship during the same period that Pope Joan stories crystallized.
[事实] Tom says medieval clerical writing often expressed fear and contempt toward female bodies.
[事实] Thomas Aquinas nevertheless had to acknowledge that Genesis presents women as well as men as images of the divine.
[推测] Christian scripture complicated, but did not eliminate, medieval intellectual misogyny.

[32:00] **Female Language for Christ and the Church**

[事实] Tom cites Christ’s maternal imagery, Anselm calling Christ a mother, and Bernard of Clairvaux comparing himself to a nursing mother.
[事实] The Church itself was imagined as mother and as the bride of Christ.
[事实] The same culture elevated the Virgin Mary and figures such as Catherine of Siena while also casting women as temptresses.
[推测] This paradox made female holiness powerful, but female priestly authority threatening.

[35:17] **Guglielma and Maifreda**

[事实] Guglielma was said to have come to Milan, lived in poverty and charity, and died around 1280.
[事实] Her tomb at Chiaravalle became a pilgrimage site associated with miracles and popular sanctity.
[事实] In 1300 inquisitors destroyed her tomb, burned her body, scattered her ashes, and arrested Abbess Maifreda.
[事实] Maifreda was later burned at the stake.
[推测] The severity of the response shows how threatening this female spiritual movement appeared.

[39:03] **A Female Age of the Spirit**

[事实] Surviving fragments say Guglielma claimed to be the Holy Spirit made flesh for the redemption of women.
[事实] The movement drew on Joachim of Fiore’s teaching that history moved through the ages of the Father, Son, and Spirit.
[事实] Maifreda and her followers believed Guglielma had risen from the dead and that Maifreda would become pope.
[事实] They expected a feminine age in which women would become pope and cardinals.
[推测] This real episode helps explain the religious anxieties behind the fictional Pope Joan.

[42:24] **Pope Joan as Cautionary Tale**

[事实] The hosts describe Pope Joan as a cautionary tale for priests, cardinals, and men.
[事实] Tom argues it also plays with the feminine aspects of the priestly role, especially celibacy and Christ’s maternal imagery.
[推测] The story briefly opens the possibility of female sacred authority, then closes it through exposure, scandal, and punishment.

[44:13] **Modern Prospects**

[事实] Tom thinks Jean de Mailly probably believed the story, but understood events as carrying moral and theological meaning.
[事实] Tom says he does not expect female Catholic priests or a female pope in their lifetimes or their children’s lifetimes.
[事实] He argues that the doctrine is so fundamental that reversing it would do immense damage to Catholic doctrinal inheritance.
[推测] The episode ends by treating Pope Joan less as history than as a window into medieval gender, power, and belief.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s main value is that it does not stop at debunking Pope Joan. It uses the legend to explain how medieval people connected stories, rituals, doctrine, gender, and institutional power.

Its strongest sections are the links between the perforated papal chairs, Gregorian reform, and the Guglielma-Maifreda case. These give the legend a richer context than a simple “true or false” treatment.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners interested in medieval Christianity, papal history, gender history, and the way legends reveal institutional anxieties. Its limitation is that some complex theological points are compressed and depend heavily on Tom’s interpretation.
