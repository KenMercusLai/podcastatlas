# Keep or Cancel? Trafalgar Square’s Statues

## 概览

This episode is The Rest Is History’s first on-location recording, staged around Trafalgar Square. The hosts use the square’s statues to ask whether monuments should be kept, removed, reinterpreted, or replaced.

The discussion moves from royal statues of Charles I and James II to Nelson’s Column, Victorian generals, the fourth plinth, George Washington, a broader anti-statue argument, and Edith Cavell. A recurring conclusion is that statues are not neutral: they express power, memory, victory, mourning, ideology, and later reinterpretation.

The hosts mostly resist blanket removal, but they also acknowledge serious counterarguments, especially when monuments honor imperial figures whose subjects or descendants are now part of Britain. Their preferred approach is case-by-case, with attention to historical context, public meaning, and what would replace the monument.

## 分段落总结

[00:30] **First On-Location Recording**

[事实] The hosts open in Trafalgar Square, describing it as their first on-location recording for The Rest Is History.

[事实] They frame the episode around statues and the question “keep or cancel.”

[事实] They begin near Charles I’s statue and say Trafalgar Square also contains Nelson and several generals.

[01:09] **Charles I And Civil War Memory**

[事实] The hosts describe the Charles I statue as London’s oldest bronze statue and connect the site to the former Eleanor Cross.

[事实] They say the statue was created during Charles I’s lifetime, hidden after Parliament ordered it destroyed, and restored after the monarchy returned.

[事实] They note that Charles I’s statue faces toward Whitehall and, symbolically, toward Parliament and Oliver Cromwell.

[推测] The hosts treat the opposition between Charles I and Cromwell as a useful physical expression of Britain’s unresolved Civil War memory.

[04:28] **James II And The Roman Model**

[事实] The hosts move to the statue of James II, made by Grinling Gibbons and put up in 1686, before James was removed in the Glorious Revolution.

[事实] They emphasize that James II is shown in Roman dress, linking early modern British statues to Renaissance and Roman traditions.

[事实] Tom argues that standalone statues of rulers were not a medieval norm in England, partly because Christianity carried suspicion toward graven images.

[推测] The James II statue is presented as evidence that public monuments are historically contingent, not a timeless or inevitable feature of city life.

[07:46] **Cancellation And Public Indifference**

[事实] The hosts say James II himself was “cancelled” by being driven from the country, and that his statue was also taken down for a time.

[事实] They observe that most passersby ignore the statue, treating it more like decorative urban furniture than political instruction.

[事实] They connect this to Ken Livingstone’s earlier criticism of obscure statues in Trafalgar Square.

[推测] The discussion suggests that a statue’s intended message can fade as public knowledge of the figure disappears.

[09:28] **Nelson’s Column And Victory**

[事实] The hosts describe Nelson’s Column as a Roman-style triumphal monument and say it was erected in the 1840s after public subscription.

[事实] They say Nelson’s victory at Trafalgar prevented French invasion and confirmed Royal Navy supremacy for more than a century.

[事实] They discuss the controversy over Nelson and slavery, including a disputed letter that may have been forged after his death.

[事实] Both hosts favor keeping Nelson’s Column.

[15:10] **George IV And The Plinths**

[事实] The hosts identify George IV as one of the figures on Trafalgar Square’s plinths and describe him as widely regarded as one of Britain’s worst kings.

[事实] They discuss his excesses, including a heavy breakfast reported by Wellington and his obesity late in life.

[事实] They joke that Trafalgar Square contains several poor monarchs: Charles I, James II, and George IV.

[推测] This segment weakens the idea that statues always honor morally exemplary people.

[17:10] **Charles James Napier And Empire**

[事实] The hosts discuss Charles James Napier, who conquered Sindh in the 1840s and was also a Peninsular War veteran.

[事实] They say the famous “Peccavi” story was a Punch joke rather than a real message from Napier.

[事实] Napier is quoted as recognizing the conquest of Sindh as a morally dubious, money-driven act.

[推测] Napier becomes an example of an imperial figure whose own words complicate simple celebration or condemnation.

[21:33] **Henry Havelock And Victorian Memory**

[事实] The hosts move to Major General Sir Henry Havelock, associated with the 1857 campaign in India.

[事实] They describe Havelock as a Baptist, morally serious, and someone who made soldiers attend Bible classes.

[事实] The statue was erected by public subscription and includes a patriotic inscription about soldiers’ suffering and valor.

[推测] The hosts treat the statue as a surviving piece of Victorian public memory, even if its values are now contested.

[23:48] **Jenner, Relevance, And Replacement**

[事实] Tom notes that Trafalgar Square has removed statues before, including Edward Jenner.

[事实] He says he would favor bringing Jenner back, especially after the recent experience of vaccination.

[事实] Tom argues that if British people of Indian descent find Havelock’s statue upsetting, that matters in deciding whether to keep it.

[事实] Dominic prefers putting Jenner on the fourth plinth rather than removing Havelock.

[25:50] **The Fourth Plinth And Admirals**

[事实] Tom suggests an equestrian statue of Elizabeth II for the fourth plinth, arguing that it would fit the square’s traditions.

[事实] The hosts briefly discuss busts of naval figures including Jellicoe, Beatty, and Cunningham.

[事实] They say Cunningham’s bust contains an empty Guinness bottle used by the sculptor.

[推测] The fourth plinth is treated as a test case for whether modern public art can match older civic monuments.

[27:31] **George Washington In London**

[事实] The hosts discuss George Washington’s statue, given by Virginia in 1921 as a post-World War I symbol of Anglo-American friendship.

[事实] They mention Washington as a slave owner and tax resister, while also noting he was born a British subject.

[事实] They discuss the fasces beside him as a Roman symbol later used by Mussolini.

[事实] They decide not to remove Washington, partly because keeping him seems magnanimous.

[30:00] **The Argument Against All Statues**

[事实] The hosts discuss a Guardian essay arguing that all statues should be removed for consistency.

[事实] Tom interprets this as a modern version of Protestant suspicion toward graven images.

[事实] They note that even admired figures can be criticized because people in the past often held views now considered unacceptable.

[推测] The episode presents total iconoclasm as intellectually consistent but politically and culturally unlikely.

[32:36] **Edith Cavell And Conflicting Meanings**

[事实] The hosts end this part at the statue of Edith Cavell, the nurse shot by the Germans on October 12, 1915.

[事实] They say Cavell helped Allied servicemen escape from behind enemy lines and became the subject of a major international scandal.

[事实] The statue includes both “patriotism is not enough” and “for king and country,” which the hosts see as partly conflicting messages.

[事实] They agree not to remove Cavell’s statue.

[推测] Cavell’s memorial offers the episode’s clearest example of a statue carrying multiple meanings at once: patriotism, sacrifice, martyrdom, and moral restraint.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its refusal to treat the statue debate as a simple fight between preservation and removal. By walking through actual monuments, the hosts show how different each case becomes once origin, funding, symbolism, and later public meaning are considered.

[推测] Its strongest moments come when the hosts connect present controversies to older patterns: Roman triumphalism, Christian iconoclasm, Protestant suspicion of images, imperial memory, and civic commemoration.

[推测] The limitation is that the conversation is informal and sometimes jokey, so some historical claims are presented as recollections rather than fully documented arguments. It works best for listeners who want a lively historical framing of monument controversies rather than a policy blueprint.
