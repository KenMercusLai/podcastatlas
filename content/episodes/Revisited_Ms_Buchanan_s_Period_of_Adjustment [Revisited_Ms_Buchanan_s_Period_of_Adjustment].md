# The Forgotten Cost of *Brown v. Board of Education*

## Overview

This episode revisits *Brown v. Board of Education*, the landmark 1954 Supreme Court decision that declared segregated public schools unconstitutional. It contrasts the Court’s reasoning with the position of the Brown family: Leola and Oliver Brown valued their daughter’s Black school and teachers but opposed a system that denied them the freedom to choose a nearby school.

Malcolm Gladwell argues that the Court framed segregation primarily as psychological harm to Black children instead of confronting the unequal distribution of power and opportunity. That framing overlooked the authority teachers exercise as gatekeepers and the importance of Black teachers to Black students.

The episode follows the consequences of this omission. Integration frequently moved Black children into white schools while closing Black schools and dismissing their educators. Tens of thousands of Black teachers lost their jobs, leaving a professional and educational deficit that, according to the episode, had still not been repaired.

## Segment Summary

[01:53] **Why This Episode Is Being Revisited**

[Fact] Gladwell introduces the episode as one of his favorite installments of *Revisionist History*, originally produced as part of a series about civil rights.

[Fact] After its original release, Barack Obama called Gladwell to say that he loved the episode.

[Fact] That conversation eventually led to a project with Obama about Reconstruction and its importance to understanding race and civil rights in America.

[04:07] **Ms. Buchanan’s “Period of Adjustment”**

[Fact] At a 2004 University of Michigan event, a speaker identified initially as Mrs. Thompson reads a termination letter sent decades earlier to teacher Darla Buchanan.

[Fact] The letter says Buchanan’s services will no longer be needed and describes the disruption as a “period of adjustment.”

[Fact] Gladwell presents that phrase as a condescending euphemism for the human costs of school integration.

[07:01] **The Topeka Challenge to Segregation**

[Fact] Topeka operated separate elementary-school systems for white and Black children during the Jim Crow era.

[Fact] The NAACP recruited 13 Black families to attempt to enroll their children in neighborhood white schools, creating a test case against segregation.

[Fact] Oliver and Leola Brown’s daughter Linda had to travel seven blocks, cross a busy road, and take a bus to Monroe School, while the white Sumner School was only four blocks from her home.

[Fact] After Sumner refused Linda’s enrollment, the local NAACP sued; the resulting case reached the Supreme Court, which unanimously ruled in 1954 that segregated public education was unconstitutional.

[10:19] **What the Brown Family Actually Wanted**

[Fact] Leola Brown said that Monroe School provided a quality education and employed qualified teachers.

[Fact] She explained that the lawsuit was prompted by the distance Linda had to travel and the principle that the school board should not dictate the family’s choice solely on racial grounds.

[Fact] The Browns’ complaint was therefore not that Black-run schools were inherently poor, but that a discriminatory system denied Black families control over their lives.

[12:15] **The Court’s Psychological Framing**

[Fact] The Supreme Court reasoned that separating Black children from white children caused psychological and educational harm and made segregated schooling inherently unequal.

[Fact] Gladwell contrasts that reasoning with Leola Brown’s account: she praised Monroe but demanded equal freedom and access.

[Fact] Drawing on historians Daryl Scott and Charles Payne, the episode describes a long tradition of portraying racial inequality as a personal or psychological problem rather than a product of political and economic structures.

[Inference] By emphasizing alleged damage within Black children, the decision could condemn segregation without fully naming the institutions and power relations that maintained racial inequality.

[16:15] **Teacher Race and Access to Gifted Education**

[Fact] Political scientists Jason Grissom and Christopher Redding found that white elementary students were more than twice as likely as similarly high-achieving Black students to receive gifted services.

[Fact] Differences in achievement, age, family income, and reported health did not eliminate the disparity.

[Fact] For high-achieving Black students, identification as gifted was about twice as likely under a Black teacher as under a white teacher; the teacher’s race produced no comparable effect for white students.

[Fact] The episode cites additional research associating Black teachers with higher test scores, different disciplinary outcomes, and a lower likelihood that Black male students will be suspended.

[20:11] **Teachers as Gatekeepers**

[Fact] A study of 100,000 Black students in North Carolina found that having one Black teacher between third and fifth grade reduced a Black boy’s later probability of dropping out of high school by 39 percent.

[Fact] Gladwell says this pattern should not be reduced to conscious racism by white teachers; teachers exercise power by recommending students, allocating attention, administering discipline, and deciding who receives opportunities.

[Fact] Leola Brown remembered Monroe’s teachers as an extended family who took a personal interest in their students.

[Inference] The research and testimony together suggest that educational equality depends not only on where children attend school, but also on who possesses authority and recognizes their potential.

[21:53] **Why Teachers Should Have Been Integrated First**

[Fact] Black educator Celestine Porter argued that integration should have begun with teachers and administrators rather than placing the entire burden on children.

[Fact] She said Black children entering white schools should have encountered Black teachers there, just as white children entering Black schools should have encountered white teachers.

[Fact] Porter recalled that Black teachers had nurtured and understood their students, whereas teachers in the receiving white schools often did not know them and were afraid of them.

[Fact] Gladwell notes that the main text of the Brown decision uses the word “teacher” only once and otherwise mentions teachers only a few times in footnotes.

[24:13] **The Moberly Firings**

[Fact] After the Brown decision, Moberly, Missouri, closed its Black Lincoln School, transferred its Black students to white schools, and dismissed all 11 Black teachers from Lincoln.

[Fact] The teachers sued but lost at trial and on appeal; in 1959, the Supreme Court declined to hear their case.

[Fact] School officials maintained that the teachers had to be compared with white educators and invoked subjective qualities such as personality, character, disposition, and adaptability.

[Fact] Even Mary Ella Timoney, whom the superintendent acknowledged was highly qualified, was rejected because she supposedly appeared superior and resentful of authority.

[Inference] The reliance on subjective “intangible” criteria allowed racial discrimination to continue under the appearance of neutral professional judgment.

[28:24] **A Profession Purged by Integration**

[Fact] The episode says that approximately 82,000 African American teachers worked in the South before *Brown* and that about half were dismissed within a decade as integration proceeded.

[Fact] Education scholar Michelle Foster documented both the mass firings and the humiliation endured by Black teachers who retained their positions.

[Fact] One retained teacher observed that none of the 15 Black teachers attending a meeting was as dark-skinned as she was, while remaining Black staff were barred from the teachers’ restroom and required to use the children’s facilities.

[Fact] The episode says that Black teachers remain substantially underrepresented relative to the share of Black students in the United States.

[30:39] **White Resistance in Topeka**

[Fact] Topeka assigned one Black teacher to a half-time position at the formerly all-white Randolph School, then asked white parents whether they objected.

[Fact] Parents offered explicitly racial and sometimes bizarre reasons for refusing to place their children with the teacher.

[Fact] The school superintendent’s letter to Darla Buchanan openly stated that the board assumed most people in Topeka would not want Black teachers teaching white children.

[Inference] The episode presents parental prejudice and institutional accommodation of it—not any deficiency among Black educators—as the practical mechanism behind their exclusion.

[32:38] **Linda Brown Reveals Who Paid the Price**

[Fact] Mrs. Thompson is revealed to be Linda Brown Thompson, the child at the center of *Brown v. Board of Education*.

[Fact] Speaking at an event marking the decision’s 50th anniversary, she interrupted her own eyewitness account to read Darla Buchanan’s termination letter.

[Fact] Gladwell concludes that Black people, particularly Black teachers and students, bore the cost of integration rather than white communities.

[Fact] The superintendent’s assurance that everything would ultimately be “best for everyone concerned” closes the narrative by returning to the euphemistic language of a “period of adjustment.”

## Podcast Commentary/Summary

The episode’s central strength is its shift in focus from the familiar image of Black children entering white schools to the largely forgotten removal of Black educators. Personal testimony, court history, and education research combine to show that desegregating enrollment did not automatically redistribute institutional power.

Its sharpest insight is the distinction between access and presumed deficiency. The Browns sought freedom from discriminatory restrictions, yet the Court justified that freedom partly by describing segregated Black education as inherently damaging. The episode argues persuasively that this reasoning obscured the value of Black schools and teachers.

[Inference] The account is deliberately revisionist and concentrates on the harms and omissions surrounding implementation rather than offering a comprehensive assessment of every legal achievement or local outcome associated with *Brown*. Its critique should therefore be understood as a correction to the standard triumphal narrative, not as a claim that ending legally enforced segregation was itself a mistake.

This episode is especially valuable for listeners interested in civil rights history, education policy, institutional racism, and the difference between formal equality and actual control over schools.
