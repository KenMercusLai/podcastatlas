# Mary Beard on Classics, Rome, and the Afterlife of the Caesars

## 概览

This episode discusses how Greece and Rome, especially Rome, have remained embedded in Western imagination through literature, art, politics, Christianity, education, and popular culture. The conversation begins with Virgil, Dante, and Mary Beard’s book *The Twelve Caesars*, using them to ask why figures known as tyrants and monsters kept reappearing in elite homes, churches, public spaces, and political imagery.

A central conclusion is that the classical past has never had one fixed meaning. Roman emperors could symbolize power, corruption, Christian history, imperial ambition, anti-imperial critique, elite education, revolution, and modern popular fascination at the same time.

The later discussion turns to the future of classics: its elitist associations, its use by empire and far-right politics, and its value as a way to understand art, literature, theatre, film, and political thought. Mary Beard argues that classics should not be defended by denial, but by confronting its complexity, expanding its boundaries, and keeping serious scholarship alive.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Virgil, Dante, and the Long Memory of Antiquity**

[事实] The episode opens with Virgil’s death in 19 BC and then jumps to Dante’s *Divine Comedy*, where Virgil appears as Dante’s guide through the underworld.

[事实] Tom Holland frames this as an example of the continuing imprint of Greece and Rome on European and world literature.

[事实] Mary Beard is introduced as the guest, and the hosts connect the discussion to her book *The Twelve Caesars*, which is about how the Caesars have been understood rather than simply about the rulers themselves.

[推测] The opening uses Virgil as a bridge between ancient literature and later cultural memory, setting up the episode’s broader theme of reception rather than ancient history alone.

[02:41] **Why the Twelve Caesars Still Matter Visually**

[事实] Mary Beard says that stately homes often display rows of Twelve Caesars, yet even classicists may walk past them without asking why they are there.

[事实] She asks why images of tyrants, corrupt autocrats, and rulers believed to have died unnatural deaths became decorations in private and public spaces.

[事实] She notes that even people who claim to know nothing about the ancient world still recognize images such as Nero fiddling while Rome burns.

[推测] Beard’s argument treats classical imagery less as inherited prestige and more as a problem: why do societies keep surrounding themselves with morally troubling ancient figures?

[05:06] **Renaissance Humanism and the Distance of the Ancient World**

[事实] The speakers discuss when Europeans began to see Greece and Rome as a vanished age rather than part of an unbroken continuum.

[事实] Beard says the standard answer would point to Renaissance humanism, which made antiquity a subject of study and marked it as different from the present.

[事实] She emphasizes that the rediscovery of antiquity was connected not only to intellectual work but also to elite culture and dynastic power.

[推测] The episode presents the Renaissance as both a recovery of antiquity and a moment when antiquity became historically separate and available for reinterpretation.

[09:08] **Christianity Inside the Roman Empire**

[事实] Beard argues that the history of Christianity is embedded in the Roman Empire.

[事实] She describes images of Augustus seeing a vision of Mary and Jesus on the day of Christ’s birth, linking Roman imperial history to Christian sacred history.

[事实] Nero appears on the ceremonial doors of St Peter’s condemning Peter and Paul to death.

[事实] Beard says early Christian history is difficult to understand without its embeddedness in the Roman Empire.

[推测] The conversation suggests that Roman emperors were not merely pagan enemies in Christian memory; they became structural figures through which Christianity narrated its own origins.

[13:01] **Dante, Nero, and Ambivalent Christian Uses of Rome**

[事实] The hosts note that Dante places Brutus and Cassius among the worst sinners because they murdered Julius Caesar.

[事实] Beard says the relation between Roman power and Christianity is always more complicated than a simple opposition between pagan persecutors and Christian victims.

[事实] She gives the example of a Transylvanian communion chalice set with Roman coins, including Nero, so that worshippers encountered imperial images while taking communion.

[推测] Roman imperial imagery functions in the discussion as both sacred frame and moral threat, making its Christian afterlife deliberately unstable.

[15:39] **Rome as Power, Prestige, and Corruption**

[事实] Beard says that when thinking about the impact of the ancient world from the Middle Ages onward, Rome matters more than Greece.

[事实] She describes Rome as associated with power, largeness, corruption, murder, failed dynasties, persecution, and a very male world.

[事实] Greek myth often reached later Europe through Roman mediation, especially through Ovid.

[事实] Beard argues that images of Roman power lasted because they were not simple rubber stamps of modern authority but remained difficult to pin down.

[推测] The episode treats Rome’s longevity as a cultural symbol as dependent on its ambiguity: it attracts because it is prestigious and disturbing at once.

[18:20] **Roman Mediation of Greek Myth and the Renaissance’s Direction**

[事实] Dominic asks whether names like Hercules rather than Heracles reflect the Roman filtering of Greek myths.

[事实] Beard agrees that later European culture mostly received these myths through Roman versions until self-conscious Hellenism encouraged renewed attention to Greek forms.

[事实] A listener asks whether the Renaissance was bound to be Greco-Roman rather than Egyptian or Persian.

[事实] Beard says it was almost bound to be Roman because Roman material remains, such as coins and busts, lay under European feet.

[推测] The Renaissance appears here not as an abstract choice of cultural ancestors but as a response to the physical and textual remains most available to Europeans.

[20:26] **Egypt, Other Antiquities, and the Limits of Greco-Roman Dominance**

[事实] Beard says the assumption that only Greeks and Romans mattered intellectually is false.

[事实] She notes that Greeks and Romans themselves knew they had learned from Egypt.

[事实] She gives Julius Caesar’s calendar reform as an example, saying he relied on Egyptian experts.

[事实] The hosts note that Renaissance Europeans were fascinated by Egypt but could not read Egyptian texts.

[推测] Beard’s position is not to abandon Greece and Rome, but to study them in relation to other ancient Mediterranean cultures.

[23:31] **Classics, Language, and Elite Education**

[事实] After the break, the discussion turns to classics as the study of Greek and Latin.

[事实] Beard says it would be naive to deny a connection between class and classics in England.

[事实] She argues that Latin in particular functioned as a gatekeeper to elite culture.

[事实] She says elite students in the 18th and early 19th centuries often focused on translating English writers such as Shakespeare and Milton into Latin rather than studying ancient culture in the modern sense.

[推测] The pointlessness of elite Latin fluency made it useful as a social marker because it signaled time, training, and access.

[27:28] **Classics, Empire, and Anti-Imperial Critique**

[事实] Dominic asks whether classics appealed to elites because of empire.

[事实] Beard says that is half true: some imperialists did see the Roman Empire as a model for Britain.

[事实] She also says the relationship was fragile, using Boudicca as an example of Britain identifying both with Rome and with resistance to Rome.

[事实] Beard argues that Rome also gave people a language for critiquing empire.

[事实] The hosts mention Hobbes, the American Revolution, and the French Revolution as examples of classical history being linked to anti-monarchical or revolutionary thought.

[推测] The discussion rejects the idea that classics has one inherent politics; its materials can justify domination or resistance depending on use.

[31:07] **Tacitus and the Critique of Autocracy**

[事实] Beard says classical examples and tropes have been used both to justify revolution and to justify oppression.

[事实] She cites Mussolini as part of classics’ poor record in underpinning European dictatorship.

[事实] She also says Tacitus offers a countercultural view of autocracy, one-man rule, and imperial corruption.

[事实] Beard recalls reading Tacitus’ *Agricola* as a left-leaning teenager and being struck by the line about making a desert and calling it peace.

[推测] Tacitus becomes Beard’s example of why classical texts can speak against empire from within the imperial tradition itself.

[34:34] **Cruelty, Slavery, and Sexual Violence in the Ancient World**

[事实] A listener asks whether classicists have downplayed the cruelty and unfairness of the Roman world.

[事实] Beard says there has been a tendency to slide from “this culture is interesting” to “this culture is admirable.”

[事实] She identifies treatment of women, slavery, and sexual violence as areas that have often been softened or euphemized.

[事实] She says students now often read Ovid’s *Metamorphoses* as a work filled with rape, whereas older teaching used terms such as “rapture.”

[事实] She notes that slavery was sometimes imagined through a nostalgic “faithful slave” model while agricultural, industrial, mining, castrated, and sex slaves were neglected.

[推测] Beard is arguing for a fuller classics that keeps literary power and moral horror in view at the same time.

[38:38] **The Colosseum and the Attraction of Roman Violence**

[事实] The hosts discuss the Colosseum as the most famous Roman monument and a site associated with gladiators.

[事实] Beard says the Romans were horrible, and one reason to study them is that they were horrible.

[事实] She says some Romans themselves questioned what they were doing when watching the slaughter of human beings.

[事实] She notes that the Colosseum has been interpreted differently over time, including as a shrine to martyred Christians in the 19th century.

[事实] Beard explains that Vespasian built the Colosseum on the site of Nero’s ornamental lake as a gesture of returning private imperial pleasure grounds to public entertainment.

[事实] She also notes that the money came from the suppression of the Jewish rebellion and the destruction of the Temple.

[推测] The Colosseum is presented as a monument whose public appeal depends on layers of violence, spectacle, politics, religion, and memory.

[42:33] **Infantilizing Antiquity and Modern Self-Righteousness**

[事实] Dominic asks whether modern people fail to face Roman brutality because they often encounter the ancient world as children.

[事实] Beard says there is a certain infantilization of antiquity, including a projection of the nuclear family and softened images of slaves and households.

[事实] She also warns against modern self-righteousness, noting that people may say “we don’t have slaves” while ignoring the conditions in which modern goods such as smartphones are made.

[事实] Beard says she would not want to go back to ancient Rome, even as an empress.

[推测] This section complicates moral comparison: modern societies may be better in some ways, but that does not make their distance from ancient exploitation clean or absolute.

[45:16] **The Future and Politics of Classics**

[事实] The hosts raise current controversy in American academic circles, including Danelle Padilla Peralta’s critique of classics as linked to white supremacy and Eurocentrism.

[事实] Beard says Peralta is smart and writes very good Roman history.

[事实] She accepts that classics has been used to legitimate horrible political movements.

[事实] She argues that critics sometimes forget the revolutionary side of classics and that far-right users of classics are often bad classicists.

[事实] Beard says classicists should explain why Sparta is not an admirable model for a modern state and should emphasize that parts of Africa were crucial to the Roman Empire.

[推测] Beard’s answer is reformist rather than abolitionist: confront the field’s abuses, widen its geography, and argue better against distortions.

[48:35] **Classics as an Embattled Discipline**

[事实] Beard says classics has probably been embattled since about the fourth century AD.

[事实] She argues that the subject contains the idea that earlier people did it better.

[事实] She notes that the Classical Association was founded in the early 20th century because people already thought classics was declining.

[事实] Beard describes classics as a discipline that has long worried about what it is for and how it should fit into a modern curriculum.

[推测] The anxiety around classics is not new; the field’s self-questioning is part of its history rather than only a recent crisis.

[50:08] **What Would Be Lost If Classics Disappeared**

[事实] A listener asks what would be lost if people stopped studying classics.

[事实] Beard says she does not advocate compulsory Latin for every 11-year-old, but she does advocate giving people the opportunity to study Latin.

[事实] She argues that Dante is harder to understand if Virgil is no longer on the cultural horizon.

[事实] She says the Coen brothers’ *O Brother, Where Art Thou?* depends on Homer remaining part of the culture.

[事实] She argues that major Western art galleries are impoverished for viewers who do not know what the painters are painting.

[推测] Beard’s defense of classics rests on shared cultural literacy, not on making everyone a specialist.

[52:00] **Popular Glamour and Scholarly Mediation**

[事实] Beard says that if formal classics disappeared, people would still desperately want to study ancient Greece and Rome.

[事实] She recalls an editor saying popular history topics sold especially well when they involved the Second World War, the Tudors, and the Romans.

[事实] The hosts suggest that Rome’s glamour may feel dangerous because it is both alien and still part of the modern world.

[事实] Beard says Greek tragedy still draws theatre audiences, but that popular enthusiasm depends on hard academic work that keeps ancient texts contemporary.

[事实] She argues that old translations such as Gilbert Murray’s early 20th-century Greek tragedy translations no longer work for present audiences because each age must mediate antiquity anew.

[推测] The episode frames scholarship as the quiet infrastructure behind films, theatre, museums, and popular fascination with antiquity.

[55:01] **Alexander the Great vs Julius Caesar**

[事实] The episode ends with a listener question asking who would win if Alexander the Great fought Julius Caesar.

[事实] Beard connects the question to Plutarch’s paired lives of Greeks and Romans, where Alexander and Caesar are compared.

[事实] Beard says that if Augustus were the comparison, she would choose Augustus, but between Alexander and Caesar she gives Caesar the edge while calling both men horrific killers of innocent people.

[事实] Dominic argues Alexander would win because he fought against larger odds and defeated Persia, while Caesar fought Gauls.

[推测] The light ending echoes the episode’s larger theme: ancient figures remain compelling partly because people keep comparing, arguing over, and morally reassessing them.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s main value is that it treats classics as a living argument rather than a fixed inheritance. Mary Beard repeatedly shows that Rome can mean power, tyranny, critique, Christianity, spectacle, and scholarship at the same time.

The strongest sections are those that complicate easy binaries: pagan versus Christian, empire versus revolution, admiration versus condemnation, elite gatekeeping versus public enthusiasm. The discussion is especially useful for listeners interested in reception history, the politics of education, and why Roman imagery still works.

A limitation is that Greece receives less sustained attention than Rome, which the speakers themselves acknowledge. Egypt, Africa, and the wider Mediterranean appear as important correctives, but mostly as prompts for broadening the frame rather than as fully developed topics.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners who already have some interest in classical culture or historical memory, but it remains accessible because the hosts anchor abstract arguments in familiar examples such as Dante, Nero, the Colosseum, *Gladiator*, and modern museums.
