# The Rest Is History Meets Dan Carlin: Big Historical Questions

## 概览

This episode brings Dan Carlin of *Hardcore History* together with Tom Holland and Dominic Sandbrook for a wide-ranging conversation built around “big questions” in history. The discussion begins with Carlin’s early podcasting career, his long-form style, and the way luck shaped his entry into podcasting just as Apple began supporting the format.

The main conversation turns into a sequence of historical hypotheticals: who would be worst to be besieged by, which world leader most shaped the 20th century, which lost ancient biographical tradition would be most valuable, and whether an independent Confederacy could still exist today.

A recurring theme is the tension between individual agency and structural forces. The speakers repeatedly ask whether figures such as Kaiser Wilhelm II, Lenin, Hitler, Mao, Truman, and Robert E. Lee changed history through personal decisions, or whether they were carried by larger political, military, technological, and social pressures.

## 分段落总结

[00:59] **Introducing Dan Carlin and the premise**

[事实] Dominic introduces Dan Carlin as the creator of *Hardcore History*, noting that Carlin has produced very long episodes on subjects such as Alexander the Great, Hitler, the Pacific War, and the Roman Republic.

[事实] Tom and Dominic frame the episode as a set of major historical questions prepared separately, joking that their choices may reveal different standards of seriousness.

[推测] The opening positions Carlin as both a guest historian-podcaster and a test case for the show’s playful approach to serious historical counterfactuals.

[02:20] **Carlin’s podcasting origins and method**

[事实] Carlin says his current-events podcast began in 2005 and his history podcast in 2006, with the launch helped by Apple beginning to support podcasts on iTunes around that time.

[事实] He describes this timing as “serendipitous luck” and connects that idea to historical questions about chance and agency.

[事实] Carlin says his first history episode was 16 minutes long, even though his later episodes became multi-hour productions.

[事实] He explains that he prepares by reading heavily, using topics where he already has some grounding, marking useful quotations, and then recording in a more freewheeling style.

[05:14] **Why ask strange historical questions**

[事实] Tom says Carlin’s work inspired them to ask large, unusual questions such as whether Alexander and Hitler are really so different.

[事实] Carlin says his original intention was to make a history show for people who already knew the history, focused on odd or difficult questions rather than basic background.

[推测] This exchange explains why the episode favors comparative judgment, counterfactuals, and moral dilemmas rather than a single chronological narrative.

[07:15] **Who would be least bad to be besieged by**

[事实] The first question asks whether it would be worse to be besieged by the Assyrians, Romans, or Mongols.

[事实] Carlin says that as a city leader he would choose the Mongols, because he thinks they had the least reliable chance of taking a fortified city compared with Assyrians or Romans.

[事实] Dominic also chooses the Mongols, arguing that immediate surrender might allow collaboration and survival.

[事实] Tom says that if he were not the leader, and especially if imagining himself as a woman with children, he might prefer the Assyrians in the hope of being deported rather than killed.

[推测] The discussion treats survival as dependent not only on the attacker but also on social status, timing, surrender, and whether enslavement is judged preferable to death.

[10:13] **Roman, Assyrian, and Mongol brutality compared**

[事实] Tom cites Polybius to say that Romans could kill inhabitants and animals when sacking a city.

[事实] Carlin says the Assyrians might spare common citizens but relocate or enslave them, while the Mongols might kill everyone if a city resisted.

[事实] The speakers discuss Caesar’s campaigns in Gaul, with Tom saying Plutarch claimed Caesar killed a million and enslaved a million, probably exaggerated but not by much.

[事实] Tom describes Caesar’s Gallic campaigns as being on a genocidal scale and notes mutilation of prisoners.

[推测] The agreed answer of “Mongols” is presented as ironic because the Mongols are normally associated with extreme terror, yet surrender or weaker siege capability could make them the least bad option in a narrow scenario.

[12:52] **Kaiser Wilhelm II and the 20th century**

[事实] Asked which world leader made the biggest difference to the 20th century, Carlin chooses Kaiser Wilhelm II.

[事实] Carlin argues that Germany’s invasion of Belgium helped turn the First World War into the event that shaped Bolshevism, Hitler, the Second World War, and the Cold War.

[事实] Dominic and Tom challenge whether blaming the Kaiser is too harsh, noting Nicholas II’s mobilization decision and the wider decision-making machinery of 1914.

[推测] Carlin’s answer treats the First World War as the key domino of the 20th century, even while acknowledging that the Kaiser did not single-handedly control events.

[14:43] **Britain, Belgium, and the scope of the First World War**

[事实] Carlin asks whether Britain would have entered the war if Germany and Russia had fought without Belgium being invaded.

[事实] Dominic argues that Germany’s military planning was built around striking France through Belgium, making a non-Belgian route difficult to imagine.

[事实] The speakers suggest that if Britain had stayed out, the United States might also have stayed out.

[事实] Carlin says Britain’s financial and imperial weakening in the war profoundly shaped the 20th century.

[推测] The conversation leaves open whether Britain’s eclipse by the United States was inevitable or merely accelerated by the war.

[17:20] **Hitler, Stalin, Mao, and alternative dominoes**

[事实] Dominic says he expected Carlin to choose Hitler, Stalin, or Mao, but Carlin replies that Hitler and Stalin were both created by the First World War.

[事实] Tom raises Mao as a possible candidate because Mao’s regime survived and China plays an escalating role in world affairs.

[事实] Carlin notes that Mao overthrew the Nationalists, who had been weakened by Japan, and that Mao’s communism was connected to the Russian Revolution.

[推测] The speakers repeatedly circle back to the Kaiser because multiple later 20th-century developments are presented as downstream of the First World War.

[22:34] **Russia without Lenin**

[事实] Carlin asks how much difference Germany made by sending Lenin back to Russia by train.

[事实] Tom and Dominic agree that Lenin’s arrival at the Finland Station mattered because he brought charisma, ruthlessness, and a clear plan to seize power.

[事实] Carlin notes that the Russian army and state were already being destabilized by massive wartime losses and mutiny.

[事实] The speakers compare removing Lenin from Russia with removing Hitler from Germany, suggesting that other authoritarian outcomes might still have emerged.

[推测] Without Lenin, Russia might still have become authoritarian, but the speakers suggest it may not have followed the same ideological path of Bolshevik rule, collectivization, and famine.

[27:34] **Nuclear weapons and decisive leadership**

[事实] Tom asks whether it may be too early to identify the most consequential 20th-century leader because nuclear war anxiety remains unresolved.

[事实] Carlin says nuclear weapons likely would have appeared eventually, but the Second World War and fear of Nazi progress accelerated their development.

[事实] The speakers mention Einstein’s letter to FDR and the possibility that without wartime urgency the bomb might have arrived later.

[事实] Tom suggests Truman’s use of atomic bombs may have been decisive because it demonstrated their horror and helped create a taboo against future use.

[推测] The episode presents nuclear weapons as both an inevitable technological development and a historically contingent political event shaped by war.

[29:04] **Truman, Hiroshima, Nagasaki, and firebombing**

[事实] Carlin says Truman had only recently become vice president and did not initially know about the atomic bomb.

[事实] Dominic argues that Truman was choosing between terrible options, including invasion of Japan after Okinawa, blockade, and continued war.

[事实] Carlin emphasizes that Japanese forces were still killing civilians in Asia and that other choices could have led to many unforeseeable consequences.

[事实] The speakers compare the atomic bombings with the firebombing of Japanese cities, especially Tokyo, and note that Hiroshima and Nagasaki remained useful targets because many other cities had already been heavily bombed.

[推测] The moral debate is framed less as a simple question of whether the bomb was evil and more as a comparison among several catastrophic wartime options.

[40:22] **Which ancient empire’s biographies would be most valuable**

[事实] After the break, Tom asks which ancient empire they would most want to have left biographies like Suetonius or Plutarch: Egypt, Persia, or Carthage.

[事实] Carlin chooses Persia because he thinks Persian libraries might have preserved knowledge from earlier civilizations such as Egypt, Assyria, Babylonia, and perhaps Mesopotamia.

[事实] Dominic notes that the Carthaginians remain especially mysterious because so little survives.

[事实] Tom says Greek biographies of Persian kings exist, but they are written from a Greek perspective.

[推测] The value of Persian biographies would lie not only in individual lives but also in access to a wider Near Eastern historical archive.

[43:00] **Why some cultures did not write biography like Greeks and Romans**

[事实] Tom explains that Persian royal ideology treated the king as the embodiment of truth and order, making gossipy personal details unsuitable.

[事实] Carlin agrees that other cultures wrote history for different purposes rather than in the Suetonius-style model familiar to modern audiences.

[事实] Carlin says Iranian listeners told him Persian history was often transmitted through other forms, including oral historians and saga-like traditions.

[推测] The discussion warns that the absence of familiar biography does not mean the absence of historical consciousness.

[44:38] **Egypt, Akhenaten, and lost personal histories**

[事实] Tom says he would choose Egypt because figures such as Tutankhamun, Akhenaten, Hatshepsut, and Ramesses II seem extraordinary.

[事实] He argues that Egyptian royal ideology dissolved individual personality into the grandeur of the pharaoh.

[事实] Carlin raises Akhenaten’s religious reforms and suggests they may have opened the door to a different style of Egyptian representation and thought.

[推测] A Suetonius- or Plutarch-style biography of Akhenaten would be valuable precisely because the surviving evidence hints at drama without giving full personal detail.

[46:43] **Could the Confederacy still exist today**

[事实] Dominic asks whether the Confederate States of America could still exist today if the American Civil War had gone differently.

[事实] Carlin says that if the Confederacy had survived, he thinks it could still exist today, though not as Robert E. Lee or Jefferson Davis imagined, because slavery would eventually disappear.

[事实] Dominic notes that the Confederacy did not need to win outright; it only needed not to lose.

[事实] Carlin says foreign powers might have had an interest in helping split the United States for power-balance reasons.

[推测] The central counterfactual depends less on battlefield conquest than on Northern willingness to continue paying the cost of war.

[48:23] **National identity and American fracture**

[事实] Dominic argues that if the Confederacy had survived for a decade or two, its national identity might have become too strong for voluntary reunion.

[事实] Carlin connects this to modern arguments that the United States contains deep regional identities and potential fracture points.

[事实] Carlin contrasts the geographically divided Civil War of 1861-65 with modern American polarization, which he says is divided down to voting districts within counties.

[推测] The discussion suggests that later generations might have treated Confederate independence as historically inevitable if it had endured.

[50:50] **Reconstruction and the long roots of division**

[事实] Dominic asks whether current American tensions can be traced back to the Civil War, Reconstruction, and unfinished business after Lincoln.

[事实] Carlin says debates over Reconstruction often ask whether there really was meaningful reconstruction at all.

[事实] He says Lincoln’s assassination and political constraints helped produce a limited form of Reconstruction.

[事实] Carlin argues that trends visible by the mid-20th century are connected to present tensions and that historians centuries later may connect the 1960s and today more closely than people do now.

[推测] Carlin treats modern U.S. polarization as deeper than the last decade, though not identical to the 19th-century Civil War divide.

[53:04] **Slavery, abolitionism, and race in a surviving Confederacy**

[事实] Tom asks whether a surviving slaveholding Confederacy and a more abolitionist North would have made another war inevitable.

[事实] Carlin cautions that Northern abolitionism should not be overstated, because many Northerners opposed slavery without believing in racial equality.

[事实] Carlin says that if the South had not fired on Fort Sumter, the North likely would not have started a war simply to abolish slavery.

[事实] He argues that slavery would eventually become untenable, but segregation and racial inequality could have lasted longer or taken a different form.

[推测] A surviving Confederacy might have resembled a richer and more secure version of South Africa in its racial order, though this remains a counterfactual judgment.

[57:41] **Why Confederate victory was difficult**

[事实] Carlin says it would have been very difficult for the Confederacy to win if the North persisted in seeking unconditional victory.

[事实] He argues the North could have stopped because the war became too costly, but if it kept fighting, the Confederacy was unlikely to win.

[事实] Dominic asks whether British involvement would change the counterfactual, and Carlin agrees it would significantly change the balance, comparing it to France’s role in the American Revolutionary War.

[事实] The hosts close by noting that they have only reached four of ten questions and will continue the conversation in a second episode.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s main strength is the way it turns familiar historical subjects into live problems of judgment. Instead of reciting settled narratives, the speakers test how much weight to place on individual decisions, military systems, ideology, contingency, and moral context.

Carlin’s contribution is strongest when he slows the conversation down and asks what assumptions sit underneath a question: whether the besieged person is a ruler or civilian, whether Britain must enter the First World War, or whether Northern abolitionism should be equated with racial egalitarianism.

The limitation is that the format moves quickly across enormous subjects, so many claims remain provisional rather than fully argued. [推测] Listeners looking for a polished single-topic history may find the episode too sprawling, while listeners who enjoy counterfactual reasoning and comparative historical debate are likely to find it especially valuable.
