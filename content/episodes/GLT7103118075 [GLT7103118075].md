# The History of the Future: Oracles, Apocalypse, and Prediction

## 概览

This episode examines why humans keep trying to know the future, from ancient oracles and biblical apocalypse to pandemic modelling, climate projections, and modern dystopian fiction.

The central argument is that visions of the future usually reveal the fears of the present. The hosts link Christian and Muslim ideas of linear time and final judgment to modern apocalyptic anxieties, especially around climate change.

The discussion then moves through technological futurism, mid-century optimism, nuclear dread, cyberpunk, environmental collapse, and audience questions on Delphi, Nostradamus, Mother Shipton, Orwell, Huxley, Halley’s Comet, the Sibylline Books, Spengler, Huntington, and Fukuyama.

## 分段落总结

[00:29] **Why Humans Look Forward**

[事实] The hosts introduce oracles, prophecies, apocalypse, and scientific projection as different ways people try to understand the future.
[事实] They connect the topic to the Glasgow UN Climate Change Conference and to uncertainty about the pandemic and lockdowns.
[事实] Dominic says studying how people imagined the future reveals their anxieties, fears, and historical context.
[推测] The episode frames prediction less as a neutral forecast than as a mirror of each age’s deepest worries.

[02:21] **Uncertainty, Expertise, and Divination**

[事实] The hosts compare ancient divination through animal entrails with modern pandemic-era graphs and online claims of expertise.
[事实] Dominic argues that “a little knowledge” can be dangerous when people with shallow understanding present confident predictions.
[事实] Tom notes that ancient diviners also believed they were skilled specialists.
[推测] The comparison suggests that modern data-driven prediction can play a similar social role to older forms of prophecy.

[03:33] **From Stable Time to Technological Change**

[事实] Dominic argues that in the 12th century people may have expected the future to look much like the present.
[事实] He says the scientific revolution shifted future-thinking toward inventions and technology rather than only dynasties or states.
[事实] Tom challenges this by saying medieval Christians did imagine a radically different future because the Bible described an endpoint.
[推测] The disagreement turns on whether “future change” means ordinary technological change or final cosmic transformation.

[04:24] **Time’s Arrow and Apocalypse**

[事实] Tom contrasts circular ideas of time with Christian and Muslim ideas of creation followed by an end of days.
[事实] He suggests modern climate anxiety may still be shaped by inherited ideas of sin, judgment, punishment, plagues, famine, and devastation.
[事实] The hosts discuss Ragnarok but note uncertainty over how much the surviving account was influenced by Christianity.
[推测] The episode implies that modern secular apocalypse stories may preserve older theological structures.

[07:23] **Progress, Technology, and Moral Expectation**

[事实] Dominic says that before about 1700, forward movement was often imagined in moral or theological terms.
[事实] He contrasts that with later expectations involving inventions, technology, and fantastical changes.
[事实] Tom notes that around the year 1000, technological development could coincide with moral reawakening, including church building and a sense that life was improving.
[推测] Modern futurism is presented as a fusion of technological acceleration and older moral narratives.

[08:52] **Samuel Madden and Moral Futurism**

[事实] Dominic cites Samuel Madden’s 1733 book *Memoirs of the Twentieth Century* as an early prediction of the future.
[事实] He says Madden imagined ideological conflict involving Jesuits, deists, the Pope, and papal power.
[事实] The hosts use this example to argue that future visions are not only about science and technology but also about morality and behavior.
[推测] Even when societies experience technological change, their predictions can remain anchored in existing religious and political obsessions.

[11:16] **Phases of Modern Dystopia**

[事实] Dominic identifies different phases of future imagination: early 20th-century invasion and world-war fears, mid-century totalitarian visions such as *1984*, Cold War nuclear apocalypse, and later concerns about computers or environmental catastrophe.
[事实] The hosts mention *The Third World War*, *Threads*, and *The War Game* as examples of nuclear-apocalypse thinking.
[事实] They describe recent future visions as often focused on virtual worlds, robots, or environmental collapse.
[事实] Tom compares James Lovelock’s climate warnings to older apocalyptic language about plague, famine, and punishment for sin.

[13:57] **Computers, Cyberpunk, and Lost Optimism**

[事实] The hosts mention Philip K. Dick, William Gibson, *Neuromancer*, *Blade Runner*, and online worlds as examples of computer-centered future imagination.
[事实] Dominic says British newspapers in the 1950s ran stories about a “robot revolution” and expected computers to create leisure and abundance.
[事实] He says 1960s futurism was often utopian, while later visions such as *Star Wars*, *Blade Runner*, and *The Road* became dirtier, darker, and more dystopian.
[推测] The discussion suggests that optimism about technology has been replaced by fears of job loss, escapism, pollution, and climate ruin.

[16:07] **Why Disaster Is Easier to Imagine**

[事实] Tom suggests that disaster may be easier and more enjoyable to describe than paradise.
[事实] He compares the Book of Revelation’s vivid destruction with its shorter, less concrete vision of the New Jerusalem.
[事实] Dominic compares this to Marxism, saying the struggle toward a new world is described more vividly than the new world itself.
[推测] Utopia appears harder to narrate than collapse because conflict and catastrophe provide clearer drama.

[17:38] **Did the Greeks Believe Delphi?**

[事实] In the audience-question section, Joel Coppersmith asks whether ancient people believed in the Delphic Oracle.
[事实] Tom says the Greeks did believe in it, otherwise they would not have consulted it.
[事实] He says Apollo’s oracle was expected to be difficult, elliptical, and “oblique.”
[事实] The hosts discuss Croesus, whose prophecy that a great empire would fall came true in a way he misunderstood.

[21:31] **Delphi as Information Network**

[事实] Tom says Delphi’s priests were the only professional priesthood in Greece and received visitors from across and beyond the Greek world.
[事实] He argues this may have made them unusually well informed about international affairs.
[事实] He discusses the Persian invasion of Greece in 480, when Athenians received prophecies about Athens burning and the “wooden wall” holding.
[推测] The Oracle’s authority may have combined religious ritual, ambiguity, and real strategic intelligence.

[23:41] **How Ancient Oracles Worked**

[事实] Dominic describes the Oracle of Ammon at Siwa as involving a stone, priests, written questions, and possible written answers.
[事实] Tom says ancient oracles often required interpretation rather than simply having a person give plain answers.
[事实] He mentions Delphi’s fumes and Dodona’s sacred oak as examples of indirect prophetic mechanisms.
[事实] The hosts say ambiguous answers made it possible to explain failed expectations as faulty interpretation.

[25:41] **Nostradamus and Mother Shipton**

[事实] Joey McCarthy asks about Nostradamus and claims he predicted Hitler, the Great Fire of London, Nagasaki, and JFK.
[事实] Tom says he read Nostradamus obsessively as a child and was frightened by prophecies about a Third World War in 1999.
[事实] The hosts describe Nostradamus’ prophecies as extremely vague.
[事实] They discuss Mother Shipton, noting that some famous predictions attributed to her were falsified in the mid-19th century.

[29:21] **Orwell, Huxley, and the Present**

[事实] Tom Watts asks whether Orwell or Huxley was right and whether *1984* is still to come or already here.
[事实] Dominic says Orwell went somewhat out of fashion after the Cold War but returned through interest in doublethink, language control, thoughtcrime, and surveillance.
[事实] He says Huxley’s *Brave New World* is more about biology and people being pacified or “blissed out.”
[事实] The hosts joke that people today live comfortable lives but still become angry on Twitter.

[31:06] **Comets, Climate, and Apocalyptic Interpretation**

[事实] Ollie Simpson asks about Halley’s Comet in 1066, which appears in the Bayeux Tapestry as an omen.
[事实] Tom says the events of 1066 would probably have happened whether or not Halley’s Comet appeared.
[事实] He cites the sixth-century Mediterranean as a time of celestial phenomena, darkened skies, climatic events, global cooling, and apocalyptic expectation.
[事实] The hosts also mention the Little Ice Age, famine, rain, and 17th-century end-times thinking.

[33:07] **The Sibylline Books**

[事实] An audience question asks whether the Sibylline Books date back to Rome’s regal period.
[事实] Tom recounts the story of a Sibyl offering Tarquin nine prophetic books, then six, then three, until he buys the remaining books.
[事实] He says it is not possible to know whether they truly dated to the regal period, but Romans believed they were ancient.
[事实] He says the books usually advised Romans what rituals to perform in crises rather than simply predicting events.

[35:34] **Spengler, Civilizations, and the End of History**

[事实] The hosts discuss Oswald Spengler, who argued for the decline of the West and imagined civilizations rising and falling.
[事实] Dominic says Spengler wrote against the backdrop of the First World War and the collapse of the late Victorian world.
[事实] They connect Spengler to later “prophetic” post-Cold War books by Huntington and Fukuyama.
[事实] The hosts say civilizational language has returned in geopolitics, though the perceived fault lines have shifted toward China and the West.

[39:17] **Closing**

[事实] The hosts say they have reached their own “end of history” for the episode.
[事实] They announce that the next episode will discuss their top ten weird wars.
[事实] They thank listeners for questions and close the episode.

## 播客点评/总结

[推测] The episode’s main value is its broad historical range: it connects ancient religion, medieval apocalypse, Enlightenment futurism, Cold War fears, cyberpunk, climate anxiety, and post-Cold War geopolitics without treating prediction as a purely modern practice.

[推测] Its strongest point is the recurring idea that societies imagine the future through the anxieties they already have. The discussion is especially effective when it compares ancient oracles with modern expert culture, online graphs, think tanks, and pandemic uncertainty.

[推测] Its limitation is that the conversation moves quickly and sometimes relies on broad cultural contrasts rather than detailed evidence. Several points are framed conversationally, with the hosts acknowledging uncertainty or hazy memory.

[推测] This episode is best suited to listeners interested in intellectual history, religion, political prophecy, science fiction, and how modern fears about climate, technology, and geopolitics echo older apocalyptic patterns.
