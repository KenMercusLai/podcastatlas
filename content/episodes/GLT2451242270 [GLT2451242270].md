# The Massacre of the Innocents and the Tay Bridge Disaster

## 概览

This episode is part of the podcast’s Christmas-season series on events associated with specific festive dates, focusing here on December 28. The first half discusses Childermas, the church commemoration of the Massacre of the Innocents, and treats the story less as straightforward history than as sacred narrative shaped by echoes of Moses, Exodus, and Herod’s reputation.

The hosts then move from biblical violence to Victorian disaster: the collapse of Scotland’s Tay Rail Bridge on December 28, 1879. They outline the bridge’s status as a symbol of modern engineering, the cost-cutting and design flaws around high winds, and the total loss of life when the train and bridge fell into the Tay.

The episode’s comic turn comes through William McGonagall, remembered as an exceptionally bad poet, whose poem on the Tay Bridge Disaster became famous precisely because its clumsy style collided with the gravity of the tragedy.

## 分段落总结

[00:10] **Childermas and the Massacre of the Innocents**

[事实] The episode begins as part of the hosts’ “12 days of Christmas” series, though they joke that it has become 13 days.
[事实] The date being discussed is Childermas, when the church commemorates the slaughter of the infant boys of Bethlehem by King Herod.
[事实] The story is presented as coming only from the Gospel of Matthew: wise men visit Herod, go to Bethlehem, find Jesus, avoid returning to Herod after an angelic warning, and Joseph takes Mary and Jesus to Egypt.
[推测] The hosts frame the subject with dark humor, using the mismatch between Christmas cheer and massacre as part of the episode’s tone.

[02:09] **Historical Doubts About the Story**

[事实] The hosts say the massacre probably did not happen historically, and if it did, it probably did not happen on the commemorated date.
[事实] They point out a calendar problem: the wise men are traditionally associated with Epiphany on January 6, while Childermas falls before that.
[事实] They note that none of the other three gospel writers mention the massacre, and Josephus, who wrote extensively about Herod, does not mention it either.
[推测] The lack of corroborating sources is treated as a major reason for doubting the story’s historical reliability.

[03:02] **Sacred History and the Moses Parallel**

[事实] The episode compares the massacre narrative to other sacred histories whose theological meaning may matter more than strict historical plausibility.
[事实] The hosts connect Jesus’ flight to Egypt and Herod’s killings with the story of Moses, Pharaoh, Exodus, and the death of the firstborn.
[事实] They describe the Matthew story as establishing a link between Jesus and Moses by echoing the Old Testament in the New Testament.
[推测] The massacre story is presented as a symbolic narrative that helps position Jesus within a broader biblical pattern.

[04:26] **Herod’s Role and Reputation**

[事实] Herod is described as a ruler notorious for killing his own children when he perceived dynastic threats.
[事实] The hosts say it is true to Herod’s character that he would be alarmed by news of a rival “king of the Jews.”
[事实] They mention a later recorded joke attributed to Augustus, saying he would rather be Herod’s pig than Herod’s child.
[推测] Herod’s violent reputation may have made him a plausible villain for Matthew’s narrative even if the specific massacre is historically doubtful.

[05:29] **Matthew, Judean Memory, and Herod’s Legacy**

[事实] The hosts say the Gospel of Matthew was probably not written by the apostle Matthew.
[事实] They place Matthew’s gospel against the backdrop of the Judean revolt and the destruction of major sites linked to Herod, including the temple, Masada, and Caesarea.
[事实] Herod is described as a major builder whose projects shaped Judean historical memory.
[事实] The hosts describe Herod as an ambivalent figure whose Jewish or Judean identity was complex.
[推测] In this reading, Herod functions as a contrast to Christ: a conventional, brutal king opposed to a figure whose kingship leads to the cross.

[07:14] **Herod as Political Survivor and Church Memory**

[事实] The hosts also acknowledge Herod’s political skill, including his shift from supporting Mark Antony and Cleopatra to aligning with Octavian.
[事实] They say Herod outlived Antony and Cleopatra and had to be brutal to survive politically.
[事实] The Innocents are described as being counted by the church as the first Christian martyrs.
[事实] The hosts connect Childermas to the Coventry Carol and mention it being sung in the ruins of Coventry Cathedral in 1940.
[推测] The discussion balances Herod’s competence as a ruler with the moral and religious memory that made him a symbol of murderous tyranny.

[09:47] **Camus and the Theological Shadow of Christmas**

[事实] The hosts preview a future discussion of Albert Camus and mention The Fall.
[事实] They say Camus imagined Christ allowing himself to be crucified because of guilt over not having been one of the Innocents.
[推测] This link reframes Christmas not only as birth and celebration, but also as a story already shadowed by suffering, guilt, and death.

[10:50] **Shift to the Tay Bridge Disaster**

[事实] After the break, the episode turns to another December 28 subject: the Tay Bridge disaster.
[事实] The hosts describe the second half as involving both death and poetry.
[事实] They introduce the Tay Rail Bridge in Scotland, crossing the Firth of Tay to Dundee.
[推测] The transition keeps the episode’s pattern of mixing tragic anniversaries with comic commentary.

[11:25] **The Bridge as Victorian Grand Project**

[事实] The Tay Rail Bridge was built in June 1878 and ran for about two miles.
[事实] It is described as a symbol of Victorian engineering, modernity, and progress.
[事实] The architect was Thomas Bouch, and the project had gone heavily over budget.
[事实] The hosts say costs were cut during construction and that Bouch made no allowance for high winds.
[推测] The bridge is presented as an example of technological confidence undermined by practical engineering failure.

[12:28] **The Collapse on December 28, 1879**

[事实] On the evening of December 28, 1879, during stormy weather, the Edinburgh Express headed onto the Tay Rail Bridge.
[事实] The train entered the bridge at about 7:13 p.m. with five carriages and a luggage van.
[事实] When the train was halfway across, a flash of light was seen, followed by darkness, and the bridge collapsed into the Tay.
[事实] The hosts say there were probably about 75 people on board, including staff, and that there were no survivors.
[事实] Thomas Bouch had been knighted for the bridge and died less than a year later from shock and distress of mind.
[推测] The disaster is presented as both a human tragedy and a devastating collapse of Victorian engineering prestige.

[14:27] **William McGonagall, the Worst Poet**

[事实] The disaster is remembered partly because it came to the attention of William McGonagall, whom the hosts call the worst poet in recorded history.
[事实] McGonagall’s family was probably Irish, and he worked as a hand-loom weaver.
[事实] He had little formal education but loved Shakespeare and recited Shakespeare while working.
[事实] The hosts tell a story in which McGonagall played Macbeth and refused to die in the final act because he believed the actor playing Macduff was jealous of him.
[推测] The anecdotes are used to portray McGonagall as both ridiculous and oddly committed to theatrical self-importance.

[16:00] **McGonagall’s Poetic Calling and Royal Ambition**

[事实] Around 1877, when McGonagall was about 50, he described experiencing an intense urge to write poetry.
[事实] He later walked from Dundee to Balmoral, about 60 miles, to offer his services to Queen Victoria.
[事实] He announced himself as the Queen’s poet, but the guards told him that Tennyson already held that role.
[事实] McGonagall believed a polite form letter from the royal household meant the Queen was interested in his poetic career.
[推测] The story presents him as deeply self-deluded, but also persistent and sincere.

[17:43] **The Tay Bridge Disaster Poem**

[事实] McGonagall wrote The Tay Bridge Disaster in 1880, and the hosts call it his masterpiece as a terrible poet.
[事实] The hosts read parts of the poem, focusing on its awkward phrasing, forced rhymes, and blunt moral lesson about stronger construction reducing the chance of being killed.
[事实] They mock the rhyme of “buttresses” with “confesses” and the flat ending of the opening verse.
[推测] The poem’s fame comes from the gap between the solemn subject and the unintentionally comic handling.

[19:26] **Circus Humiliation and Tragic Reputation**

[事实] The hosts say McGonagall later worked in a circus, where he read poetry while audiences pelted him with eggs, flour, and other food.
[事实] They describe him as a tragic figure who was not fully unaware of his reputation.
[事实] They conclude that the Tay Bridge poem is remembered because of the incongruity between the disaster’s seriousness and the poem’s insensitivity.
[推测] The episode treats McGonagall as comic, but also as someone whose public mockery had a sad dimension.

[事实] The hosts briefly preview the next episode.
[事实] They mention Thomas Becket and the massacre at Wounded Knee as upcoming subjects.

## 播客点评/总结

This episode’s value lies in the way it connects religious narrative, historical skepticism, political memory, engineering failure, and literary comedy without pretending they are the same kind of evidence. The discussion of the Massacre of the Innocents is especially useful because it separates theological meaning from historical likelihood.

The Tay Bridge section works as a compact case study in Victorian ambition, risk, and public disaster. Its strongest comic material comes from William McGonagall, but the hosts repeatedly keep the underlying tragedy in view.

[推测] The episode is best suited to listeners who enjoy history delivered through conversation, irony, and cultural references rather than a strictly linear academic lecture. Its limitation is that some major topics, such as Matthew’s authorship or the Judean revolt, are only sketched because they are being saved for other episodes.
