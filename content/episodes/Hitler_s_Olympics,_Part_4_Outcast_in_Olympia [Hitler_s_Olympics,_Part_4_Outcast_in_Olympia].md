# Outcast in Olympia: Avery Brundage and the Making of an Olympic Believer

## Overview

This episode traces the rise of Avery Brundage, the self-made Chicago builder who became one of the most influential figures in modern Olympic history. Born into poverty and driven by discipline, ambition, and an intense faith in rules, Brundage saw amateur sport as a refuge from the corruption of ordinary life.

His defining experience came at the 1912 Stockholm Olympics. There he encountered an international world of prestige and privilege—but also abandoned the decathlon before its final event. The episode argues that this failure haunted him, shaped his rigid devotion to amateurism, and may have influenced his later treatment of Jim Thorpe.

By 1934, Brundage was close to achieving his dream of joining the International Olympic Committee. Yet that opportunity arrived as Nazi persecution exposed a fundamental contradiction in his Olympic creed: could sport truly remain separate from politics when the host country was excluding Jewish athletes?

## 分段落总结

[00:44] **Pierre de Coubertin’s Olympic religion**

[事实] Pierre de Coubertin, founder of the modern Olympic Games, imagined the Olympics as a modern religion founded on amateurism, internationalism, discipline, and peace.

[事实] The modern Games began in Athens in 1896, but the 1912 Stockholm Olympics became a turning point by bringing together athletes from nearly every continent and introducing events including the decathlon and an arts competition.

[事实] Coubertin won the 1912 Olympic literature prize with “Ode to Sport,” submitted under a pseudonym.

[推测] The poem functions as a revealing statement of Olympic ideology even though the presenters repeatedly mock its literary quality.

[04:04] **Introducing Avery Brundage**

[事实] Avery Brundage competed at Stockholm in 1912 and later became central to both the 1936 Berlin Games and the development of the modern Olympic movement.

[事实] The episode frames him as more complicated than Charles Sherrill: neither an incompetent aristocrat nor a simple villain.

[事实] The series’ broader question is why some people recognize evil while others cannot or will not.

[推测] Brundage’s outsider origins initially make him appear closer to the perceptive Dorothy Thompson than to privileged Nazi sympathizers such as Sherrill, making his later choices more troubling.

[07:54] **An impoverished childhood shaped by rules and striving**

[事实] Brundage grew up poor in Chicago, worked as a paperboy, traveled seven miles to school, and was abandoned by his father at the age of five.

[事实] He excelled academically and trained with improvised equipment because his school lacked athletic facilities.

[事实] At university he studied engineering, competed in track and field, joined numerous organizations, and sought leadership positions.

[事实] The presenters characterize his worldview as intensely rule-bound and cite his effort to reserve school colors for varsity athletes.

[推测] His relentless productivity and frequent references to being unpopular suggest that he turned social rejection into proof of his own superiority.

[11:47] **Sport as refuge from a corrupt world**

[事实] Working in construction exposed Brundage to defective materials, nepotism, and other practices that offended his sense of fairness.

[事实] In unpublished writing, he condemned the injustice and misery produced by greed, ignorance, and incompetent people in authority.

[事实] His biographer Alan Guttmann described amateur athletics as Brundage’s refuge from an imperfect world.

[推测] Sport appealed to him because measurable performance seemed to promise the meritocracy and objective justice he could not find in business or society.

[13:17] **Stockholm and conversion to the Olympic ideal**

[事实] After receiving an invitation to the 1912 Olympics, Brundage quit his job, traveled to New York, and sailed to Stockholm with the American team.

[事实] Stockholm introduced him to royal ceremonies, banquets, balls, receptions, and an international elite far removed from his Chicago upbringing.

[事实] His unpublished account described his arrival in unusually romantic language, calling the landscape a “fairyland.”

[推测] The presenters believe that Stockholm gave Brundage his first glimpse of the prestigious life he wanted and converted him to Coubertin’s vision of sport as fair, meritocratic, and above politics.

[19:29] **Building wealth and pursuing Olympic power**

[事实] After Stockholm, Brundage created a successful Chicago construction business and built factories, hotels, viaducts, and luxury apartment buildings.

[事实] Architectural historians describe him as technically innovative, notably in finding ways to pour and cure concrete during winter.

[事实] He earned a reputation for honesty, including returning unused funds when projects came in under budget.

[事实] By 1928, he was president of the American Olympic Committee and a prominent advocate of amateur sport.

[推测] His wealth was not merely an end in itself; it helped finance his pursuit of status and authority within the Olympic movement.

[23:55] **The outsider who wanted to rule the aristocrats**

[事实] The International Olympic Committee was dominated by aristocratic men whose privilege contrasted sharply with Brundage’s self-made career.

[事实] The presenters describe him as embodying twentieth-century meritocratic ambition while the IOC represented an older hereditary elite.

[事实] Despite possessing wealth, influence, and a construction empire, Brundage continued to seek acceptance within the IOC’s strict hierarchy.

[推测] He may have simultaneously envied and despised its members, believing himself more capable while still craving the status they possessed.

[推测] His desire to enter this elite circle may also have been driven by shame about his origins and his performance in Stockholm.

[27:29] **The decathlon and Brundage’s defining failure**

[事实] The decathlon combines ten events, ending with the 1,500 metres after competitors have already completed nine tests of speed and strength.

[事实] Brundage performed respectably through eight events in 1912 but withdrew before the javelin and therefore avoided the final 1,500 metres.

[事实] Jim Thorpe dominated the competition and won by the largest points margin in Olympic decathlon history.

[事实] Brundage later called his withdrawal an “inexcusable error” and said that failing to finish was unforgivable.

[推测] The presenters argue that he withdrew because he feared being humiliated in the 1,500 metres before the royalty and Olympic leaders whose approval he desired.

[推测] By quitting, Brundage violated the Olympic principle he later championed: that struggle and participation matter more than victory.

[34:43] **Jim Thorpe, amateurism, and a lasting grievance**

[事实] Brundage maintained that sport should be pursued for love of the game rather than money and defended a strict definition of amateurism throughout his career.

[事实] Thorpe lost his decathlon and pentathlon medals after authorities discovered that he had received about a dollar per game while playing minor-league baseball.

[事实] When Brundage later controlled the Olympic movement, he repeatedly rejected efforts to restore Thorpe’s medals.

[事实] Thorpe biographer David Maraniss believes Brundage’s 1912 defeat and jealousy of Thorpe affected his later treatment of him.

[推测] Brundage’s rigid enforcement of amateurism may have allowed him to transform personal shame and resentment into impersonal-seeming rules.

[37:47] **Seeking redemption through Olympic authority**

[事实] After 1912, Brundage trained, built his business empire, and rose through amateur sports administration until he led the American Olympic Committee.

[事实] In 1934, an IOC member wrote that Brundage deserved nomination to the committee because of his knowledge of American sport.

[推测] His ascent can be understood as an attempt to earn his way back into the idealized “Olympia” whose principles he believed he had betrayed.

[推测] The episode presents Brundage’s later anger and vindictiveness as potentially rooted in the knowledge that he himself had failed to live up to his Olympic ideal.

[40:09] **The Nazi challenge to the Olympic faith**

[事实] Brundage’s opportunity to join the IOC coincided with Nazi Germany’s exclusion of Jewish athletes from sports clubs.

[事实] Opponents of the Berlin Games argued that the Olympics should be removed from Nazi Germany because its policies contradicted Olympic principles.

[事实] “Ode to Sport” also contains language about perfecting the race, destroying “unhealthy seed,” and preserving purity.

[推测] The episode’s closing challenge is that Brundage’s Olympic creed was not necessarily incompatible with Nazi racial ideology; some of its own language could be read as supporting exclusion and eugenic thinking.

[42:09] **Hollywood’s simplified Brundage**

[事实] In a post-credits discussion, the presenters criticize the 2016 film *Race* for casting Jeremy Irons as Brundage and depicting the character with a moustache.

[事实] They regard the moustache as a misleading marker of the dandified elite to which the real, austere Brundage did not belong.

[事实] They conclude that the film’s version is less interesting than the historical Brundage.

[推测] The humorous discussion reinforces the episode’s central claim that reducing Brundage to a conventional villain obscures the ambition, insecurity, and ideological contradictions that made him consequential.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s greatest strength is its psychological portrait of Brundage. It connects his poverty, self-discipline, social ambition, Olympic failure, business success, and devotion to amateurism without presenting him as a one-dimensional villain. The recurring use of “Ode to Sport” effectively contrasts the lofty Olympic ideal with the exclusions hidden inside it.

Its account of the 1912 decathlon is especially compelling because it turns a seemingly minor athletic withdrawal into a possible key to Brundage’s later career. The episode is careful to identify major parts of this interpretation as speculation, while supporting them with Brundage’s own description of his failure as unforgivable.

The main limitation is that psychological inference sometimes carries more weight than direct evidence. The claim that fear of humiliation shaped Brundage’s withdrawal and later hostility toward Thorpe is persuasive but not conclusively established by the transcript.

This episode will particularly suit listeners interested in Olympic history, the politics of amateurism, institutional power, and the ways personal insecurity can become embedded in supposedly neutral rules.
