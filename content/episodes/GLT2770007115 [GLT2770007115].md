# Captain Cook’s Final Voyage: From Divine Welcome to Death in Hawaii

## 概览

This episode examines the final phase of Captain James Cook’s third voyage and the mystery of why the Hawaiians who welcomed him with extraordinary ceremony later killed him at Kealakekua Bay. It follows Cook from his first contact with Hawaii through his failed search for the Northwest Passage, his return to the islands, and the escalating confrontation that ended his life.

A central question is whether Hawaiians regarded Cook as the god Lono. One interpretation argues that his arrival, route around Hawaii, ships’ appearance, and ceremonial reception coincided strikingly with the Makahiki festival. A rival interpretation considers this a colonial projection and suggests that Cook was treated as an exceptional chief occupying a position between the human and divine. The hosts conclude that the Hawaiian worldview of 1779 cannot be reconstructed with certainty.

The episode also presents Cook as a leader under severe physical and psychological strain. His increasingly erratic discipline, failed Arctic mission, disproportionate reactions, and decision to take the Hawaiian king hostage helped transform mutual suspicion into violence.

Cook’s death was subsequently recast in Britain as the martyrdom of a humane Enlightenment hero. The hosts argue that both this heroic image and modern portrayals of Cook as the embodiment of colonial brutality risk turning a complicated historical person into a symbol for later ideological debates.

## 分段落总结

[03:00] **Cook’s Final Journal and the Central Mystery**

[事实] Cook’s last surviving journal entry described an enormous Hawaiian welcome at Kealakekua Bay, including gifts, red cloth, and a visit to a temple.

[事实] He wrote the entry on 17 January 1779, exactly four weeks before he was beaten and stabbed to death at the same location.

[事实] The episode asks why the same community that received Cook with apparent religious ceremony later killed him.

[推测] The contrast between his ceremonial welcome and violent death has encouraged interpretations that frame the entire encounter as a ritual drama.

[06:15] **First Contact with Hawaii**

[事实] Cook’s expedition left England in 1776 aboard the Resolution and Discovery to search for the Northwest Passage from the Pacific side of North America.

[事实] On 18 January 1778, the expedition sighted Oahu and Kauai, islands then unknown to Europeans but inhabited by a sophisticated Polynesian society with farming, fishing, temples, priests, chiefs, and kings.

[事实] The British were surprised that Hawaiian speech resembled Tahitian, while the Hawaiians appeared to assume that all people spoke related languages.

[事实] Lieutenant John Williamson panicked during an early shoreline encounter and fatally shot a Hawaiian, probably a chief, but the incident did not immediately destroy relations.

[推测] This first killing demonstrated how quickly curiosity and trade could turn into violence through fear, crowding, and incompatible expectations.

[12:37] **The Failed Search for the Northwest Passage**

[事实] Cook left Hawaii after less than two weeks and sailed north along the Pacific coast of North America, through the Bering Sea, and toward the Arctic.

[事实] The expedition encountered Indigenous peoples around Vancouver Island and the Chukchi people in eastern Siberia, while surgeon William Anderson died from tuberculosis.

[事实] On 18 August 1778, the ships reached an impassable wall of Arctic ice near Icy Cape and abandoned the attempt to proceed farther north.

[事实] Cook became increasingly volatile, at one point insisting that his weakened crew eat repellent walrus meat and reacting furiously when they petitioned against it.

[推测] The failed mission, deaths and illnesses among his confidants, harsh conditions, and burden of command appear to have contributed to a serious deterioration in Cook’s judgment.

[19:27] **Return to Hawaii and Fraying Discipline**

[事实] The expedition returned to Hawaii in November 1778 after ten months of hardship, and the crews initially received an exceptionally friendly welcome.

[事实] Cook relaxed his earlier restrictions on sexual contact between sailors and local women, despite previously fearing the transmission of venereal disease.

[事实] He also attempted to preserve the ships’ rum by making a sugarcane drink and angrily threatened to withhold grog when the crew refused to drink it.

[推测] These episodes reinforced the impression among later observers that Cook’s discipline and emotional stability were deteriorating.

[23:34] **The Ceremonial Reception at Kealakekua Bay**

[事实] In January 1779, the ships anchored at Kealakekua Bay, where hundreds of canoes surrounded them and large numbers of Hawaiians crowded aboard.

[事实] Priests and chiefs restored order, wrapped Cook in red cloth, offered him pigs and coconuts, and repeatedly used a word heard by the British as “Orono.”

[事实] Cook was led to a temple containing human skulls and an altar, anointed with pig fat, seated near an idol, and later conducted through crowds who prostrated themselves before him.

[事实] Surgeon David Samwell interpreted the ceremony as investing Cook with the title and dignity of Orono, a status associated with divinity.

[推测] Cook seems to have accepted the ceremonies as gestures of welcome and respect without understanding the obligations or expectations they may have created.

[28:56] **Was Cook Regarded as the God Lono?**

[事实] Anthropologist Marshall Sahlins argued that Cook arrived during the Makahiki fertility festival celebrating the return of Lono, the god of abundance.

[事实] Cook’s route around Hawaii, the white sails of his ships, the white cloths displayed by the crowds, and his arrival at the festival’s destination closely resembled the ritual journey of Lono’s effigy.

[事实] Gananath Obeyesekere rejected the claim that Hawaiians literally saw Cook as a god, arguing that “Lono” could also designate a ruler and that the divine interpretation reflected colonial projection.

[推测] The categories of “human,” “divine,” and “supernatural” used in modern Western debate may be inadequate for describing how eighteenth-century Hawaiians understood Cook.

[推测] Because Hawaiian society was profoundly transformed after European contact, the precise meaning of Cook’s reception may no longer be recoverable.

[34:09] **The King’s Visit and an Expected Departure**

[事实] On 26 January, the Hawaiian king Kalaniʻōpuʻu—called “Terreeoboo” by the British—visited Cook in great state and presented valuable food, clothing, and featherwork.

[事实] Hawaiians treated Cook’s shore-based observatory and scientific instruments as sacred objects.

[事实] In the following days, the king and chiefs repeatedly asked when the expedition would leave and appeared relieved when Cook said it would depart soon.

[事实] The British sailed away on 4 February after an emotional farewell, but a storm split the Resolution’s foremast and forced Cook to choose between continuing to Maui and returning to Kealakekua.

[推测] The lavish royal visit may have been intended as a ceremonial conclusion, making the expedition’s unexpected return especially disruptive.

[42:46] **An Unwelcome Return**

[事实] Cook returned to Kealakekua Bay on 11 February, finding the shore unusually quiet and receiving a much colder welcome.

[事实] Hawaiians showed less interest in ordinary trade goods, sought daggers, mocked the British, and became involved in repeated thefts and confrontations.

[事实] The king appeared disturbed by Cook’s return and demanded to know why the ships had come back and how long they intended to remain.

[推测] Hawaiians may simply have been exhausted by the visitors’ consumption of food and resources or worried about foreign occupation.

[推测] If Cook had been associated with Lono, his return after the festival ended may have violated the ritual calendar or exposed him as an ordinary man who had deceived them.

[46:16] **Violence Escalates**

[事实] After a Hawaiian stole tools, the British caught and flogged him, provoking warnings from a chief that further violence would bring retaliation.

[事实] British sailors pursuing stolen property were surrounded, beaten, pelted with stones, and publicly humiliated.

[事实] Cook ordered muskets loaded with full-sized balls rather than small shot and stated that force had become necessary to prevent Hawaiians from believing they had gained an advantage.

[事实] During the night, a cutter belonging to the Discovery was stolen, leading Cook to blockade the bay and authorize fire against escaping canoes.

[推测] By this stage, each side interpreted the other’s actions as evidence of hostility, making even familiar practices such as theft, punishment, and hostage-taking far more dangerous.

[52:00] **The Attempt to Take the King Hostage**

[事实] Cook decided to take Kalaniʻōpuʻu aboard the Resolution as a hostage until the stolen cutter was returned, a tactic he had previously used elsewhere in Polynesia.

[事实] He went ashore with Lieutenant Molesworth Phillips and ten Marines, believing Hawaiian resistance would collapse before musket fire.

[事实] The king initially agreed to accompany Cook, but one of his wives and several chiefs intervened near the beach and urged him not to go.

[事实] Hundreds of armed Hawaiians gathered as chanting, humming, conch shells, and offshore gunfire intensified the confusion.

[推测] Cook’s reliance on technological superiority caused him to underestimate both the crowd’s determination and the political consequences of abducting the king.

[56:49] **The Battle on the Beach and Cook’s Death**

[事实] No surviving account was written by someone directly beside Cook on the beach; the most accepted reconstruction relies heavily on Samwell, who interviewed participants afterward.

[事实] Hawaiians threw stones, the Marines fired, and Williamson’s launch withdrew after apparently misinterpreting Cook’s signal to approach and rescue the shore party.

[事实] Several Marines dropped their unloaded muskets and fled into the sea, while others were surrounded and killed; Phillips returned through the water to rescue a drowning Marine.

[事实] Cook was struck from behind with a club, stabbed in the neck, beaten in the shallows, and killed after being left as the last British man standing on the beach.

[推测] Cook’s failure to flee may have reflected the naval officer’s code against displaying fear, confusion over the boats’ withdrawal, or a degree of fatalism, but the evidence cannot establish his motive.

[64:40] **Cook’s Remains and Hawaiian Beliefs**

[事实] Captain Charles Clerke rejected immediate large-scale reprisals and prioritized recovering the expedition’s foremast and Cook’s body.

[事实] Hawaiians first returned a piece of Cook’s burned flesh and later delivered his hands, scalp, skull, jaw, feet, and other bones, some of which had been salted or exposed to fire.

[事实] Cook’s remains were committed to the sea on 22 February, and the ships departed the following day.

[事实] Some Hawaiians reportedly asked when Cook would return, while one recorded statement declared that the god Cook was not dead but sleeping and would come again.

[推测] These statements could reflect genuine belief in Cook’s supernatural return, ritual language of condolence, or an attempt to seek reconciliation; the episode treats their meaning as unresolved.

[68:14] **The Expedition’s Return and Cook’s Martyrdom**

[事实] The expedition made another unsuccessful attempt to find the Northwest Passage before returning home in October 1780, with William Bligh performing much of the navigation.

[事实] News of Cook’s death reached London eleven months after the event, by which time he was celebrated as a national hero and martyr.

[事实] British paintings commonly portrayed him restraining his men from firing and dying as “a victim of his own humanity,” although the episode argues that his final signal was probably a request for rescue.

[事实] Cook’s widow Elizabeth received a royal pension, survived him by 56 years, and died in 1835 without living descendants.

[推测] The martyr narrative suppressed awkward evidence about Cook’s worsening temper, violent reprisals, and mistakes during his final voyage.

[71:40] **Cook’s Contested Legacy**

[事实] Cook’s voyages charted much of the Pacific, contributed to the identification of numerous species, advanced methods of combating scurvy, and revealed the richness of Polynesian civilizations to European audiences.

[事实] Earlier biographies emphasized his relative tolerance and restraint compared with other European explorers, while recent critics portray him as an agent of colonial violence.

[事实] The hosts conclude that his third voyage complicates the image of Cook as a consistent representative of Enlightenment ideals because those ideals and his official instructions were internally contradictory.

[推测] Cook’s comparatively reserved personality may make it easier for later generations to project onto him either the virtues of Enlightenment and empire or the evils of Western expansion.

[推测] His achievements remain extraordinary, but the final voyage shows how prolonged strain, cultural incomprehension, and unequal power could undermine his professed ideals.

## 播客点评/总结

The episode’s greatest strength is its combination of chronological storytelling with a serious examination of historical interpretation. It reconstructs the chain of decisions leading to Cook’s death while repeatedly distinguishing documented events from later heroic imagery and disputed anthropological theories.

Its discussion of the Lono controversy is particularly valuable because it resists a simple answer. Rather than confidently translating Hawaiian ritual into modern Western categories, the hosts emphasize the limits imposed by cultural distance, colonial disruption, and incomplete sources.

A limitation is that much of the Hawaiian perspective reaches the audience through British journals and later Western scholarship. The hosts acknowledge this problem, but the surviving evidence still gives British officers far more individual voices and motives than it gives the Hawaiians.

[推测] The episode is especially suitable for listeners interested in exploration, colonial encounters, anthropology, leadership under pressure, and the construction of historical reputations. Its broader lesson is that Cook’s death resulted less from a single ritual or betrayal than from accumulated exhaustion, misunderstanding, coercion, and escalating violence.
